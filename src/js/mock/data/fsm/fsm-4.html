As explained in Chapters 2 and 3, a sentencing court’s first step in imposing a guidelines sentence is to identify the defendant’s criminal history score and the seriousness ranking of his crime. Having done that, the court then locates the block on “[t]he centerpiece of the sentencing guidelines,” “the 99-block Sentencing Guidelines Grid,” where the crime seriousness ranking and criminal history score intersect. State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 487, 847 P2d 834 (1993).

“Each grid block contains what is called a ‘presumptive sentence,’” 315 Or at 487, i.e., “the sentence provided in a grid block for an offender classified in that grid block by the combined effect of the crime seriousness ranking of the current crime of conviction and the offender’s criminal history or a sentence designated as a presumptive sentence by statute.” OAR 213-003-0001(16). A presumptive guidelines sentence is a term of imprisonment, a term of probation, or a straight jail term. A solid black line on the sentencing grid—the “dispositional line,” OAR 213-003-0001(7)—separates the imprisonment from the probationary/straight jail grid blocks. Forty-six of the 99 grid blocks specify probationary/straight jail presumptive sentences. Davis, 315 Or at 487 n 8. The other 53 grid blocks specify presumptive sentences of imprisonment.

This chapter discusses felony sentences of imprisonment, including those stemming from the 53 presumptive imprisonment grid blocks. It also discusses various aspects of felony prison sentences, including the ways in which a defendant may be relieved from serving his entire prison sentence. Finally, it discusses post-prison supervision (PPS)—the guidelines version of parole. Chapter 5 discusses probationary and straight jail sentences, including the 46 probationary/straight jail grid blocks.

§ 4-1. OAR 213-005-0001 PLACE AND TERM OF INCARCERATION.

OAR 213-005-0001 sets out the basic requirements of imprisonment grid blocks:

(1) If an offense is classified in a grid block above the dispositional line, the presumptive sentence shall be a term of imprisonment within the durational range of months stated in the grid block. The sentencing judge should select the center of the range in the usual case and reserve the upper and lower limits for aggravating and mitigating factors insufficient to warrant a departure.

(2) Terms of incarceration 12 months or less shall be served at the direction of the supervisory authority. Terms of incarceration greater than 12 months shall be served in the legal and physical custody of the Department.

(3) Notwithstanding the term of imprisonment imposed by the sentencing court, and as authorized by the court pursuant to ORS 137.750 for crimes committed on or after December 5, 1996, an offender who enters and successfully completes a special alternative incarceration program, in accordance with the rules and procedures adopted by the Department of Corrections pursuant to ORS 421.500 et. seq., may be released early to serve the term of post-prison supervision imposed as part of the original sentence.

(4) Notwithstanding section (2) of this rule:

(a) Terms of incarceration 12 months or less imposed pursuant to ORS 166.070(2) shall be served in the legal and physical custody of the Department; and

(b) Offenders sentenced under 2011 Or Laws ch 598 shall serve a mandatory minimum term of incarceration of 90 days, without reduction for any reason.

§ 4-1.1. Presumptive Sentences.

OAR 213-005-0001(1) explains that each presumptive imprisonment grid block states a range of months the sentencing court normally must use in imposing a term of imprisonment. This range of months is called the presumptive range. If the court sentences within the presumptive range, it must specify a particular number of months. See State v. Montazer, 133 Or App 271, 273, 891 P2d 662, rev den, 321 Or 268 (1995) (court erred in imposing sentence of “up to 18 months”). The court may depart from the presumptive range—i.e., impose a “departure sentence” of above or below the high or low end of the range—but only when there are “substantial and compelling reasons” to do so.1 State v. Woodin, 131 Or App 171, 175-76, 883 P2d 1332 (1994) (“[u]nder the guidelines, the maximum sentence is the presumptive sentence, unless the court finds substantial and compelling reasons to impose a departure”). As discussed in Chapter 9, if the court sentences within the presumptive range, the criminal code imposes strict limitations on the defendant’s ability to challenge the sentence on appeal.

The first sentence of OAR 213-005-0001(2) must be read in conjunction with ORS 137.124(2)(b). The rule provides, “Terms of incarceration 12 months or less shall be served at the direction of the supervisory authority.” OAR 213-005-0001(2). Although this mirrors the text of ORS 137.124(2)(a), ORS 137.124(2)(b) states:

“Notwithstanding paragraph (a) of this subsection, when the court imposes a sentence upon conviction of a felony that includes a term of incarceration that is 12 months or less, the court shall commit the defendant to the legal and physical custody of the Department of Corrections if the court orders that the term of incarceration be served consecutively to a term of incarceration that exceeds 12 months that was imposed in a previous proceeding or in the same proceeding by a court of this state upon conviction of a felony.”

When read together, the rule and the statute establish that all felony sentences of 12 months or less must be served locally (in county jail), even if they are consecutive and exceed a 12-month total. State v. Nason, 184 Or App 114, 55 P3d 525 (2002) (per curiam). But if a felony sentence of less than 12 months is consecutive to a felony sentence of greater than 12 months, both sentences are served in a state Department of Corrections (DOC) facility.

ORS 213-005-0001(4)(a) creates an exception to the general requirement that felony sentences of 12 months or less, and that are not consecutive to a sentence of greater than 12 months, must be served locally. Any sentence for a conviction under ORS 166.070(2) must be served in the DOC. Moreover, ORS 213-005-0001(4)(b) creates a mandatory minimum term of 90 days for felony driving under the influence of intoxicants (DUII). See Section 4-2.9.

ORS 213-005-0001(3) authorizes certain defendants’ early release from incarceration for successfully completing certain programs. Those programs are discussed later in this chapter.

4-2. STATUTORILY MANDATED IMPRISONMENT.

Many classes of defendants are subject to statutory sentencing schemes, each of which is discussed below.

It is important to recognize that sometimes, more than one of the schemes will apply to a defendant. Regardless of that, the guidelines still apply to defendants who are subject to the statutory schemes. The terms of imprisonment specified by the guidelines and the statutory schemes often conflict. OAR 213-009-0001 and ORS 137.637 address this conflict, and resolve it in favor of the longer, or the longest, of the sentences. OAR 213-009-0001 states:

(1) If a mandatory prison sentence is required or authorized by statute, the sentence imposed shall be that determinate sentence or the sentence under these rules whichever is longer.

(2) If the provisions of ORS 137.635, require the imprisonment of an offender for whom the grid provides presumptive probation, the offender shall be imprisoned for a duration determined as follows:

(a) 11-12 months for an offense classified in Grid Block 7-I;

(b) 12-13 months for an offense classified in Grid Block 7-H;

(c) 13-14 months for an offense classified in Grid Block 7-G; and

(d) 14-15 months for an offense classified in Grid Block 7-F.

(3) Notwithstanding subsection (1) of this rule, if it is the first time an offender is subject to the provisions of ORS 161. 610(4)(a), the court may impose a lesser sentence in accordance with these rules.

Application of this provision and its parallel statute, ORS 137.637, are addressed in the discussions of the statutory schemes.

§ 4-2.1. ORS 137.635 Defendants.

ORS 137.635 is a recidivist scheme intended to ensure that certain repeat offenders serve prison sentences, and that they serve those sentences in their entirety. The electorate created ORS 137.635 in 1988 when it adopted Ballot Measure 4. Or Laws 1989, ch 1, § 4. The measure was placed on the ballot by initiative petition. The chief petitioner was former Congressman Denny Smith, so the measure sometimes is called “Denny Smith.” This manual usually refers to the law as ORS 137.635, or as Measure 4.

ORS 137.635 applies only to certain crimes committed on or after January 1, 1990. State v. Haydon, 116 Or App 347, 349, 842 P2d 410 (1992); State v. Gouveia, 116 Or App 86, 88, 840 P2d 753 (1992); Or Laws 1989, ch 1, § 4. Because the guidelines apply to all crimes committed on or after November 1, 1989, ORS 137.010(1), all ORS 137.635 defendants are guidelines defendants.

The class of defendants to whom the statute applies is exclusive. ORS 137.635(2). It includes defendants sentenced for any of 10 crimes—murder and the first-degree versions of manslaughter, assault, kidnapping, rape, sodomy, unlawful sexual penetration, burglary, arson, or robbery—and who have one or more “predicates,” i.e., prior convictions, for any of those same 10 crimes. ORS 137.635(2). When a court sentences such a defendant, it must impose a determinate sentence of imprisonment. ORS 137.635(1). (As discussed in Chapter 1, a determinate sentence is one which a separate entity, such a parole board, may not set aside.)

Sometimes a court imposes sentence on a crime that is not subject to ORS 137.635, concurrent to a sentence on a crime that is subject to ORS 137.635. In that scenario, if the court declares the defendant eligible for earned-time credit on the sentence for his non-ORS 137.635 conviction, the Department of Corrections (DOC) must award him earned-time credit on the sentence for his non-ORS 137.635 conviction, notwithstanding that he is serving that sentence concurrent with his sentence on his ORS 137.635 conviction.

EXAMPLE: Samson v. Brown, 310 Or App 319, 486 P3d 59 (2021), a state habeas corpus case, stemmed from a criminal prosecution. Samson had incurred two non-ORS 137.635 convictions and one ORS 137.635 conviction. The trial court imposed 40-month sentences on each of the non-ORS 137.635 convictions, and declared Samson eligible for earned-time credit on both sentences. The court imposed a 60-month sentence on Samson’s ORS 137.635 conviction, so he was ineligible for earned-time credit on that sentence. The court ordered the 40-month sentences consecutive to each other, but concurrent to the 60-month sentence. If Samson earned maximum earned-time credit of 20% on the non-ORS 137.635 sentences, ORS 421.121(2), he would serve 64 months [(40 + 40) – 20% = 64] on those sentences. While serving those sentences, he first would complete the 60-month ORS 137.635 sentence.

Despite the express terms of the judgment, the DOC fabricated a mass incarceration-oriented theory. It theorized that because the consecutive non-ORS 137.635 sentences were concurrent with the ORS 137.635 sentence, Samson could not earn time credit on the non-ORS 137.635 sentences, so denied him earned-time credit eligibility. Samson, 310 Or App at 320.2 Samson petitioned from the DOC’s fabricated theory. The circuit court granted state habeas relief and ordered the DOC to abort its fabricated theory. Appallingly, the state Department of Justice embraced the DOC’s fabricated a mass incarceration-oriented theory, and appealed. But the Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court’s order, granting Samson state habeas relief. Id. at 328.

Woods v. Hendricks, 328 Or App 207, ___ P3d ___ (2023) is a follow up from Samson v. Brown. Plaintiff Woods was convicted of attempted second-degree robbery, and had a "60-month sentence" "into two parts: a 42-month term served concurrent to" a separate crime sentenced under Measure 11, "and an 18-month term served consecutive to" that separate Measure 11 crime. 328 Or App at 210-11. "Under plaintiff’s interpretation, he would be considered for a 20-percent reduction of the entire 60-month sentence, reducing his sentence * * * by 12 months. Whereas, under the superintendent’s interpretation, plaintiff would be considered for a 20-percent reduction of only the 18-month term, reducing his sentence by about 3.5 months." Id. at 211. The Court of Appeals "conclude[d] that the sentencing judgment for [attempted second-degree robbery] provides that plaintiff is eligible for earned-time credit under ORS 421.121 for the entire sentence for that count, the trial court erred in dismissing plaintiff’s habeas petition." Id. at 215. Thus, his sentence on that count could be reduced to 48 months.

On the other hand, the utility of prior convictions, which would be used as “predicates” to subject a current sentence to ORS 137.635, may be collaterally attacked on the ground that the prior convictions are void, because they were obtained in violation of constitutional requirements. If they are void, they may not be used as ORS 137.635 predicates. See Chapter 1. Apart from that, ORS 137.635 presently allows bench trials using the preponderance standard, rather than jury trials using the reasonable doubt standard, on the existence of a defendant’s record of prior convictions. To that extent, the present scheme is subject to attack under Sixth Amendment jury trial principles. See Chapter 1.

ORS 137.635(1) speaks in terms of convictions “as defined in” specified Oregon statutes. See generally ORS 137.635(2). An out-of-state conviction, State v. Rosson, 145 Or App 574, 931 P2d 807, rev den, 325 Or 369 (1997), or an Oregon conviction under a statutory predecessor to the current criminal code provision, State v. Andre, 142 Or App 285, 920 P2d 1145, rev den, 324 Or 229 (1996), may be used as a predicate if it has the same elements—the same definition—as a crime that ORS 137.635(2) enumerates. This element-matching definition implicates the Sixth Amendment jury trial requirement that applies to the “characteristics” of prior convictions that recidivist schemes would use as predicates. Section 1-7.8.1 discusses this requirement. In situations where the state would use sister-state or federal court convictions as predicates, defense counsel should argue that the state must meet the Sixth Amendment requirements in order to do so.

However, a prior conviction qualifies as a predicate only if the defendant was convicted of it before committing the crime for which he would be sentenced under ORS 137.635. State v. Allison, 143 Or App 241, 923 P2d 1224 (in banc), rev den, 324 Or 487 (1996). This means that for the statute to apply, the order of a defendant’s crimes and convictions must have been: predicate crime, conviction, current crime, conviction. Current crime, current crime, conviction, conviction does not work (notwithstanding that it might work under other recidivist schemes, such as the guidelines criminal-history scoring system, see Chapter 3). See also State v. Casiano, 214 Or App 509, 166 P3d 599 (2007) (where court, without objection, erroneously relied on current crime as predicate, to impose sentence under ORS 137.635, appellate court exercised discretion to address error and to reverse and remand for resentencing).

ORS 137.635(3) states: “When the court imposes a sentence under this section, the court shall indicate in the judgment that the defendant is subject to this section.” State v. Clark, 146 Or App 590, 933 P2d 984 (1997) (trial court erred in refusing to sentence defendant in accordance with ORS 137.635). This provision indicates that unless the judgment subjects the defendant to ORS 137.635, the DOC may not treat him as a Measure 4 defendant.

PRACTICE TIP: Notwithstanding the clear language of ORS 137.635(3), the DOC has been known to subject a defendant to ORS 137.635 even if the judgment is silent on the matter. The DOC overlooks the fact that a judgment’s silence on ORS 137.635’s application may be the product of plea negotiations, or may reflect the fact that the trial court found that the Measure 4 predicate is void. To try to fend off the possibility of the DOC invalidly subjecting a defendant’s sentence to Measure 4, defense counsel should ask that rather than leaving the judgment silent about ORS 137.635, the judgment should expressly state that the defendant is not subject to ORS 137.635. Presumably, the DOC would be disinclined to invalidate such an express term. Cf. Gaynor v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 165 Or App 609, 614, 996 P2d 1020 (2000) (Parole Board erred in correcting erroneously short PPS term).

§ 4-2.1.1. Guidelines Accommodation of ORS 137.635.

After the guidelines first took effect, there was a flurry of litigation over whether they limited the sentence a court could impose on an ORS 137.635 defendant. Guidelines commentary addressed this issue, stating:

“The [State Sentencing] Guidelines Board concluded that, absent a clear policy expression to the contrary, the legislature intended [for the guidelines] to be consistent with Ballot Measure 4, 1988 General Election (codified as ORS 137.635), and all statutes requiring or authorizing mandatory prison sentences. [OAR 213-009-0001] implements that interpretation of legislative intent.”

Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 151 (1989) (hereafter Implementation Manual).

On reconsideration in State v. Haydon, the Court of Appeals essentially embraced this commentary. The court held that the guidelines “limit[] the length of prison terms for offenders subject to ORS 137.635.” 116 Or App at 355. See also State v. Sullivan, 172 Or App 688, 19 P3d 1001 (2001) (fact that defendant was subject to ORS 137.700 mandatory minimum and ORS 137.635 did not authorize trial court to impose ORS 161.605 statutory maximum sentence).

§ 4-2.1.2. ORS 137.635 Defendants Convicted of Level 7 Crimes.

Ordinarily, the presumptive sentence for defendants in grid blocks 7-F to 7-I is three years’ probation. But the court may not impose probation on Measure 4 defendants. ORS 137.635(1). OAR 213-009-0001(2) establishes special presumptive sentencing ranges for Measure 4 defendants who fall into those four grid blocks.

The special presumptive ranges apply only to defendants convicted of level 7 first-degree burglary and who have criminal history scores of F or G. This is because level 7 burglary is the only crime listed in ORS 137.635 that may be ranked as low as level 7. A Measure 4 defendant must have at least one prior felony conviction, and, as explained in Chapter 3, a defendant with at least one prior felony conviction will have a criminal history score of at least as high as G. Moreover, if a Measure 4 defendant incurs a consecutive sentence for an additional offense having a “true” sentencing grid block of higher than 7-I, but the “shift-to-I” rule (see Chapter 8) requires the court to use grid block 7-I, the special sentencing range for grid block 7-I does not apply. Instead, the incarceration limitations of standard grid block 7-I apply. State v. Nicholas, 118 Or App 232, 235-36, 846 P2d 1181 (1993). Consequently, the special ranges in grid blocks 7-H and 7-I will never be used to sentence a Measure 4 defendant.

§ 4-2.2. Firearm-Minimum Sentences.

In pertinent part, the firearm-minimum statute, ORS 161.610, provides:

“(4) The minimum terms of imprisonment for felonies having as an element the defendant’s use or threatened use of a firearm in the commission of the crime shall be as follows:

“(a) Except as provided in subsection (5) of this section, upon the first conviction for such felony, five years, except that if the firearm is a machine gun, short-barreled rifle, short-barreled shotgun or is equipped with a firearms silencer, the term of imprisonment shall be 10 years.

“(b) Upon conviction for such felony committed after punishment pursuant to paragraph (a) of this subsection or subsection (5) of this section, 10 years, except that if the firearm is a machine gun, short-barreled rifle, short-barreled shotgun or is equipped with a firearms silencer, the term of imprisonment shall be 20 years.

“(c) Upon conviction for such felony committed after imprisonment pursuant to paragraph (b) of this subsection, 30 years.

“(5) If it is the first time that the defendant is subject to punishment under this section, rather than impose the sentence otherwise required by subsection (4)(a) of this section, the court may:

“(a) For felonies committed prior to November 1, 1989, suspend the execution of the sentence or impose a lesser term of imprisonment, when the court expressly finds mitigating circumstances justifying such lesser sentence and sets forth those circumstances in its statement on sentencing; or

“(b) For felonies committed on or after November 1, 1989, impose a lesser sentence in accordance with the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.

“(6) When a defendant who is convicted of a felony having as an element the defendant’s use or threatened use of a firearm during the commission of the crime is a person who was waived from juvenile court under ORS 137.707 (5)(b)(A), 419C.349, 419C.352, 419C.364 or 419C.370, the court is not required to impose a minimum term of imprisonment under this section.”

As seen, a first firearm offense carries a discretionary minimum of five years, or of 10 years if the firearm was silenced, sawed off, or fully automatic. A second firearm offense carries a mandatory minimum of 10 years, or of 20 years if the firearm was silenced, sawed off, or fully automatic. A third firearm offense carries a mandatory minimum of 30 years.

Various cases explain the interplay between the guidelines and the firearm-minimum statute. For example, in State v. Walker, 117 Or App 527, 842 P2d 817 (1992) (per curiam), rev den, 315 Or 644 (1993), the sentencing court imposed both a 120-month firearm-minimum sentence and a 65-month guidelines sentence. Applying OAR 213-009-0001 and ORS 137.637, the Court of Appeals held that because the guidelines sentence was shorter than the firearm-minimum sentence, the court erred in imposing the guidelines sentence in addition to the longer firearm-minimum (although the error was harmless, because it had no adverse consequences). 117 Or App at 528.

Conversely, in State v. Stalder, 117 Or App 289, 844 P2d 225 (1992), the Court of Appeals said that because the defendant’s guidelines sentence was longer than the firearm-minimum sentence, the guidelines sentence controls. But Stalder further explained that “[t]he mandatory limitations of OAR 213-009-0001(1) and ORS 137.637 apply only to the incarceration term. They do not apply in any way to limit the application of the other provisions of ORS 161.610.” 117 Or App at 292 (citation omitted).

The “other provisions of ORS 161.610” to which Stalder refers are the enhancement provisions of ORS 161.610(4)(b)-(c) for subsequent firearm-minimum offenses. Stalder thus requires the sentencing court to designate in the judgment that a firearm-minimum defendant is in fact such a defendant, even if OAR 213-009-0001(1) and ORS 137.637 require the court to impose a longer sentence from a different statutory scheme. 117 Or App at 292. Imposing the firearm minimum establishes a clear record so that in the event of a subsequent firearm-minimum conviction, the sentencing court will enhance the defendant’s firearm-minimum sentence as required by ORS 161.610(4)(b) or (c).

Moreover, the “enhancement fact” scheme, ORS 136.760, et seq., which is discussed in Chapter 1, may apply in imposing repeat firearm-minimum sentences.

The repeat firearm-minimum statute is a recidivist scheme. Prior firearm-minimum convictions serve as predicates, which authorize imposing repeat firearm-minimum sentences. Because the prosecution seeks repeat firearm-minimum sentences, the prosecution has the burden of proving the existence of prior firearm-minimum convictions in order to use them as predicates. See Chapter 3 (discussing ORS 137.079(5)(c) and OAR 213-004-0013(3)).

Thus, predicates are used to enhance a defendant’s sentence. Generally, the prosecution must prove to the jury beyond a reasonable doubt facts that would be used to enhance a defendant’s sentence. Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 US 466, 120 S Ct 2348, 147 L Ed 2d 435 (2000) (which Chapter 1 discusses). But there is an exception to the Apprendi rule, that being “the fact of a prior conviction[.]” 530 US at 490. Because a prior conviction serves as a predicate, proof of the existence of the predicate is exempt from the Apprendi rule.

But then there is an exception to the Apprendi rule’s exception, created by Shepard v. United States, 544 US 13, 125 S Ct 1254, 161 L Ed 2d 205 (2005) (discussed in Chapter 1). When the prosecution must prove not just the existence of a predicate, but also must prove factual characteristics of the predicate, the prosecution must prove the characteristics in accordance with the Apprendi rule. Shepard, 544 US at 24–26. Because proof of the factual characteristics is subject to the Apprendi rule, that proof is subject to the “enhancement fact” scheme. ORS 136.760, et seq.3

EXAMPLE: State v. Bray, 342 Or 711, 168 P3d 983 (2007) provides an example of Shepard imposing the Apprendi rule in proving the characteristics of prior convictions used as predicates. There, the trial court relied on a non-juried finding of Aggravating Factor D, persistent involvement in similar offenses, to impose a departure. The finding was grounded on the defendant’s record of prior convictions. But the factor could be used only if the priors had certain characteristics—specifically, that the priors, in juxtaposition to the defendant’s current crimes, reflected “persistent involvement in similar offenses.” The finding of that characteristic was non-juried, so the trial court’s use of Factor D to impose an aggravated departure violated the Apprendi rule. Bray, 342 Or at 724.

As explained, in order to use a prior conviction as a predicate to impose a repeat firearm-minimum sentence, the prosecution must prove not just the existence of the conviction, but also that the crime of conviction involved use of a firearm and, thus, that the prior conviction was a firearm crime. At least arguably, the use of a firearm is a factual characteristic of the prior conviction. Under Shepard and Bray, proof of the factual characteristic is subject to the Apprendi rule, so also is subject to the “enhancement fact” scheme. ORS 136.760, et seq.

PRACTICE TIP: Quite similarly, various other recidivist schemes in Chapter 4 apply when current crimes of convictions may be used as predicates if the stem from “separate criminal episodes.” But Section 1-7.9.1 explains that predicates’ “separate criminal episodes” are characteristics, so proof of those characteristics are subject to the Apprendi rule and to the “enhancement fact” scheme. ORS 136.760, et seq. Moreover, the Chapter 1 Update enlarges Section 1-7.9.1, in that it addresses both State v. Thornsberry, 315 Or App 287, 501 P3d 1 (2021) and State v. Mallory, 213 Or App 392, 403, 162 P3d 297 (2007). In Thornsberry, the trial court erred in applying the major felony sex offender law, ORS 137.690, because the prosecution failed to comply with the Apprendi rule in proving that current crime predicates stemmed from separate episodes. Similarly, in Mallory, the court held that the repeat property offender (RPO) scheme, ORS 137.717, permits using crimes sentenced. In cases where the prosecution seeks to use current crimes as predicates, but fails to comply with the Apprendi rule and the “enhancement fact” scheme to prove the predicates’ stem from separate episodes, defense counsel should raise the proper objections to application of the various recidivist schemes.

Assuming he otherwise meets the program’s eligibility requirements, a firearm-minimum defendant may be eligible for the DOC’s earned-early release program. Haffey v. Keeney, 84 Or App 607, 735 P2d 16 (1987); ORS 161.610(3). The earned-early release program is discussed later in this chapter.

§ 4-2.2.1. Waivers of Firearm-Minimum Sentences.

The firearm-minimum statute says that for a defendant’s first firearm-minimum offense, the court need not impose the five- or 10-year term provided in ORS 161.610(4)(a). Instead, the court has discretion to “impose a lesser sentence in accordance with the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.” ORS 161.610(5)(b); see also OAR 213-009-0001(3). But unlike the situation in pre-guidelines cases, where the finding of “mitigating circumstances” guides the court in choosing between imposing a firearm-minimum or shorter sentence, ORS 161.610(5)(a), in guidelines-era cases, no findings are required for the court to decline to impose a first-time gun-minimum sentence. ORS 161.610(5)(b).

Similarly, a court is not required to impose firearm minimums on juvenile defendants who were remanded to adult court either under the ORS 137.707, or under the juvenile code’s remand provision. ORS 161.610(6). This is true even if the remanded juvenile is a repeat firearm-minimum offender.

§ 4-2.2.2. Firearm-Minimum Sentences & Consecutive Terms.

A firearm-minimum sentence is not a “presumptive” sentence, State v. Johnson, 125 Or App 655, 658, 866 P2d 1245 (1994); therefore, it is, by default, a “departure” sentence. See OAR 213-003-0001(5) (“‘[d]eparture’ means a sentence * * * which is inconsistent with the presumptive sentence for an offender”). First-time firearm minimums are discretionary rather than mandatory, so they should be subject to the guidelines’ 400 percent rule’s limits on consecutive sentences. Johnson, 125 Or App at 659; State v. Clark, 114 Or App 631, 632, 836 P2d 183 (1992). On the other hand, repeat firearm minimums are mandatory, so are excluded from the limitation of the 400 percent rule, which is discussed in Chapter 7. Accord State v. Langdon, 330 Or 72, 999 P2d 1127 (2000) (400 percent rule does not apply to mandatory Measure 11 minimums).

Firearm minimums may be run consecutive only with other types of sentences, but they may not be run consecutive with each other. That is because a court may impose, at most, only one firearm minimum in a given case. State v. Claggett, 245 Or App 491, 263 P2d 1109 (2011) (per curiam); State v. Thiehoff, 169 Or App 630, 10 P3d 322 (2000); State v. Cleveland, 148 Or App 97, 99, 939 P2d 94, rev den, 325 Or 621 (1997).

When the defendant incurs multiple convictions that are eligible for imposition of a gun-minimum, the court is not required to impose the minimum on the first eligible count on which it imposes sentence. Instead, the court is free to impose the minimum on any eligible count. State v. Pouncey, 303 Or App 365, 370-71, 464 P3d 448 (2020).

Moreover, a court may not treat a defendant as a repeat firearm-minimum defendant until “after [he has finished] punishment” for a prior firearm-minimum sentence. ORS 161.610(b) and (c). In other words, a court may not impose a repeat firearm-minimum sentence unless the defendant committed his subsequent firearm-minimum offense after he completed serving his prior firearm-minimum sentence. State v. Wells, 82 Or App 283, 286, 728 P2d 533 (1986) (dicta); State v. Grant, 60 Or App 165, 166, 653 P2d 242 (1982) (per curiam).

§ 4-2.2.3. Other Limitations on Firearm-Minimum Sentences.

A court may not impose a firearm minimum unless the state pled and the trier of fact found beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant personally used a firearm. State v. Wimberly, 152 Or App 154, 157-58, 952 P2d 1042 (1998). If only the defendant’s accomplice used a firearm, the court may not hold the defendant vicariously liable and impose a firearm minimum. State v. Wedge, 293 Or 598, 608-09, 652 P2d 773 (1982). Regardless of a defendant’s intent, without actual or threatened use of a firearm, a sentencing court may not impose a firearm minimum. E.g., State v. Pies, 104 Or App 646, 651, 802 P2d 702 (1990). The defendant must have used or threatened the use of the firearm as a firearm, and not, for example, as a club, to be liable for a firearm minimum. State v. Harris, 174 Or App 105, 25 P3d 404 (2001). Furthermore, the defendant’s prior firearm-related conviction must be an Oregon conviction. State v. Hilton, 187 Or App 666, 677, 69 P3d 779 (2003) (trial court erred in basing repeat, 10-year firearm minimum on prior firearm conviction from Virginia).

Finally, a defendant who uses an inoperable firearm, but threatens to use it as a firearm, is subject to the firearm minimum statute. ORS 161.610(2). But the firearm minimum statute does require proof of a culpable mental state; instead, the mental state will depend solely on the underlying charge to which firearm minimum statute is attached. State v. Giron-Cortez, 322 Or App 274, 282-84, 519 P3d 879 (2022), rev allowed, 370 Or 822 (2023).

Nevertheless, and as noted above, the Supreme Court has allowed review of the lower courts’ decisions. The Media Release states that the Supreme Court has allowed

“review of a Court of Appeals decision that affirmed the trial court’s denial of his motion for a judgment of acquittal on a charge of third-degree assault and an associated gun-minimum element of the sentence imposed following his conviction on that charge. On review, the issues are:

“(1) What mental state attaches to the ‘use or threatened use of a firearm’ element of ORS 161.610, which requires an enhanced sentence if that element is satisfied?

“(2) Is evidence that a defendant accidentally discharged a firearm during the commission of the felony of being a felon in possession of a firearm sufficient to establish ‘use or threatened use’ of the firearm under ORS 161.610?

“(3) Is evidence that defendant showed the victim and a friend a firearm during a conversation in a bar, and that the firearm accidentally discharged as he was putting it away, injuring both defendant and the victim, sufficient to establish that he was acting recklessly with an extreme indifference to the value of human life at the time of the discharge?”

Finally, the use of prior firearm convictions may be collaterally attacked on grounds that the convictions are void, because they were obtained in violation of constitutional requirements. If they are void, they may not be used as repeat firearm-minimum predicates. See Chapter 1. Apart from that, the firearm-minimum statute presently allows bench trials using the preponderance standard, rather than jury trials using the reasonable doubt standard, on the existence of a defendant’s record of prior firearm convictions. To that extent, the present scheme is subject to attack under Sixth Amendment jury trial principles. Id.

§ 4-2.3. ORS 137.700 & ORS 137.707 Sentences.

In 1994, the electorate adopted Ballot Measure 11. It mandated mandatory minimum prison terms for specified, violent felonies. Its minimum terms apply to defendants 15 years of age and older, and require that covered juveniles be tried as adults. See ORS 137.709 (overturning State v. Godines, 236 Or App 404, 236 P3d 824 (2010)).4 Measure 11 is codified at ORS 137.700 for adults, and at ORS 137.707 for juveniles.

Since its enactment, the legislature added eight crimes to the lists in the statutory codifications, so there now are 24 “Measure 11” felonies. Moreover, for adults the legislature has significantly increased the length of the minimum terms for four of the original 16 crimes. So for adults, there now are 28 “Measure 11” sentences.

Except in the extremely limited situations discussed in Section 4-2.3.1, trial courts may not impose less than the Measure 11 minimum terms. But under certain circumstances, courts may impose greater terms. Moreover, a defendant convicted of a listed crime on an accomplice-liability theory is as susceptible to a Measure 11 sentence as his principal. State v. Cobb, 224 Or App 594, 198 P3d 978 (2008).

Defendants must serve the minimum terms in their entirety. For example, they may not earn good-time credit reductions in their mandatory minimum sentences. But if the court imposes a sentence greater than the minimum, the defendant may be eligible to earn good-time after completing the minimum. ORS 137.700(1); ORS 137.707(2).

EXAMPLE: The court imposes a 150-month sentence on a defendant who is not subject to ORS 137.635, and who was convicted of a crime carrying a 90-month minimum under ORS 137.700. He may be eligible for alternative incarceration sanctions such as good-time once he completes the 90-month minimum. But his good-time eligibility applies only to the 60-month “overage.”

The provisions of ORS 137.700 and 137.707, which allow courts to impose sentences greater than the mandatory minimums, are not identical. The statute for adults says, “The court may impose a greater sentence if otherwise permitted by law, but may not impose” a sentence of less than the mandatory minimum. ORS 137.700(1). For example, the court may impose a presumptive or departure guidelines sentence, or a firearm-minimum, that is longer than the ORS 137.700 minimum, so long as the guidelines or the firearm-minimum statute authorizes the longer sentence.

On the other hand, the statute for juveniles says, “The court may impose a greater presumptive term if otherwise permitted by law, but may not impose a lesser term.” ORS 137.707(2) (emphasis added). By its terms, this statute authorizes imposing only presumptive guidelines sentences that are greater than the mandatory minimum. For example, if a guidelines departure would be greater than the mandatory minimum and otherwise would be authorized, ORS 137.707(2) precludes its imposition. In juvenile cases, the court thus is limited to two sentencing options: impose the mandatory minimum, or impose a presumptive guidelines sentence that happens to be greater than the mandatory minimum.

In State ex rel Caleb v. Beesley, 326 Or 83, 949 P2d 724 (1997) and State ex rel Huddleston v. Sawyer, 324 Or 597, 932 P2d 1145, cert den, 522 US 994 (1997), the Supreme Court rejected virtually all conceivable general challenges to the measure’s state and federal constitutionality. See also Alvarado v. Hill, 252 F3d 1066 (9th Cir 2001) (rejecting juvenile defendant’s due process, equal protection, and cruel and unusual punishment attacks on Measure 11’s sentencing scheme). There is, however, one remaining general challenge: that the measure was enacted in violation of the due process requirements of lawmaking. See Delaware Tribal Business Comm. v. Weeks, 430 US 73, 97-98, 97 S Ct 911, 51 L Ed 2d 173 (1977) (Stevens, J., dissenting) (citing Hans A. Linde, Due Process of Lawmaking, 55 Neb L Rev 197 (1976)). Cf. Kohler v. Tugwell, 292 F Supp 978, 981 (ED La 1968) (rejecting claim that a “ballot [so] misled voters” that they “were * * * denied * * * Due Process of Law”), aff’d mem, 393 US 531 (1969).

PRACTICE TIP: In Sawyer, the Oregon Supreme Court also rejected two federal constitutional claims. One is an Equal Protection claim; the other is a republican Guarantee Clause claim (the latter of which the court rejected on grounds that it lacked jurisdiction to decide the claim). The court’s decisions on those two claims are not final—only a United States Supreme Court’s decision would be. Unless and until the federal claims are finally adjudicated, trial and appellate counsel should raise the claims. Likewise, counsel should raise the due process of lawmaking claim mentioned above. This will exhaust state court remedies on those federal claims, thus ensuring that defendants may renew the claims in federal habeas corpus.

Sawyer left open the possibility that a defendant could successfully challenge a mandatory minimum on grounds that applying it specifically to his case would constitute cruel and unusual and disproportionate punishment under Article I, section 16, of the Oregon Constitution. Sawyer, 324 Or at 612 (explaining that one reason for rejecting claim that Measure 11 eviscerates state constitutional right of allocution is that “[d]uring allocution a defendant may argue that the mandatory minimum, as applied to the particular crime, would be unconstitutionally cruel and unusual or disproportionate”). Defendants have frequently made this type of challenge, but with extremely limited success. The contours of these challenges are discussed in Chapter 1.

The 2005 Legislature’s House Bill 3511 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2006, chapter 1 (Special Session)), which it passed during its 2006 special session, amended ORS 137.700 by specifying 300-month minimums for adults convicted of the first-degree versions of rape, sodomy, unlawful sexual penetration, and kidnapping when the victim was less than 12 years of age and the defendant kidnapped the victim with the intent to commit first-degree rape, sodomy, or sexual penetration. HB 3511 applies to crimes committed on or after its April 24, 2006 effective date. Or Laws 2006, ch 1, § 3(1) (Spec Sess).5 The 300-month minimum is the same as the minimum for murder. ORS 137.700(2)(a)(A). HB 3511’s mandate of a minimum term for non-homicide offenses, which is equivalent to the minimum for murder, is subject to the constitutional challenges discussed in Chapter 1.

Another possible offender-specific claim is that a charge carrying a mandatory minimum is a product of the district attorney’s “overcharging.” To make this claim, defense counsel must develop a factual record challenging the selection process the district attorney’s office uses in its charging decisions. State v. Cunningham, 320 Or 47, 64-67, 880 P2d 431 (1994). See also Emily Simon, Measure 11 Project Moves Forward, The Oregon Defense Attorney, May 1998, at 4. But see State v. Acker, 175 Or App 145, 27 P3d 1071 (2001) (rejecting claim that the defendant’s rights under the state constitution’s equal privileges and immunities clause were violated when the prosecutor considered the defendant’s age when determining whether to offer a plea bargain to a lesser-included offense that would have allowed the defendant to avoid the mandatory minimum).

Finally, applying the mandatory minimum statute to a defendant convicted of a crime that is a Class B felony is not disproportionate under Article I, section 16 simply because there are many non-Measure 11 Class A felonies carrying sentences less severe than Class B Measure 11 felonies. State v. Ferman-Velasco, 333 Or 422, 41 P3d 404 (2002). On the other hand, the statute’s automatic-remand provision for 15- to 17-year-old juveniles may be attacked on due process grounds. Mendez-Alcaraz v. Gonzales, 464 F3d 842, 845 (9th Cir 2006) (Ferguson, J., dissenting).

§ 4-2.3.1. Relief from Measure 11 Sentences.

ORS 137.712 provides an “exit door” from ORS 137.700 and 137.707 sentencing. It gives trial courts extremely limited authority to impose lesser sentences on persons convicted of some of the listed crimes.

Similar in concept to “double-barreled” aggravated departures, see Chapter 7, the exit door authorizes sentencing defendants to probation. Based on the initial findings that ORS 137.712(1)(a) and (2) require, the trial court may move the defendant out of the applicable mandatory statute and into the guidelines where he necessarily would face a presumptive sentence of imprisonment. Based on the second and separate set of findings ORS 137.712(1)(b) requires, the court then may impose a “dispositional departure” sentence of probation.

ORS 137.712 specifies certain factual circumstances that “lock” the exit door to a defendant. In State v. Crescencio-Paz, 196 Or App 655, 103 P3d 666 (2004), the Court of Appeals rejected the claim that the state constitutional requirements of State v. Wedge require the state to plead and prove beyond a reasonable doubt the circumstances that would lock the exit door.

The Court of Appeals has had occasion to address claims that, based on a misconstruction of the statutes that lock the exit door, trial courts erroneously held that they must impose mandatory minimum sentences. One such case was State v. Arnold, 214 Or App 201, 203, 164 P3d 334 (2007). There, the defendant was convicted of second-degree robbery under an aid-and-abet theory. The defendant’s accomplice, but not the defendant himself, represented that he was armed with a weapon. The only provisions that possibly locked the exit door on defendant Arnold were ORS 137.712(2)(d)(B) and (C), which apply when “the defendant” represented that he was armed. The trial court essentially relied on a vicarious-liability theory to hold that under these provisions, the accomplice’s representation that he was armed locked the exit door on the defendant.

The Court of Appeals reversed. It explained that the “locking” provisions’ use of the term “the defendant” establishes that the provisions are offender-specific, rather than offense-specific, and that a vicarious-liability theory may not be used to apply the locking provisions. See Chapter 2 (discussing offender- vs. offense-specific distinction, including that only offense-specific statutes may be applied under a vicarious-liability theory). The court vacated the “defendant’s sentence and remand[ed] the case to the trial court so that it may decide, based on a correct understanding of the law, whether to impose a sentence under ORS 137.712.” Arnold, 214 Or App at 215.

The exit door is locked to a defendant if he has a prior conviction for any one of the numerous crimes listed in ORS 137.712(4). A previous conviction includes any finding of guilt on a crime entered in the court register before sentencing on the mandatory minimum crime, provided that crimes stemmed from separate criminal episodes. State v. Isbell, 178 Or App 523, 529-30, 38 P3d 272 (2002); ORS 137.712(6)(b).

EXAMPLE: A defendant with no prior record is found guilty of three ORS 137.700 crimes. Counts 1 and 2 stem from the same criminal episode, but Count 3 stems from a separate criminal episode. The jury verdict form is entered. Assuming all other eligibility criteria are met, under Isbell, the defendant will be eligible for lesser guidelines sentencing on Counts 1 and 2. But because those counts stemmed from a separate criminal episode from Count 3, the findings of guilt on Counts 1 and 2 render the defendant ineligible for lesser guidelines sentencing on Count 3.

To the extent that the application of the locking provisions would be based on the defendant’s record of prior convictions, he may collaterally challenge the utility of the priors on the ground that they are void. See Chapter 1.

A defendant sentenced to a lesser prison sentence is automatically eligible for sentencing reductions under the good-time statute, ORS 421.121, and other similar statutes. See ORS 137.712(1)(a) (“[w]hen the court imposes a sentence under this subsection, the person is eligible for a reduction in the sentence as provided in ORS 421.121 and any other statute”). See also Dugger v. Schiedler, 174 Or App 585, 27 P3d 498 (2001) (where trial court imposed lesser guidelines sentence in lieu of mandatory minimum sentence, DOC erred in denying defendant consideration for earned-early release time reductions). In other words, if a defendant is sentenced outside the mandatory minimum statute by virtue of ORS 137.712, his eligibility for alternative incarceration programs is not contingent on the trial court declaring him eligible under ORS 137.750; instead, under ORS 137.712(1) the defendant’s eligibility is automatic. The trial court has no say in the matter.

§ 4-2.4. Repeat Property Offender Sentences.

The repeat property offender (RPO) law, ORS 137.717, establishes another of the state’s many recidivist schemes used to aggravate punishment. The RPO specifies the special presumptive sentences6 for persons convicted of certain property crimes if they have a specified history of committing property crimes.

On the other hand, this use of prior convictions may be collaterally attacked on the ground that the convictions are void, because they were obtained in violation of constitutional requirements. If they are void, they may not be used as RPO predicates. See Chapter 1. Apart from that, the RPO presently allows bench trials using the preponderance standard, rather than jury trials using the reasonable doubt standard, on the existence of a defendant’s record of prior convictions. To that extent, the present scheme is subject to attack under Sixth Amendment jury trial principles. Id.

In order to sentence a defendant under the RPO law, ORS 137.717, predicates “must be proven pursuant to [the PSI statute,] ORS 137.079.” ORS 137.717(9) (emphasis added). Consider, State v. Sumpter, 227 Or App 513, 206 P3d 1088 (2009). The Court of Appeals held that the trial court erred by sentencing the defendant under the RPO law, because the prosecution failed to prove the predicates in the manner required by ORS 137.717(9) and ORS 137.079—i.e., that priors had to be proven via a PSI, in order to use them as predicates. Sumpter, 227 Or App at 519.

The RPO defines prior convictions as “[c]onvictions occurring before, on or after July 1, 2003.” ORS 137.717(10)(b)(A). Unlike the guidelines, which expressly require that certain juvenile adjudications be used in criminal-history scoring, ORS 137.717 does not mention using juvenile adjudications as predicates. It only states that prior “convictions” are to be used as predicates. ORS 137.717(10)(b). By definition, a juvenile adjudication is not a “conviction.” State v. Harris, 339 Or 157, 118 P3d 236 (2005); State v. Thompson, 166 Or App 370, 998 P2d 762 (2000); State v. Trice I, 146 Or App 15, 933 P2d 345 (1997); State v. Stewart, 321 Or 1, 892 P2d 1013 (1995); State ex rel Juvenile Dept. v. Reynolds, 317 Or 560, 857 P2d 842 (1993). Therefore, ORS 137.717 prohibits using juvenile adjudications as predicates.

For a prior conviction to count as a predicate, the defendant must have been convicted “under” specified statutes found in the current Oregon criminal code. See generally ORS 137.717(1) and (2). This aspect of the statute differs from ORS 137.635, which does not say convicted “under.” It says convicted “as defined in” specified Oregon statutes. See generally ORS 137.635(2).

Because ORS 137.717(1) and (2) use the term convicted “under” specified Oregon statutes, that much of the statute counts only convictions “under” those specified statutes. That provision does not mention convictions under predecessor Oregon statutes, so such convictions should not count as ORS 137.717 predicates. Cf. Andre, 142 Or App 285 (owing to ORS 137.635(2)’s phrase, “as defined in,” convictions under predecessor Oregon statutes do count as ORS 137.635 predicates).

The RPO also defines prior convictions as “[c]onvictions entered in any other state or federal court for comparable offenses.” ORS 137.717(10)(b)(B) (emphasis added). This comparability provision implicates the Sixth Amendment jury trial requirement that applies to the “characteristics” of prior convictions that recidivist schemes would use as predicates. Section 1-7.8.1 discusses this requirement. In situations where the state would use sister-state or federal court convictions as predicates, defense counsel should argue that the state must meet the Sixth Amendment requirements in order to do so.

In any event, in State v. Baker, 298 Or App 521, 447 P3d 526 (2019), the court relied on State v. Carlton, 361 Or 29, 43, 388 P3d 1093 (2017), to determine whether an out-of-state (or foreign) conviction could be used as a predicate under the repeat property offender statute. ORS 137.717. In Carlton, the court held that in the context of the felony sex offender statute, ORS 137.719, to treat an out-of-state conviction as a predicate, the out-of-state conviction’s elements must be “the same as or nearly the same as the elements of an Oregon felony sex crime, not to offenses that merely share a core similarity with such an offense.” In Baker, the court concluded “that ‘comparable offenses’ has the same meaning in ORS 137.717 as it does in ORS 137.719.” 298 Or App at 526. The court then concluded that the elements of the defendant’s California conviction for shoplifting were not the same or nearly the same as the elements of the Oregon crimes of second-degree burglary, second-degree theft, or attempted second-degree theft.

Apart from that, a conviction obtained in a federal Article I court, such as a military court, may not be used as an RPO predicate, despite the RPO’s mentioning of previous convictions in “federal court.” This is because the RPO was intended only to capture convictions incurred in federal Article III courts. State v. Earls, 246 Or App 578, 267 P3d 171 (2011) (trial court erred in using military court conviction as an RPO predicate).

As originally enacted, a prior conviction could count as an ORS 137.717 predicate only if the defendant were found guilty of the prior before committing the crime for which he would be sentenced under the statute. Accord State v. Allison (discussed in Section 4-2.1). But on July 1, 2001, ORS 137.717(5) took effect. It specifies that for crimes committed on or after July 1, 2001, a crime may be used as a predicate so long as it stems from a separate criminal episode from the crime for which the defendant would be sentenced under the statute. ORS 137.717(5)(a). The requirements for a separate-episode finding, and possible challenges to using separate-episode findings to treat current crimes of conviction as predicates, are discussed in Chapter 1. See also State v. Nesbit, 274 Or App 694, 361 P3d 649 (2015) (where state failed to prove that defendant’s aggravated first-degree theft convictions on Counts 1, 2, and 3 stemmed from separate criminal episodes, trial court erred in imposing RPO sentences on Counts 2 and 3).

ORS 137.717 sentences are not necessarily mandatory. They are “designated as a presumptive sentence by statute.” OAR 213-003-0001(16). ORS 137.717(3) says a trial court may impose a sentence “other than” the term provided in ORS 137.717(1). This is completely different from ORS 137.700 and 137.707, which say a court may impose only a “greater sentence,” but not a “lower sentence than” the statutory minimum. ORS 137.700(1). Moreover, ORS 137.717(3)(b) says a court may impose “other than” the specified sentence if it does so by departure. Consequently, a court may impose sentence less than the ORS 137.717 term, so long as valid mitigating factors justify the term. Likewise, the court may impose an aggravated departure, so long as valid aggravating departure factors justify the term. Chapter 7 discusses the requirements for departure sentencing.

But a defendant may receive a mitigated departure on only one RPO conviction; that is, a defendant receiving a mitigated departure on an RPO conviction prohibits him from receiving a mitigated departure on any future RPO convictions. ORS 137.717(6)(b). If a defendant is sentenced for multiple RPO convictions in a single sentencing hearing, the trial court’s imposition of a mitigated departure on one of the convictions prohibits the court from imposing mitigated departures on any other of the convictions (although the court gets to choose on which conviction to impose the departure). State v. Watkins-McKenzie, 286 Or App 569, 400 P3d 1012 (2017), rev dismissed, 363 Or 224 (2018).

Moreover, a defendant subject to the RPO law is ineligible for a mitigated departure if the defendant was “on probation, parole or post-prison supervision for a crime listed in subsection (1) of [the RPO law] at the time of the commission of the current crime of conviction[.]” ORS 137.717(6)(a). For example, in State v. Actkinson, 319 Or App 832, 511 P3d 444 (2022), the defendant was subject to the RPO law, but had committed his crime when he was on probation for a prior conviction of first-degree theft (a crime listed in subsection (1)). In the prior proceeding, the court had relied on ORS 161.705 to reduce the defendant’s conviction to a Class A misdemeanor. The defendant argued that ORS 137.717(6)(a) did not render him ineligible for a mitigated departure, because his prior conviction was for a misdemeanor, not for a felony. But the Court of Appeals rejected that argument, stating: “[A] defendant who commits his or her current property crime while on supervised release for one of the property crimes listed in ORS 137.717(1) will be disqualified from receiving a downward [mitigated] departure sentence even though the judge or the prosecutor treated the earlier crime as a Class A misdemeanor.” Actkinson, 319 Or App at 843.

Conversely, in State v. Hampton, 327 Or App 28, ___ P3d ___ (2023), the defendant, who had three convictions subject to RPO sentencing, was already on probation for a second-degree theft. The Court of Appeals concluded that ORS 137.717(1) does not encompass second-degree theft, and, therefore, that the trial court "erred in concluding that defendant was ineligible [for a mitigated downward departure] based on ORS 137.717(6)(a)[.]" 327 Or App at 37.7 Nevertheless, the Court of Appeals acknowledged that "[o]n remand, the court should consider the other requirements of ORS 137.717(6)—subparagraphs (b), (c), and (d)—on which it did not previously make findings," to determine whether those subparagraphs could deny the defendant a mitigated downward departure sentence on one of his counts. Id.

Finally, in State v. Langdon the Supreme Court held that mandatory minimum sentences are not subject to the guidelines’ limits on consecutive sentences. Because ORS 137.717 sentences are presumptive rather than mandatory, they should be subject to the guidelines limitations on total consecutive sentences; specifically, they should be subject to its 200 and 400 percent rules. For further information about those rules, see Chapter 8.

Unfortunately, State v. Hicks, 249 Or App 196, 275 P3d 195 (2012), holds that ORS 137.717 sentences are not subject to the guidelines limitations on total consecutive sentences. Hicks does so by construing ORS 137.717 sentences as mandatory, rather than as standard “presumptive” sentences. But for three reasons, they are not mandatory, because as explained above: (i) ORS 137.717 defines its sentences as “presumptive,” (ii) OAR 213-003-0001(16) includes within its definition of “[p]resumptive sentence” any “sentence designated as a presumptive sentence by statute,”; and (iii) ORS 137.717(4)(b) authorizes the court to impose “[a] departure sentence[.]” As Chapter 7 explains, “a departure sentence” includes any type of departure, including a mitigated (or “downward”) departure of less than the presumptive sentence. Unless the defendant is sentenced under the provisions ORS 137.717(1)(a) or (b) or (3), ORS 137.717(6), nothing prohibits a court from imposing less than an ORS 137.717 presumptive sentence, so long as the court bases that decision on valid mitigating factors that support “substantial and compelling reasons” to depart ORS 137.717(4)((b). Therefore, unless ORS 137.717(6) applies—and it won’t, in the vast majority of cases—ORS 137.717 sentences are not “mandatory.” They are presumptive sentences, which are subject to the guidelines limitations on total consecutive sentences.

PRACTICE TIP: With the exception of the small number of defendants whose sentencing is subject to ORS 137.717(6), Hicks wrongly holds that ORS 137.717 sentences are “mandatory” so are not subject to the guidelines limitations on total consecutive sentences. In appropriate cases, defense counsel should seek re-litigation of the matter that was at issue in Hicks.

In addition, defense counsel should be aware that the 2017 Legislature’s House Bill 3078 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2017, section 673) amended the RPO so as to reduce the severity of its sentencing scheme in certain respects. Or Laws 2017, ch 673, §§ 5 and 6. The bill’s Section 5 amendments became operative January 1, 2018, Or Laws 2017, ch 673, § 12(1), but the bill’s Section 6 amendments do not become operative until January 1, 2023. Or Laws 2017, ch 673, § 12(2). Defense counsel should consult the OCDLA publication, 2017 Legislative Analysis of Criminal & Juvenile Laws, for details about these amendments.

Notably, the state challenged HB 3078’s provisions that reduced the RPO sentences for identity theft and first-degree theft. Relying on the theory that the sentences were subject to the two-thirds supermajority requirements of Article IV, section 33, of the Oregon Constitution, which House Bill 3078 did not obtain in the House or the Senate, the state claimed the reduced sentences were void. But in State v. Vallin, 364 Or 295, 434 P3d 413, adhered to on recons, 364 Or 573, 437 P3d 231 (2019), the Supreme Court held that the sentences were not subject to section 33’s supermajority requirements, so were validly enacted by simple majority votes.

§ 4-2.5. Murder Sentences.

Before the guidelines took effect on November 1, 1989, murder defendants were sentenced solely under former ORS 163.115. That statute mandated indeterminate life terms of imprisonment, with a mandatory minimum of 10 years. The statute also authorized trial courts to add as many as 15 years to the mandatory minimum, for a maximum minimum term of 25 years. State v. Jones, 315 Or 225, 232, 844 P2d 188 (1992).

The guidelines “supersede[d] the requirement * * * to impose [a] * * * life sentence on a murder conviction.” State v. Cannon, 135 Or App 561, 564, 900 P2d 592 (1995). Instead, the court could sentence a guidelines-era murder defendant under the guidelines determinate sentencing system or, alternatively (and illogically), under the minimum-term provisions of former ORS 163.115. State v. Morgan, 316 Or 553, 856 P2d 612 (1993).

If the defendant is convicted of a murder he committed when he was at least 18 years old, and if he knew the victim was pregnant, the presumptive sentence is life without the possibility of parole. ORS 163.155(1) and (4). But if the trier of fact (the jury, or the judge if the defendant validly waives jury) finds sufficient mitigating circumstances, the court must impose a sentence of life with the possibility of parole after the defendant serves a determinate minimum of 30 years. ORS 163.155(1) and (5).

If the defendant is convicted of a murder when he was 15, 16, or 17 years old, and if he knew the victim was pregnant, the court must impose a sentence of life with a minimum term of 30 years. ORS 163.155(1) and (5). However, the defendant will become parole eligible after serving 15 years worth of the 30-year minimum. ORS 144.397.

The 2019 Legislature’s Senate Bill 1013 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2019, chapter 635) is primarily known for its reductions in the application of the state’s aggravated murder law, so its curtailment of imposition of the death penalty.8 But the bill also significantly revised the sentencing scheme for murder, and did so by bifurcating the crime into first-degree murder, ORS 163.107; ORS 163.111, and second-degree murder. ORS 163.115.

SB 1013 took “effect on the 91st day after the date on which the 2019 regular session of the Eightieth Legislative Assembly adjourn[ed] sine die.” Or Laws 2019, ch 635, § 31. The legislature adjourned sine die on June 30, 2019. Ninety-one days after that date is September 29, 2019, so that is the bill’s effective date. See Legislative Website, https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2019R1/Downloads/MeasureDocument/SB1013.

SB 1013 further states:

“Section 3 of this 2019 Act[, which are codified at ORS 163.107,] and the amendments to ORS * * * 163.115 * * * by section[] * * * 4 * * * of this 2019 Act apply to crimes committed before, on or after the effective date of this 2019 Act that are the subject of sentencing proceedings occurring on or after the effective date of this 2019 Act.”

Or Laws 2019, ch 635, § 30 (emphasis added).

Regardless of the date of the crime, under SB 1013’s Section 30, any murder sentenced on or after September 29, 2019 effective date is subject to the bill’s sentencing schemes. Depending on the nature of the crime, it will be classified as aggravated murder, first-degree murder, or second-degree murder. As explained in the Chapter 2 Update, all three crimes are unranked, and trial courts are prohibited from assigning seriousness rankings to those crimes. OAR 213-004-0003; OAR 213-004-0004; OAR 213-017-0001.

If the murder is classified as aggravated murder, and the defendant was at least 18 years old when he committed the crime, the possible sentences are death, life without the possibility of parole, and an indeterminate term of life—i.e., life with the possibility of parole. ORS 163.105(1)(a). If the sentence is an indeterminate term of life, the defendant must serve a determinate minimum of 30 years before becoming parole eligible. ORS 163.105(1)(c). But if the defendant was 15, 16, or 17 years old when he committed the crime, he “is not subject to a sentence of death or life imprisonment without the possibility of release or parole.” ORS 137.707(2). He is subject only to an indeterminate life sentence, with a 30-year minimum. ORS 163.105(1)(c). But the minimum term too is indeterminate, because the defendant will become parole eligible after serving an determinate term of 15 years worth of the 30-year minimum. ORS 163.105(1)(c); ORS 144.397.9

If the murder is classified as first-degree murder, and the defendant was 15, 16, or 17 years old when he committed the crime, the presumptive sentence is life with the possibility of parole after 30 years. ORS 163.107(2)(a). But the defendant will become parole eligible after serving 15 years worth of the 30-year minimum. ORS 144.397. Moreover, the defendant may become eligible for a “second look” hearing, as authorized by ORS 420A.203(1)(a)(B).

If the murder is classified as first-degree murder, and the defendant was at least 18 years old when he committed his crime, the presumptive sentence is life with the possibility of parole. ORS 163.107(2)(a). The defendant is required to serve a minimum of 30 years before becoming parole eligible. Id. Moreover, ORS 163.107(2)(b) ostensibly authorizes an aggravated-departure sentence of life without the possibility of parole, if the court “state[s] on the record the reasons for imposing the sentence.” But the statute only ostensibly authorizes an aggravated departure in adult, first-degree murder prosecutions, because the departure scheme is subject to constitutional challenges—challenges which even career prosecutors have acknowledged are viable10—that would prohibit imposing a departure sentence of life without the possibility of parole. The Appendix to this Update provides a template for use in challenging ORS 163.107(2)(b)’s departure scheme.

PRACTICE TIP: The template recognizes that in an adult first-degree murder prosecution, the state may seek to prove “enhancement facts,” see Chapters 1 and 7, during the case’s trial and/or sentencing stages. The constitutional challenges to ORS 163.107(2)(b)’s aggravated-departure scheme would prohibit the state from proving enhancement facts during the trial and/or sentencing stages. Consequently, the template is crafted as a pretrial motion in limine, intended to prohibit the state from seeking to prove enhancement facts during those stages. If the trial court grants the motion, the court would have no “reasons” for imposing the aggravated-departure sentence of life without the possibility of parole. The only sentence the court could impose would be the presumptive sentence of life with the possibility of parole after serving the 30-year determinate minimum.

If the murder is classified as second-degree murder, and the defendant was at least 15 years old when he committed the crime, the court must impose a life sentence of imprisonment. ORS 163.115(5)(a). If the defendant was 15, 16, or 17 years old when he committed the crime, he will become parole eligible after serving 15 years’ worth of the 25-year minimum. ORS 144.397. Moreover, the defendant may become eligible for a “second look” hearing, as authorized by ORS 420A.203(1)(a)(B). But if the defendant was at least 18 years old when he committed the crime, he must serve a determinate, 25-year minimum before becoming parole eligible. ORS 163.115(5)(b).

As originally written, the guidelines doubling limit on aggravated departures, discussed in Chapter 7, did not apply to murder. A court could impose a term of months equivalent to a life term (but it could not impose such a term on a remanded juvenile). State v. Davilla, 157 Or App 639, 972 P2d 902 (1998), rev den, 334 Or 76 (2002). However, the 1993 Legislature amended the doubling limit by applying it to murder cases. The amendment applies to crimes committed on or after November 1, 1993. Davilla, 157 Or App at 647 n 9.

On April 1, 1995, Ballot Measure 11 (1994) took effect. It requires mandatory minimum terms of 300 months for murder defendants aged 15 years or older. ORS 137.700(2)(a)(A) (for adults); ORS 137.707(4)(a)(A). As of that date, a murder defendant’s sentence was the 300-month mandatory minimum, the applicable guidelines sentence (but not an aggravated departure, as discussed below), or the sentence specified by one of the state’s recidivist schemes, whichever was greater.

On June 30, 1995, the 1995 Legislature’s House Bill 3439 took effect. Among other things, it restored the requirement that trial courts impose life terms in murder cases. State v. Francis, 154 Or App 486, 490-91, 962 P2d 45 (1998). From June 30, 1995 to October 23, 1999, when the 1999 Legislature’s House Bill 3586 took effect, there was no authority for releasing a murder defendant before he finished serving the life term. This meant that his life term was a “true life” sentence—a sentence otherwise reserved for the greater crime of aggravated murder. State v. Wille, 317 Or 487, 858 P2d 128 (1993). Consequently, the restored life-term authority violated the proportionality requirement of Article I, section 16, of the Oregon Constitution, for it required a greater sentence for the lesser-included offense of murder than was available for the greater offense of aggravated murder. State v. McLain, 158 Or App 419, 974 P2d 727 (1999). See also State v. Davis, 216 Or App 456, 463–66, 174 P3d 1022 (2007) (explaining history of 1999 amendment restoring life-sentence authority for murder).

The Court of Appeals decided McLain on February 17, 1999. Under that decision, the life term requirement was constitutionally invalid. A trial court could impose only the applicable determinate prison sentence, plus a life term of PPS. A defendant had to be released onto PPS after completing his determinate prison term.

HB 3586 amended Oregon Laws 1989, chapter 790, section 28 by restoring parole authority for murder defendants. That restored authority eliminated the proportionality violation announced in McLain. The bill thereby restored trial court authority to impose life terms for murders committed on or after the bill’s October 23, 1999 effective date. For murders committed on or after that date, a court must impose an indeterminate life term of imprisonment, a determinate prison sentence, and a life term of PPS. The Board of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision may release a defendant before he completes service of the life term.

HB 3586 also created retroactive authority to parole defendants convicted of murders committed before the bill’s October 23, 1999 effective date. In State v. Haynes, 168 Or App 565, 7 P3d 623, rev den, 331 Or 283 (2000), the Court of Appeals rejected the claim that applying the retroactive authority to crimes committed on or after HB 3439’s June 30, 1995 effective date, but before McLain’s February 17, 1999 date of decision, is prohibited as ex post facto.

On the other hand, because of Cannon, a guidelines-era murder defendant who committed his crime before HB 3439 took effect on June 30, 1995 was not given notice that he faced a potential life sentence. Cannon, 135 Or App at 561. Moreover, the proportionality problem inherent in the statutory scheme when the court decided McLain defeated trial court authority to impose life sentences. As a consequence, HB 3586’s restoration of parole, which eliminated the proportionality problem and restored trial court authority to impose life sentences, is harsher than the law that was in effect before HB 3586’s October 23, 1999 effective date. This is because the parole board may decline to exercise its authority to parole a murder defendant, and thereby keep him in prison longer than his maximum term without a life sentence. See Garner v. Jones, 529 US 244, 120 S Ct 1362, 146 L Ed 2d 236 (2000) (retroactive application of changes in parole board practice and procedure resulting in longer prison terms violates ex post facto); Davis, 216 Or App at 462 (accepting state’s concession that restoration of life-term authority resulted in harsher sentences). For those reasons, applying HB 3586 to guidelines-era murder defendants who committed their crimes before the June 30, 1995 date of decision in Cannon should be prohibited as ex post facto. Likewise, murder defendants who committed their crimes during the “McLain window”—i.e., on or after the February 17, 1999 date of decision in McLain, but before HB 3586’s October 23, 1999 effective date—also had no notice that they faced a potential life sentence. Therefore, applying House Bill 3586 to them also is prohibited as ex post facto. State v. Giles, 254 Or App 345, 293 P3d 1085 (2012) holds

Finally, in State v. Ambill, 282 Or App 821, 385 P3d 1110 (2016), rev den, 361 Or 439, 361 Or 524 (2017), the state asked the court to rely on aggravating factors the jury had found, to impose, as a minimum term accompanying the mandatory indeterminate life sentence, a determinate, aggravated-departure sentence of 538 months.11 The trial court held that it could not impose a minimum term that exceeds the 25-year minimum specified in ORS 161.115. ORS 137.700 (for adults); ORS 137.707 (for juveniles).

The state appealed, and the Court of Appeals affirmed, explaining:

“If a sentencing court were authorized to impose a minimum term of confinement greater than the 25-year term in ORS 163.115(5)(b), the risk that those convicted of aggravated murder would be more favorably treated than those convicted of murder would remain. Again, in the light of the legislature’s express objective to solve the particular [proportionality] problem identified by McLain, we decline to construe the statute so as to render that solution incomplete in the absence of any other textual or contextual indicators compelling such a conclusion.

“For the foregoing reasons, we are persuaded from the text, context, and legislative history of ORS 163.115 that the legislature intended an offender convicted of murder to be sentenced to an indeterminate life sentence with the 25-year minimum term of incarceration specified in ORS 163.115(5)(b), and that the legislature did not intend for a sentencing court to impose a different minimum term of incarceration under the guidelines or any other source of law. We therefore affirm the judgment of the trial court.”

Ambill, 282 Or App at 833.

§ 4-2.6. Repeat Felony Sex Offender Sentences.

The 2001 Legislature’s Senate Bill 370 created a “three-strikes” recidivist scheme called the repeat felony sex offender (RFSO) law. It is codified at ORS 137.719. It took effect on July 31, 2001, Or Laws 2001, ch 884, § 16, so it applies in the sentencing of crimes committed on or after that date. See, e.g., ORS 161.035(4) (amendments to criminal code generally apply only to crimes committed on or after amendments’ effective date); see also Or Const Art I, § 21 (banning ex post facto laws); US Const Art I, § 10 (banning state’s enactments of ex post facto laws).

Under the RFSO, a defendant’s third conviction for a felony sex crime that is subject to sex-offender registration under ORS 181.594 carries a “presumptive sentence” of “true-life”—i.e., a sentence of “life imprisonment without the possibility of release or parole if the defendant has been sentenced for sex crimes that are felonies at least two times prior to the current sentence.” ORS 137.719(1) (emphasis added).

The Supreme Court has held that to the extent ORS 137.719 is applied to “incorrigible criminal[s]”—i.e., to “those who cannot or will not conform their behavior to criminal statutes”—imposing presumptive true-life sentence, including multiple consecutive true-life sentences, does not violate Article I, section 16’s prohibitions on disproportionate sentences and cruel and unusual punishments. State v. Wheeler, 343 Or 652, 680, 175 P3d 438 (2007). On the other hand, as with any other presumptive sentence, the court may impose a lesser sentence as a mitigated guidelines departure. ORS 137.719(2). See Chapter 7.

In State v. Davidson II, 360 Or 370, 380 P3d 963 (2016), the court declared unconstitutional as applied the consecutive true-life sentences the trial court imposed on the defendant’s two Class C felonies, seriousness level 6 convictions. On remand, the trial court relied on ORS 137.719(2) to impose consecutive 90-month sentences, for a 180-month total, and a lifetime term of post-prison supervision (PPS).

Then came State v. Davidson III, 307 Or App 478, 478 P3d 570 (2020), aff’d, 369 Or 480, 507 P3d 246 (2022). There, the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court’s sentencing decision. The Court of Appeals acknowledged that no law whatsoever authorized the trial court to impose a lifetime term of PPS. Recognizing that, the Court of Appeals also reversed the trial court’s decision to impose a lifetime term of PPS. 307 Or App at 496-97. But more significantly, the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court’s decision to impose consecutive 90-month sentences. Id. at 489.

Still, the case continued. As just shown in the citation above, the prosecution sought Supreme Court review.

The court allowed review and affirmed both aspects of the Court of Appeals decision. Regarding the consecutive sentences, the Supreme Court explained:

“Based on our examination of the text and context of that statute, we conclude that, in ORS 137.719(2), the words ‘departure sentence’ in the phrase departure sentence authorized by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission’ refers to a departure from the presumptive sentence of life without the possibility of parole set out in ORS 137.719(1), but that the nonspecific reference to ‘the rules’ in that phrase is to all of the felony sentencing guidelines rules, including the sentencing grid block and

the rules placing limits on departures from grid block sentences. And that is so because, when the legislature enacted ORS 137.719 in 2001, it would have understood that, when those rules used the phrase presumptive sentence,’ that phrase referred to a grid block presumptive sentence and not to the statutory presumptive sentence set out in ORS 137.719(1), and, thus, the legislature had to have intended that sentencing courts use the felony sentencing guidelines grid block and departure rules and limitations in determining an appropriate departure sentence. For those reasons, we conclude that the trial court was required to impose a sentence authorized by the felony sentencing guidelines, and it erred in failing to do so. On remand, therefore, the trial court must impose a sentence using the guidelines grid block as a starting point and departing from there based on findings of substantial and compelling reasons, as permitted under the felony sentencing guidelines rules.”

State v. Davidson III, 369 Or 480, 502-03, 507 P3d 246 (2022).

In addition, the Supreme Court stated: “Any term of post-prison supervision that the trial court imposes also must conform to the requirements of the felony sentencing guidelines rules.” 369 Or at 503 n 6. Thus, the court acknowledged that the trial court judge illegally imposed a lifetime term of PPS.

ORS 137.719(3) defines ORS 137.719(1)’s phrase “has been sentenced.” ORS 137.719(3) provides:

“For purposes of this section:

“(a) Sentences for two or more convictions that are imposed in the same sentencing proceeding are considered to be one sentence; and

“(b) A prior sentence includes:

“(A) Sentences imposed before, on or after July 31, 2001; and

“(B) Sentences imposed by any other state or federal court for comparable offenses.”

As seen, ORS 137.719(3)(a) prohibits using crimes sentenced in the same hearing as predicates to impose RFSO sentences on other crimes sentenced in the same hearing. Thus, only crimes previously sentenced in other proceedings may be used as RFSO predicates. See Gordon v. Hall, 232 Or App 174, 187, 221 P3d 763 (2009) (where one of petitioner’s two predicates, which he incurred in California, ended in term of probation at a time when probation was not considered a “sentence” both in California and in Oregon, petitioner had only one predicate so was ineligible for sentencing under ORS 137.719). But see State v. Molette, 255 Or App 29, 296 P3d 594 (2013) (where trial court, without objection, relied on Texas conviction as predicate, and where Texas court imposed probation when probation was not a “sentence” in Texas but was a sentence in Oregon, appellate court declined to exercise discretion to review claim of error, for exercise would have required resolving open question regarding choice of laws). But as discussed in Chapter 1, this use of prior convictions may be collaterally attacked on the ground that the convictions are void, for they were obtained in violation of constitutional requirements. If they are void, they may not be used as ORS 137.719 predicates. Apart from that, ORS 137.719 presently allows bench trials using the preponderance standard, rather than jury trials using the reasonable doubt standard, on the existence of a defendant’s record of prior convictions. To that extent the present scheme is subject to constitutional attack. See Chapter 1.

State v. Kirkpatrick, 302 Or App 62, 66, 455 P3d 485 (2020) (under State v. Carlton, a conviction under 18 USC § 2252(a)(1) is not comparable to a conviction under ORS 163.686; therefore, the trial court erred in using the conviction as a predicate to sentence under ORS 137.719).

ORS 137.719(3)(b)(B) authorizes using as a predicate a sentence for an out-of-state or a federal court conviction for a sex crime, if the conviction is for a “comparable offense[.]” In State v. Carlton, 361 Or 29, 43, 388 P3d 1093 (2017), the court held that in determining whether an out-of-state or a federal court sex-crime conviction is for a “comparable offense,” the elements of the out-of-state or federal court conviction must be “the same as or nearly the same as the elements of an Oregon felony sex crime, not to offenses that merely share a core similarity with such an offense.”

But it is important to recognize that this comparability provision implicates the Sixth Amendment jury trial requirement that applies to the “characteristics” of prior convictions that recidivist schemes would use as predicates. Section 1-7.8.1 discusses this requirement. In situations where the state would use sister-state or federal court convictions as predicates, defense counsel should argue that the state must meet the Sixth Amendment requirements in order to do so.

Moreover, consistent with the RPO and State v. Earls, the RFSO law’s authorizing the use of previous, “federal court” convictions as predicates, ORS 137.719(3)(b)(B), should prohibit using Article I federal court convictions, and allow the use only of Article III federal court convictions.

§ 4-2.7. Repeat Methamphetamine Offenders.

A defendant convicted of certain crimes of manufacture of a controlled substance, where (a) the substance was methamphetamine, (b) the crime was committed on or after August 16, 2005, and (c) the defendant has at least two or prior convictions for certain methamphetamine related crimes that stem from separate criminal episodes, falls under the repeat methamphetamine offender (RMO) law. ORS 475.935. The defendant is ineligible for probation. In addition, his presumptive sentence is 19 months’ imprisonment, or the sentence specified by his grid block, whichever is greater. The court may impose a mitigated “durational” departure (but not a “dispositional” departure, see Chapter 7, which discusses durational vs. dispositional departures), but of no less than one-half the length of the defendant’s presumptive sentence.

As with the state’s other recidivist schemes, the use of prior convictions as RMO predicates may be collaterally attacked on the ground that the priors are void, because they were obtained in violation of constitutional requirements. If they are void, they may not be used as RMO predicates. See Chapter 1. Apart from that, the RMO law presently allows bench trials using the preponderance standard, rather than jury trials using the reasonable doubt standard, on the existence of a defendant’s record of prior convictions. To that extent the present scheme is subject to constitutional attack. See Id. Moreover, the requirements for a separate-episode finding, and possible challenges to using separate-episode findings to treat current crimes of conviction as predicates, are discussed in Chapter 1.

The RMO authorizes using as predicates “[c]onvictions entered in any other state or federal court for comparable offenses.” ORS 475.935(3)(b). Consistent with State v. Carlton, discussed in Section 4-2.6, this comparability provision should be construed to mean the elements of the out-of-state or federal court conviction must be “the same as or nearly the same as the elements of an Oregon felony sex crime, not to offenses that merely share a core similarity with such an offense.” 361 Or at 43.

Moreover, it is important to recognize that this comparability provision implicates the Sixth Amendment jury trial requirement that applies to the “characteristics” of prior convictions that recidivist schemes would use as predicates. Section 1-7.8.1 discusses this requirement. In situations where the state would use sister-state or federal court convictions as predicates, defense counsel should argue that the state must meet the Sixth Amendment requirements in order to use those convictions as predicates.

Finally, consistent with the RPO and State v. Earls, see Section 4-2.4, the RMO law’s authorization to use “federal court” convictions as predicates, ORS 137.719(3)(b)(B), should be read as allowing the use of Article III federal court convictions, so as prohibiting the use of Article I federal court convictions (such as military court convictions).

§ 4-2.9. Ballot Measure 73.

In 2010, the voters approved Ballot Measure 73, called the “the Oregon Crimefighting Act.” Or Laws 2011, ch 1, §1. It created two new recidivist schemes, as follows.

§ 4-2.9.1. “Three Strikes” Felony DUII.

Measure 73 created ORS 811.011, which classifies a DUII as “a Class C felony if the defendant has been convicted of driving under the influence of intoxicants in violation of ORS 813.010, or its statutory counterpart in another jurisdiction, at least two times in the 10 years prior to the date of the current offense.” ORS 813.011(1). As previously mentioned, see Section 4-1.1, ORS 813.011(3) and ORS 213-005-0001(4)(b) specify a 90-day mandatory minimum jail term for a person convicted under ORS 813.011(1). For a conviction from a foreign jurisdiction to qualify as a predicate under this recidivist scheme, the conviction must have been obtained under a statutory scheme that is “either remarkably similar or have the same use, role, or characteristics” as the Oregon scheme.

In State v. Guzman/Heckler, 366 Or 18, 455 P3d 485 (2019), the court relied on State v. Carlton to determine whether an out-of-state (or foreign) conviction could be used as a predicate under the felony driving under the influence of intoxicants statute, ORS 813.010. In Carlton, the court held that in the context of the felony sex offender statute, ORS 137.719, to treat an out-of-state conviction as a predicate, the out-of-state conviction’s elements must be “the same as or nearly the same as the elements of an Oregon felony sex crime, not to offenses that merely share a core similarity with such an offense.” In Guzman/Heckler, the court held that “in determining which foreign offenses qualify as statutory counterparts to ORS 813.010, the appropriate inquiry is close element matching. * * * [O]ffenses with elements that are not ‘the same as or nearly the same,’ Carlton, 361 Or at 43, as those of ORS 813.010(1)” may not be used as predicates. Guzman/Heckler, 366 Or at 47. Applying that test, the court held in Guzman that the trial court erroneously used the defendant’s Kansas conviction for driving under the influence as a predicate. In Heckler, the court held that the trial court erred in using the defendant’s Colorado conviction for driving while ability impaired as a predicate.

Cf. State v. Rose, 327 Or App 665, 672, ___ P3d ___ (2023) (defendant's former Colorado conviction of "driving while ability impaired" did not require proof that defendant's alcohol and/or drug use necessarily had any actual effect on his driving, the conviction did not require a determination of impaired driving, and therefore is not a conviction described in ORS 813.215(1)(a)(B); consequently, the Court of Appeals "reverse[d] defendant’s conviction and remand[ed] for reconsideration of defendant’s petition for diversion");

§ 4-2.10. Repeat Class A Felony Sex Offenders Law.

The 2017 Legislature’s Senate Bill 1050 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2017, chapter 699) established life without parole as the presumptive sentence for persons convicted of the state’s three Class A felony sex offenses—i.e., the first-degree versions of rape, sodomy, and unlawful sexual penetration—“if, at the time of the offense, the defendant [had] a prior conviction” for any of those same three crimes. 137.725(1) (emphasis added). An Oregon conviction, “[a]n equivalent federal offense,” and “[a]n equivalent offense in another state,” ORS 137.725(3)(d) and (e), may serve as a predicate for application of this sentencing scheme.

ORS 137.725 took effect January 1, 2018. Owing to ex post facto prohibitions, the bill applies only to new crimes committed on or after that date.

Note that under ORS 137.725(1), a prior conviction qualifies as a predicate only if the defendant had incurred the conviction before he committed his new crime. Consequently, its application will be limited in a manner that most of the state’s other recidivist schemes are not.

EXAMPLE: A defendant with no prior convictions for ORS 137.725 crimes is charged with and convicted of two ORS 137.725 crimes, which stem from separate criminal episodes. The defendant is sentenced in open court for the first crime. But because the defendant did not incur that conviction before he committed the second crime, the first conviction may not be used as a ORS 137.725 predicate to sentence the defendant on the second conviction.

“The court may impose a sentence other than the presumptive sentence * * * if the court imposes a departure sentence authorized by the [Felony Sentencing Guidelines] based upon findings of substantial and compelling reasons.” ORS 137.725(2) (emphasis added). This subsection establishes that ORS 137.725’s presumptive sentences are not mandatory, so authorizes mitigated (downward) departures in accordance with the guidelines’ departure-sentence provisions. See Chapter 7.

Finally, it is important to recognize that the “equivalent offense” provision, which allows the use of federal and sister-convictions to be used as predicates, implicates the Sixth Amendment jury trial requirement that applies to the “characteristics” of prior convictions that recidivist schemes would use as predicates. Section 1-7.8.1 discusses this requirement. The Dangerous Offender sentencing scheme, ORS 161.725 to 161.737, is a hybrid indeterminate-determinate scheme.12 It authorizes a 30-year maximum indeterminate component that requires a determinate minimum. § 4-3. RELIEF FROM INCARCERATION SENTENCES.

The principal difference between pre-guidelines and virtually all guidelines prison sentences is that a defendant may not be paroled after serving some portion of a guidelines sentence. Or Laws 1989, ch 790, § 28 (limiting Board’s parole authority to pre-guidelines cases, and to murder cases). Nevertheless, there are several ways in which a guidelines defendant may get a portion of his prison term reduced.

First, a defendant must be given presentence incarceration credit—i.e., credit for any time served in jail prior to his sentencing. Nissel v. Pearce, 307 Or 102, 105-07, 764 P2d 224 (1988); State v. Jones, 113 Or App 228, 832 P2d 459, on recons, 117 Or App 174, 175, 842 P2d 466 (1992). See also OAR 291-100-0080 (regulating credit for time served for presentence incarceration). This includes pre-sentence incarceration time the defendant served while held in an out-of-state facility on an Oregon charge. State ex rel Curry v. Thompson, 156 Or App 537, 541-42, 967 P2d 522 (1998) (defendant entitled to pre-sentence incarceration credit for time served in Washington state on an Oregon warrant).

Moreover, “[w]hen a judgment of conviction is vacated and a new sentence is thereafter imposed upon the defendant for the same crime, * * * the period of detention and imprisonment theretofore served shall be deducted from the * * * [new] sentence” imposed upon reconviction.” ORS 137.370(3). For sentencing hearings held on or after August 1, 2015, both presentence incarceration time, and time served on a sentence imposed for a conviction that later was vacated, may be credited as time served on a lesser included or greater inclusive offense, or for “[a]ny other crime constituting a violation of Oregon law within the same county designated by the sentencing court in the judgment as having been committed as part of the same criminal episode as the crime for which sentence was imposed,” ORS 137.370(2) and (3). Finally, “a person who is confined as the result of a sentence for a crime or conduct that is not directly related to the crime for which the sentence is imposed, or for violation of the conditions of probation, parole or post-prison supervision, [may] receive presentence incarceration credit for the time served in jail toward service of the term of confinement,” “[u]nless the court expressly orders otherwise[.]” ORS 137.370(4).

Denying credit for time served violates double jeopardy. North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 US 711, 718-19, 89 S Ct 2072, 23 L Ed 2d 656 (1969); State v. Turner, 247 Or 301, 315, 429 P2d 565 (1967). The denial also may violate equal protection. Arthur W. Campbell, Law of Sentencing, § 8:7 (3d ed. 2004).14 If the sentencing judge denies such credit, the defendant may obtain relief on direct appeal (if the issue has not been waived). State v. McEahern, 126 Or App 201, 203–04, 867 P2d 568 (1994). If the DOC refuses to give such credit and the refusal poses imminent harm to the defendant, then under Nissel v. Pearce his remedy lies in state habeas corpus. But if the posed harm is not imminent, the defendant’s remedy lies in mandamus. Thompson, 156 Or App at 539. See also Cullop v. Offender Information & Sentence Computation Center, 226 Or App 167, 203 P3d 276 (2009) (consistent with Thompson).

Next, under the DOC’s earned-early release program, a defendant who is not declared ineligible by statute or court order may earn a so-called “good-time” reduction in his sentence, of up to 20% of his total prison sentence. ORS 421.121(2).15

Moreover, OAR 291-097-0231(2) provides:

“In determining whether an AIC will receive earned time credits for the review period under consideration, AIC performance will be evaluated in two areas: [i] half credit for compliance with the case plan and [ii] half credit for maintaining appropriate institution conduct. The only possible determination for each area is noncompliance or compliance.”

See also Dugger v. Schiedler (where trial court validly imposed lesser guidelines sentence in lieu of mandatory minimum sentence, DOC erred in denying defendant consideration for good-time reductions); Haffey, 84 Or App 607 (defendants serving firearm-minimum sentences are eligible for good-time, albeit in a limited form); OAR 291-100-0100(2) (regulating good-time credit). Moreover, he may earn a good-time sentence reduction for the period he spent in jail prior to sentencing. OAR 291-97-0020(8). But in no case may a defendant earn good-time reductions that would “be used to shorten the term of actual prison confinement to less than six months.” ORS 421.121(3). Thus, if a defendant’s prison term is six months or shorter, he is ineligible for good-time reductions. However, if a term of six months or less is to be served locally under ORS 137.124, the local jail may have a good-time credit program for which the defendant is eligible regardless of ORS 421.121(3). ORS 169.110.

One means—and in fact the usual means—that inmates earn “good time” credit is by working in some job or another. Moreover, in 1994, voters approved Ballot Measure 17 (codified as Article I, section 41, of the Oregon Constitution), which effectively ordered inmates to work. This order was sanctioned by the initial version of Article I, section 34, which in 1859 the voters authorized legal slavery of inmates who were “duly convicted” of crimes.

But in November 2022, the voters approved (by a comfortable margin) Ballot Measure 112, which amended Article I, section 34 by removing section 34’s text that authorized legal slavery of inmates who were “duly convicted” of crimes. With that text removed, section 34 no longer allows enslaving inmates.16 Conversely, Article I, section 41, ordered inmates to work, explicitly authorized legal slavery; thus, amended section 34 and section 41 are irreconcilable. The 2022 amendment to section 34 is later in time than section 41; therefore, amended section 34 impliedly amended section 41, so that section 41 no longer can order inmates to work. State ex rel. Adams v. Powell, 171 Or App 81, 102, 15 P3d 54 (2000), rev dismissed, 334 Or 693 (2002) (“the doctrine of implied amendment is alive and well in Oregon jurisprudence when it is necessary to carry out the intent of the voters”).

This means that going forward, inmates who could earn “good time” likely would want to work so they earn “good time.” But now, thanks to Measure 112’s amendment, inmates who could earn “good time” most likely would consent to work so they would earn good time. But consider inmates who are serving “hard time” mandatory sentences; that is, sentences denying them earning any “good time.” Those inmates can flat out refuse to consent to work.

The third form of prison-sentence reductions is found in ORS 144.126. It authorizes the Board of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision to advance the release date of a defendant who is serving a prison sentence if the board determines that owing to the defendant’s medical condition, “continued incarceration is cruel and inhumane and that advancing the release date of the [defendant] is not incompatible with the best interests of the prison and society[.]” Id.

The next form of relief is the state’s alternative incarceration programs (AIPs). Section 4-3.2 discusses the special requirements for AIP eligibility. If a defendant “successfully complete[s]” an AIP, the DOC “may release [him] on post-prison supervision.” ORS 421.508(3). OAR 291-062-0100 to -0160. One AIP is the state’s “cognitive restructuring” AIP. ORS 421.504. The state’s other AIP is its “intensive alternative incarceration addiction program.” ORS 421.506. ORS 421.540; OAR 291-062-0100 to -0160.

Moreover, it is important to recognize that the “exit door” to Measure 11 sentencing (ORS 137.700 and ORS 137.707) states in relevant part: “When the court imposes a sentence under this subsection, the person is eligible for a reduction in the sentence as provided in ORS 421.121 and any other statute[.]” ORS 137.712(1) (emphasis added). Like ORS 421.121, which authorizes “a reduction in the sentence” for earned-time credit, “any other statute” would include the alternative incarceration program (AIP) statutes, ORS 421.504 and ORS 421.506. Therefore, under pertinent maxims of construction, a defendant convicted of a Measure 11 crime, but, thanks to the “exit door” is sentenced to a shorter, Guidelines prison term, should be eligible for AIP programs just as he is eligible for earned time. See State v. Hutchins, 214 Or App 260, 164 P3d 318 (2007) (ejusdem generis maxim of construction); King City Rehab, LLC v. Clackamas County, 214 Or App 333, 164 P3d 1190 (2007) (noscitur a sociis maxim of construction).

But the DOC has adopted an administrative rule. It states that a defendant “who is serving a sentence, including a sentence imposed under ORS 137.712, for a crime listed in ORS 137.700, 137.707 * * * is not eligible to participate in alternative incarceration programs”(AIP). OAR 291-062-0130(3) (emphasis added). This rule denies AIP eligibility for defendants convicted of Measure 11 crimes, but, thanks to the “exit door,” are sentenced to Guidelines prison terms. This denial conflicts with the statute mandating “exit door” defendants’ eligibility “for a reduction in the sentence as provided in ORS 421.121 and any other statute[.]” ORS 137.712(1) (emphasis added). The conflict between ORS 137.712(1) and OAR 291-062-0130(3) must be resolved in favor of the statute, thus rendering OAR 291-062-0130(3) void. E.g., Cook v. Workers’ Compensation Dept., 306 Or 134, 138, 758 P2d 854 (1988) (when an administrative rule conflicts with a statute, the statute controls). If an AIP otherwise would be AIP eligible imminent, the inmate could challenge the OAR by state habeas corpus. Nissel v. Pearce, 307 Or 102, 764 P2d 224 (1988). But if the inmate’s AIP eligibility is not imminent, the inmate’s remedy lies in mandamus. Cullop v. Offender Information & Sentence Computation Center, 226 Or App 167, 203 P3d 276 (2009); State ex rel Curry v. Thompson, 156 Or App 537, 967 P2d 522 (1998).

In any event, the 2021 Legislature modified the DOC’s authority to

“suspend or terminate an alternative incarceration program in its entirety for more than five consecutive days unless the department has considered all other alternative actions, including modification of the program or the delivery of the program by correspondence or other remote means, and determined that continuing to deliver the program is not feasible.”

ORS 421.507(2).

In addition, the legislature mandated the DOC to “establish a process for providing regular updates and explanations, in writing, to adults in custody participating in an alternative incarceration program about changes to or decisions concerning the program, and any effect on release dates.” ORS 421.509(1). Moreover, the DOC “shall establish a process to ensure that alternative incarceration program counselors adequately communicate with adults in custody who are participating in the program, including providing accurate information about the alternative incarceration program and changes to or decisions concerning the program.” ORS 421.509(2). The DOC “shall designate a staff member at each department facility who shall be accessible to adults in custody to provide accurate information about alternative incarceration programs.” Or Laws 2021, ch 501, § 4(3).

Finally, the legislature mandated the DOC to ensure that its special alternative incarceration and intensive alternative incarceration addiction programs “be trauma-informed and gender-responsive[.]” ORS 421.504(1)(e); ORS 421.506(6).

Another form of prison-sentence relief is the concept of a sentence of discharge. ORS 137.010(7)(d) and ORS 161.715 authorize such sentences.

Still another form of prison-sentence relief is gubernatorial commutation. Or Const, Art V, § 14. However, Kaplan and Mayhew explain that gubernatorial commutation had become moribund, as sentence mitigation authority. Kaplan and Mayhew summarize:

“In this Article, we analyze the historical use of the clemency power at both the federal and state levels, including the factors that occurred during the 20th century that resulted in both presidents and governors gradually using the power less frequently up until the 1980s. We examine how the ‘war on crime’ and other political and legal changes, including the imposition of new mandatory minimum sentencing laws during the 1980s and 1990s, has led to mass-incarceration at both a national and Oregonian level. We discuss how this new punitive sentencing and incarceration philosophy has resulted in a general souring of the use of the pardon power and is now seen as a challenge to powerful prosecutors who generally oppose clemency as an extra-judicial attack on their own policies. In looking at the current prison population in Oregon, we argue that the current Governor should use her pardon power as a tool to mitigate some of the prevalent injustice in Oregon.”

Aliza B. Kaplan & Venitia Mayhew, The Governor’s Clemency Power: An Underused Tool to Mitigate the Impact of Measure 11 in Oregon, 23 Lewis & Clark L Rev 1285 (2020) (italics omitted).

Former Governor Kate Brown exercised her clemency authority at a rate this author has never before seen. It seems plausible that Kaplan and Mayhew’s article encouraged Governor Brown to make frequent use of her clemency authority.

More to the point, that at a specific time, Governor Brown had exercised her clemency authority to such an extent that she “granted clemency to approximately 1,026 convicted felons[.]” Marteeny v. Brown, 321 Or App 250, 253, 517 P3d 343 (2022). Relators brought a mandamus action, claiming “that the commutations here were procedurally flawed, and unlawful for a variety of reasons[.]” Id. But the Court of Appeals August 10, 2022 Media Release, which summarized the Court of Appeals opinion id. at 292-93, rejected the relators’ arguments. The summary of the court’s opinion stated

“that the clemency power of presidents and governors traces its origins to the earliest days of English common law. The arguments and emotions present in this case echo through the centuries. The power to pardon, sitting within a singular executive—be they monarch, president, or governor—has always been controversial, seemingly at odds with legislative determination and judicial decision-making. Whenever it has been used, it has been lauded by some, and condemned by others. The court was not called on to judge the wisdom of the Governor’s clemency of these 1,026 individuals; that is a political question. The court was tasked solely with determining [Governor Brown’s] authority to do so under Oregon law. And on that narrow question, the court concluded that the commutations at issue were a lawful exercise of the broad clemency power afforded Oregon governors by constitution and statute.”

The next form of prison-sentence relief applies to juveniles who were remanded to and convicted in adult court. After serving one-half of their sentences, these juvenile defendants become eligible for so-called “second-look” hearings before the sentencing court. ORS 420A.203 and 420A.206. “At the conclusion of the hearing * * * the court shall order” either “that the person serve the entire remainder of the sentence,” or “that the person be conditionally released * * * at such time as the court may order[.]” ORS 420A.203(4)(a)(A) and (B).

Under the terms of ORS 137.707, juveniles who are 15, 16, or 17 years old when they commit Measure 11 crimes are automatically remanded to adult court. Because of that automatic remand, they are ineligible for second-look hearings.

But Senate Bill 1008 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2019, chapter 634) changes all that—first by eliminating the automatic-remand provision. SB 1008 requires instead that a juvenile, charged with one or more Measure 11 crimes, may be tried as an adult only if, after a hearing on the matter, the juvenile court remands the juvenile to adult court. ORS 419C.349(1)(a). Moreover, the bill states that under specified circumstances, a juvenile who is remanded to adult court, to stand trial for Measure 11 crimes and is convicted of them, is eligible for a second-look hearing. ORS 420A.203(1)(a)(B).

Yet another provision of SB 1008 fundamentally alters the nature of certain sentences imposed on juveniles who were tried as adults. The provision applies to anyone convicted of crimes committed before turning 18 years of age, and was sentenced to a prison term of greater than 15 years. The person becomes parole eligible after completing the first 15 years of the sentence. ORS 144.397.

§ 4-3.1. Senate Bill 936 Limitations on Relief.

The 1997 Legislature’s Senate Bill 936 limits availability of most forms of relief discussed above. The bill is the “spawn” of 1996’s Ballot Measure 40. Section (1)(j) of that measure would have disentitled a defendant from eligibility for early-release programs unless authorized by the trial court. In Armatta v. Kitzhaber, 327 Or 250, 959 P2d 49 (1998), the Supreme Court declared Measure 40 invalid in its entirety. Nevertheless, the electorate’s subsequent adoption of Ballot Measure 74 (1999) (codified at Or Const Art I, § 44) effectively restored the constitutional provision the Armatta court invalidated. Consequently, Senate Bill 936’s provisions, limiting the availability of the various forms of relief, are constitutionally obligatory.

SB 936 primarily was codified at ORS 137.750 and 137.752. Although the bill took effect June 12, 1997, ORS 137.750 and 137.752 apply retroactively to crimes committed on or after the ill-fated Measure 40’s December 5, 1996 intended effective date. State v. Pettigrew, 185 Or App 313, 59 P3d 594 (2002); State v. Woods, 165 Or App 551, 997 P2d 275 (2000).

In State v. Grimes, 163 Or App 340, 986 P2d 1290 (1999), the Court of Appeals held that applying ORS 137.750 and 137.752 to crimes committed before SB 936’s June 12, 1997 effective date, but on or after Measure 40’s December 5, 1996 intended effective date, is not ex post facto. But in State v. Fugate, 332 Or 195, 26 P3d 802 (2001), the Oregon Supreme Court held that retroactive application of one of SB 936’s evidentiary provisions is ex post facto. This holding may overrule Grimes sub silentio.

Under ORS 137.750 and 137.752, the court must authorize participation in early-release programs unless it finds substantial and compelling reasons not to. State v. Berger, 284 Or App 156, 161, 392 P3d 792 (2017). Counsel may challenge the validity of a court’s reasons to refuse to declare a defendant eligible, just as counsel may challenge a court’s departure factors. See Section 9 (discussing appellate review of departure factors). This includes challenges based on the claim that the findings are subject to the state constitutional jury trial requirements discussed in Chapter 1. But see State v. Clark, 205 Or App 338, 345-46, 134 P3d 1074, rev den, 341 Or 245 (2006) (rejecting claim that findings are subject to federal constitutional jury trial requirements).

State v. Messer, 317 Or App 803, 507 P3d 337 (2022) provides authority for a situation where the trial court erred in claiming that for “substantial and compelling reasons,” it denied the defendant eligibility for earned time and alternative sentencing programs under ORS 137.750(1). The “defendant contend[ed] that the fact that, before her arrest, she did not apologize to both victims’ families after being rebuffed by the first family member she contacted, did not amount to a substantial and compelling reason.” Messer, 317 Or App at 804. The Court of Appeals held,

“In these circumstances, defendant’s failure to apologize to the family of one of the victims, particularly after being angrily rebuffed by a member of the first victim’s family, is not sufficiently exceptional to amount to a substantial and compelling reason to deny her eligibility. Indeed, it is an open question whether the failure to admit to a crime prior to arrest can be used against a defendant in the subsequent criminal prosecution.”

Id. at 805.

Conversely, in Bacon v. Cain, 327 Or App 673, ___ P3d ___ (2023), mitigating factors could establish "substantial and compelling reasons," ORS 137.750(1), "allowing petitioner eligibility for alternative incarceration programs." Cain, 327 Or App at 678. There, Bacon's trial attorney offered no mitigating factors to offset the prosecution's offer of aggravating factors. But on post-conviction, Bacon's post-conviction attorney claimed (a) that trial counsel's failure to offer mitigating factors provided inadequate assistance, Or Const, Art I, § 11, and (b) that Bacon's post-conviction attorney presented mitigating evidence. The Court of Appeals accepted that the post-conviction attorney's mitigating evidence established "substantial and compelling reasons," and "[r]eversed and remanded with instructions to grant post-conviction relief on petitioner’s claim that trial counsel was inadequate under Article I, section 11, for failing to present mitigating evidence at sentencing[.]" Id. at 679.

The eligibility declaration must appear in the judgment. If no such declaration appears, a defendant’s supervisory authority will exclude him from eligibility for early-release programs. ORS 137.750(2); ORS 137.752(2).

Trial courts occasionally fail to consider, and thus to decide, whether defendants are eligible for early-release programs. In such a case, the judgment will be silent about the defendant’s eligibility, and the defendant will be ineligible. ORS 137.750(2); ORS 137.752(2). Irrespective of the filing of an appeal, the trial court retains permanent authority to modify the judgment by ordering the defendant eligible for early-release programs. ORS 137.754.

PRACTICE TIPS: In a case where the record is silent, counsel should move to modify the judgment to declare that the defendant is eligible for early-release programs. Counsel should explain that if the court grants the motion, a copy of the order should be sent to the appropriate incarceration authority. Counsel also should explain that the court may not deny the motion without a hearing. This is because denying the motion without a hearing would violate the defendant’s statutory and constitutional rights to notice and an opportunity to be heard on the court making a detrimental sentencing decision. See Chapter 1 (discussing such rights).

Furthermore, trial courts sometimes base an early-release program eligibility denial on the same substantial and compelling reasons they based aggravated departure sentences. By doing so, the court effectively relies on the same conduct twice to aggravate punishment. Such a sentencing decision is subject to the double jeopardy and due process challenges discussed in Chapter 1.

As previously explained in Section 4-2.3.1, there is one situation in which a defendant’s program eligibility is not contingent on a trial court order declaring him eligible. If the court sentences an ORS 137.700 or 137.707 defendant to a lesser guidelines sentence under ORS 137.712, the defendant is automatically “eligible for a reduction in the sentence as provided in ORS 421.121 and any other statute.” ORS 137.712(1)(a) (emphasis added). ORS 421.121 is the statute authorizing time reductions for good behavior, in guidelines-era cases. ORS 137.712(1)(a)’s emphasized phrase “and any other statute” refers to ” ORS 421.504’s and ORS 421.506’s AIPs. This should mean that if the court imposes a lesser guidelines sentence on a defendant who otherwise would be subject to ORS 137.700 or 137.707, ORS 137.712(1) automatically makes him AIP eligible.

§ 4-4. OAR 213-005-0002 TERM OF COMMUNITY POST-PRISON SUPERVISION.

One of the hallmarks of the guidelines is that in virtually all non-straight jail and non-probationary cases, the trial court must impose a term of post-prison supervision (PPS). Guidelines’ commentary explains, “Under the sentencing guidelines system the role of ‘parole’ in setting the actual length of imprisonment is ended. * * * [T]he Guidelines Board adopted a system of post-prison supervision.” Implementation Manual at 91. It is important to keep in mind that PPS is similar to but not the same as parole; each operates within uniquely different sentencing systems. See generally Id. 181-92 (describing parole board’s PPS responsibilities under the guidelines system). See also Kowalski v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 194 Or App 156, 93 P3d 831 (2004), rev den, 338 Or 16 (2005) (noting board’s opinion that it has “considerably more flexibility in imposing sanctions for parole violations” than it has for PPS violations); State v. Dizick, 137 Or App 486, 489, 905 P2d 250, rev den, 322 Or 490 (1996) (trial court erred by imposing parole under former ORS 144.305 on an offender whose crime occurred after November 1, 1989).

OAR 213-005-0002 states the principles of PPS terms:

(1) A term of community supervision is part of the sentence for any felony offender who is sentenced to the legal and physical custody of the Department or to the supervisory authority. This term of community supervision shall be described as post-prison supervision. Departures on the duration of post-prison supervision shall not be allowed.

(2) The duration of post-prison supervision shall be determined by the crime seriousness category of the most serious current crime of conviction:

(a) One year for Crime Categories 1-3, two years for Crime Categories 4-6 and three years for Crime Categories 7-11.

(b) Notwithstanding section (2)(a) of this rule, the following periods of post-prison supervision shall apply:

(A) For an offender convicted of murder or aggravated murder the period shall be as established in OAR 213-005-0004.

(B) For an offender sentenced as a sexually violent dangerous offender the period shall be as established in OAR 213-005-0004.

(C) The duration of the term of post-prison supervision as designated in ORS 144.103.

(3) The term of post-prison supervision shall begin upon completion of the offender’s prison term or such term as directed by the supervisory authority. For offenders successfully completing the alternative incarceration program (boot camp) described in ORS 421.500 et. seq., the term of post-prison supervision begins upon release pursuant to ORS 421.508(3).

(4) The term of post-prison supervision, when added to the prison term, shall not exceed the statutory maximum indeterminate sentence for the crime of conviction. When the total duration of any sentence (prison incarceration and post-prison supervision) exceeds the statutory maximum indeterminate sentence described in ORS 161.605, the sentencing judge shall first reduce the duration of post-prison supervision to the extent necessary to conform the total sentence length to the statutory maximum.

(5) The duration of post-prison supervision established under section (2) of this rule does not apply to dangerous offender departure sentences, which shall be governed by ORS 144.232.

§ 4-4.1. Post-Prison Supervision Terms, Generally.

Albeit with rare exceptions discussed later, for felonies committed on or after November 1, 1989, a sentence that includes a term of incarceration must also include a term of PPS. ORS 137.010(10). State v. Pinkowsky, 111 Or App 166, 826 P2d 10 (1992) exemplifies the rigidity of the PPS requirement. There the trial court orally imposed a term of PPS but neglected to include it in the judgment. The Court of Appeals ruled that even after the defendant finished serving his term of imprisonment, the trial court could modify the judgment to include the PPS term. 111 Or App at 169. See also State v. Lewis, 150 Or App 257, 945 P2d 661 (1997) (PPS requirement applies to defendants sentenced under ORS 137.700 and 137.707).

Under OAR 213-005-0002(1), departures as to the term of PPS are impermissible. See State v. Rund, 115 Or App 382, 837 P2d 563 (1992) (per curiam) (where provision authorized only a 36-month PPS term, court erred in imposing a 60-month term). Unless the defendant is a dangerous offender, or was convicted of aggravated murder, murder, or certain sex crimes, the sentencing court must impose the term specified in OAR 213-005-0002(2). Under OAR 213-005-0002(2), the seriousness level of a defendant’s crime determines the duration of his PPS term. These terms are:

• Levels 1 to 3, one year.

• Levels 4 to 6, two years.

• Levels 7 to 11, three years.

For example, in State v. Horton, 327 Or App 256, ___ P3d ___ (2023), the defendant was convicted of a crime seriousness rating of level 6. The trial court imposed a three-year PPS term. The Court of Appeals agreed that because the defendant was convicted of a level 6 crime, the maximum PPS term is two years. 327 Or App at 266-67. "Accordingly, [the court] remand[ed] for resentencing[.]" Id. at 267.17

A defendant begins serving his PPS term after he finishes his incarceration term (including his term in an alternative incarceration facility). OAR 213-005-0002(3). If a court imposes multiple PPS terms for multiple crimes, the defendant begins serving all of his PPS terms upon his release from incarceration. Thus, multiple PPS terms effectively “merge” into a single term, even if the defendant’s incarceration terms are consecutive. State v. Barnum, 154 Or App 24, 26, 959 P2d 1007 (1998). But see Norris v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 237 Or App 1, 9, 238 P3d 994 (2010) (explaining that multiple terms of PPS run concurrently upon release from custody). Furthermore, a defendant’s PPS term commences not only when he is released from incarceration, but when he should have been released if he were imprisoned for longer than the law allows. Baty v. Slater, 161 Or App 653, 984 P2d 342 (1999), on recons, 164 Or App 779, 995 P2d 1176, rev den, 331 Or 191 (2000). See also Mecham v. Hill, 217 Or App 144, 146, 148, 174 P3d 1051 (2007) (reaffirming Baty). In effect, the defendant’s “dead time”—incarceration time served in excess of what the law allows—is credited against his PPS term.

EXAMPLE: The law allows a defendant’s imprisonment for 13 months, to be followed by a two-year PPS term. Although the trial court imposed a valid, two-year PPS term, it imposed an invalid 18-month prison term. The defendant appeals the prison term and wins, but by the time he wins he has served the entire 18-month prison sentence and is halfway through his PPS term. Under Mecham and Baty, the date the defendant should have been released from imprisonment—a date five months sooner than the date he actually was released—becomes the implied commencement date of his PPS term. In this example, the implied commencement date effectively shortens the defendant’s PPS term by five months.

The ambiguous phrase found at the end of OAR 213-005-0002(3)18—stating that the PPS term may begin upon “such term as directed by the supervisory authority”—was not intended to authorize the DOC to commence PPS terms at some time other than when the defendant is released from imprisonment. The legislative history, which may be obtained from the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission, shows that the phrase was intended only to implement provisions of the 1995 Legislature’s Senate Bill 1145, and to require that unless felony sentences are concurrent with or consecutive to prison sentence of greater than one year, felony sentences of less than one year must be served in local jail rather than in a DOC facility. ORS 174.020(3) (in construing a statute, the court shall “give the weight to [a statute’s] legislative history that the court considers to be appropriate”).

OAR 213-005-0002(4) limits the sum of the defendant’s term of incarceration and term of PPS to the applicable maximum indeterminate term specified by ORS 161.605 (five years for a Class C felony, 10 years for a Class B felony, 20 years for a Class C felony). The legislature adopted OAR 213-005-0002(4) in 1993, and it applies only to offenses committed after its effective date. Or Laws 1993, ch 692, § 1(1).

If the sum of the incarceration term plus the PPS term would exceed the maximum indeterminate sentence for the crime, the sentencing court must “reduce the duration of [PPS] to the extent necessary to conform the total sentence length to the statutory maximum.” State v. McCormick, 185 Or App 491, 60 P3d 1089 (2002).

§ 4-4.3. Post-Prison Supervision Terms for Dangerous Offenders.

OAR 213-005-0002 does not prescribe the term of PPS for a person found to be a dangerous offender (as opposed to a sexually violent dangerous offender) under ORS 161.725 and 161.735. As OAR 213-005-0002(5) explains, the defendant’s term of PPS is specified by ORS 144.232. In pertinent part that statute says, “An offender released under this section shall serve the remainder of the sentence term [sic] imposed under ORS 161.725, 161.735 and 161.737 on post-prison supervision[.]” ORS 144.232(4). Thus, a dangerous offender’s PPS term is the difference between his dangerous-offender term of incarceration, and the portion of the term he actually serves.

EXAMPLE: A sentencing court imposes the maximum dangerous-offender term of 30 years. See ORS 161.725. After the defendant has served 20 years, the Parole Board finds that he is in remission from the condition that made him dangerous and releases him from prison. The defendant’s PPS term will be the remainder of his dangerous-offender sentence, so 10 years.

§ 4-4.4. Supervision of Persons on Post-Prison Supervision.

ORS 144.085 divides PPS terms into two parts: (1) a minimum term of supervised PPS, and (2) a term of unsupervised PPS. In pertinent part, the statute provides:

“(1) All prisoners sentenced to prison for more than 12 months shall serve active periods of parole or post-prison supervision as follows:

“(a) Six months of active parole or post-prison supervision for crimes in crime categories one to three;

“(b) Twelve months of active parole or post-prison supervision for crimes in crime categories four to 10;

“(c) Prisoners sentenced as dangerous offenders under ORS 161.725 and 161.735, for aggravated murder under ORS 163.105 or for murder under ORS 163.115 shall serve at least three years of active parole or post-prison supervision;

“(d) Prisoners sentenced for violating or attempting to violate ORS 163.365, 163.375, 163.395, 163.405, 163.408, 163.411, 163.425 or 163.427 shall serve a term of active parole that extends for the entire term of the offender’s sentence or a term of active post-prison supervision as provided in ORS 144.103; and

“(e) Prisoners sentenced for robbery in the first degree under ORS 164.415 or for arson in the first degree under ORS 164.325 shall serve three years of active parole or post-prison supervision.”

ORS 144.085.

A defendant’s completion of the supervised portion of his PPS term does not automatically mean that his status will be changed to non-supervised. In pertinent part, ORS 144.085(3) provides:

“No sooner than 30 days prior to the expiration of an offender’s active parole or post-prison supervision period as provided in subsection (1) of this section, the supervisory authority may send to the State Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision a report requesting the board to extend the active supervision period or to return the offender to active supervision status, not to exceed the supervision term imposed by the sentencing court under the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission and applicable laws, if the offender has not substantially fulfilled the supervision conditions or has failed to complete payment of restitution.”

Thus, if the Board “finds the offender has not substantially fulfilled the supervision conditions or has failed to complete payment of restitution,” the Board “may extend the active supervision period or return the offender to active supervision status, not to exceed the supervision term imposed by the sentencing court under the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission and applicable laws[.]” ORS 144.085(4). In addition, “[d]uring the pendency of any violation proceedings, the running of the supervision period and the sentence is stayed, and the board has jurisdiction over the offender until the proceedings are resolved.” ORS 144.085(5).

§ 4-4.5. OAR 213-005-0003 Supervisory Responsibility.

OAR 213-005-0003 establishes who shall be responsible for supervising a person on PPS:

When a term of post-prison supervision is imposed as part of a sentence, the offender shall serve the term of supervision in the community under the supervision of the Department of Corrections or a corrections agency designated by the Department.

Under this provision, defendants serve PPS at the direction of the DOC. The provision authorizes the DOC to transfer supervision over a defendant to a local government’s supervisory office, or to another state’s supervisory office (through an interstate agreement). Nevertheless, the DOC retains ultimate responsibility over the defendant’s supervision.

§ 4-4.6. OAR 213-005-0004 Post-Prison Supervision for Murder, Aggravated Murder, and Sexually Violent Dangerous Offenders.

OAR 213-005-0004 establishes the term of PPS for persons convicted of murder and aggravated murder, or found to be a sexually violent dangerous offender:

(1) The term of post-prison supervision for an offender serving a sentence for murder or aggravated murder shall be for the remainder of the offender’s life, unless the Board finds a shorter term appropriate. In no case shall the term of supervision be less than three years.

(3) The term of post-prison supervision for an offender found by the sentencing court to be a sexually violent dangerous offender pursuant to ORS 137.765 shall be for the remainder of the offender’s life.

(4) The limit on sanctions for post-prison supervision violations provided in OAR 213-011-0004(3) shall not apply to offenders on post-prison supervision as provided by section (3) of this rule.

(5) Offenders on post-prison supervision as provided by section (3) of this rule may be incarcerated up to 180 days for any violation of post-prison supervision. The sanction may be imposed repeatedly during the term of the post-prison supervision for subsequent post-prison supervision violations. § 4-4.6.1. Intent of Provision.

Under OAR 213-005-0004(1), the sentencing court must impose a life term of PPS on all persons sentenced for murder or aggravated murder. The court may not impose a shorter PPS term. Accord State v. Stewart, 123 Or App 432, 433, 859 P2d 1200, rev den, 318 Or 246 (1993) (court erred in imposing on murder defendant 20-year term of PPS). But see State v. Gaynor, 130 Or App 99, 106-107, 880 P2d 947 (1984), rev den, 320 Or 508 (1995) (declining to exercise discretion to review error where sentencing court imposed 36 months of PPS on murder defendant and state invited the error). The rule confers authority on the board to shorten the life term of PPS if it “finds a shorter term appropriate.” OAR 213-005-0004(1). But in no case may the board shorten a murder defendant’s PPS term to less than three years. State v. Kennedy, 196 Or App 681, 691, 103 P3d 660 (2004). The board lacks the authority to lengthen a term of PPS, even if the court-imposed term is legally incorrect. Gaynor v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 165 Or App 609, 614, 996 P2d 1020 (2000).

OAR 213-005-0004(1)’s life-term requirement did not always exist. The 1993 Legislature created the requirement and expressly limited the application of the rule to “offenses committed on or after November 1, 1993.” Or Laws 1993, ch 692, § 1(1). For murders committed before that date, the PPS requirement of former OAR 253-05-002(2)(c) (1989) provided a PPS term of three years. By its plain terms, former OAR 253-05-004(1) (1989) authorized a sentencing court to impose a life term of PPS only if the murder defendant was serving a life sentence pursuant to ORS 163.115. But see State v. Morgan, 316 Or 553, 560, 856 P2d 612 (1993) (concluding that even though one convicted of murder after November 1, 1989 may no longer be sentenced to “imprisonment for life” under ORS 163.115(3)(a) (1989), one convicted of murder remains subject to former OAR 253-05-004(1) (1989)).

State v. Nobles, 306 Or App 1, 8, 473 P3d 1108 (2020) (“[g]iven Morgan’s construction of the applicable sentencing law, * * * we conclude that the trial court did not err in imposing a lifetime PPS term on defendant’s murder conviction,” committed in August 1993, “even though it imposed defendant’s prison term under the sentencing guidelines and did not impose a ‘life sentence’”).

See also State v. Cannon, 135 Or App 561, 564, 900 P2d 592 (1995) (following “likely legislative intention” stated in Morgan).

As previously discussed, regardless of the date of the murder, at least three years worth of a murder defendant’s PPS term must be supervised. Moreover, OAR 213-005-0004(2) (discussed below) makes clear that the limitations on incarceration sanctions for PPS violations do not apply to murder defendants.

§ 4-5. OAR 213-011-0001 CONDITIONS OF POST-PRISON SUPERVISION.

OAR 213-011-0001 specifies procedures the board must follow in setting a defendant’s conditions of PPS:

(1) The Department shall prepare a proposed release plan for each offender prior to the offender’s release from prison.

(2) The proposed release plan shall be submitted to the Board not less than sixty (60) days prior to the offender’s release and shall include:

(a) A description of support services and program opportunities available to the offender;

(b) The recommended conditions of supervision;

(c) The level of supervision which shall be consistent with the offender’s risk assessment classification;

(d) Any conditions necessary to assist the reformation of the offender; and

(e) Any other conditions and requirements as may be necessary to promote public safety.

(3) If the proposed release plan is not approved by the Board, the Board shall return the plan to the Department with its recommended modifications. The Department shall submit a revised plan to the Board not less than ten days prior to the offender’s release.

(4) If the revised plan is not acceptable to the Board, the Board shall determine the provisions of the final plan prior to the offender’s release.

§ 4-5.1 Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-011-0001 is corollary to ORS 144.096 and 144.098. The guidelines provision may conflict with the statutes in certain respects. To the extent they conflict, the statutes were adopted later in time, so they impliedly amend the guidelines provision. Vedder, 206 Or App at 430-31.

ORS 144.096 provides:

“(1)(a) The Department of Corrections shall prepare a proposed release plan for an inmate and submit the proposed release plan to the State Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision prior to the inmate’s release.

“(b) If the proposed release plan is not approved by the board, the board shall return the plan to the department with its recommended modifications. The department shall submit a revised plan to the board prior to the inmate’s release.

“(c) If the revised plan is not acceptable to the board, the board shall determine the provisions of the final plan prior to the inmate’s release.

“(d) The department, in consultation with the board, shall by rule establish deadlines by which a proposed release plan described in paragraph (a) of this subsection and a revised plan described in paragraph (b) of this subsection must be submitted to the board prior to an inmate’s release.

“(e) If an inmate was sentenced under section 29, chapter 649, Oregon Laws 2013, and the release plan recommends that the inmate participate in a reentry court, the board shall provide a copy of the release plan to the reentry court.”

ORS 144.098 provides:

“(1) When the State Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision or a local supervisory authority responsible for correctional services for an inmate reviews an inmate’s release plan prior to approval of the plan as required by ORS 144.096, it may interview the inmate and may review the following information:

“(a) Reports of any physical, psychiatric or psychological examinations of the inmate;

“(b) The presentence investigation report specified by ORS 144.791 or, if no such report has been prepared, a report of similar content prepared by institutional staff;

“(c) The record of the inmate’s conduct during confinement; and

“(d) Any other information relevant to the inmate’s reintegration into the community that may be submitted by the inmate, the inmate’s attorney, the victim of the crime, the Department of Corrections, local corrections agencies or any other person.

“(2) If the board reviews a release plan, the board must attempt to notify the victim before the review of the release plan by sending written notice to the victim if the victim requests to be notified and furnishes the board with a current address. The notice must inform the victim that the victim may submit information concerning the inmate and the crime to the board for the board’s consideration.

“(3) The department or local corrections agency shall provide to the board or local supervisory authority reviewing the release plan any psychiatric or psychological reports held by the department or local corrections agency regarding the inmate. However, if the psychiatrist or psychologist who prepared the report or any treating psychiatrist or psychologist determines that disclosure to the inmate of the contents of the report would be detrimental to the inmate’s mental or emotional health, the psychiatrist or psychologist may indorse upon the report a recommendation that it not be disclosed to the inmate. The department or local corrections agency may withhold from the board or supervisory authority reviewing the plan any report so indorsed.”

ORS 144.096, ORS 144.098, and OAR 213-011-0001 make clear that conditions of PPS, as opposed to the duration of a PPS term, are set by the board or the local supervisory authority. A sentencing court may only recommend conditions of PPS. State v. Dodson, 111 Or App 643, 644, 825 P2d 662 (1992). The court errs if it imposes PPS conditions or what amount to conditions of supervision. State v. Rubio, 248 Or App 130, 273 P3d 238 (2012) (court committed apparent error by ordering defendant to have no contact with victims); State v. Reed, 235 Or App 470, 237 P3d 826 (2010) (Court of Appeals exercises its discretion in favor of reviewing unpreserved but apparent error committed by imposing PPS condition).

When a defendant is nearing completion of his term of incarceration, the DOC or the local supervisory authority prepares a release plan. ORS 144.096. The agency must attempt to notify the victim before reviewing inmates’ release plans. ORS 144.098(2). The board or local supervisory authority must review and approve the plan before the inmate’s release. In doing so, the agency reviews any psychological and psychiatric reports contained in the inmate’s file. ORS 144.096; ORS 144.098. The release plan must include proposed conditions of PPS.

Counsel should be wary of OAR 213-011-0001(2)(c), which states that “[t]he level of [post-prison] supervision * * * shall be consistent with the offender’s risk assessment classification[.]” (Emphasis added.) This wariness stems from the lack of reliability of risk assessment testing, requiring the development and use of a “toolbox” to challenge risk assessment testing. As Philipsborn explains:

“A lawyer’s need for a toolbox arises in part because different theories, categories, and classes of such assessments exist; there are scientific and technical questions about the reliability of risk assessment practices in criminal justice risk assessment; the individual doing the assessment may not have an adequate background, training, or knowledge of the reliability of the assessment procedure being used. Another important reason for preparing a toolbox is Professor Christopher Slobogin’s observation in his book on ‘dangerousness’ adjudications about ‘[t]he courts’ nonchalance toward prediction testimony.’”

John Philipsoborn, A Basic Risk Assessment Toolbox: Aiming for Adequate Lawyering During the Spread of Risk Assessments, The Champion, Jan/Feb 2020, at 36 (quoting Christopher Slobogin, Proving the Unprovable: The Role of Law, Science, & Speculation in Adjudicating Culpability & Dangerousness 108 (2007)).

“[T]he Board must give notification to certain officials when an offender is to be released to post-prison supervision. ORS 144.260. When an offender is to be released, the Board must notify the Department of Corrections, the sentencing judge, the district attorney and sheriff or arresting agency. Similarly, if the Department of Corrections releases the offender, the Department must notify the sentencing judge and the district attorney at least 30 days prior to the offender’s release. Both the Board and the Department must also notify the victim if so requested.”

Implementation Manual at 188.

§ 4-5.3. Permissible Conditions of Post-Prison Supervision and Notification to Defendant.

ORS 144.102 enumerates the conditions of PPS the Board may impose. It further requires the board or the local supervisory authority (as the case may be) to provide the defendant a written copy of his PPS conditions.

In Martin v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 327 Or 147, 155, 957 P2d 1210 (1998), the court held that the findings of fact and conclusions of law requirement of the Administrative Procedure Act does not constrain the board’s imposition of PPS conditions. But the court also set the requirements for imposing valid PPS conditions. The board must provide, on the record, “some kind of explanation connecting the facts of the case (which would include the facts found, if any) and the” condition imposed. 327 Or at 157. Under the extreme facts of Martin, the court affirmed the board’s imposition of a PPS condition generally barring the defendant from entering his victim’s county of residence. Id. at 159-60. On the other hand, in Ferry v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 293 Or App 216, 427 P3d 1123 (2018), the board erred in imposing PPS a condition prohibiting the defendant from contacting the victim’s family, because the condition was not required and the board did not exercise its discretion in determining whether the condition was appropriate.

Moreover, Weems/Roberts v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 347 Or 586, 227 P3d 671 (2010), establishes that the board may base PPS conditions not only on the nature of the crimes for which the defendant was sentenced and is now being released onto PPS, but also on the nature of the defendant himself.

In Weems/Roberts, the defendants were convicted of non-sexual offenses, but the board imposed special conditions of PPS that are designed for sex offenders. The defendants argued that PPS conditions must be based solely on their crimes of conviction, so the special conditions were invalid. The Supreme Court disagreed. It determined that the plain text of ORS 144.102(4)(a)19 authorizes the board to impose special conditions based on the individual circumstances of “the person” on supervision, rather than solely on the circumstances of the offense. The court concluded that statutory context confirms this construction of ORS 144.102(3)(a)—specifically, ORS 144.098(1), which authorizes the board to consider “any information relevant to the inmate’s reintegration into the community”; and ORS 144.096(4)(d) and (f), which mandate the Department of Corrections to propose to the board special conditions of PPS as may be necessary “to promote public safety” and “to assist the reformation of the inmate.” See also Simpson v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 237 Or App 661, 241 P 3d 347 (2010) (given record that was before the board, of 22-year-old conviction for sex offense, plus circumstances surrounding current conviction, board could reasonably conclude sex offender treatment necessary as condition of PPS for drug conviction).

In Schmult v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 306 Or App 350, 474 P3d 920 (2020), the board imposed a special condition PPS that essentially barred the petitioner from using any computer for any reasons without prior approval from his supervisor. The Court of Appeals reversed, stating:

“[W]e can think of no reason that requiring a supervising officer’s permission (and potentially a sex-offender treatment provider’s additional permission) before petitioner uses any computer for any purpose would be essential to advancing the board’s goals of promoting public safety and assisting in petitioner’s reformation. In imposing the challenged condition on petitioner, the board therefore acted beyond its statutory authority with respect to imposing special conditions of post-prison supervision.”

306 Or App at 358 (court’s emphasis; citation omitted).

Similarly, in Crombie v. Board of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 325 Or App 312, 528 P3d 1171 (2023), the Court of Appeals rejected a PPS condition where the board had imposed an expansive prohibition on access to electronic devices, in addition to other conditions already imposed on petitioner, including a condition that he have no contact whatsoever with minors. Looking to petitioner’s individual circumstances, as required by ORS 144.102(4)(a) and existing precedent, the board could not reasonably view it as essential to public safety or petitioner’s reformation that petitioner have no access to any electronic devices with communications capability.20

Finally, the Supreme Court’s Media Release issued on December 5, 2018 states that the court allowed review in Tuckenberry v. Bd. of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 291 Or App 843, 419 P3d 818 (2018) (affirmed without opinion), and will consider the following issues:

“(1) Is a PPS condition that prohibits a person from entering into or participating in any intimate relationship or intimate encounters without prior written permission from his parole officer broader than necessary to achieve the goals of PPS?

“(2) Is a PPS condition that prohibits a person from entering into or participating in any intimate relationship or encounters without prior written permission from his parole officer unconstitutionally vague and overbroad?”

§ 4-6. OAR 213-011-0002 RESPONSIBILITY FOR POST-PRISON SUPERVISION.

Like OAR 213-005-0003 discussed in Section 4-4.5, OAR 213-011-0002 explains that the DOC ultimately is responsible for the supervision of a defendant on PPS:

Upon release from prison, the offender shall be supervised by the Department or the corrections agency designated by the Department.

§ 4-6.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-011-0002 is a corollary of ORS 144.104(1), which states: “Upon release from prison, the person shall be supervised by the Department of Corrections or the corrections agency designated by the department.” Commentary states that OAR 213-011-0002 “eliminates any uncertainty about supervisory responsibilities under the new program of [PPS]. It assigns that responsibility to the corrections officers or Community Corrections staff who now supervise parolees in the community.” Implementation Manual at 189.

§ 4-7. OAR 213-011-0003 LEVEL OF POST-PRISON SUPERVISION.

OAR 213-005-0003 explains how long a defendant will be supervised while on PPS. OAR 213-011-0003 addresses the intensity level of the supervised portion of a defendant’s PPS term:

(1) During the term of post-prison supervision, the Department or its designee may adjust the level of supervision and recommend to the Board revisions to the conditions of supervision appropriate to the offender’s conduct in the community.

(2) Notwithstanding section (1) of this rule, a person shall be subject to intensive supervision for the full period of post-prison supervision if the person is identified by the sentencing court, the Board or a supervisory authority to be a sexually violent dangerous offender pursuant to ORS 137.765 or 144.635.

§ 4-7.1 Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-011-0003 is a corollary of ORS 144.102(2) and 144.104(2). In pertinent part, ORS 144.102(2) states: “The board or the supervisory authority shall determine, and may at any time modify, the conditions of post-prison supervision[.]” ORS 144.104(2) provides: “During the period of post-prison supervision, the supervisory authority may adjust the level of supervision and recommend to the State Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision revisions to the conditions of supervision appropriate to the released person’s conduct in the community.”

Commentary to these provisions states:

“While the initial level of supervision is set in the release plan, the Department should adjust the amount of supervision to be consistent with the offender’s conduct in the community.

“The degree of supervision should be increased if the offender’s behavior indicates the need for added supervision. Additional supervision is recommended if needed to encourage the offender’s compliance with the supervisory conditions or to provide added public safety. Conversely, the level of supervision should be reduced if the offender complies with the conditions of probation and demonstrates an ability to function in the community without further participation in criminal activity.”

Implementation Manual at 191-92.

In Tuckenberry v. Bd. of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 365 Or 640, 451 P3d 227 (2019), the Oregon Supreme Court considered a special condition of PPS that the board had imposed, which prohibited the defendant from entering into or participating in an intimate relationship, or intimate encounters, without prior written permission from his parole officer. The court held that the condition could not reasonably be “consider[ed] necessary” within the meaning of ORS 144.102(4)(a), to advance the board’s statutory goals. Tuckenberry, 365 Or at 656. Accordingly, the court reversed the board’s imposition of the special condition.

In Denning v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 325 Or App 26, ___ P3d ___ (2023), the board, relying on ORS 144.102(4)(b)(J), imposed on the petitioner a polygraph condition of PPS. On appeal, the petitioner asserted that imposing that condition infringed on his right against self-incrimination under the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution. The Court of Appeals held that the board’s imposition of the condition did not run afoul of the petitioner’s Fifth Amendment rights. Nevertheless, the court further concluded that the petitioner could choose, on a question-by-question basis, to invoke the Fifth Amendment privilege, the exercise of which cannot be punished under the terms of the supervision condition itself.

§ 4-8. OAR 213-011-0004 POST-PRISON SUPERVISION SANCTIONS.

OAR 213-011-0004 specifies sanctions which may be imposed for violations of PPS conditions:

(1) The supervisory authority shall use a continuum of administrative sanctions for violation of the conditions of post-prison supervision. The sanction continuum shall include: adjustments to the level of supervision, modification of or addition to the conditions of community supervision as approved by the Board, and any other appropriate available local sanction.

(2) If the violation of post-prison supervision is new criminal activity or if the supervisory authority finds that the continuum of sanctions is insufficient punishment for any violation of the conditions of post-prison supervision, the supervisory authority may request the Board to impose the most restrictive local options available including incarceration in jail.

(3) If requested to impose the most restrictive local option available pursuant to subsection (2), the Board shall hold a hearing to determine whether incarceration in jail is appropriate and may impose an appropriate term of incarceration up to ninety (90) days for a technical violation and up to one hundred and eighty (180) days for conduct constituting a crime. Except as provided in OAR 213-005-0004(2) during the full term of post-prison supervision, for violations of the conditions of supervision, an offender may not be required to serve more than:

(a) Six months of incarceration if the term of post-prison supervision is one year;

(b) Nine months of incarceration if the term of post-prison supervision is two years; or

(c) Twelve months of incarceration if the term of post-prison supervision is three years or longer.

(4) An offender ordered to serve a term of incarceration in jail as a sanction for a post-prison supervision violation is not eligible for earned-credit time or transitional leave.

(5) An offender ordered to serve a term of incarceration in jail as a sanction for a post-prison supervision violation shall receive credit for time served in a state or local correctional facility on the supervisory violation prior to the Board’s imposition of a term of incarceration in jail.

§ 4-8.1. History of Provision.

Commentary explains that a defendant’s

“supervisory authority is directed to use a continuum of local sanctions to punish the offender in the community for supervision violations. If the supervisory authority determines that local sanctions are insufficient punishment of the violation, the agent may request the Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision to impose a prison term sanction.”

Implementation Manual at 192 (citations and footnote omitted).

The 1993 Legislature amended OAR 213-011-0004. The 1993 amendments apply to crimes committed on or after November 1, 1993. In response to the 1995 Legislature’s Senate Bill 1145, in 1997, the legislature again amended OAR 213-011-0004. SB 1145 requires that felony sentences of one year or less be served in a local jail (unless they are consecutive to sentences of a year or longer). ORS 137.124(2)(a)-(b).

The current version of OAR 213-011-0004 applies to crimes committed on or after November 1, 1997. Furthermore, the current version is unlike its two predecessors in that it authorizes only local jail-term sanctions for PPS violations. ORS 144.108 authorizes sanctions to be served in the state correctional system. See Section 4-8.3.

§ 4-8.2. Intermediate Sanctions for Post-Prison Supervision Violations.

OAR 213-011-0004(1) authorizes the imposition of intermediate sanctions, i.e., local non-imprisonment sanctions. It is corollary to and supplemented by ORS 144.106, which provides:

“(1) Except as otherwise provided by rules of the Department of Corrections and the State Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision concerning parole and post-prison supervision violators, the supervisory authority shall use a continuum of administrative sanctions for violations of the conditions of post-prison supervision.

“(2) The sanction continuum shall include adjustments to the level of supervision and, as approved by the board or the local supervisory authority that imposed the initial conditions of post-prison supervision:

“(a) Modification of or additions to the conditions of supervision; and

“(b) Any other appropriate available local sanctions including, but not limited to, jail, community service work, house arrest, electronic surveillance, restitution centers, work release centers, day centers or other local sanctions established by agreement with the supervisory authority.

“(3) An offender may not be confined in a restitution center, work release center or jail for more than 15 days for a violation of conditions of post-prison supervision unless:

“(a) The Department of Corrections, county corrections agency or supervisory authority imposes a local sanction under subsection (1) of this section;

“(b) A reentry court imposes a local sanction under section 29, chapter 649, Oregon Laws 2013; or

“(c) The board or its designated representative initiates a hearing for the purpose of imposing a sanction under ORS 144.107 or 144.108.

“(4) A hearing before the board is not required if the department, a county corrections agency, the supervisory authority or the court imposes a local sanction under subsection (3) of this section. However, the board may conduct a hearing under the procedures in ORS 144.343 and 144.347 and impose a different sanction on the offender than that imposed by the department, a county corrections agency, the supervisory authority or the court.”

According to commentary, ORS 144.106

“allow[s] the State Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision to delegate the authority to impose graduated sanctions in lieu of return[ing the offender] to prison for minor violations. [It p]ermits imposition of the sanction without a hearing unless the sanction requires confinement for more than 15 days. [It d]eletes the requirement of a hearing in those cases where a revocation is based on a conviction for a new crime for which the offender has been convicted and sent to prison. [Finally, it a]llows the use of citations to direct appearance at a violation hearing.”

Supplement Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 30 (1992).

The provision allowing for short periods of incarceration without a pre-deprivation hearing violates due process. Morrissey v. Brewer, 408 US 471, 92 S Ct 2593, 33 L Ed 2d 484 (1972). See also Young v. Harper, 520 US 143, 117 S Ct 1148, 137 L Ed 2d 270 (1997) (defendants released onto parole-like program to reduce prison overcrowding must be given Morrissey hearing before being returned to prison).

§ 4-8.3. Prison Sanctions for Post-Prison Supervision Violations.

The original, 1993, and 1995 versions of OAR 213-011-0004 set out the procedure for imposing PPS-violation sanctions on defendants who committed their crimes before July 14, 1997. Those provisions also set the duration of sanctions that may be imposed when a defendant violates PPS and is returned to prison. The board was to return a defendant to prison only if it “determines that local sanctions are insufficient to punish the offender’s supervision violations.” Implementation Manual at 194.

The guidelines provisions are corollary to ORS 144.108, which provides:

“(1) If the violation of post-prison supervision is new criminal activity or if the supervisory authority finds that the continuum of sanctions is insufficient punishment for a violation of the conditions of post-prison supervision, the supervisory authority may:

“(a) Impose the most restrictive sanction available, including incarceration in jail;

“(b) Request the State Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision to impose a sanction under subsection (2) of this section; or

“(c) Request the board to impose a sanction under ORS 144.107.

“(2) If so requested, the board or its designated representative shall hold a hearing to determine whether incarceration in a jail or state correctional facility is appropriate. Except as otherwise provided by rules of the board and the Department of Corrections concerning parole and post-prison supervision violators, the board may impose a sanction up to the maximum provided by rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission. In conducting a hearing pursuant to this subsection, the board or its designated representative shall follow the procedures and the offender shall have all the rights described in ORS 144.343 and 144.347 relating to revocation of parole.

“(3) A person who is ordered to serve a term of incarceration in a jail or state correctional facility as a sanction for a post-prison supervision violation is not eligible for:

“(a) Earned credit time as described in ORS 169.110 or 421.121;

“(b) Transitional leave as defined in ORS 421.168; or

“(c) Temporary leave as described in ORS 169.115 or 421.165 (1987 Replacement Part).

“(4) A person who is ordered to serve a term of incarceration in a state correctional facility as a sanction for a post-prison supervision violation shall receive credit for time served on the post-prison supervision violation prior to the board’s imposition of the term of incarceration.”

§ 4-8.3.1. Maximum Sanctions for Individual Post-Prison Supervision Violations.

For cases involving crimes committed on or after July 14, 1997, where the Board decides to impose a local sanction under OAR 213-011-0004 rather than a prison sanction under ORS 144.107, the maximum incarceration sanction the Board may impose for an individual violation is: (a) 90 days for a technical violation (i.e., a violation of a condition of PPS which does not constitute a new crime), or (b) 180 days for a violation which constitutes a new crime. OAR 213-011-0004(3). In all situations involving crimes committed before July 14, 1997, identical 90- and 180-day limitations apply. Former OAR 253-11-004(3) (1993).

The 1997 Legislature’s Senate Bill 56 created ORS 144.107. It specifies the maximum prison terms the Board may impose as sanctions for PPS violations in cases involving crimes committed on or July 14, 1997. ORS 144.107(3) explains that the statute applies to defendants who are on PPS:

(a) following a prison term of more than 12 months,

(b) for a crime ranked at Level 8 or higher,

(c) for a Measure 11 crime,

(d) as part of a dangerous-offender sentence, or

(e) under ORS 144.103.

In such cases the Board may impose a sanction of up to a 12-month prison term. Furthermore, the board may adopt administrative rules allowing it to impose up to 24 months’ imprisonment. ORS 144.107(4). On top of that, the Board may adopt rules allowing it to impose a sanction which

“may not exceed the greater of:

“(a) The length of incarceration plus the length of post-prison supervision imposed by the court; or

“(b) The maximum term of imprisonment imposed by the court.”

ORS 144.107(5).

The board has promulgated OAR 255-075-0072, which regulates the issuance of re-release orders after revocations of PPS. The board also has promulgated OAR 255-075-0073, which regulates the imposition of PPS revocation sanctions of greater than 12 months’ incarceration.

The board cites ORS 144.346 and 144.395 as authority for the portions of OAR 255-075-0072 that purport to authorize the revocation of PPS (to be followed by re-release upon completion of a revocation sanction). But ORS 144.346 and 144.395 expressly pertain to parole, not to PPS. With the exception of persons sentenced for murder and aggravated murder, guidelines-era defendants are ineligible for parole. Or Laws 1999, ch 782, § 2. See also State ex rel Engweiler v. Felton, 350 Or 592, 598, 260 P3d 448 (2011) (the legislative note following ORS 144.110 “effectively operates as an exception to the date limitation in ORS 144.050 on the board’s parole authority, permitting it to continue to exercise that authority over prisoners convicted of aggravated murder after November 1, 1989.”). Instead, guidelines defendants incur PPS terms. No statute authorizes the board to promulgate rules authorizing revocations of PPS terms. To the extent OAR 255-075-0072 authorizes the revocation of PPS terms, and to the extent OAR 255-075-0073 PPS revocation sanctions, the rules are invalid. Coquille School Dist. 8 v. Castillo, 212 Or App 597, 607, 159 P3d 338 (2007) (“‘It is a fundamental principle of administrative law that an administrative body possesses only those powers that the legislature grants, and that it cannot exercise authority that it does not possess. * * * Instead, [its] powers are restricted to those conferred expressly by statute or by necessary implication.’” (quoting Gaynor, 165 Or App at 612)).21

Furthermore, OAR 255-075-0072(4) states:

“Revocation of post-prison supervision stops the period of post-prison supervision from running while the offender is serving time in custody for a revocation sanction. The re-release order following a revocation sanction shall include a re-calculation of the post-prison supervision expiration date to account for the time the offender was in custody serving the revocation sanction.”

(Emphasis added).

Thus, based on a non-existent authority to revoke PPS, OAR 255-075-0072(4) authorizes the “tolling” of PPS terms while a defendant serves his sanction. But neither ORS 144.346 nor ORS 144.395 (or any other statute) authorizes such tolling. To the extent OAR 255-075-0072(4) authorizes tolling, it too is invalid. Coquille School Dist., 212 Or App at 607. Consequently, a defendant’s PPS term continues to run while he serves a sanction.

§ 4-8.3.2. Total Sanctions for Repeated Violations.

With the exception of murder defendants and dangerous offenders, the Board’s authority to impose prison sanctions for repeated violations is limited. The limitations vary, depending on the date of the crime.

§ 4-8.3.2.1. Total Sanctions for Crimes Committed On or After July 14, 1997.

If a defendant repeatedly violates PPS imposed on a crime committed on or after July 14, 1997 and ORS 144.107 does not apply (or is not used), the board may impose local jail sanctions until it has given the defendant a total of: (a) six months if he was serving a one-year PPS term, (b) nine months if he was serving a two-year PPS term, and (c) 12 months if he was serving a PPS term of three years or longer. OAR 213-011-0004(3)(a)-(c).

EXAMPLE: A defendant is released onto a two-year term of PPS. Shortly after his release he commits a new-crime violation. The board gives him the maximum local sanction of six months in jail. Shortly after his release from serving that term, he commits a second new-crime violation. For this second new-crime violation, the board may not give him another six-month sanction. This is because a second six-month sanction, when combined with the first six-month sanction, would exceed by three months the nine-month limit of OAR 213-011-0004(3)(b). For the second new-crime violation, the board could give the defendant only a three-month sanction.

The limits on total imprisonment sanctions create an anomaly. After enough violations, a defendant who is not subject to ORS 144.107 may reach a point where he is immune from further incarceration sanctions. Consider the defendant in the above example. Upon his release from serving the second incarceration sanction, the defendant may repeatedly violate PPS without concern for further incarceration sanctions. See Frey v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 151 Or App 267, 269, 948 P2d 738 (1998) (court accepts Board’s concession that once defendant has served incarceration sanction total of 180 days, no more incarceration sanctions are available). He would however be subject to various types of non-incarceration local sanctions.

§ 4-8.3.2.2. Crimes Committed Before September 15, 1993.

The six- , nine- , and 12-month limitations set out in OAR 213-011-0004(3) do not apply to PPS terms imposed on crimes committed before September 15, 1993. For such crimes, the limitations are set by former OAR 253-11-004(3) (1989). No matter the duration of his PPS term, if the defendant repeatedly violates PPS, the board may impose incarceration sanctions until it has given him a total of 180 days. State v. Barnum, 154 Or App at 26 and n 2.

EXAMPLE: Shortly after his release onto PPS for a crime committed before September 15, 1993, a defendant commits a new-crime violation. The board imposes the maximum sanction of 180 days’ incarceration. Under former OAR 253-11-004(3) (1989), that sanction exhausts the board’s sanctioning authority for the defendant. After the defendant is released from incarceration, he may violate PPS without concern for additional incarceration sanctions. See Frey, 151 Or App at 269. But he still would be subject to various types of non-incarceration sanctions.

§ 4-8.3.3. Total Sanctions for Repeated Violations of Post-Prison Supervision Terms Stemming from Murder or Aggravated, Sexually Violent Dangerous Offender, and Dangerous Offender Sentences.

OAR 213-011-0004(3)’s limitations on sanctions do not apply to a defendant who is on PPS for murder or aggravated murder, or who is found to be a sexually violent dangerous offender. OAR 213-005-0004(2). See Jones v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 231 Or App 256, 218 P3d 904 (2009), rev den, 347 Or 718 (2010) (concluding that the limit on sanctions in former OAR 253-11-004(3) (1989) did not apply to a murder defendant who received a life sentence pursuant to ORS 163.115). Moreover, for murders committed on or after July 14, 1997, ORS 144.107 will apply.

Similarly, for a crime for which a defendant received a dangerous-offender sentence, the Board may impose up to a 180-day prison term for a violation, regardless of whether the violation constitutes a new crime. ORS 144.232(4)(a). And again, ORS 144.107 will apply for crimes committed on or after July 14, 1997.

§ 4-8.3.4. Early Release and Pre-Sentence Imprisonment Credit for Post-Prison Supervision Violators.

ORS 144.108(3) and OAR 213-011-0004(4) make clear “that a term of imprisonment imposed for post-prison supervision violations may not be reduced by earned-credit time or transitional leave.” Implementation Manual at 195. Thus, the defendant must serve his sanction’s entire term. If the defendant serves the sanction locally, he may be eligible for sentence reductions under the local authority’s sentence-reduction programs. Furthermore, the defendant “shall receive credit for time served in a state or local correctional facility on the supervisory violation prior to the Board’s imposition of a” violation sanction. OAR 213-011-0004(5).

§ 4-8.3.5. Appellate Review of Sanctions for Post-Prison Supervision Violations.

Under ORS 144.335(1), a defendant may “appeal an adverse decision by the Board after the offender has exhausted his or her administrative review options under the Board’s own rules.” Implementation Manual at 196. See also Or Laws 2001, ch 661 (amending ORS 144.335, and overturning Quintero v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 329 Or 319, 986 P2d 575 (1999)). A murder defendant’s re-release following service of a PPS sanction renders judicial review of the board’s decision moot because there is “no collateral consequence relating to the expiration of his [lifetime] PPS term.” Rondeau v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 232 Or App 488, 491, 222 P3d 753 (2009). Cf. Baty, 161 Or App at 656 (release on PPS did not moot appeal because, if appellant prevailed, it would affect when the PPS portion of the sentence ended).
