OCDLA Felony Sentencing Manual

Felony Sentencing in Oregon: Guidelines, Statutes, Cases, 2019 edition

Includes January 2024 Update by Jennelle Meeks Barton.

This chapter discusses appellate court jurisdiction and review authority over cases presenting Felony Sentencing Guidelines and related sentencing issues.

The right to jurisdiction over a direct appeal, and the scope of direct appellate review, are two distinct concepts, both of which are controlled by statute.” State v. Cook, 108 Or App 576, 580, 816 P2d 697 (1991), rev den, 312 Or 588 (1992) (citing State v. Carmickle, 307 Or 1, 762 P2d 290 (1988)). “Appealability”—i.e., the right to jurisdiction over an appeal—“is concerned with whether an appeal can be taken at all. Usually, but not always, appeals lie only from final judgments and orders.” State v. Montgomery, 294 Or 417, 420, 657 P2d 668 (1983) (court’s emphasis). On the other hand, “[r]eviewability generally involves the consideration of a variety of rulings and orders made by the court, usually before judgment.” 294 Or at 420.

Consequent to 2017 legislation, Senate Bill 896 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2017, chapter 529), two distinct sets of statutes control the right to appeal, and the scope of appellate review. One set of statutes are those created by SB 896, which apply only to “judgment[s] or order[s] entered by the trial court on or after the effective date of this 2017 Act.” See Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 28. The Act became effective on January 1, 2018. ORS 171.022.

The second set of statutes are the former statutes SB 896 repealed. The former statutes apply only to judgments and orders entered before SB 896’s January 1, 2018 effective date. See Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 26.1

Because the volume of cases controlled by the former statutes now is small and getting smaller, this chapter’s focus is on SB 896’s newly created statutes. But this chapter largely addresses case law applying the former statutes. This is because SB 896’s legislative history—which courts may consider, see ORS 174.020(1)(b) and (3)—shows that the bill was the product of a work group that “the Appellate Commissioner for the Oregon Court of Appeals requested the Oregon Law Commission to sponsor * * * to overhaul the procedural law governing appeals in criminal cases.” Report of the Direct Criminal Appeals Work Group on HB 896 26 (2017) (hereafter Work Group Report).2 The Work Group

“focused on reorganizing, streamlining, and clarifying existing statutory provisions. In addition, the Work Group proposes to codify some case law, to modernize some older statutory provisions, and to make a few substantive changes to the law * * * .

“ * * * The bill does not render existing appellate court decisions immaterial. Cases interpreting retained provisions remain significant, and other appellate decisions may provide context for amendments to this bill.”

Work Group Report at 27 (emphasis added).

Thus, to the extent SB 896 re-codified former statutes without substantively changing their texts, case law construing the former statutes should apply when construing the new statutes. This is because “amendatory acts do not change the meaning of preexisting [statutory] language further than is expressly declared or necessarily implied.” Fifth Avenue Corp. v. Washington Co., 282 Or 591, 597-98, 581 P2d 50 (1978), quoted in Wal-Mart Stores v. City of Cent. Point, 341 Or 393, 399, 144 P3d 914 (2006).

State v. Davis-McCoy, 300 Or App 326, 454 P3d 48 (2019) provides an example. There, the court revoked the defendant’s probation and, relying on the terms of the plea agreement, imposed a 28-month prison term. On appeal, the Court of Appeals relied on ORS 138.105(9), which bars appellate review of stipulated sentences. The court held that the defendant’s claim of sentencing error was not reviewable.

In its holding, the court relied on State v. Silsby, 282 Or App 104, 386 P3d 172 (2016), rev den, 360 Or 752 (2017), which interpreted ORS 138.105(9)’s predecessor, former ORS 138.222(4)(d) (2015), repealed by Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 26. The court explained:

“Given the textual similarities between the two statutes, and the evident legislative intent to restate the existing limits on reviewability set forth in former ORS 138.222(2)(d) (2015)—including the limitation on review of stipulated sentences described in Silsby—we hold that defendant’s challenge to his stipulated sentence upon revocation is not reviewable under ORS 138.105(9).”

Davis-McCoy, 300 Or App at 330 (footnote omitted). See also State v. Neill, 324 Or App 608, ___ P3d ___ (2023) (although OAR 213-010-0002(2) limits probation revocation sanction to presumptive maximum, because defendant stipulated to the 60-month probation-revocation sanction as part of his plea agreement, ORS 138.105(9) barred appellate review of his challenge).

§ 9-1. APPEAL & REVIEW OF SENTENCE IMPOSED FOR FELONY COMMITTED ON OR AFTER NOVEMBER 1, 1989.

As mentioned, the appeal and review statutes SB 896 created control in cases involving judgments or orders entered on or after January 1, 2018. See Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 28.

For defendants, ORS 138.035 controls appellate jurisdiction.3 ORS 138.035 states:

“(1)(a) A defendant may take an appeal from the circuit court, or from a municipal court or a justice court that has become a court of record under ORS 51.025 or 221.342, to the Court of Appeals from a judgment:

“(A) Conclusively disposing of all counts in the accusatory instrument or conclusively disposing of all counts severed from other counts;

“(B) Convicting the defendant of at least one count ; and

“(C) Imposing sentence on all counts of which the defendant was convicted.

“(b) For the purposes of this subsection, if the trial court merges a determination of guilt on one count with a determination of guilt on another count and imposes a sentence on the merged determinations of guilt, the trial court has conclusively disposed of the merged counts.

“(2)(a) A defendant may appeal a judgment ordering payment of restitution but not specifying the amount of restitution.

“(b) A defendant may appeal a supplemental judgment awarding restitution.

“(3) A defendant may appeal a judgment or order extending a period of probation, imposing a new or modified condition of probation or of sentence suspension, or imposing or executing a sentence upon revocation of probation or sentence suspension.

“(4) A defendant may appeal an amended or corrected judgment entered after the judgment of conviction and sentence.

“(5) A defendant may cross-appeal when the state appeals pursuant to ORS 138.045 (1)(d).”

State v. Grant, 322 Or App 318, 519 P3d 568 (2022) explains application of ORS 138.035(3). On one of the defendant’s counts of conviction, the trial court had imposed bench probation but later converted the sentence to formal probation. Defendant challenged that conversion, but the Court of Appeals rejected the challenge. Despite the change from bench to formal, the court “conclude[d] that the judgment continuing defendant’s probation on Count 2 [did] not qualify as an appealable judgment under ORS 138.035(3).” 322 Or App at 323.

ORS 138.105 controls review authority of felony sentences in defendants’ appeals.4 In relevant part, ORS 138.105 provides:

“(7) Except as otherwise provided in subsections (8) and (9) of this section, the appellate court has authority to review any sentence to determine whether the trial court failed to comply with requirements of law in imposing or failing to impose a sentence.

“(8) Except as otherwise provided in subsection (9) of this section, for a sentence imposed on conviction of a felony committed on or after November 1, 1989:

“(a) The appellate court has no authority to review:

“(A) A sentence that is within the presumptive sentence prescribed by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.

“(B) A sentence of probation when the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission prescribe a presumptive sentence of imprisonment but allow a sentence of probation without departure.

“(C) A sentence of imprisonment when the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission prescribe a presumptive sentence of imprisonment but allow a sentence of probation without departure.

“(b) If the trial court imposed a sentence that departs from the presumptive sentence prescribed by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission, the appellate court’s authority to review is limited to whether the trial court’s findings of fact and reasons justifying a departure from the sentence prescribed by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission:

“(A) Are supported by the evidence in the record; and

“(B) Constitute substantial and compelling reasons for departure.

“(c) Notwithstanding paragraph (a) of this subsection, the appellate court has authority to review whether the sentencing court erred:

“(A) In ranking the crime seriousness classification of the current crime or in determining the appropriate classification of a prior conviction or juvenile adjudication for criminal history purposes.

“(B) In imposing or failing to impose a minimum sentence prescribed by ORS 137.700 or 137.707.”

§ 9-1.1. Appellate Review Limitations.

Later sections of this chapter discuss the main features of appellate jurisdiction and review. This section discusses certain limitations in obtaining review in certain cases.

Also for defendants, ORS 138.085 imposes additional, albeit non-jurisdictional, limitations on defendants’ appeals. ORS 138.085 states:

“(1) If a defendant appeals a judgment of conviction based only on a plea of guilty or no contest, the notice of appeal must:

“(a) Include a statement that the defendant has reserved an issue for appeal under ORS 135.335; or

“(b) Identify a colorable claim of error reviewable under section 13 of this 2017 Act.

“(2) If a defendant appeals from any of the following judgments or orders, the notice of appeal must identify a colorable claim of error reviewable under section 13 of this 2017 Act:

“(a) A trial court’s judgment or order:

“(A) Revoking probation;

“(B) Extending the period of probation;

“(C) Imposing a new condition of probation;

“(D) Modifying an existing condition of probation; or

“(E) Revoking a sentence suspension; or

“(b) A judgment resentencing the defendant pursuant to a decision by an appellate court or a circuit court granting post-conviction relief.

“(3) The requirements of subsections (1) and (2) of this section are not jurisdictional, but the appellate court may dismiss the appeal if the notice of appeal fails to contain the required statement or fails to identify the colorable claim of error and the defendant fails to correct the deficiency after having been given the opportunity to do so.”

If a defendant wishes to appeal from a sentence imposed subsequent to (i) a guilty and no contest plea, (ii) a probation revocation or a change in the terms and conditions of probation, or (iii) a resentencing hearing held on remand from appellate and post-conviction courts, the defendant must demonstrate a “colorable claim of [sentencing] error.” See ORS 138.085(2). The phrase “colorable claim of error” includes “seemingly valid, genuine, or plausible claims of error or substantial and nonfrivolous claims of error.” State ex rel. Juvenile Dept. v. Balderas, 172 Or App 223, 230, 18 P3d 434 (2001). See, e.g., State v. Hikes, 261 Or App 30, 34, 323 P3d 298, rev den, 355 Or 380 (2014) (Where the defendant pleaded guilty, his “contention that the factual bases on which the trial court relied are insufficient to support a finding of ‘substantial and compelling reasons’ is subject to review as an error in the imposition of a sentence, and his contention also presents a colorable claim of error.”); State v. Brewer, 260 Or App 607, 320 P3d 620, rev den, 355 Or 380 (2014) (where defendant, after pleading guilty, unsuccessfully sought relief from mandatory minimum sentence, his claim that trial court erred in denying such relief was a “colorable claim” that Court of Appeals could consider); State v. Stubbs, 193 Or App 595, 91 P3d 774 (2004) (claim that trial court erred in amending executed judgment by increasing sentence by two months may not be a reviewable claim; nevertheless, it is a “colorable claim,” so it is appealable).

The state’s right to appeal and review of sentences is limited to appeals of felony sentences, ORS 138.045(1)(f)-(h); ORS 138.115(4)-(6); thus, the state has no right to appeal sentences imposed on misdemeanor convictions.5

With one exception, the state’s appeal statute does not authorize the state to appeal a trial court ruling in a probation-violation action. See State v. Roy, 198 Or App 209, 108 P3d 88 (2005). The exception is found in ORS 138.045(1)(h). It applies to cases involving defendants who were sentenced to probation under ORS 137.712, which authorizes sentencing courts, in extremely limited situations, to impose lesser guidelines sentences, including probation, in lieu of some of the mandatory minimum sentences specified by ORS 137.700 and 137.707. Under ORS 137.712, if the sentencing court imposed a guidelines term of probation on such a defendant, and the court later rejects a claim that the defendant violated probation by committing a new crime, the state may appeal that rejection. ORS 138.045(1)(h).

For detailed information about the time limitations for filing appeals, including defendants’ cross-appeals, see Daniel C. Bennett & Paul L. Smith, Criminal Law, ch 28 (Oregon State Bar CLE 2013).

§ 9-3. OAR 213-005-0005 JUDGMENT OF CONVICTION.

OAR 213-005-0005 creates certain form requirements for judgments:

Each judgment of conviction for an offense committed on or after the effective date of these rules shall state the length of incarceration and the length of post-prison supervision. The judgment of conviction shall also expressly provide that if the offender violates the conditions of post-prison supervision, the offender shall be subject to sanctions imposed by the supervisory agent or additional incarceration imposed by the Board in accordance with these rules.

§ 9-3.1. Intent of Provision.

To be appealable, the document imposing the conviction and sentence must be denominated “judgment,” Montgomery, 294 Or at 420, or be an “order” that, “by law, [is] subject to appeal by a party.” ORS 138.005(1). When filing an appeal, defense counsel must be careful to identify, and to attach to the defendant’s notice of appeal, the precise document from which the defendant wishes to appeal. See State v. Fowler, 350 Or 133, 252 P3d 302 (2011) (where defendant appealed from original judgment, but the error he on which he sought appellate review was contained only in a supplemental judgment, fact that defendant did not appeal from supplemental judgment deprived appellate courts of jurisdiction over appeal from supplemental judgment and thereby deprived defendant of ability to raise matter on appeal).

OAR 213-005-0005 requires the sentencing court to state in the judgment the defendant’s specific term of incarceration. See State v. Tremillion, 111 Or App 375, 376, 826 P2d 95 (per curiam), rev den, 313 Or 300 (1992) (court erred in sentencing the defendant to his grid block’s presumptive range of 15-18 months, rather than to a specific term within that range). In cases where the guidelines require a term of post-prison supervision (PPS) (see Chapter 4, discussing PPS terms), the court must impose one. See State v. Pinkowsky, 111 Or App 166, 169, 826 P2d 10 (1992) (because a “judgment of conviction must include both the term of incarceration and the term of” PPS (court’s emphasis), where sentencing court orally imposed PPS term but neglected to state term in judgment, court could, even after defendant finished serving term of incarceration, modify judgment to include PPS term).

The provisions of the written judgment control over the sentencing court’s oral pronouncement of judgment, State v. Talamantes, 134 Or App 166, 169, 894 P2d 1182 (1995), but only as to those matters the judgment is required to contain. For example, although the court is required to state on the record its substantial and compelling reasons for imposing a departure sentence, ORS 137.671(2); OAR 213-008-0001, the court is not required to state those reasons in the judgment. OAR 213-005-0005. Consequently, a party may not challenge a departure sentence on the ground that the judgment does not state the departure reasons. Austin v. McGee, 140 Or App 263, 267, 915 P2d 1027 (1996) (where court orally stated on record its reasons for imposing consecutive sentences, it did not err by failing to state those reasons in written judgment).

§ 9-4. LIMITATIONS ON APPELLATE REVIEW.

As previously explained, ORS 138.105 controls the scope of appellate review of sentencing issues in defendants’ appeals. ORS 138.115 controls the scope of appellate review of sentencing issues in state’s appeals. Unless otherwise noted, this section is limited to discussing the scope of review in defendants’ appeals.

ORS 138.105(8)(a) and (9) set out four limitations that bar appellate review. If a sentencing issue falls within any of the four specific limitations, the issue may not be reviewable. See State v. Adams, 315 Or 359, 365, 847 P2d 397 (1993).

§ 9-4.1. Presumptive Sentences.

ORS 138.105(8)(a)(A) states the first of the limitations on appellate review. If the sentencing court properly determines the defendant’s grid block and imposes a presumptive sentence, i.e., “the sentence provided in a grid block for an offender classified in that grid block * * * or a sentence designated as a presumptive sentence by statute,” OAR 213-003-0001(16), the term of imprisonment is not reviewable. Unless otherwise noted, this section is limited to discussing the scope of review in defendants’ appeals. Guyton, 126 Or App at 147-48 (after holding that sentencing court properly determined defendant’s grid block and imposed presumptive sentence, Court of Appeals ruled that challenge to term of incarceration was not reviewable).

This limitation is narrow. As Guyton shows, the limitation does not bar review of a claim that the sentencing court improperly determined a defendant’s grid block before imposing a presumptive sentence using the improper grid block. See also State v. Martin, 320 Or 448, 451-52, 887 P2d 782 (1994) (after considering and rejecting defendant’s challenge to criminal-history score, court holds that proper grid block was used to impose non-reviewable presumptive sentence).

Moreover, ORS 138.105(8)(a)(A) does not bar review of a claim that an ancillary sentencing provision is in error. When a court imposes a presumptive sentence, it also may or must impose various terms that are ancillary to the presumptive sentence. For example, if the court imposes a presumptive sentence of incarceration, in virtually all cases it also must impose a term of PPS. See Chapter 4. If the ancillary sentencing term is unauthorized, ORS 138.105(8)(a)(A) does not bar review of a claim against the unauthorized ancillary term. Instead, ORS 138.105(7) applies. It states that “the appellate court has authority to review any sentence to determine whether the trial court failed to comply with requirements of law in imposing or failing to impose a sentence.” Id. ORS 138.105(7) allows review of the claim against the unauthorized ancillary term. See, e.g., State v. McFee, 136 Or App 160, 901 P2d 870 (1995), rev dismissed, 323 Or 662 (1996) (erroneous PPS term, imposed ancillary to incarceration term, is reviewable).

Finally, ORS 138.105(8)(a)(A) does not bar review of a claim against the validity of sentences imposed pursuant to various criminal code provisions, which provide for specific “presumptive’ sentences.” The guidelines include such sentences within the definition of “presumptive sentence.” OAR 213-003-0001(16) (“’[p]resumptive sentence’ means * * * a sentence designated as a presumptive sentence by statute”). If a trial court erroneously relies on a statutorily prescribed presumptive sentence to impose such a sentence, the claim against the court’s reliance on the statute is reviewable. See State v. Althouse, 359 Or 668, 676, 375 P3d 475 (2016) (challenge to “presumptive sentence” imposed under ORS 137.719 is reviewable). This is because “the phrase * * * ‘[a]ny sentence that is within the presumptive sentence prescribed by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission’—can have only one referent: The phrase refers to a sentence that comes within the range of presumptive sentences prescribed by a sentencing guidelines grid block.” Althouse, 359 Or at 676 (emphasis added). See also State v. Webster, 280 Or App 217, 219-21, 380 P3d 1165 (2016) (following Althouse); State v. Denson, 280 Or App 225, 229, 380 P3d 1165 (2016) (same).

§ 9-4.2. Optional Probation.

ORS 138.105(8)(a)(B) states the second limitation on appellate review. Under it, the state may not obtain appellate review of a sentencing court’s imposition of optional probation. There is, however, an exception. If the sentencing court improperly determined that defendant was eligible for optional probation, the state may obtain appellate review of that determination. State v. Schuh, 112 Or App 362, 364-65, 829 P2d 1040, rev den, 314 Or 176 (1992).

The converse of ORS 138.105(8)(a)(B) is ORS 138.105(8)(a)(C), which states the third limitation on review. If the sentencing court properly could have imposed a term of optional probation but declined to do so, under ORS 138.105(8)(a)(C), the defendant may not obtain appellate review of that decision. State v. Swisher, 116 Or App 129, 131-32, 840 P2d 1339 (1992), rev den, 315 Or 312 (1993). But consistent with Schuh, if the appellate court declined to impose optional probation based on erroneous conclusion that the defendant was legally ineligible, the defendant should be allowed to obtain review.

§ 9-4.3. Stipulated Sentences.

ORS 138.105(9) sets out the fourth limitation on review. It bars review of only those parts of the sentence to which the defendant stipulated. State v. Kephart, 320 Or 433, 440, 887 P2d 774 (1994) (applying former ORS 138.222(2)(d), whose text is consistent with the text of ORS 138.105(9)).

Thus,

“[i]n light of the many indications of legislative intent that we have explored above, we conclude that the scope of the bar to appellate review—now set out in ORS 138.105(9)—is limited in the way that this court described in Kephart and Martin (and that the Court of Appeals later described in Capri and Davis). The bar applies only when the parties’ agreement is ‘a stipulation as to sentencing of a kind described by ORS 135.407.’ Kephart, 320 Or at 447.”

State v. Rusen, 369 Or 677, 693, 509 P3d 628 (2022).

State v. Wheeler, 320 Or App 273, 511 P3d 1142 (2022) (per curiam) (because the “[d]efendant stipulated with the state to specific grid block classifications,” the Court of Appeals could not review defendant’s challenge to “a sentence ‘that result[ed] from a stipulated sentencing agreement’ with the state, ORS 138.105(9)”); Davis-McCoy, 300 Or App at 330 (where court imposed stipulated probation revocation sanction of 28 months’ imprisonment, ORS 138.105(9) barred appellate review of sanction);

See also State v. Silsby, 282 Or App 104, 113, 386 P3d 172 (2016), rev den, 360 Or 752 (2017) (court declined to review imposition of 80-month probation-revocation sanction, because, although term was longer than guidelines authorized, defendant stipulated to it as part of plea agreement); State v. Capri, 248 Or App 391, 395-96, 273 P3d 290 (2012) (reviewing and reversing as plain error an unlawfully long PPS term when the parties had stipulated to the prison term, but not to the PPS term); State v. Young, 188 Or App 247, 251-52, 71 P3d 119, rev den, 336 Or 125 (2003) (limitation of review of stipulated sentences may not be used to bar the court from considering a party’s jurisdictional challenges; nor does limitation bar review of the types of stipulated sentences set out in ORS 135.407, where defendant “agreed that, if he breached the plea agreement and was convicted of additional charges * * * the entire range of sentences for that crime would be available”); Blackledge v. Morrow, 174 Or App 566, 570-71, 26 P3d 851, rev den, 332 Or 558 (2001) (where defendant stipulated to sentence greater than guidelines and ORS 161.605 allowed, his stipulation nevertheless barred appellate review of sentence’s validity).6

§ 9-4.4. Other Issues Related to Sentencing.

Former ORS 138.222(2)(e) addresses sentencing claims that are not barred by former ORS 138.222(a)-(d), but that neither former ORS 138.222(3) nor (4) identify as reviewable. Former ORS 138.222(2)(e) says that such claims are not reviewable. Adams, 315 Or at 365. See also State v. Sanchez, 160 Or App 182, 187-88, 981 P2d 361 (in banc), rev den, 329 Or 318 (1999) (claim that sentence is invalid, because trial court did not first receive court-appointed interpreter’s qualifications as required by statute, not reviewable). But ORS 138.105 contains no provision mirroring former ORS 138.222(2)(e). Consequently, the appellate review limitations imposed by former ORS 138.222(2)(e) may not apply to appeals from judgments or orders entered on or after January 1, 2018.

§ 9-5. DEPARTURE SENTENCES.

ORS 138.105(8)(b) sets out a two-part test for determining the validity of departure sentences. See Chapter 7. In State v. Wilson, 111 Or App 147, 151, 826 P2d 1010 (1992), the court explained that departure sentences are reviewed under the two-part test set out in the Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual (1989) (hereafter Implementation Manual). It states:

“1) The Evidentiary Test: Are the facts stated by the sentencing judge in justification of the departure supported by the record?

“2) The Law Test: Are the reasons stated on the record for the departure adequate to justify a sentence outside the standard range (e.g. [sic], are they consistent with the purposes of the sentencing guidelines rules)?”

Implementation Manual at 124.

This two-part test must be applied in accordance with the constitutional principles explained in Chapter 1. For example, if after a jury trial, the trial court intends to impose a “aggravated-departure sentence,” it must base its departure system on “aggravating factors” the jury found beyond a reasonable doubt. The court then determines (i) whether the factors are supported by the record—which they will be, if the jury properly found them, see also ORS 136.785(5) (a jury verdict of guilty on an enhancement fact “may not be reexamined by the court”)—and (ii) whether they provide “substantial and compelling reasons” to warrant the imposition of an aggravated departure.

The trial court is permitted to base its substantial and compelling determination on a single departure factor. Wilson, 111 Or App at 151 n 4. But in cases where the sentencing court relies on multiple departure factors, and the appellate court invalidates one or more but not all of the factors, as a general principle the court will remand for resentencing. For example, in State v. Toledo, 175 Or App 280, 281, 28 P3d 1194 (2001), “the [trial] court found more than one factor in support of departure, [but] it did not find that any [one] of the factors independently would support departure. Therefore, resentencing [was] required” consequent to Court of Appeals invalidation of just one of the factors. See also State v. Balkin, 134 Or App 240, 243, 895 P2d 311, rev den, 321 Or 397 (1995) (where one of seven aggravating factors was invalid, court remanded for resentencing); Talamantes, 134 Or App at 169 (where one-third of departure factors were invalid, court remanded for resentencing).

To try to reduce the possibility of a remand in a case where the sentencing court relied on multiple factors, the court may make the type of finding the Toledo court referenced, i.e., an “individually sufficient” finding. In such a finding, the court would declare that it would impose the departure even if it were relying on any one of its cited factors. In the trial court made an “individually sufficient” finding, the appellate court will affirm if it validates even just one of the multiple factors on which the trial court relied. State v. Jenniches, 187 Or App 658, 663, 69 P3d 771, rev den, 335 Or 578 (2003); State v. Perez, 186 Or App 122, 130, 61 P3d 945 (2003); State v. Williams, 131 Or App 85, 87, 883 P2d 918 (1994), on recons, 133 Or App 191, 891 P2d 3, rev den, 321 Or 512 (1995).

PRACTICE TIP: In a case where the trial court made an “individually sufficient” finding, defense counsel should argue that the finding is invalid unless the sentencing court, on the record, independently examines each of its aggravating factors and concludes that each factor individually warrants the departure sentence imposed. If the court fails to make such a record, counsel should object on grounds that the “individually sufficient” finding does not explain “why the circumstances are so exceptional that imposition of the presumptive sentence would not accomplish the purposes of the guidelines.” Wilson, 111 Or App at 151. See also State v. Mitchell, 136 Or App 99, 103, 900 P2d 1083 (1995) (where court failed to fully explain how aggravating factor warranted departure, case remanded for resentencing). Moreover, counsel should ground that objection on the due process principle that where a sentencing court makes “a new finding of fact that was not an ingredient of the offense charged” and that would be used to increase punishment, the court must make “findings adequate to make meaningful any appeal that is allowed.” Specht v. Patterson, 386 US 605, 608-10, 87 S Ct 1209, 18 L Ed 2d 326 (1967).

An appellate court will not review a claim that the sentencing court erred in refusing to consider or to find that either a mitigated or an aggravated departure sentence is warranted. State v. Thomas, 117 Or App 533, 534, 844 P2d 936 (1992) (per curiam), rev den, 317 Or 272 (1993); Wilson, 111 Or App at 150-51.

If the sentencing court properly finds that departure factors apply, an appellate court “has no authority to review the length of the departure sentence, so long as it is within the [guidelines’] limitations” on the duration of departure sentences (discussed in Chapter 7). State v. Barber, 113 Or App 603, 606, 832 P2d 51 (1992).

In situations where a trial court found departure factors, a party could have but did not challenge the departure factors on appeal, but, for other reasons, the case is remanded for resentencing, on remand, and on any subsequent appeal following the remand, the party will be precluded from challenging the departure factors.

For example, in the bench-trial case of State v. Reinke I, 245 Or App 33, 260 P3d 820 (2011) (per curiam), aff’d, 354 Or 98, 309 P3d 1059, adh’d to as modified on recons, 354 Or 570, 316 P3d 286 (2013), the trial court found dangerous-offender departure factors. ORS 161.737(1). On appeal, the defendant did not challenge the factors, but secured a remand for resentencing on other grounds. 245 Or App at 34. After his resentencing, where the factors again were used, the defendant again appealed and now challenged the factors. The Court of Appeals held that under the law of the case doctrine, the defendant was precluded from challenging the factors. The court explained:

“Defendant had the opportunity to assign error in his first appeal to the initial sentencing court’s finding that he was a dangerous offender. Because he did not challenge that finding in his first appeal, the finding is binding on remand under the doctrine of law of the case. See, e.g., Washer v. Clatsop Care and Rehab. District, 98 Or App 232, 235, 778 P2d 987 (1989) (explaining doctrine); cf. Kennedy v. Wheeler, 356 Or 518, 524-31, 341 P3d 728 (2014) (clarifying distinction between preservation and law-of-the-case principles). Our reversal of defendant’s dangerous-offender sentence and concomitant remand for resentencing did not reverse the trial court’s finding under ORS 161.725 to 161.737 that defendant is a dangerous offender. See State v. Zolotoff, 275 Or App 384, 395-96, 365 P3d 131 (2015) (clarifying that resentencing court may rely on findings properly made in original sentencing hearing). Hence, the resentencing court did not err by relying on the finding made by the initial sentencing court that defendant is a dangerous offender and by sentencing him accordingly.”

State v. Reinke II, 289 Or App 10, 18, 408 P3d 249 (2017).

§ 9-6. LEGAL ERRORS IN IMPOSING SENTENCE.

Under ORS 138.105(7), an appellate court may review a claim that “[t]he trial court failed to comply with requirements of law in imposing or failing to impose a sentence.” Former ORS 138.222(4)(a). These statutes permits the appellate “court to review a sentencing issue when the sentence that was imposed was an authorized sentence, but the trial court is asserted to have erroneously determined that the defendant was not eligible for a different, also authorized, sentence.” State v. Arnold, 214 Or App 201, 211-12, 164 P3d 334 (2007) (modifying State v. Lavitsky, 171 Or App 506, 17 P3d 495 (2000) (en banc), rev den, 332 Or 430 (2001)). See also State v. Barrett, 350 Or 390, 255 P3d 472 (2011) (where victim’s constitutional rights to notice were violated in the course of defendant’s sentencing, court reversed and remanded for resentencing notwithstanding that trial court imposed otherwise lawful sentence); State v. Pemberton, 226 Or App, 285, 288-89, 203 P3d 326 (2009) (where court based sentencing decision on incorrect construction of pertinent statutes, court’s sentencing decision was erroneous, notwithstanding that sentence fell within court’s range of sentencing discretion); State v. Curry, 209 Or App 31, 146 P3d 348 (2006) (addressing defendant’s claim that on remand for resentencing from federal habeas corpus court, trial court erred in declining to consider his proffered mitigation evidence).

Review authority under ORS 138.105(7) covers a broad range of subjects. For example, the statutes authorize appellate courts to review such claims as that the sentencing court:

• Violated the defendant’s statutory and constitutional rights of allocution and to counsel by substantively modifying the sentence without affording the defendant a hearing. State v. DeCamp, 158 Or App 238, 242, 973 P2d 922 (1999).

• Erred in merging or refusing to merge convictions. State v. Davis, 265 Or App 425, 335 P3d 322 (2014), rev den, 356 Or 837 (2015); State v. Sumerlin, 139 Or App 579, 583, 913 P2d 340 (1996).7 See also ORS 138.105(5)(b) (“[t]he appellate court has authority to review whether the trial court erred by not merging determinations of guilt of two or more offenses, unless the entry of separate convictions results from an agreement between the state and the defendant”)

• Impermissibly imposed conditions of PPS. State v. Wright, 128 Or App 88, 90, 875 P2d 1174 (1994).

• Incorrectly held that the guidelines do not authorize downward departure sentences under any circumstances. Thomas, 117 Or App at 534 (dicta).

• Imposed an impermissibly long term of PPS. State v. Jones, 129 Or App 413, 416–17, 879 P2d 881 (1994).

• Imposed an impermissibly long jail term as a condition of probation. State v. Holliday, 110 Or App 426, 431-32, 824 P2d 1148, rev den, 313 Or 211 (1992).

• Erred “[i]n ranking the crime seriousness classification of the current crime[.]” ORS 138.105(8)(c)(A). See, e.g., State v. Cantrell, 125 Or App 458, 460, 865 P2d 1323 (1993) (reviewing claim, but concluding that the sentencing court did not err in ranking unranked offense).

• Erred “in determining the appropriate classification of a prior conviction or juvenile adjudication for criminal history purposes.” ORS 138.105(8)(c)(A).

• Erred in “includ[ing] a * * * a constitutionally void conviction * * * in proving criminal history.” Holliday, 110 Or App at 430.8

• Erred in using an unauthenticated criminal history summary to calculate his criminal history score. State v. Delgado, 111 Or App 162, 164-65 n 2, 826 P2d 1014 (1992).

• Erred in using an expunged conviction to assess the defendant’s criminal-history score. State v. Leslie, 134 Or App 366, 369-70, 895 P2d 342, rev den, 321 Or 397 (1995).

• Erred in imposing a mandatory minimum sentence under ORS 137.700 or 137.707. ORS 138.105(8)(c)(B).

§ 9-7. PRESERVATION AND WAIVER.

“Generally, a party seeking review of an order or ruling must have made an appropriate motion or objection in order to lay a foundation for appellate review.” Montgomery, 294 Or at 420. This “laying of a foundation” is the concept of “preservation”—i.e., “preserving” the claim of error for appeal. As a general rule, the appellate court will not review a claim that is not “preserved.”

During the first few years of the guidelines era, the Court of Appeals regularly reached unpreserved claims of sentencing error. The court may have done so, because it wanted to quickly establish a body of case law to guide the bench and bar.

But beginning principally with State v. Slawson, 123 Or App 573, 860 P2d 876 (1993), the court became less inclined to reach unpreserved claims. Furthermore, even in some of its pre-Slawson cases, the court declined to review unpreserved claims. For example, in State v. Kennedy, 113 Or App 134, 831 P2d 712 (1992), the defendant sought appellate review of an unpreserved challenge to an aggravated-departure sentence. The Court of Appeals declined to review the claim. It explained that because the final decision on whether to depart is discretionary, the appellant must have objected at sentencing to preserve the issue for appeal. 113 Or App at 138-39.

Moreover, also in the early years of the guidelines era, the appellate courts made clear that to preserve a claim for appeal, the appellant’s objection must have been specific. For example, in State v. Orsi, 108 Or App 176, 180, 813 P2d 82 (1991), the court said that merely objecting to a departure sentence is not enough:

“If a defendant does not agree that the sentencing court’s reasons [to depart] are substantial and compelling, it is incumbent on him to indicate the basis of his objection. Without an objection, the sentencing court is not put on notice that its explanation or analysis may be flawed, and no error is preserved.”

108 Or App at 180 (emphasis added).

Thus, to ensure that the issue is preserved for appeal, a party must articulate specific bases for his objections to the court’s reasons for imposing a departure. See State v. Fults, 343 Or 515, 518, 173 P3d 822 (2007) (where defendant expressly took no objection to trial court’s failure to state any reasons for aggravated departure, Court of Appeals erred in reviewing unpreserved challenge to departure); State v. Fults, 219 Or App 305, 182 P3d 267 (2008) (on remand from Supreme Court, Court of Appeals held that it should not have exercised its discretion to review an unpreserved claim against departure sentence, because, among other reasons, defendant may have made a strategic choice to expressly decline to object to trial court’s imposition of a departure not supported by valid aggravating factors). Indeed, only the bases the defendant articulates necessarily are preserved for appeal. State v. Brown, 132 Or App 443, 447-48, 888 P2d 1071, rev den, 321 Or 137 (1995); State v. Guzman, 121 Or App 673, 674, 855 P2d 1140 (1993). See also State v. Anderson, 201 Or App 340, 343, 118 P3d 855 (2005) (where trial court “entertained objections” to sentence it had just imposed, and defendant voiced specific objections to the sentence, the objections were preserved, for “[t]he court was fairly apprised of defendant’s position and had an opportunity to respond”).

Cf. Holguin-Hernandez v. United States, 589 US ___, 140 S Ct 762, 766-67, 206 L Ed 2d 95 (2020) (where defendant did not expressly argue that the sentence was unreasonably long, but did argue that sentence was greater than necessary to accomplish the goals of sentencing, defendant’s objection was sufficient to challenge the sentence on the ground of reasonableness).

EXAMPLES: The state recommends a departure based on aggravating Factors A and B. Defense counsel says, “I object to a departure.” Nothing is preserved. Or counsel says without explanation, “I object to those departure factors.” Again, nothing is preserved. Or counsel says, I object to Aggravating Factor A, because,” and then explains the basis of his objection. The stated ground for the challenge to Factor A is preserved, but there is no preserved challenge to Factor B. To preserve challenges to both factors, counsel must state: “I object to Factors A and B, because,” and then explain the bases of his objections to both factors. Having done so, counsel need not again object after the court formally imposes a departure sentence based on Factors A and B.

There are exceptions, or possible exceptions, to preservation requirements. The main one, discussed below in Section 9-7.1, involves claims of “plain error.”

But a separate possible exception applies when the appellate court already has remanded the case for resentencing owing to one claim of sentencing error. In that circumstance, the appellate court then may review unpreserved claims of error to avoid their recurrence at resentencing. Passing on the unpreserved claims helps facilitate an error-free resentencing. State v. Galvin, 152 Or App 275, 280-81, 954 P2d 800 (1998). But see State v. Crowell, 198 Or App 564, 567, 109 P3d 390 (2005) (although court reversed and remanded for resentencing, it declined to address second claim of sentencing error that was not preserved).

State v. Probst, 339 Or 612, 124 P3d 1237 (2005) suggests another possible exception to the preservation requirement. There, the defendant collaterally attacked one of three prior DUII convictions that were used to aggravate her current DUII conviction from a misdemeanor to a felony. She attacked the prior conviction by making a prima facie case that it was uncounseled and hence was constitutionally invalid so could not be used to aggravate her conviction. See Chapter 1 (explaining collateral attacks on prior convictions). Under long-standing preexisting case law, the defendant’s prima facie case shifted to the state the burden of showing that the defendant validly waived counsel. The Court of Appeals held that the state failed to meet its burden, and reversed and remanded.

But in State v. Colgrove, 370 Or 474, 500-01, 521 P3d 456 (2022), the Supreme Court further explained:

“In the context of DUII diversion, ORS 138.105(5) precludes a defendant from obtaining direct appellate review of legal rulings made during the lengthy post-plea diversion process. However, it appears that, if a trial court dismisses a defendant’s DUII charge, the state is permitted to appeal and obtain review of the court’s post-plea rulings that led to the dismissal. This case highlights that distinction, which appears to be a function of the interrelationship between the DUII diversion scheme and the law governing criminal appeals. The inability of a defendant to obtain direct appellate review of a trial court’s post-plea legal decisions during the DUII diversion process is curious, particularly when the legislature has enacted a detailed statutory scheme that affords DUII defendants various rights and, in some circumstances, imposes duties on the trial courts. Now that the practical interrelationship between the DUII diversion scheme and the law governing criminal appeals is clearly in focus, the legislature, if it chooses to do so, can revisit and recalibrate its enactments and permit defendants who plead guilty or no contest to DUII to challenge on direct appeal the legal rulings that were made as part of the post-plea diversion process as long as the law that it enacts is consistent with any limits imposed by state and federal law.”

On review the Supreme Court abandoned the preexisting law and instead held that the defendant not only must show that the prior conviction was uncounseled—which she did—but also that her waiver of counsel was constitutionally invalid—which she did not try to do, because under the preexisting case law, that was not her burden. But rather than hold that the defendant failed to preserve the claim, and thereby affirm her felony DUII conviction, the court concluded that her failure to preserve the claim stemmed from her reasonable reliance on long-standing case law that burdened the state with showing that the defendant’s waiver was valid. The court remanded the case to the trial court, where the defendant would have the opportunity to try to prove that her waiver was invalid. 339 Or at 629-30.

Consequently, Probst should stand for the proposition that if a party reasonably relies on preexisting case law when it foregoes making a particular argument, and if the case law subsequently is abandoned to the party’s detriment, the party’s failure to make the newly created objection may not constitute a waiver. Instead, the party’s case may be remanded with the opportunity to make the newly created objection.

Another exception to the preservation requirement applies when the defendant had no opportunity to object to the sentence. In those circumstances, the defendant’s failure to object is excused.

For example, at the time of sentencing in State v. Partain, 298 Or App 449, 446 P3d 561 (2019), the trial court imposed no restitution in one of his consolidated cases. But later, in the judgment of conviction and sentence for that particular case, the court imposed a restitution order. Thus, the defendant had no opportunity to object to the restitution order. Excusing his failure to object, the Court of Appeals said, “[D]efendant was not required to preserve the challenge that he raises on appeal because the error appeared for the first time in the judgment.” 298 Or App at 452 (citing State v. Lewis, 236 Or App 49, 52, 234 P3d 152, rev den, 349 Or 172 (2010)). See also State v. Ralston, 310 Or App 470, 486 P3d 822 (2021) (citing Lewis and holding that where trial court, for first time in judgment, imposed $2,225 fine, when at sentencing court orally imposed only $2,000 fine, defendant was excused from failing to object to the condition; fine vacated); State v. Worthey, 302 Or App 140, 143, 460 P3d 545 (2020) (citing Lewis and holding that where trial court, for first time in judgment, imposed special condition of probation prohibiting defendant from possessing marijuana or cannabis products, defendant was excused from failing to object to the condition).

§ 9-7.1. Discretionary Review of Unpreserved Claims.

Ailes v. Portland Meadows, Inc., 312 Or 376, 823 P2d 956 (1991) is the lead case on appellate court authority to address unpreserved claims. But is important to recognize that appellate court authority to review unpreserved claims is discretionary. Unlike preserved claims, appellate courts have no legal obligation to review unpreserved. Appellate courts may decline to review unpreserved claims for any reason, or for no reason.

An appellate court has discretion to review an unpreserved claim of sentencing error, raised for the first time on appeal, if the claim of error is one of law apparent on the face of the record, but only if the facially apparent legal error is “egregious,” State v. Morgan, 124 Or App 229, 231, 862 P2d 539 (1993), rev den, 319 Or 150 (1994), or that other policy reasons militate in favor of reviewing the unpreserved claim. See ORAP 5.45(2).

For an error to be facially apparent, it must be “‘obvious, not reasonably in dispute.’” State v. Farmer, 317 Or 220, 224, 856 P2d 623 (1993) (quoting State v. Brown, 310 Or 347, 355, 800 P2d 259 (1990)); State v. Langdon, 151 Or App 640, 643, 950 P2d 410 (1997), aff’d, 330 Or 72, 999 P2d 1127 (2000); State v. Burch, 134 Or App 569, 572, 896 P2d 10 (1995). For example, if a party’s challenge requires the construction of a statute, the claim is not facially apparent unless preexisting case law supports the party’s construction. State v. McCoy, 152 Or App 393, 394, 952 P2d 572 (per curiam), rev den, 327 Or 83 (1998). See also State v. Molette, 255 Or App 29, 296 P3d 594, rev den, 353 Or 788 (2013) (court declined to exercise discretion to review claim of error, for exercise would have required resolving choice of laws issue).

The law as it exists at the time the appellate court issues its decision is used to determine whether a claim of error is “plain.” E.g., State v. McCormick, 185 Or App 491, 493, 60 P3d 1089 (2002) (under principle from State v. Jury, 185 Or App 132, 57 P3d 970 (2002), change in case law occurring after defendant’s sentencing, but which was to his benefit, transformed unpreserved and unreviewable claim of sentencing error into reviewable claim of “plain error”). Thus, if the law was unsettled at the time of sentencing, but became settled in the defendant’s favor by the time of the appellate court’s decision, the defendant’s unpreserved claim may be reviewed as “plain” error. E.g., State v. Chand, 203 Or App 218, 220-21, 125 P3d 38 (2005) (where law at time of sentencing allowed trial court to find existence of juvenile adjudications used in criminal history scoring, but Supreme Court subsequently held in State v. Harris, 339 Or 157, 118 P3d 236 (2005) that Sixth Amendment principles required jury finding of existence of such adjudications, Court of Appeals reviewed defendant’s unpreserved Sixth Amendment claim as plain error).

But suppose the law was settled by prior appellate court decisions, but, for the first time on appeal, the defendant sought to have those decisions modified or reversed. This situation presented itself in State v. Merrill, 303 Or App 107, 113, 463 P3d 540 (2020), adh’d to as modified on recons, 309 Or App 68, 481 P3d 441 (2021). For the first time on appeal, the defendant requested the Court of Appeals to overrule a prior, controlling Court of Appeals decision. The Court of Appeals excused the defendant from general preservation requirements. The court explained that an objection at trial would have been futile due to adverse, controlling case law, and that raising the issue for the first time on appeal would not result in any unfairness to the trial court or the opposing party. 303 Or App at 113.

For a plain error to be egregious, the defendant must show that he is “substantially prejudiced” by it. Galvin, 152 Or App at 279; State v. Graham, 125 Or App 516, 519, 865 P2d 490 (1993). Numerous cases provide guidance on what constitutes egregious error, including:

State v. Wagner, 319 Or App 399, 411, 509 P3d 731 (2022) (where the defendant was convicted of a Class B felony carrying a statutory maximum of 10 years, and trial court imposed a 120-month prison sentence followed by a post-prison supervision term (PPS), the Court of Appeals accepted the defendant’s claim of plain error that the trial court imposed a total sentence, of 10 years plus three years, that exceeded the 10-year statutory maximum);

State v. Welsh, 267 Or App 8, 340 P3d 132 (2014) (imposing consecutive sentences without making requisite findings under ORS 137.123(5)); State v. Glazier, 266 Or App 824, 340 P3d 90 (2014) (per curiam), rev den, 356 Or 685 (2015) (imposing three-year PPS term, when only two-year term was authorized); State v. Andrade, 262 Or App 203, 325 P3d 48 (2014) (imposing consecutive “Ballot Measure 11” sentences under mistaken belief that such sentences must be run consecutively); State v. Monro, 256 Or App 493, 301 P3d 435 (2013) (relying on grid-block 9-A to impose consecutive aggravated departure of 144 months, “shift-to-I rule” required using grid-block of 9-I, which allowed departure maximum of 72 months); State v. Reed, 235 Or App 470, 237 P3d 826 (2010) (imposing conditions of PPS); State v. Casiano, 214 Or App 509, 166 P3d 599 (2007) (relying on ORS 137.635 to impose sentence, appellate court exercised discretion to address error); State v. Watkins, 207 Or App 346, 142 P3d 86, rev den, 341 Or 579 (2006) (per curiam) (violating OAR 213-005-0002(4) by imposing 36-month prison term and 36-month PPS term on Class C felony); State v. Jaime, 186 Or App 368, 371, 63 P3d 53 (2003) (violating “two-for-one” rule by using juvenile adjudications on Class A person misdemeanors to increase his criminal-history score); McCormick, 185 Or App at 493 (violating OAR 213-005-0002(4) by imposing a five-year prison term and a five-year PPS term on Class C felony); State v. Skelton, 153 Or App 580, 593, 957 P2d 585, rev den, 327 Or 448 (1998) (imposing consecutive term 80 months longer than authorized); Galvin, 152 Or App at 279 (imposing 12-month sentence when only six months was authorized); State v. Lundstedt, 139 Or App 111, 114, 911 P2d 349 (1996) (imposing consecutive term two months longer than authorized); State v. Rood, 129 Or App 422, 426, 879 P2d 886 (1994) (imposing 18-month term of incarceration, when only a 90-day term was authorized and the sentencing court declared at sentencing that it would impose only a 60-day term); Jones, 129 Or App at 416–17 (imposing 60-month PPS term, when only a 36-month term was authorized); Morgan, 124 Or App at 231-32 (imposing sentence nearly three times longer than authorized); and State v. Gouveia, 116 Or App 86, 88, 840 P2d 753 (1992) (subjecting defendant to ORS 137.635, and thereby disabling him from earning goodtime credit and becoming eligible for any form of early release).

However, if the defendant’s claim of prejudice from the error is based on speculation, the court may decline to address the claim. State v. Gutierrez, 199 Or App 521, 112 P3d 433 (2005).

Other policy reasons on which an appellate court may base its decision to exercise discretionary review an unpreserved claim include:

“the competing interests of the parties; the nature of the case; the gravity of the error; the ends of justice in the particular case; how the error came to the court’s attention; and whether the policies behind the general rule requiring preservation of error have been served in the case in another way, i.e., whether the trial court was, in some manner, presented with both sides of the issue and given an opportunity to correct any error.”

Ailes, 312 Or at 382 n 6.

One policy reason for exercising discretionary review is the state having no valid interest in a defendant serving an unlawful sentence. In State v. Wierson, 216 Or App 318, 172 P3d 281 (2007) states a policy reason for exercising discretionary review. There, the state had conceded that a sentencing error was “apparent on the face of the record, but argue[d] that [the appellate] court should not exercise its discretion to correct it because” leaving the error in place would “mean[], at most, that defendant will serve an additional three months of post-prison supervision.” 216 Or App at 319. Thus, the state’s position essentially was that a three-month over-length PPS term did not sufficiently prejudice the defendant to warrant discretionary review. The court rejected that position, stating: “Because the state has no valid interest in having defendant serve an unlawful sentence, we choose to exercise our discretion to address the error.” Id.

In State v. Brown, 224 Or App 580, 198 P3d 953 (2008), the court reached an unpreserved claim that the trial court violated the rule from Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 US 466, 120 S Ct 2348, 147 L Ed 2d 435 (2000) (see Chapter 1) when it imposed an aggravated departure sentence. The court based its decision on the following considerations: (1) the record did not establish that the jury verdict itself supported the enhancements beyond a reasonable doubt, (2) there was no evidence that the defendant strategically declined to preserve the error, and (3) there existed no evidence that it was certain or probable that a jury would find any aggravating factors on remand. See also State v. Sosa, 224 Or App 658, 199 P3d 346 (2008) (based on two considerations, court reached unpreserved challenge to erroneous criminal-history calculation: (1) the error resulted in the defendant being sentenced to an additional 50 months of imprisonment; and (2) the possibility that, had the error not occurred, the trial court may not have imposed the same sentence).

Another policy reason for exercising discretionary review applies if the state confesses error the appellate courts are more likely to exercise discretionary review. See Rood, 129 Or App at 425; Jones, 129 Or App at 416.

Still another policy reason applies if the “defendant did not encourage the error, and the failure to object is understandable. It is entirely rational for defense counsel, perceiving that a court is upset with a defendant, to try to minimize further harm by remaining silent.” State v. Moore II, 305 Or App 21, 28, 469 P3d 283 (2020).

On the other hand, State v. Wedebrook, 316 Or App 1, 501 P3d 1084 (2021) was just the opposite. The prosecution sought a 20-month prison sentence, and the defendant sought a probation sentence. The court chose to impose probation, but wanted to impose a jail term as a condition of probation. The court asked defense counsel if 90 days was the maximum jail term as a condition. “[D]efense counsel offered that ‘as far as custody units my understanding * * * is that anything under a year stays at county. * * * [D]efense counsel added, ‘I think your discretion is your discretion.” 316 Or App at 3 (emphasis added). The court then imposed a 12-month jail term condition of probation.

On appeal, the defendant raised a “plain error” claim, relying on OAR 213-005-0013(1)(c), which, under the defendant’s grid-block, limits the jail term condition of probation to 90 days.9 The state conceded error, but the Court of Appeals rejected the concession. It explained:

“Given the parties’ respective positions as to what might be an appropriate sentence, it is possible to view defense counsel’s statements suggesting that the court was not limited to a 90-day jail sanction to be part of a ‘tactical or strategic choice’ that benefited defendant. The plausible tactical choice defendant could have made was that, when the court was considering either a prison sentence or a probation plus jail sentence, if the court understood that it was limited to a 90-day jail term under the probation regime, it may well have opted for the prison sentence. By either nudging the court toward imposing the erroneous jail sentence or leaving the error uncorrected when it was announced, defendant avoided, to her benefit, a potential prison sentence.

“That defendant was instrumental in bringing about the error dissuades us from exercising our discretion to correct it. Despite the admittedly erroneous sentence, we will not exercise our discretion to correct it.”

316 Or App at 3-4 (citation omitted).

But a concession does not guarantee the court will exercise discretionary review. For example, in State v. Diaz-Guillen, 245 Or App 110, 261 P3d 80 (2011), the state conceded that trial court imposed overlength prison and PPS terms, but the terms were shorter than valid terms with which the overlength terms were or would be concurrent. The court held, “In light of the harmlessness of the errors and our confidence that a remand to the trial court would have no practical effect on defendant’s total aggregate sentence, we decline to exercise our discretion to correct the trial court’s errors.” 245 Or App at 118. See also State v. Howell, 225 Or App 146, 149, 200 P3d 610 (2009) (“where there is ‘no legitimate debate’ that a jury would find the facts necessary to support an enhanced sentence, the defendant has no significant interest in resentencing, whereas the state does have a significant interest in avoiding an unnecessary resentencing,” court declined to address unpreserved claim of Apprendi error; quoting State v. Ramirez, 343 Or 505, 513, 173 P3d 817 (2007), on recons, 344 Or 195, 179 P3d 673 (2008)).

Furthermore, the party’s failure to ask the sentencing court to correct the error under ORS 137.172 (discussed in Section 9-11.2) militates against reviewing an unpreserved claim. For example, in State v. Chesnut, 283 Or App 347, 351-52, 388 P3d 1237 (2017), the court declined to exercise discretion to correct a clerical error, which error could have been avoided if defendant had raised the issue below, and was the type of error that easily could have been corrected under former ORS 138.083 (2015), repealed by Oregon Laws 2017, chapter 529, § 26. On the other hand, in State v. Truong, 249 Or App 70, 274 P3d 873 (2012), the court exercised its discretion to correct a plain-error violation of 400-percent rule (see Chapter 8), despite the defendant’s failure file a motion to correct. The court explained, “[T]he gravity of the error—an additional four-month prison term—and the other factors pertaining to [the court’s] discretion outweigh that failure.” 249 Or App at 75. But it is important to recognize that a party’s filing such a motion to correct, which the trial court denies, does not transform a previously unpreserved claim into a preserved one, and does not itself make an unpreserved claim reviewable on appeal. State v. Layton, 163 Or App 37, 41, 986 P2d 1221 (1999), rev den, 330 Or 252 (2000).

Another situation that militates against reviewing an unpreserved claim applies where, through plea negotiations, the defendant received various charge and sentencing concessions. In the absence of an objection, the appellate court will not order relief from the trial court’s erroneous imposition of a prison sentence. State v. Quintero, 160 Or App 614, 982 P2d 543 (1999).

Yet another situation that militates against reviewing an unpreserved claim applies where the appellate court believes that based on the record, the trial court will reimpose the same sentence. In State v. Jenniches, the defendant, contemporaneous with the filing of his brief, filed a motion to correct a sentencing error with the trial court. When the trial court denied the motion, it court “stat[ed] that ‘the appropriate remedy is a resentencing to effectuate the court’s intention.’ (Emphasis added.)” 187 Or App at 664. “Based on that declaration,” the Court of Appeals “conclude[d] that, on remand, the [trial] court would impose the same total term of imprisonment.” Id. Notwithstanding that both the state and the Court of Appeals agreed the trial court erred, the court declined to address an unpreserved claim of sentencing error. The court explained that the defendant “was not prejudiced by the sentencing errors, [and that] the gravity of the errors is insufficient to support an exercise of our discretion to correct them.” Id. at 665. See also State v. Hylton, 230 Or App 525, 216 P3d 900 (2009).

Finally, appellate counsel occasionally may confront sentencing errors that not only are not preserved, but were “invited” either by trial counsel’s explicit accession or even by his request. Such situations implicate the “invited error” doctrine, which is a profound policy reason against reviewing unpreserved claims.

For example, in State v. Nolasco-Lara, 249 Or App 111, 117, 274 P3d 880 (2012), the court refused to exercise discretion to correct an excessive PPS term as plain error, because the plea agreement expressly recommended the excessive term, the defendant “encouraged the judge to consider imposing [the erroneous term] by agreeing to the plea,” and the defendant may have “made a strategic choice not to object” in order to get the benefit of the plea deal. Similarly, in State v. Kammeyer, 226 Or App 210, 214, 203 P3d 274 (2009), the court declined to address an unpreserved claim of error, because defense counsel was “actively instrumental” in causing the error. See also Fults, 343 Or at 523 (where defendant expressly took no objection to trial court’s failure to state any reasons for aggravated departure, Court of Appeals erred in reviewing unpreserved challenge to departure).

§ 9-8. MOOTNESS.

The appellate courts historically have refused to consider sentencing issues that have become moot. See, e.g., State v. Lincoln, 5 Or App 138, 141, 483 P2d 92 (1971) (challenge to sentence rendered moot by expiration of sentence and complete absence of collateral consequences). The appellate courts apply the mootness doctrine in guidelines cases. E.g., State v. Webb, 262 Or App 1, 324 P3d 522 (2014) (defendant’s sentence for new crimes, which carried longer PPS term into which PPS term for older would merge upon release from prison term for new crimes, rendered moot defendant’s challenge to older crime’s prison term); State v. Jenkins, 191 Or App 617, 609, 83 P3d 390, rev den, 337 Or 160 (2004) (appellate court’s reversal of defendant’s convictions and remand for retrial rendered moot his alternative claims of sentencing error); State v. Dick, 169 Or App 649, 10 P3d 315 (2000) (claim of error in revoking probation moot where defendant had already completed sentence); State v. Lewis, 121 Or App 494, 496, 854 P2d 1009, rev den, 318 Or 26 (1993) (challenge to condition of probation rendered moot by sentencing court’s subsequent revocation of probation); State v. Miller, 114 Or App 235, 237, 835 P2d 131 (1992) (challenge to duration of term of PPS rendered moot by sentencing court’s subsequent correction of term’s duration).

Defense counsel should be wary of the state’s attempts to use the mootness doctrine after the defendant has served the entirely of an unauthorized prison term he is challenging. This is because the defendant’s completion of the prison the term does not necessarily render the claim moot.

For example, if the unauthorized incarceration term was the first sentence imposed in a consecutive “string” of sentences, correcting the unauthorized term’s duration would advance the completion date of the entire string. The unauthorized completed term thus has a continuing adverse effect until the defendant completes service of the entire string. Challenges to the unauthorized term are not moot until then. Accord Garlotte v. Fordice, 515 US 39, 43, 115 S Ct 1948, 132 L Ed 2d 36 (1995).

Furthermore, except when “straight” jail sentences are imposed (see Chapter 5), in most guidelines cases, a PPS term must follow an incarceration term. See State v. Little, 116 Or App 322, 842 P2d 414 (1992). A defendant’s service of an unauthorized sentence or unauthorized portion of a sentence10 should be credited against his term of PPS. Thus, and as referenced above in State v. Webb, completing an unauthorized prison term does not render the issue moot until the defendant has also completed his PPS term. Baty v. Slater, 161 Or App 653, 984 P2d 342 (1999), on recons, 164 Or App 779, 995 P2d 1176, rev den, 331 Or 191 (2000). See also Williamson v. Schiedler, 196 Or App 302, 101 P3d 364 (2004) (owing to pendency of PPS terms, imposition of over-length consecutive sentences was not moot).11

But see Dunn v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 310 Or App 249, 487 P3d 410 (2021) (challenge to PPS violation moot even though petitioner has additional PPS time remaining, because PPS would be served concurrently to PPS in new sentence).

Moreover, a defendant’s discharge from PPS does not moot his appeal of conditions of PPS if the state is seeking payment for costs related to those conditions. Roberts v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 221 Or App 278, 282, 190 P3d 397 (2008).

But see Bowers v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 309 Or App 566, 483 P3d 22 (2021) (challenge to PPS conditions was moot when petitioner did not show that board sought payment).

EXAMPLE: In State v. Holliday, the sentencing court imposed a 21-month term of incarceration and a 36-month PPS term. The Court of Appeals held that only a 19-month term of incarceration was authorized, 110 Or App at 431–32, but it did not so rule until after the defendant completed the unauthorized 21-month term. Thus, the defendant served two months of “dead time.” On remand the sentencing court credited the defendant’s “dead time” against his PPS term. As a practical matter, the Court of Appeals’ decision resulted in the defendant serving only a 34-month PPS term.

Finally, Crombie v. Board of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 325 Or App 312, ___ P3d ___ (2023), involved a PPS matter that usually would be rejected on mootness grounds, but the Court of Appeals decided it on the merits. The court did so by relying ORS 14.175, which allows acts, policies, or practices of public bodies that are capable of repetition and likely to evade judicial review. The legislative enactment states:

“In any action in which a party alleges that an act, policy or practice of a public body, as defined in ORS 174.109, or of any officer, employee or agent of a public body, as defined in ORS 174.109, is unconstitutional or is otherwise contrary to law, the party may continue to prosecute the action and the court may issue a judgment on the validity of the challenged act, policy or practice even though the specific act, policy or practice giving rise to the action no longer has a practical effect on the party if the court determines that:

“(1) The party had standing to commence the action;

“(2) The act challenged by the party is capable of repetition, or the policy or practice challenged by the party continues in effect; and

“(3) The challenged policy or practice, or similar acts, are likely to evade judicial review in the future.”

In Crombie, the court had a policy to impose a no-computer-use condition whenever a computer was used in connection with the crime. In holding that the condition exceeded the Board’s authority, the court observed that the analysis was case-specific and that the petitioner was forbidden to communicate with juveniles by a different condition. Further, because electronic communication is so widespread, a prohibition “cannot be imposed in a rote manner.” 325 Or App at 324.

§ 9-9. APPELLATE COURTS’ REMEDIAL AUTHORITY.

ORS 138.257 authorizes appellate court remedial authority:

“(1) Except as otherwise provided in this section, the appellate court may affirm, reverse, vacate or modify the judgment or order, or any part thereof, from which the appeal was taken.

“(2) Subject to Article VII (Amended), section 3, Oregon Constitution, the appellate court shall not reverse, modify or vacate a trial court judgment or order if there is little likelihood that any error affected the outcome.

“(3) Except as provided in subsection (4) of this section, if the court reverses, vacates or modifies a judgment or order, or any part thereof, the court may do so with or without remanding the case and with or without instructions.

“(4)(a) The appellate court shall remand the case to the trial court:

“(A) If the appellate court, in a case involving multiple convictions, reverses at least one conviction and affirms at least one other conviction.

“(B) If the appellate court determines that the trial court, in imposing or failing to impose a sentence in the case, committed an error that requires resentencing.

“(b) In a case remanded under this section, the trial court, after issuance of the appellate judgment, may impose a new sentence for any conviction.

“(5) If the appellate court reverses a conviction without remanding, upon issuance of the appellate judgment, the trial court shall follow the procedures described in ORS 135.680 concerning the defendant’s release.”

Former ORS 138.222(5)(a), which still applies in cases involving judgments entered before January 1, 2018, requires the appellate court to remand a case for resentencing on all counts if it determines that one or more, but not all, of the sentences originally imposed are unauthorized. See State v. Sierra I, 349 Or 604, 607, 247 P3d 759 (2011) (on reconsideration requested by state, making remand instructions “more specific” to reflect former “ORS 138.222(5)(b) require[ment] * * * to remand for resentencing in this case,” where two of three kidnapping convictions were reversed in original opinion). But if the appellate court remands for resentencing without vacating the original, invalid judgment, the original sentences remain in effect pending resentencing. State v. Lewis, 137 Or App 108, 111, 903 P2d 391 (1995), rev den, 322 Or 613 (1996).

See also State v. Moore III, 308 Or App 724, 482 P3d 222 (2021) (when Court of Appeals issued opinion that remanded for resentencing, and defendant subsequently moved to dismiss appeal, Court of Appeals denied motion because defendant failed to justify dismissal). Accord State v. Lasheski, 312 Or App 714, 493 P3d 1118 (2021) (where defendant failed to present evidence or legal authority sufficient to overcome the presumption against post-opinion dismissal in this case, the Court of Appeals denied his motion; however, the court granted defendant leave to file a new motion to dismiss within 14 days of publication of the opinion).

The text of ORS 138.257(4)(b) seems consistent with former ORS 138.222(5). Consequently, and as previously explained, Sierra I, discussed above, and the cases discussed below, which applied the former statute, should be equally applicable to the new statute. Wal-Mart Stores, 341 Or at 399; Fifth Avenue Corp., 282 Or at 597-98. As previously explained, these cases support construing ORS 138.257(4)(b) consistently with former ORS 138.222(5).

If a court originally imposed sentence on more than one count, and the Court of Appeals rules that even just one of the sentences was unauthorized, on remand the court resentences on all counts. See, e.g., State v. Walraven, 187 Or App 728, 732, 69 P3d 835, rev den, 335 Or 656 (2003) (where court reversed and remanded for resentencing on one ground, but declined to address other sentencing claim because it was not preserved, court explained that “[o]n remand, defendant is free to present his [currently unpreserved] argument to the trial court, which has discretion to consider it”); State v. Ramsey, 184 Or App 468, 56 P3d 484 (2002) (where jury could have based defendant’s true-life sentence for aggravated murder on consideration of charges of burglary and robbery that were reversed, court reversed true-life sentence and remanded for resentencing); Galvin, 152 Or App at 280-81; State v. Hagan, 140 Or App 454, 458, 916 P2d 317 (1996) (where record makes clear trial court would have imposed different sentence had it been aware of legal obstacles to sentence it did impose, but record does not make clear what that different sentence would have been, appellate court will remand for resentencing); State v. Campbell, 130 Or App 263, 881 P2d 829, rev den, 320 Or 453 (1994) (where appellate court remanded for resentencing, on remand trial court was free to recalculate defendant’s grid block); State v. Dvorak, 124 Or App 578, 580, 863 P2d 1314 (1993), rev den, 318 Or 351 (1994). Thus, a trial court errs when it declines to consider resentencing issues, because it believes it lacks authority to completely resentence the defendant. See, e.g., State v. Tate, 282 Or App 320, 327, 386 P3d 182 (2016) (where case was before trial court on remand “for resentencing,” trial court erred in failing to allow defendant to make legal arguments against sentence of life without parole); State v. Hollingquest, 241 Or App 1, 250 P3d 366 (2011) (same).

The same, complete-resentencing principle applies where the appellate court reverses even just one of the defendant’s multiple convictions and remands for resentencing. State v. Skaggs, 275 Or App 557, 364 P3d 355 (2015) (where Court of Appeals held that trial court erred in failing to merge convictions, proper remedy is to remand not only to merge convictions, but for resentencing); State v. Zolotoff, 275 Or App 384, 365 P3d 131 (2015) (where Court of Appeals remanded for resentencing owing to erroneous failure to merge certain convictions, trial court erred in refusing to conduct resentencing to consider defendant’s request for sentence reduction); State v. Bordeaux, 220 Or App 165, 185 P3d 524 (2008); State v. Davis, 216 Or App 456, 473, 174 P3d 1022 (2007) (explaining that reversal of a conviction requires complete resentencing). See also State v. Sheikh-Nur, 285 Or App 529, 539, 398 P3d 472 (2017) (merger of some convictions requires complete resentencing on remand, because “the legislature understood, when it enacted [former] ORS 138.222(5)(b), that the statute would require resentencing on remand of convictions on all charges tried together with a charge for which a conviction is reversed on appeal, including charges consolidated for trial after the initial filing of separate charging instruments”); State v. Muyingo III, 225 Or App 156, 162 & n 1, 200 P3d 601, adhered to as clarified, 226 Or App 327, 203 P3d 365 (2009); State v. Taylor, 133 Or App 503, 513, 892 P2d 697, on recons, 134 Or App 501, 895 P2d 357 (1995) (where appellate court reversed one of defendant’s two convictions, court remanded for resentencing on remaining conviction).

For example, State v. Claborn I, 214 Or App 166, 162 P3d 374, rev den, 343 Or 555 (2007) involved a defendant who had been convicted of kidnapping and multiple other charges. The Court of Appeals reversed the kidnapping conviction and remanded for sentencing. On remand the defendant relied on the Supreme Court’s (then-impending) decision in State v. Rodriguez/Buck, 347 Or 46, 217 P3d 659 (2009) to object to the reimposition of a mandatory minimum sentence. The trial court declined to consider his objection; instead, the court merely deleted the reversed charge, and then reimposed all the same sentences as before. In State v. Claborn II, 237 Or App 488, 491, 240 P 3d 66 (2010), the Court of Appeals again remanded the case for resentencing, because the trial court erred in declining to consider the defendant’s Rodriguez/Buck-based objection to the mandatory sentence. The Court of Appeals expressly instructed the trial court to consider the defendant’s objection.

But if on remand the trial court would have no permissible options besides correcting the error that is the basis for remand, the appellate court will not remand for complete resentencing. For example, in State v. McLaughlin, 243 Or App 214, 258 P3d 1241, on recons, 244 Or App 691, 260 P3d 814, on further recons, 247 Or App 334, 269 P3d 104 (2011) (per curiam), the Court of Appeals held that because the prosecution failed to present evidence of the nature and amount of restitution at or before sentencing, the trial court erroneously imposed restitution. The Court of Appeals then wrestled with the proper remedy. On the defendant’s petition for reconsideration, the court explained that on remand “the trial court [would have] no basis and [did] not retain authority to impose restitution”; consequently, the court said, “[W]e need not remand the case for resentencing.” 243 Or App at 692. On the state’s petition for further reconsideration, the Court of Appeals reversed course. It remanded for resentencing, because the trial court “committed an error that requires resentencing[.]” 247 Or App at 337 (court’s emphasis). The Court of Appeals explained that the “trial court’s original sentence indicated its desire to have defendant compensate the victim”; therefore, the trial court retained authority to lawfully impose a compensatory fine if it chose. Id. at 337-38. See also State v. Rubio, 248 Or App 130, 273 P3d 238 (2012) (remanding “for entry of corrected judgments deleting erroneous terms relating to contact with the victim,” instead of for full resentencing, because there were no other permissible options for the trial court on resentencing).

The broad resentencing authority of ORS 138.257(4)(b) and former ORS 138.222(5)(a) “applies to all errors for which review is authorized[.]” State v. Edson, 329 Or 127, 139, 985 P2d 1253 (1999). Previously, that authority did not apply without exception. For example, under State v. Smith II, 323 Or 450, 453-54, 918 P2d 824 (1996), the court could not change, to the defendant’s detriment, a sentence he had completely served. See State v. Bisby, 212 Or App 86, 93, 157 P3d 262, rev den, 343 Or 160 (2007) (where post-conviction court remanded case for resentencing, trial court erred in restructuring sentences by modifying defendant’s previously-served sentence; court explained that in State v. Smith II, the Supreme Court “held that such a modification was beyond the authority of the sentencing court, and nothing since that case has altered the court’s holding”). Commentators insist that double jeopardy principles also prohibit trail courts, on remand, from modifying sentences defendants have fully served. See Arthur W. Campbell, Law of Sentencing, § 8:14 (3d ed. 2004)).

But in State v. Sierra II, 361 Or 723, 399 P3d 987 (2017), a case which serves as a significant extension of the court’s decision in State v. Partain, 349 Or 10, 239 P3d 232 (2010)12—the trial court, at resentencing on remand, imposed a sentence 26 months longer than what the court had originally imposed. 361 Or at 725-26. This included changing to the defendant’s detriment certain sentences he already had completed. The Supreme Court affirmed the increased sentence the defendant’s double jeopardy objections based on North Carolina v. Pearce. In doing so, the court effectively overruled Smith II and rejected the double jeopardy theory. Sierra II, 361 Or at 728-34.13

Notably, ORS 138.257(4)(b) and former ORS 138.222(5)(a) do not apply to resentencings ordered by a post-conviction court. Such a resentencing order does not necessarily “result in a blank slate on which the parties [are] free to reopen all sentencing issues.” State v. Coburn, 146 Or App 653, 660, 934 P2d 579 (1997). The order will result in a “blank slate” only if the post-conviction court expressly so orders. Bogle v. Armenakis, 184 Or App 326, 56 P3d 420 (2002) (citing Brock v. Baldwin, 171 Or App 188, 14 P3d 651 (2000), rev den, 332 Or 56 (2001)).

Consider Curry. There, the defendant’s case was remanded to the trial court by a federal habeas corpus court, owing to an ineffective assistance claim involving the defendant’s criminal-history score. On appeal from the remand proceeding, the defendant claimed that the trial court erred in refusing to consider his proffered mitigation evidence relating to his reformation while in prison since his original sentencing. The Court of Appeals held that the relief the habeas court ordered pertained solely to the defendant’s criminal-history score. Other aspects of his sentence were not at issue, so the court rejected the defendant’s claim. Curry, 209 Or App at 37.

§ 9-9.1. No Adverse Consequences.

If the appellate court determines that the defendant suffered “no adverse consequences” from the sentencing error, it will not remand for resentencing. Tremillion, 111 Or App at 376. This concept is codified at ORS 138.257(2), which states: “ Subject to Article VII (Amended), section 3, Oregon Constitution, the appellate court shall not reverse, modify or vacate a trial court judgment or order if there is little likelihood that any error affected the outcome.”

The no-adverse-consequences doctrine is the sentencing analog to the harmless-error doctrine used in trial-error contexts.14 But the doctrine may not be used to affirm an invalid sentence by speculating that on remand, the sentencing court may reimpose the sentence as a departure sentence. State v. Foster, 186 Or App 466, 469, 63 P3d 1269 (2003); Galvin, 152 Or App at 279-80.

If the appellate court acknowledges that the trial court committed a sentencing error, but the record makes clear that on remand the trial court would reimpose the same aggregate sentence as before, and that the trial court could do so validly, the appellate court may apply the no-adverse-consequences doctrine to declare the sentencing error harmless. Compare State v. Quintero-Martinez, 220 Or App 497, 188 P3d 350 (2008) (where trial court invalidly imposed 120-month consecutive sentence, but record made clear that on remand court could and would reimpose same sentence validly, appellate court affirmed) with Truong, 249 Or App 70, 274 P3d 873 (2012) (where court imposed erroneously long consecutive sentence, and record did not reflect whether on remand the court would restructure sentences to reach same consecutive total, Court of Appeals exercised discretion to reach plain error and to reverse) and State v. Marshall, 219 Or App 511, 183 P3d 241 (2008) (where trial court invalidly imposed 124-month consecutive sentence, but record was not clear whether on remand court would reimpose same sentence, appellate court remanded for resentencing).

Another situation where the appellate courts may declare that a sentencing error has no adverse consequences is where the trial court’s sentencing error involves a nullity. For example, the Department of Corrections has exclusive legal authority to calculate a defendant’s presentence incarceration credit against an original prison sentence. See Chapter 4. A trial court lacks authority to grant or deny such credit, so its erroneous denial of such credit is nullity. As such, the error has no adverse consequences. See State v. Phaneuf, 219 Or App 640, 184 P3d 1136 (2008).

Other cases applying the no-adverse-consequences doctrine include State v. Nelson, 119 Or App 84, 849 P2d 1147 (1993). There the Court of Appeals held that although the sentencing court erred in using criminal history score H rather than score I, the defendant’s sentence would have been the same under either score. Given that, “the error did not prejudice [the defendant] and, therefore, he is not entitled to relief.” 119 Or App at 89-90.

See also State v. Carrera, 307 Or App 387, 393-95, 477 P3d 458 (2020) (where trial court erred in setting defendant’s grid-block at 8-C, instead of 8-D, 56-month departure sentence was within grid-block 8-D’s maximum departure limit and was authorized by plea agreement, so no adverse consequences in using wrong grid-block).

Similarly, in State v. Dummitt, 115 Or App 487, 491, 839 P2d 246 (1992), the court held that although the sentencing court erred when it failed to “merge” an additional offense’s probationary term into the primary offense’s PPS term, the probationary term was “surplusage” and obviated any need for relief.

But State v. Franks, 143 Or App 384, 923 P2d 1302, rev den, 324 Or 488 (1996) explains the type of situation where the appellate court will not apply the no-adverse-consequences doctrine to a sentencing error. There, the sentencing court set the defendant’s grid block at 7-B, which carries a presumptive maximum of 30 months’ incarceration. Without declaring a departure, the court imposed a 36-month term. On appeal the state claimed that the sentencing court did not err in imposing the 36-month term, because the court should have used grid block 7-A, which authorizes a presumptive maximum of 36 months. The Court of Appeals refused to consider the state’s claim, because it did “not cross-assign error to the trial court’s conclusion that defendant’s * * * conviction * * * fell in gridblock 7-B. ORAP 5.57. See also Graham, 125 Or App at 519.” 143 Or App at 387.

§ 9-9.2. Summary Affirmances.

Commentary explains that under former ORS 138.222(6), the “appellate courts may establish rules for the summary disposition of cases without merit.” Implementation Manual at 164. Although the appellate courts never availed themselves of the authority to create such rules, the 1995 Legislature created a statute the courts may use to expedite meritless appeals. In pertinent part, that statute provides:

“[T]he Court of Appeals, on its own motion or on the motion of the respondent, may summarily affirm, without oral argument, the judgment after submission of the appellant’s brief and without submission of the respondent’s brief if the court finds that no substantial question of law is presented by the appeal. Notwithstanding ORS 2.570, the Chief Judge of the Court of Appeals may deny or, if the petitioner does not oppose the motion, grant a respondent’s motion for summary affirmation. A dismissal of appeal under this section constitutes a decision upon the merits of the appeal.”

ORS 138.225.

This statute creates a criminal appeal analog to summary judgment motions filed in civil cases in trial courts. The sole limitation on using ORS 138.225 is that the respondent may not file a motion for summary affirmance until after the appellant has filed his or her brief.

The 1995 Legislature created a second statute the parties may use to expedite appeals, if they agree the cases should be remanded. The statute provides:

“(1) On joint motion of the parties to an appeal in a criminal case, the appellate court may vacate the judgment or order from which the appeal was taken and remand the matter to the trial court to reconsider the judgment or order, or any intermediate decision by the trial court. On remand, the trial court shall have jurisdiction to enter a modified judgment or order, or to reenter the vacated judgment or order.

“(2) After entry of a judgment or order under subsection (1) of this section, either party may appeal in the same time and manner as an appeal from the original judgment or order.”

ORS 138.227.

There also is an appellate court rule that largely parallels, but is not identical to, ORS 138.227. The rule states:

“(1) On joint motion of the parties to any appeal, a department of the Court of Appeals may decide the merits of an appeal by unpublished order if the department determines;

“(a) The appeal does not present a substantial question of law;

“(b) All parties to the appeal agree both on the correct resolution of all questions raised on appeal and on the appropriate disposition of the appeal; and

“(c) A published opinion would not significantly benefit the bench, the bar, or the public.

“(2) Parties seeking relief based on the assertion that the appeal does not present a substantial question of law must include a sufficient statement of facts of the case to show that all of the questions raised on appeal are grounded in those facts.

“(3) Parties are discouraged from moving for relief under this subsection when resolution of the merits of the appeal would require the appellate court to try the cause anew upon the record or to make one or more factual findings anew upon the record. The Court of Appeals will exercise its discretion to grant relief under this subsection in such cases only in exceptional circumstances.”

ORAP 10.35.

The rule differs from ORS 138.227, in that unlike the statute, ORAP 10.35 permits the appellate court to decide the merits, and to give the trial court directions on remand.

§ 9-9.3. Written Appellate Opinions.

Addressing former ORS 138.222(6), commentary explains, “If the trial court is reversed, the appellate court must issue a written opinion. If the trial court is not reversed, a written opinion is optional at the discretion of the appellate court.” Implementation Manual at 164. This requirement ensures that the appellate courts will establish a “common law” of sentencing to provide guidance to attorneys and sentencing judges in future cases.

§ 9-11. TRIAL COURT AUTHORITY OVER RESENTENCING & POST-SENTENCING PROCEEDINGS.

This section discusses trial court resentencing authority following remands from reviewing courts. The section then discusses trial court authority post-sentencing authority to correct sentencing errors raised by one or both of the parties, or on the court’s own initiative. After that, the section discusses trial court authority to stay sentences pending appeal, including by ordering a defendant’s release from incarceration pending appeal.

§ 9-11.1. Trial Court Resentencing Authority.

A considerable body of case law regulates trial court authority over cases that a reviewing remanded for resentencing. That is, the case law regulates the sentences a trial court may impose on the individual counts of a case remanded for resentencing.

As explained in Section 9-9, then a trial court remands for “resentencing,” it must resentence on all counts. As a starting point in determining the validity of the trial court’s exercise of its resentencing authority, the test “is whether the trial judge could have imposed the new sentence in the original proceeding.” State v. Froembling, 237 Or 616, 619, 319 P2d 390, cert den, 379 US 937 (1964).

But in some cases on remand for resentencing, the trial court may wish to impose a lengthier sentence than it did at original sentencing. The court’s authority to do is limited by the principles of North Carolina v. Pearce, State v. Sierra II, and State v. Partain.

These cases announce and apply the due process principle that “vindictiveness against a defendant for having successfully attacked his first conviction must play no part in the sentence he receives after a new trial.” Pearce, 395 US at 725. Moreover, because “the fear of such vindictiveness may unconstitutionally deter a defendant’s exercise of the right to appeal or collaterally attack his first conviction, due process also requires that a defendant be freed of apprehension of such a retaliatory motivation on the part of the sentencing judge.” Id.

The Court announced the two prophylactic rules to “assure the absence of such a motivation”:

“[W]henever a judge imposes a more severe sentence upon a defendant after a new trial, the reasons for his doing so must affirmatively appear. Those reasons must be based upon objective information concerning identifiable conduct on the part of the defendant occurring after the time of the original sentencing proceeding. And the factual data upon which the increased sentence is based must be made part of the record, so that the constitutional legitimacy of the increased sentence may be fully reviewed on appeal.”

Id. at 726.

Imposing a greater sentence, following a defendant’s successful appeal, is presumed to be vindictive and violative of due process. Pearce, 395 US at 725. To overcome this presumption, the trial court must identify, as the basis for imposing the greater sentence, relevant conduct that came to light after imposition of the original sentence. United States v. Jackson, 181 F3d 740, 746 (6th Cir 1999). But see State v. Church, 262 Wisc 678, 665 NW2d 141, 154-55 (2003) (the trial court’s belief that the defendant has not been rehabilitated since the time of the original sentencing is insufficient to overcome Pearce’s presumption of vindictiveness).

When Partain adopted the due process principles from Pearce, the court had to overrule its decades-old decision in State v. Turner, 247 Or 301, 429 P2d 565 (1967). Turner construed ORS 138.020 as drawing a “bright line” rule that in most situations prohibited a trial court from imposing a greater sentence than the original, after a defendant’s reconviction following a successful appeal. Within a few hours after the Supreme Court issued its decision in Partain, the decision came under intense criticism. The defense bar identified various constitutional theories to try to mitigate Partain’s impact, including one which involves the court’s overruling of Turner. This theory involves the fact that in overruling Turner, the Partain court primarily by relied on 1993 amendments to former ORS 138.222(5), which truly had nothing to do with the Turner doctrine. See State v. Partain, 228 Or App 329, 335, 208 P3d 526 (2009), rev’d, 349 Or 10, 239 P3d 232 (2010). Defendants who appealed before the Partain decision had no reason to foresee Partain’s abandonment of the Turner doctrine. Similar in concept to the bar on ex post facto legislation, when a state’s highest court has construed a statute in a manner favorable to the accused, due process prohibits the retroactive application of a non-foreseeable and unfavorable change in that construction. Douglas v. Buder, 412 US 430, 432, 93 S Ct 2199, 37 L Ed 2d 52 (1973) (per curiam); Bouie v. City of Columbia, 378 US 347, 353-54, 84 S Ct 1697, 12 L Ed 2d 894 (1964). Because the Supreme Court’s abandonment of the Turner doctrine was not foreseeable, the abandonment should apply prospectively only, i.e., only to appeals filed on or the Supreme Court’s date of decision in Partain.

Other defense bar theories, which seek to try to mitigate Partain’s impact, include:

1. On remand in Sierra II, the state, relying on ORS 136.790 and 136.792, for the first time gave notice of its intent to prove, and did prove, four aggravating factors. Sierra II, 361 Or at 744-45. The trial court relied on these factors to impose an increased sentence, in the form of an aggravated departure. The Supreme Court held that the trial court’s reliance on the factors was a non-vindictive reason for its sentencing decision. Id. at 745. But as explained in Chapter 1, in a case on remand for resentencing, former and double jeopardy principles may preclude the state from alleging aggravating factors it did not allege originally. Moreover, Chapter 1 explains that only offender-specific factors, and offense-specific factors that were deferred to the sentencing phase, may be tried in a resentencing. These types of objections were sharply limit, or even altogether prohibit, trials on aggravating factors, to be used to impose the sort of increased sentence that Sierra II allows.

2. Partain explains that a trial court’s imposition of a greater sentence than the original sentence, after a defendant’s successful appeal, carries a presumption of judicial vindictiveness. The court may overcome that presumption by making a record establishing that the basis for the greater sentence is relevant conduct that came to light after imposition of the original sentence. But in other contexts where the trial court relies on facts to impose sentence greater than what the law presumes—i.e., aggravating factors to impose a departure instead of a presumptive guidelines sentence—those facts are subject to the Sixth Amendment principles of Apprendi and its progeny. See Chapter 1. Likewise, facts on which a trial court would rely to overcome the presumption of vindictiveness and impose greater than the “presumptive” (original) sentence are as subject to the Apprendi rule as are aggravated-departure factors.

3. Assuming that facts on which a trial court would rely to overcome the presumption of vindictiveness are subject to the Apprendi rule, the defendant must be given advance notice of the alleged facts. See ORS 136.790. See Chapter 1. But under federal double jeopardy principles, facts subject to the Apprendi rule are crime elements. See Sattazahn v. Pennsylvania, 537 US 101, 123 S Ct 732, 154 L Ed 2d 588 (2003). See also Washington v. Recuenco, 548 US 212, 126 S Ct 2546, 2553, 165 L Ed 2d 466 (2006) (to determine whether violation of Apprendi rule is harmless, aggravating factor is treated as a crime element). Thus, double jeopardy prohibits adding new crime elements subsequent to a defendant’s successful appeal, thereby foreclosing the trial court’s ability to find facts necessary to overcome the presumption of vindictiveness.

There are, however, definite limitations on the extent to which trial courts may impose lengthier sentences than what they originally imposed. For example, in Sierra II, a judge other than the one who presided over the defendant’s original sentencing presided over the defendant’s resentencing. The state “argued that” in that situation, “the Pearce requirements do not apply, as a categorical matter[.]”Sierra II, 361 Or at 737. With respect to Pearce’s first prophylactic rule, that “whenever a judge imposes a more severe sentence upon a defendant after a new trial,” Pearce, 395 US at 726, the Sierra II court rejected the state’s claim. It stated: “[W]e decline to adopt a categorical rule that a difference in judges makes the first prophylactic rule adopted in Pearce inapplicable.” 361 Or at 743.

Another limitation involves situations where “a defendant is unable to establish the presumption of improper motive[.]” State v. Febuary, 361 Or 544, 558, 396 P3d 894 (2017). “[T]he defendant [still] may show a due process violation by ‘affirmatively prov[ing] actual vindictiveness,’ with proof that an improper motive caused the ‘more severe’ subsequent sentence, such as statements by the judge demonstrating actual vindictiveness.” Febuary, 361 Or at 558 (quoting Wasman v. United States, 468 US 559, 569, 104 S Ct 3217, 82 L Ed 2d 424 (1984); emphasis added).

Ultimately, however, the Febuary court held that there was no “evidence that the trial court acted vindictively or out of an improper motive when sentencing him on remand. The trial judge acted within the requirements of due process and did not violate the Pearce rule.” Id. at 565.

State v. Reinke II is similar. There, the trial court, in a bench trial, made both standard aggravated-departure and dangerous-offender departure findings. In the defendant’s original sentencing, the court imposed what it denominated a 280-month dangerous-offender sentence. That sentence was unauthorized in several respects, including because the court did not specify whether the 280-month sentence was an indeterminate term authorized by ORS 161.725(1), or a determinate term authorized by ORS 161.737(2). In the defendant’s first appeal, the Court of Appeals reversed and remanded for resentencing. Reinke I, 245 Or App at 34. On remand, the trial court imposed a statutorily compliant sentence, comprised of a 30-year indeterminate dangerous-offender sentence and, in conjunction, a 120-month determinate minimum sentence. So “[i]n correcting [the] errors” in the original sentence, “the [resentencing] court imposed a sentence that increased the incarceration term of defendant’s sentence from that which had been originally imposed.” Reinke II, 289 Or App at 14.

In his second appeal, the defendant challenged the new sentence on the ground that it violated the Pearce principles announced in Partain. In response, “[t]he state contend[ed] that, because the sentence imposed by the initial sentencing court was legally erroneous, the initial sentence cannot serve as the benchmark to determine whether the sentence imposed on remand is more severe.” Reinke II, 289 Or App at 16. The Court of Appeals rejected that argument, stating: “Legal error in an initial sentence does not foreclose a determination that a subsequent sentence can trigger a presumption of vindictiveness.” Id.

Ultimately, the court rejected the defendant’s Partain-based challenge. It held:

“Because the resentencing court recognized that defendant’s initial sentence was legally erroneous and that the court was required to impose a lawful sentence on remand, which it sought to do in imposing the sentence that it did, we conclude that the resentencing court articulated ‘wholly logical, nonvindictive reasons’ for the longer sentence that it imposed on remand. Thus, the sentence did not violate defendant’s due process rights.”

Reinke II, 289 Or App at 17.

On the other hand, in State v. Robledo, 282 Or App 96, 386 P3d 136 (2016), the court found actual vindictiveness. There, the defendant was convicted for a second time, after a successful first appeal. At sentencing following his reconviction, the trial court imposed a sentence “79 months [longer] than it had imposed after defendant’s first conviction.” 282 Or App at 99. The court “gave two reasons for the increased sentence on remand: defendant ‘required’ the victims to testify multiple times and defendant lacked remorse.” Id. at 101. The Court of Appeals held, “Because the reasons that the sentencing court gave for the more severe sentence on remand do not satisfy us that the sentence was not a product of vindictiveness towards defendant, we remand for resentencing.” Id. at 103. See also State v. Criswell, 282 Or App 146, 162, 386 P3d 58 (2016) (sentencing court does not overcome presumption of vindictiveness if it fails to explain basis for more severe sentence).

State v. Bradley II, 281 Or App 696, 383 P3d 937 (2016) provides another example of affirmative proof of actual vindictiveness. The defendant previously had appealed his convictions on 12 counts. The Court of Appeals reversed nine of those 12 counts, leaving only three standing. 281 Or at 698. The trial court had imposed a 215-month total sentence on the 12 convictions, with 115 months worth of that total on the three counts left standing after the defendant won his first appeal.

On remand, the trial court severed the affirmed counts from the reversed counts, and resentenced the defendant on the affirmed counts. The court then imposed a 183-month total sentence on the three counts left standing. That total was 68 months longer than the original sentence on those counts, but was 32 months shorter than the 215-month total the trial court originally had imposed on all 12 counts. For that reason, the Court of Appeals held, “[T]here is no presumption in this case that the sentence was vindictive,” so “the defendant must affirmatively prove actual vindictiveness.” Id. at 701 (internal quotations omitted).

The trial court based its resentencing decision on a belief “that it was ‘entitled to consider the sexual abuse of the child whose cases were reversed and remanded’ and the ‘strong possibility’ that the state might not elect to prosecute those charges if the sentence on the affirmed counts was ‘sufficient.’” Id. at 702. The defendant “argue[d] that ‘[t]he procedure employed by the court, severing the indictment for sentencing yet using the unconvicted conduct against defendant, gives the appearance of vindictiveness.’” Id. at 701-02.

The Court of Appeals agreed with defendant. It held: “[T]he record demonstrates that the trial court did what Partain is intended to protect against; it imposed a sentence that effectively punished defendant for his success on appeal.” Id. at 702-03. Addressing the trial court’s “conclu[sion] that it was ‘entitled to consider the sexual abuse of the child whose cases were reversed and remanded’ because of the ‘strong possibility’ that ‘[t]he State might elect not to prosecute him on the other child’s case[;] [t]hey might consider this sentence sufficient,’” id. at 702-03, the Court of Appeals said, “That conclusion is problematic.” Id. at 703. The court continued:

“[T]he state dismissed the reversed counts once defendant had received an increased sentence on the affirmed counts.

“Defendant’s sentence should not have been increased such that the prosecution would be relieved of its burden to prove the reversed counts beyond a reasonable doubt. That is the essence of punishing defendant for his success on appeal. * * * To the extent that the court on resentencing after an appeal relies on an impermissible consideration in increasing the sentence imposed on particular counts, the defendant establishes that the sentence is vindictive. Because the trial court based its decision to increase the sentence for the affirmed counts on the reversed counts that were still pending prosecution, the trial court exceeded the applicable limits under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Thus, defendant has affirmatively proved actual vindictiveness.”

Id.. at 703-04.

Moreover, prior to Partain, the courts created various exceptions to the then-controlling Turner doctrine. Those exceptions should apply equally, post-Partain.

For example, a state’s appeal of a defendant’s sentence cannot possibly chill the defendant’s exercise of his right to appeal. To the contrary, the state’s appeal should actually impel the defendant to exercise his right to defend himself on appeal. In any event, state’s appeals do not implicate the Partain doctrine’s rationale, so the doctrine does not apply to them.

Consistent with Turner-era case law, the Pearce doctrine also should not apply if the original sentence was illegally short. State v. Horsley, 168 Or App 559, 561–62, 7 P3d 646 (2000). If a defendant received an illegally short sentence, appealed his conviction, won a remand, and on remand his convictions were affirmed, the trial court may replace the original, illegally short sentence with the longer valid sentence. See State v. Alvarez, 168 Or App 393, 7 P3d 616, rev den, 331 Or 244 (2000). See also Davis, 216 Or App at 469–70 (explaining Turner doctrine, and effectively reaffirming Alvarez by holding that imposing greater sentence than original, when greater sentence is only sentence law allows, does not violate Turner doctrine).This exception is best explained in State v. Key, 54 Or App 575, 635 P2d 1039 (1981), rev den, 292 Or 568 (1982)—a case on which the court later relied in Alvarez:

“[T]he policy of Turner is not applicable to cases where the sentence following the first trial was erroneous as a matter of law, as opposed to being within the trial court’s discretion, and the trial court on remand corrects the error. The state may not appeal from a sentence, even though it is erroneous as a matter of law, [former] ORS 138.060, but ought not to be precluded from having a lawful sentence imposed following the second conviction. Although we recognize that such a rule may discourage an appeal by the defendant, we see no compelling reason why the defendant has a right to rely on an unlawfully imposed sentence.”

Key, 54 Or App at 579-80 (emphasis added; footnote omitted).

As seen, the fact that the state could not appeal from the erroneously short sentence the trial court originally imposed was critical to the Key court’s creation of the exception to the Turner doctrine. That was the situation in Alvarez, too. There, the trial court imposed an erroneous sentence that was shorter than what the law mandated, and did so before the legislature created former ORS 138.222(4)(c), which authorized the state to obtain appellate review of that sort of sentencing error. The Court of Appeals held that under Key, the Turner doctrine did not apply, so did not prohibit the trial court from correcting the error on remand by imposing the legally mandated longer sentence. Alvarez, 168 Or App at 395.

PRACTICE TIP: The 1989 Legislature first authorized the state to appeal legally erroneous sentences imposed in felony cases. Former ORS 138.060(1)(e). See also ORS 138.045(1)(f) (in cases involving judgments and orders entered on or after January 1, 2018, authorizing state’s appeals of legally erroneous felony sentences). Consequently, in a felony case where at original sentencing the trial court imposed an erroneously short sentence, from which the state could have appealed but did not appeal, and from which the defendant did appeal and prevailed, on remand defense counsel should argue that the Key and Alvarez exception does not apply. Instead, counsel may argue that on remand in such cases, preclusion principles such as equitable or judicial estoppel, or law of the case, prohibit the state from seeking correction of the erroneous sentence. Put differently, in such cases, counsel may argue that the defendant has the “right to rely on [the] unlawfully imposed sentence.” Key, 54 Or App at 580.

State v. Muyingo III addressed another exception to the Turner doctrine, which should now apply equally under the Pearce doctrine. In State v. Muyingo I, 171 Or App 218, 15 P3d 83 (2000), rev den, 332 Or 431 (2001), the Court of Appeals reversed several of the defendant’s convictions and remanded for a new trial. On remand the state opted to dismiss the reversed counts, and then to seek resentencing on the remaining counts. On one of the remaining counts, Count 19, the court originally imposed a consecutive 32-month sentence. On remand the court imposed a consecutive 59-month aggravated-departure sentence on Count 19. The defendant again appealed. He challenged the departure on various grounds, including that the increase in Count 19’s sentence violated both the then-extant Turner doctrine, and the Apprendi rule.

The claimed Apprendi rule violation was not preserved. Nevertheless, the Court of Appeals exercised its discretion to treat the claim as plain error, and, without addressing the Turner-based claim, reversed and remanded for resentencing. State v. Muyingo II, 197 Or App 320, 105 P3d 899 (2005), rev den, 340 Or 359 (2006), vacated and rem’d, 345 Or 315-16, 195 P3d 63 (2008). After denying the defendant’s petition, the Supreme Court, relying in its intervening decisions in Ramirez and Fults, which forbid reviewing unpreserved Apprendi claims of the sort defendant Muyingo raised, allowed the state’s petition and vacated and remanded to the Court of Appeals for reconsideration. See State v. Muyingo II, 345 Or 316, 195 P3d 63 (2008).

Thus came Muyingo III. On remand from the Supreme Court, the Court of Appeals declined to exercise its discretion to review the unpreserved Apprendi claim, and thereby rejected it. Muyingo III, 225 Or App at 158. Having rejected that claim, the court then addressed the defendant’s Turner claim. The court explained that even with the “adjustment of defendant’s sentence on count 19, defendant’s total consecutive sentences on remand (like defendant’s original sentence) imposed 120-months’ imprisonment.” Muyingo III, 225 Or App at 160. Significantly, the court further explained that

“this is not a situation in which the total sentence defendant was to serve was increased after remand. Rather, defendant contends that, because he prevailed in his prior appeal and certain convictions were set aside, he could not be resentenced to serve the same total amount of time that he received previously. That is, defendant argues that, under Turner, a successful appeal requires that a defendant receive a lesser sentence after remand. Defendant cites no case that supports that proposition.”

Id. at 166 n 4.

Thus, the court grounded its rejection of the claim not on a repudiation of the Turner doctrine, but on the recognition that the doctrine prohibits the trial court from imposing a greater, total sentence on remand than it imposed originally. To be sure, Muyingo’s post-remand, 59-month sentence on Count 19 was 17 months greater than his original sentence on that count. But his post-remand total consecutive sentence of 120 months was exactly the same as his original, total consecutive sentence. That is what counted under the Turner doctrine, so it also should be what counts post-Partain.

And indeed in Febuary, the Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals’ application of the “aggregate approach,” used in Muyingo III, to affirm the trial court’s imposition of a post-remand sentence of 12 months in jail on a particular count, where it originally imposed probation on that count. 361 Or at 546-47. Rejecting the defendant’s challenge to the jail sentence as violative of Pearce, the Febuary court held: “For the purposes of establishing a Pearce violation, a sentence is ‘more severe’ when the total length of the second sentence exceeds that of the first, regardless of the counts of conviction.” 361 Or at 563 (emphasis added).

The pre-Partain case of State v. Gildersleeve, 230 Or App 348, 215 P3d 117 (2009) states an exception to the Turner doctrine that is conceptually similar to the one applied in Muyingo III. The defendant was convicted and sentenced in state court, and appealed. While his appeal was pending, he was convicted and sentenced in federal court, with a portion of his federal sentenced ordered to run consecutively to his state court sentence. At resentencing in his state court case, the defendant claimed that when considered in conjunction with a consecutive sentence imposed by a federal court, the new state court sentence exceeded his original state court sentence in violation of the Turner doctrine. The Court of Appeals rejected his claim, stating: “[A] defendant’s sentence for a federal crime, imposed by a federal court, [has no] bearing on the application of the Turner rule—a rule that is directed to Oregon courts sentencing offenders for Oregon crimes and is based on the Supreme Court’s interpretation of a state statute, ORS 138.020.” Gildersleeve, 230 Or App at 353.

§ 9-11.2. Corrections of Sentencing Errors.

When a sentencing court corrects an error, it usually does so on order of an appellate court, a post-conviction court, or a federal habeas corpus court. But in some situations a sentencing court may correct an error sua sponte, as well as on motion of a party. Horsley, 168 Or App at 561. Moreover, pre-guidelines case law indicates that upon identifying a sentencing error, a court not only has the power, but also the duty to correct the error, because the erroneous sentence is void. State v. Leathers, 271 Or 236, 240, 531 P2d 901 (1975); State v. Cotton, 240 Or 252, 254, 400 P2d 1022 (1965).

ORS 137.172 authorizes motions to the sentencing court to correct sentencing errors. It provides:

“(1) The trial court retains authority after entry of judgment of conviction or a supplemental judgment, including during the pendency of an appeal, to modify the judgment, including the sentence, to correct any arithmetic or clerical errors or to delete or modify any erroneous term in the judgment. The court may correct the judgment either on the motion of one of the parties or on the court’s own motion after written notice to all of the parties.

“(2) If the trial court enters a corrected judgment under this section during the pendency of an appeal, the trial court administrator shall immediately provide a copy of the corrected judgment to the appellate court.”

Id. (emphasis added).

As seen, ORS 137.172(1) authorizes only the trial court to correct erroneous terms in the judgment (be they arithmetic, clerical, or plain errors). Thus, the Parole Board lacks authority to correct a erroneous terms in the judgment. Gaynor v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 165 Or App 609, 996 P2d 1020 (2000). All the board may do is request the trial court, or one of the parties, to take steps to correct the error.

Thus, if no appeal is pending, the trial court does not submit a copy of the corrected judgment to the appellate court. But if an appeal is pending, so the trial court submits a corrected judgment to the appellate court, ORS 138.275 also applies. It provides:

“If the appellate court, during the pendency of an appeal, receives from the trial court an amended, corrected or supplemental judgment or an amended or corrected appealable order, the appellate court shall notify the attorney of record for the state and the attorney of record for the defendant or, if the defendant is not represented by an attorney, the defendant.”

Id.

Also if an appeal is pending, so ORS 137.172(2) and ORS 138.275 apply, ORS 138.105(4) also applies. It provides:

“(a) When an appeal is pending and the trial court enters an amended, corrected or supplemental judgment, or an amended or corrected order that is appealable under ORS 138.060, section 3 of this 2017 Act or any other statutory provision:

“(A) If the appellant intends to assign error to any part of the amended, corrected or supplemental judgment, or amended or corrected order that is appealable, the appellant shall file an amended notice of appeal from such judgment or order not later than 30 days after the appellant receives notice that such judgment or order has been entered.

“(B) If the appellant does not intend to assign error to any part of the amended, corrected or supplemental judgment, or amended or corrected order that is appealable, the appellant need only file a notice of intent to proceed with the appeal not later than 30 days after the appellant receives notice that such judgment or order has been entered. The notice of intent to proceed is not jurisdictional.

“(b) As used in this subsection, ‘appellant’ means the attorney of record in the appellate court for the appellant or, if the appellant is not represented by an attorney, the appellant personally.”

Id.

As seen, ORS 137.172, ORS 138.275, and ORS 138.105(4) provide the procedural requirements for defense motions to correct sentencing errors. But the statutes do not define what is meant by ORS 137.172(1)’s phrase, “erroneous term in the judgment.” The statute’s legislative history and case law provide a definition.

ORS 137.172(1) has a statutory predecessor, that being former ORS 138.083. The 1989 Legislature created the former statute in the same bill in which it approved the guidelines. See Or Laws 1989, ch 790, § 20. The legislature did so to “create[] a mechanism to correct * * * errors * * * under the new sentencing guidelines without appellate court intervention.” Implementation Manual at 159.

As originally written, former ORS 138.083 authorized only a 60-day period during which sentencing courts could correct “arithmetic or clerical errors[.]” Senate Bill 228 (1995) (enrolled as Oregon Laws 1995, chapter 109, section 1) amended the statute by eliminating the 60-day time limit, and by authorizing sentencing courts to correct “any erroneous term in the judgment.” Former ORS 138.083(1).

The Court of Appeals has suggested that the phrase “erroneous term in the judgment” encompasses any sentence component that could be challenged for the first time on appeal as facially apparent legal error. See, e.g., Arellano, 149 Or App at 92-93; Graham, 143 Or App at 88. If so, then ORS 137.172 may be used to correct any plainly erroneous term in the judgment.

The history of the 1995 amendments to former ORS 138.083 supports this view. SB 228 was filed pre-session at the request of the Department of Justice. At a public hearing, then solicitor general Virginia Linder testified that SB 228 was intended to change former ORS 138.083 in two ways:

“It would first eliminate the 60-day limitation [of former ORS 138.083], so that the trial court has an unlimited period of time to make those corrections; and secondly it would expand the authority slightly to include any erroneous term in the judgment of conviction, which would get to other kinds of plain errors in the sentence. Under Oregon law at present there is a considerable body of case law that suggests that trial courts actually have inherent authority to fix those kinds of things in civil and criminal judgments both. But we’ve worried that the statute might be read as a limitation on that inherent authority, so we’d like to get rid of the 60-day limit and open it up just a bit.”

Tape Recording, Senate Judiciary Committee, SB 228, Jan. 24, 1995, Tape 18, Side A (statement of Virginia Linder) (emphasis added). This “plain error” limitation on the permissible scope of motions to correct under former ORS 138.083 should equally apply to motions under ORS 138.172.

Trial court authority to correct errors also is restrained by the general principle that they lose jurisdiction to modify a sentence upon its execution. See State ex rel. O’Leary v. Jacobs, 295 Or 632, 634, 669 P2d 1128 (1983). With respect to a prison sentence, execution occurs upon delivery of the defendant to the custody of the DOC to begin serving the sentence. See State v. Golden, 293 Or App 14, 25, 426 P3d 172 (2018) (“the trial court plainly erred when it entered [an] amended judgment in [a particular] case * * * because that judgment modified defendant’s sentence after it had been executed”); Young, 188 Or App at 252 (“a trial judge’s power to modify or vacate a valid sentence ends once the sentence has been executed”). See also State v. Rossi, 216 Or App 168, 169, 171 P3d 1031 (2007) (where court made new sentence concurrent with preexisting sentences, new sentences were “put into effect” immediately, thereby depriving court of jurisdiction to modify sentence); DeCamp, 158 Or App at 243 (all sentences in a consecutive “string” of sentences are simultaneously executed upon the defendant’s delivery to DOC to serve the string); State v. Hamlin, 151 Or App 481, 487, 950 P2d 335 (1997), rev den, 327 Or 173 (1998) (where court imposed concurrent sentences, execution of one sentence deprived court of jurisdiction to modify other sentences). A “straight” jail sentence is executed upon the individual’s confinement in the jail. ORS 137.330(1). A felony probationary sentence is executed “either when the judgment is entered or on the date of sentencing, if that is the clear intent of the judgment.” State v. Hoffmeister, 164 Or App 192, 196 n 2, 990 P2d 910 (1999).

Execution divests a court of jurisdiction to modify a sentence only if the sentence is valid. If the sentence is erroneous it is void, which grants the court jurisdiction to correct the error notwithstanding the sentence’s execution. Horsley, 168 Or App at 561–62 (citing Leathers and Cotton). See also State v. Easton, 204 Or App 1, 5–6, 126 P3d 1256 (2006) (en banc) (following Horsley). But before a court may act on its own motion, it first must notify the parties in writing of its intended action. State v. French, 208 Or App 652, 145 P3d 305 (2006).

Trial court authority to correct errors does not extend to a valid sentence that the court imposed, but that the court subsequently wishes it had not imposed because the sentence will be served contrary to the court’s intent. In State v. Nason, 184 Or App 114, 55 P3d 525 (2002) (per curiam), the trial court intended that the incarceration sentences it imposed on two felonies be served in state prison. The court imposed consecutive 12-month terms, thinking that because of their 24-month total, they would be served in state prison, as opposed to county jail. But the court “overlooked ORS 137.124(2), which requires that sentences of 12 months or less be served in local custody.” Nason, 184 Or App at 115. When the court learned of this, it entered an amended judgment imposing consecutive 13- and 11-month sentences. Such a total sentence in fact would be served in state prison, because one of the sentences was greater than 12 months. See ORS 137.124(2)(b). The Court of Appeals vacated the amended judgment. Because the original sentences accurately reflected what the court imposed at sentencing and were valid, “the amended judgment” was improper, for it did “not merely correct a clerical or arithmetic error.” Nason, 184 Or App at 115.

Somewhat similarly, in State v. Johnson, 242 Or App 279, 255 P3d 547 (2011), the trial court purported to correct a sentencing error by modifying the judgment to specify that its total prison sentence would be consecutive to the sentence imposed by a preexisting judgment on an assault conviction. The Court of Appeals first held:

“[Former] ORS 138.083(1)(a) authorizes a court to ‘correct any arithmetic or clerical errors or to delete or modify any erroneous term in the judgment.’ There is no question in this case that the judgment did not contain any arithmetic or clerical errors. We also conclude that it did not include any ‘erroneous term.’ As defendant points out, the trial court was not required to impose consecutive sentences, so there is no legal error, and the judgment conforms to the sentence that the court pronounced orally, so there is no factual error.4 Thus, [former] ORS 138.083(1)(a) did not furnish authority for the court to modify the judgment.

____________

“4 We express no opinion as to whether a judgment includes an ‘erroneous term’ if it is clear from the record that the trial court intended to include a particular term in its ruling and judgment but neglected to do so in either. The record in this case does not indicate that the court intended to order that the sentence * * * be served consecutively to the sentence on the assault conviction, so that issue is not presented in this case.”

242 Or App at 285.

Moreover, the Johnson court said, even if the record reflected that the defendant had agreed to consecutive time, “[t]he state cites no other statute (or any case law) in support of its assertion that a trial court has inherent authority to modify the judgment to reflect sentences to which a defendant agreed, and we are aware of none.” Id. at 286.

In State v. Gilbert, 248 Or App 657, 274 P3d 223 (2012), the original judgment imposed concurrent sentences. Nothing in the original judgment, nor in the oral record of the original sentencing proceeding, reflected an intent that any of the sentences would run consecutively. Later the court entered an amended judgment stating that although the case’s sentences would run concurrently, they would run consecutively to the sentences imposed in a separate case. The court stated that the consecutive sentences were what it had intended all along. On appeal, the state argued that the failure to impose consecutive sentences constituted an “erroneous term” that could be modified under former ORS 138.083. The Court of Appeals rejected that argument and held that the trial court lacked the authority to enter the amended the judgment, because the originally imposed concurrent sentences were not an “erroneous term”:

“[B]ecause the trial court did not expressly provide that the sentences in [this] case * * * be served consecutively to the sentences imposed in [the other] case * * * , either orally at the [original sentencing] hearing or in the [original] judgment, the court may not later amend the judgment to reflect its unexpressed intention to make the sentences consecutive, and ORS 138.083(1)(a) does not authorize the trial court to amend the sentence.”

Id. at 663. Cf. State v. Estey, 247 Or App 25, 29, 268 P3d 772 (2011) (where written judgment failed to reflect imposition of consecutive terms that court orally imposed at sentencing, judgment contained error that former ORS 138.083(1) authorized court to correct).

Significantly, there is no time limit on filing motions to correct sentencing errors. This was first established in State v. Harding, 222 Or App 415, 193 P3d 1055 (2008), on recons, 225 Or App 386, 202 P3d 181, vacated on other grounds, 347 Or 368, 223 P3d 1029 (2009). There, the defendant relied on former ORS 138.083(1) to multiple corrections, including to seek relief from an aggravated-departure sentence imposed in violation of the Apprendi rule. The trial court held that it lacked jurisdiction over the motion.

On appeal, the Court of Appeals explained that a party may file an error-correction motion long after initial sentencing, because former “ORS 138.083(1) contains no time limitation on when a court may exercise its authority to correct an erroneous term in a judgment.” Harding, 225 Or App at 390. The court then reversed on the ground that under former ORS 138.083(1), the trial court had discretion to consider the defendant’s motion, but “was not required to correct the asserted error.” Harding, 222 Or App at 421 (court’s emphasis). The court remanded for reconsideration, with orders for the trial court to properly exercise its discretion to determine whether, and how, to correct the Apprendi error. Harding at 422.

On the state’s petition for reconsideration, the Court of Appeals explained that it had jurisdiction over the defendant’s appeal. Although the trial court denied that part of the defendant’s motion based on the Apprendi error, the trial court allowed a separate part of his motion. Consequently, the trial court entered a new judgment from which the defendant could appeal, which gave the Court of Appeals jurisdiction. Harding, 225 Or App at 389.

Although the Supreme Court vacated the Court of Appeals decision in Harding, the Court of Appeals subsequently reaffirmed the Harding principles. It first did so State v. Lewallen, 262 Or App 51, 324 P3d 530, rev den, 355 Or 880 (2014), and did so again in State v. Redmond, 295 Or App 453, 460, ___ P3d ___ (2018).

The part of the Harding principles stating that the trial court had discretion but “was not required to correct the asserted error,” 222 Or App at 421, could be a subject for reconsideration. Redmond, Lewallen, and Harding did not address the separate principles that “[a] sentence must be in conformity with the governing statute; any non-conforming sentence is void for lack of authority and thus totally without legal effect.’ (Citations omitted.)” State ex rel. Huddleston v. Sawyer, 324 Or 597, 615, 932 P2d 1145, cert den, 522 US 994 (1997) (quoting Leathers, 271 Or at 240). Moreover, at no time did the Court of Appeals address the separate principle that when a trial court determines that it has imposed an unauthorized, and hence a void sentence, it has a corollary “duty to take corrective action and to impose a valid sentence.” Leathers, 271 Or at 240.

Defense counsel may argue that under these separate principles, the trial court lacks discretion over whether to consider the error. Instead, the court has the legal duty to consider and to correct the error. Because the Redmond, Lewallen, and Harding courts did not address these principles, their decisions not foreclose this argument. See Stranahan v. Fred Meyer, Inc., 331 Or 38, 54, 11 P3d 228 (2000) (describing scope of stare decisis doctrine).

In addition to the fact that there is no time limit on filing motions to correct sentencing errors, there is no requirement of the pendency of an appeal to file such a motion. Instead, the error-correction statute “merely allows the trial court to ‘retain’ its * * * authority to correct an erroneous sentence ‘irrespective of any notice of appeal.’ * * * That means that the trial court’s authority is retained regardless of whether a notice of appeal has been filed.” Horsley, 168 Or App at 563 (court’s emphasis). In other words, Horsley explains, all the statute does is create an exception to the general principle that the trial court loses jurisdiction, and thus the power to modify the judgment, once an appeal has been filed. See, e.g., Jacobs, 295 Or at 636 (discussing principle).

If the motion seeks an administrative correction, as opposed to a substantive one, the defendant need not be present, or have validly waived his right to be present, for the court to take correction active. See State v. Riley, 195 Or App 377, 97 P3d 1269 (2004) (where trial court’s correction of error was purely administrative and did not involve disputed facts or the exercise of judicial discretion, correction of error without prior notification to defendant was harmless). But if the motion seeks a substantive correction, the defendant must be present, or have validly waived his right to be present. ORS 137.030 (requiring defendant’s presence during felony sentencing hearing). The court commits reversible error if it substantively amends the judgment without the defendant’s presence or a valid waiver.

See State v. Priester, 325 Or App 574, 584, ___ P3d ___ (2023) (“[b]ecause [a condition of probation] was not made clear—either in the record prior to sentencing or as presented in open court—that * * * condition was imposed for the first time in the judgment,” and the Court of Appeals “remand[ed] for resentencing”). See also

State v. Donner, 230 Or App 465, 215 P3d 928 (2009); State v. Rickard, 225 Or App 488, 201 P3d 927 (2009); Rossi, 216 Or App at 169; State v. French, 208 Or App 652, 658, 145 P3d 305 (2006); State v. Jacobs, 200 Or App 665, 671-72, 117 P3d 290 (2005); State v. Kennedy, 196 Or App 681, 691-92, 103 P3d 660 (2004); State v. Whitlock, 187 Or App 265, 268-69, 65 P3d 1114 (2003); State v. Gibson, 183 Or App 25, 32, 51 P3d 619 (2002). In the event of such an error, the case will be remanded for resentencing. On remand the parties may be allowed to offer evidence and arguments supporting or opposing the trial court’s reimposition of the amended judgment. Whitlock, 187 Or App at 271.

A party may appeal from an adverse order correcting a sentencing error, Stubbs, 193 Or App at 607, but a party may not appeal from a trial court order that completely denies a motion to correct sentencing errors. State v. Hart, 188 Or App 650, 72 P3d 671, rev den, 336 Or 126 (2003) (abrogating contrary dictum from Gaynor). This is because there will be no new judgment from which to appeal, as required by ORS 138.020. But because direct appeal is not an available remedy, mandamus may be. Sawyer, 324 Or at 608. This is particularly true for those defendants for whom post-conviction relief also is not available. See, e.g., Miller v. Lampert, 340 Or 1, 125 P3d 1260 (200) (on PCR, certain petitioners may not claim retroactive benefit of Apprendi v. New Jersey).

But as seen in Harding, when the trial court partially grants and partially denies a correction motion, the court will issue an amended judgment from which the moving party may appeal. ORS 138.020. Harding, 222 Or App at 418-19. See also Lewallen, 262 Or App at 54 (reaffirming Harding).

Consider State v. Larrance, 270 Or App 431, 347 P3d 830 (2015). There, the defendant sought to modify the judgment by correcting three errors: the imposition of (i) PPS terms on misdemeanor convictions, (ii) conditions of PPS, and (iii) a PPS term following a five-year prison term on a Class C felony. The trial corrected the erroneous imposition of PPS on the misdemeanors, left the other errors intact, and entered an amended judgment. The defendant appealed, and the Court of Appeals vacated and remanded. It explained that a trial court’s

“decision to modify or not to modify a term contained in a judgment is highly discretionary. Moreover, a court’s decision to not modify a judgment under ORS 138.083 is not appealable. However, when a court amends a judgment, it must not do so in a manner that abuses its discretion. Here, because the trial court intended to give a lawful sentence, but misapprehended the law and ultimately decided the matter in reliance on that erroneous view of the law, the court abused its discretion. Accordingly, we vacate the amended judgment and remand to the trial court for further consideration of the matter.”

Larrance, 270 Or App at 440 (internal quotations and citations omitted).

Later, in State v. Dizick, 285 Or App 1, 395 P3d 945 (2017), the court modified Larrance. The Dizick court essentially held that the only reason the Larrance court reversed was that the trial court had said “it would ‘comply with the sentencing rules and statutes,’” 270 Or App at 439, but then “fail[ed] to correct the erroneous terms of the judgment after stating its intent to modify the judgment to do so.” Dizick, 285 Or App at 8.

In Dizick, the state moved to correct a sentencing error, which the defendant answered by seeking corrections that would benefit him. Although the trial court granted the state’s motion, it denied the defendant’s. The Court of Appeals affirmed that denial, explaining that the trial court:

“offered appropriate reasons for denying defendant’s proposed modifications, explaining that defendant could have challenged the grid block scores on direct appeal * * * or in a petition for post-conviction relief * * * . Therefore, even to the extent that the trial court’s determinations at the 1996 hearing were erroneous, under the circumstances, the trial court did not abuse its discretion.”

Id. at 9.

Later still, in State v. Redmond, the court returned to Larrance to hold that the trial court erred in denying the defendant’s motion to correct an error committed in violation of the “200% rule.” See Chapter 8. The court held that it “must vacate and remand to the trial court to reconsider its ruling,” “because the [trial] court’s exercise of discretion was based on a mistaken understanding of the 200-percent rule[.]” Redmond, 295 Or App at 462 (citing Larrance, 270 Or App at 440).

Apart from ORS 137.172 and former ORS 138.083, a third statute, ORS 137.754, expressly provides trial court authority to correct sentencing errors. It applies exclusively in situations where the trial court denies eligibility for early-release programs under ORS 137.750 and 137.752. See Chapter 4 (discussing early-release program eligibility). In pertinent part, ORS 137.754 states:

“Notwithstanding any other provision of law, a sentencing court retains authority after entry of a judgment of conviction to modify its judgment and sentence to comply with the requirements of ORS 137.750 or 137.752 when:

“ * * * * *

“(3) The judgment and sentence failed to comply with the provisions of ORS 137.750 or 137.752.”15

“[U]nless the court finds on the record in open court substantial and compelling reasons to order that the defendant not be considered for such leave, release or program.” ORS 137.750(1). Thus, if a trial court, denies early-release program eligibility without finding substantial and compelling reasons, the denial is erroneous and the defendant may seek correction under ORS 137.754.

In State v. Hammond, 218 Or App 574, 180 P3d 137 (2008) (en banc), the Court of Appeals essentially held that an erroneous denial of an ORS 137.754 motion is not subject to appeal under the provisions of ORS chapter 138. But the court left open the question of whether such a denial may be appealed under ORS chapter 19. See Hammond, 218 Or App at 583 n 6.

On the other hand, ORS chapter 19’s provisions authorizing appeals from “special statutory proceedings” very well may apply to such appeals. For example, ORS 19.205(3) states in pertinent part: “An order that is made in the action after a general judgment is entered and that affects a substantial right * * * may be appealed in the same manner as provided in this chapter for judgments.” Moreover, by virtue of ORS 138.065, the criminal code now expressly authorizes appeals from special statutory proceedings. It states: “A party may appeal a judgment or order deciding a special statutory proceeding as provided in ORS 19.205.” Id.

By definition, a judgment that erroneously denies early-release program eligibility is a “general judgment.” ORS 18.005(7) (“‘[g]eneral judgment’ means the judgment entered by a court that decides all requests for relief in the action”). When a defendant moves under ORS 137.754 for issuance of an order correcting an erroneous denial of early-release program eligibility, he seeks review of “[a]n order that [was] made in the action after [the] general judgment [was] entered[.]” ORS 19.205(3). Furthermore, an order denying an ORS 137.754 motion “affects a substantial right” of the defendant, ORS 19.205(3)—specifically, his right to be eligible for early-release programs, absent valid, substantial and compelling findings. See State v. Clark, 205 Or App 338, 341, 134 P3d 1074, rev den, 341 Or 245 (2006) (describing potential benefits which flow from alternative-program eligibility). Consequently, a defendant should argue that under ORS 138.065, he has the right to appeal an order denying an ORS 137.754 motion “in the same manner as provided in [chapter 19] for judgments.” ORS 19.205(3).16 See State v. Arms, 60 Or App 400, 402, 653 P2d 1004 (1982), rev den, 294 Or 461 (1983) (under ORS 19.205(2)(c),17 a defendant acquitted in a criminal action could appeal an order to pay the cost of appointed counsel); State v. Young, 24 Or App 5, 7, 544 P2d 179 (1976) (proceeding on defendant’s motion to set aside conviction under ORS 137.225 is a special statutory proceeding; thus, state could appeal from trial court order granting such motion).18 Cf. Gibbons & Gibbons, 153 Or App 377, 397 n 3, 956 P2d 1069, recons den, 155 Or App 262, 964 P2d 1050 (1998) (“denial of motion to set aside default judgment pursuant to ORCP 71B(1)(a) is [an] appealable order under [former] ORS 19.205(2)(c)”).

§ 9-11.3. Supplemental Judgments of Restitution.

A defendant may appeal from a judgment that imposes restitution without specifying the amount of restitution. The authority includes ORS 137.105, which in pertinent part provides:

“(1) The trial court retains authority during the pendency of an appeal to determine restitution and to enter a supplemental judgment specifying the amount and terms of restitution or an order denying restitution.

“(2) If the trial court enters a supplemental judgment or an order under subsection (1) of this section during the pendency of an appeal, the trial court administrator shall immediately provide a copy of the supplemental judgment or the order to the appellate court.”

In turn, ORS 138.035 states in pertinent part:

“(2)(a) A defendant may appeal a judgment ordering payment of restitution but not specifying the amount of restitution.

“(b) A defendant may appeal a supplemental judgment awarding restitution.”

(Emphasis added.)

But ORS 138.105 apparently conflicts with ORS 138.035(2)(a), which states: “On appeal from a judgment ordering payment of restitution but not specifying the amount of restitution, the appellate court has no authority to review the decision to award restitution.” ORS 138.105(6) (emphasis added). How this apparent conflict will be resolved remains to be seen.

§ 9-11.4. Release Pending Appeal.

As explained in Section 9-11.1, upon execution of a defendant’s sentence, the trial court generally loses jurisdiction over the defendant’s case. Among the exceptions to that general rule is that trial courts retain authority to stay sentences pending appeal. This is expressed in ORS 138.285(1), which states:

“(a) A justice, municipal or circuit court may enter an order in a criminal action as defined in ORS 131.005 staying execution of a sentence, or a portion of a sentence, pending the resolution of an appeal.

“(b) Except for good cause shown, a motion for an order under this section must be filed in the trial court no later than the filing of a notice of appeal. The trial court retains jurisdiction to enter an order under this section irrespective of whether a notice of appeal has been filed.”

ORS 138.285(2) lists a series of factors trial courts are to consider in deciding whether to order a stay and release pending appeal. Most of these factors are similar to those which trial courts consider in making pretrial release decisions. But some of the factors are peculiar to releases pending appeal. They state:

“(g) The likelihood that an appellate court will reverse the sentence, or the portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed or will reverse the judgment of conviction that includes the sentence, or the portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed; and

“(h) If an appellate court has issued a decision reversing the sentence, or a portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed or reversing the judgment of conviction that includes the sentence, or the portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed:

“(A) Whether the reversal or any other relief described in the appellate decision will result in the defendant having completed serving the term of incarceration imposed; and

“(B) Whether the appellate decision remands the case for a new trial.”

ORS 138.125(2).

Note that ORS 138.285(2)(h) will apply long after the defendant has filed his notice of appeal. Thus, an appellate court issuing “a decision reversing the sentence, or a portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed or reversing the judgment of conviction that includes the sentence, or the portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed” automatically constitutes “good cause shown” for filing “a motion for an order” after “the filing of a notice of appeal.” ORS 138.285(1)(b).

The 2013 Legislature’s Senate Bill 39 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2013, chapter 151) created this statute. Prior to SB 39, there was a great deal of uncertainty about whether trial courts had authority to stay sentences pending appeal—particularly if the sentences had been executed. The passage of SB 39 eliminated much, but not all, of this uncertainty. For example, whether trial court stay-and-release authority applies to an incarceration term imposed as a condition of probation remains an open question. See 3 Criminal Law § 28.22 (Oregon State Bar CLE 2005).

Trial court stay-and-release authority pending appeal must be distinguished from pretrial situations, in that a defendant generally has a constitutional right to bail release pending trial. A defendant has no constitutional right to release from incarceration pending appeal. Instead, such a release is discretionary with the trial court. See Priest v. Pearce, 314 Or 411, 419, 840 P2d 65 (1992).

An appellate court has no authority to order a defendant’s release pending appeal, and a defendant may not appeal from a trial court order denying his motion for release pending appeal. See State v. Wimber, 108 Or App 1, 5, 814 P2d 169 (1991). But see ORS 19.350 (allowing for the filing of a stay directly to the Court of Appeals when it is clear that filing with the circuit court would be futile). A defendant’s only possible remedy from a trial court’s denial of release is state habeas corpus. Whether that remedy actually exists is an open question. See Wimber, 108 Or App 5 at n 3.

PRACTICE TIP: Trial courts are most likely to order releases pending appeal in situations where they are persuaded that on appeal the defendant may win a reversal, or a reversal and remand for a new trial. Consequently, a motion for release should contain at least a colorable argument demonstrating the defendant’s probability of success on appeal. Moreover, the best times to bring such a motion are (i) immediately after sentencing, when the potential appellate issues are still fresh in the trial court’s mind; or (ii) after the defendant has won a reversal in the Court of Appeals, but issuance of the appellate judgment, which formally ends the appeal and returns the case to the trial court for further proceedings consistent the court’s decision, see ORS 19.450, is delayed because the state is considering or is petitioning for Court of Appeals reconsideration or for Supreme Court review. Cf. ORS 138.650(3) (where post-conviction court orders relief that would allow petitioner’s release from incarceration, but state’s appeal stays affect of post-conviction court’s order, trial court (but not post-conviction court) may order stay of sentence and petitioner’s release on security and/or conditions pending state’s appeal).

§ 9-11.5. Petitioning for Reconsideration of Conviction & Sentence.

Note: Professor Aliza Kaplan, who is the director of the Criminal Justice Reform Clinic at the Lewis and Clark Law School, edited this section.

At the request of the Lewis and Clark Law School’s Criminal Justice Reform Clinic, the 2021 Legislature’s Committee on Judiciary and Ballot Measure 110 Implementation sponsored, and the legislature enacted, Senate Bill 819 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2021, chapter 414). The bill takes a significant step in retracting the state’s mass-incarceration policies. It does so by reducing in length needlessly long sentences.19

The bill’s primary component in pertinent part states that

“a person who was sentenced for a felony offense other than aggravated murder and the district attorney of the county in which the person was sentenced may jointly petition the sentencing court for reconsideration of a conviction or sentence if the original sentence no longer advances the interests of justice[.]”

ORS 137.218(1)(a).

As seen, the bill seeks to reduce needlessly long sentences by authorizing courts to reconsider sentences that “no longer advance[] the interests of justice.” Id.; ORS 137.218(3)(e). In a reconsideration proceeding, the court considers a non-exclusive list of “post-conviction factors when determining whether to grant the petition[.]” ORS 137.218(3). If the court grants the petition, it may impose a new sentence that is shorter than the original sentence, ORS 137.218(2)(c), but the court is prohibited from imposing a new sentence that is “greater than the original sentence.” ORS 137.218(2)(d). Moreover, no matter what new sentence the court imposes, the defendant must receive credit for time served. ORS 137.218(5).

EXAMPLE: The defendant’s presumptive maximum sentence was 60 months. Based on an unconstitutional, non-sanctified non-enumerated aggravating factor (see Chapter 7), the court imposed a maximum aggravated departure sentence of 120 months. Acknowledging that the departure was imposed on unconstitutional grounds, the parties jointly propose a new sentence; specifically, the 60-month presumptive maximum sentence. By the time of the reconsideration hearing, the defendant already had served 48 months, and had accrued maximum earned time credit of 20% (see Chapter 4). At the reconsideration hearing, the court accepts the petition and imposes the proposed sentence of 60 months. The defendant now has only 12 months left. But the Department of Corrections (DOC) should credit his 20% earned time against the new, 60-month sentence, reducing it to 48 months [60 - (60 x 0.2) = 48]. Because the defendant already had served 48 months, the DOC should promptly release him onto post-prison supervision (see Chapter 4).

There are, however, three limitations to obtaining reconsideration hearings. First and foremost is that without the prosecution’s support, the petition cannot be submitted. ORS 137.218(1)(a). Prosecutors who oppose mass-incarceration policies—that is, “reform prosecutors”—are most likely to join in reconsideration petitions.

Second, if the original sentence is of a mandatory nature, it sets a legal “floor” on the court’s authority to reduce the defendant’s sentence. ORS 137.218(2)(b). But suppose, for example, that the defendant’s sentence has two features: its maximum length is a 144-month aggravated departure, and within that maximum is a 90-month Ballot Measure 11 minimum (see Chapter 4). If the petition calls for reducing the sentence from 144 months to 90 months, the court could approve the petition.

But suppose the defendant’s conviction carries solely a mandatory minimum sentence. The court would be prohibited from reducing the length of that sentence. ORS 137.218(2)(b). But the parties’ petition may call for replacing the defendant’s conviction with an alternative conviction that does not carry a mandatory minimum, which would support imposing a shorter sentence. Id. The court could accept the petition by vacating the original conviction, imposing the alternative conviction, and imposing the shorter sentence. ORS 137.218(2)(c).

The third limitation is that the prosecution must notify the crime victim, “in a trauma informed manner,” of the parties’ petition, must do so not less than 30 days before the reconsideration hearing, and must “provide the victim with access to available victim advocates and other related services.” ORS 137.218(4)(a). “At the hearing * * * the court shall provide an opportunity for victims to make a statement in person, in writing or through a representative.” ORS 137.218(4)(b).

But victim opposition does not “veto” the petition. Certainly the court must consider the victim opposition. See, e.g., ORS 137.218(3)(d) (the court may consider “[t]he safety of the victim”). But the court makes the final decision, based on all factors presented.
