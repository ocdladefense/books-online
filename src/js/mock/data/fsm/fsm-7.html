As explained in Chapters 2 and 3, a sentencing court’s first step in imposing a guidelines sentence is identifying the defendant’s criminal history score and the seriousness ranking of his crime. Having done that, the court then locates the block on the sentencing guidelines grid—see Section 1-2—where the crime seriousness ranking and criminal history score intersect. State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 487, 847 P2d 834 (1993).

“Each grid block contains what is called a ‘presumptive sentence,’” 315 Or at 487, i.e., “the sentence provided in a grid block for a defendant classified in that grid block by the combined effect of the crime seriousness ranking of the current crime of conviction and the offender’s criminal history or a sentence designated as a presumptive sentence by statute.” OAR 213-003-0001(16). A presumptive guidelines sentence is a term of imprisonment, probation, or “straight jail.” A solid black line on the sentencing grid—the “dispositional line,” OAR 213-003-0001(7)—separates the imprisonment grid blocks from the probationary/straight jail grid blocks. Forty-six of the 99 grid blocks are probationary/straight jail. Davis, 315 Or at 487 n 8. The other 53 grid blocks are imprisonment.

This chapter discusses the guidelines provisions authorizing a sentencing court to impose a “[d]eparture” sentence, i.e., a sentence that is “inconsistent with the presumptive sentence for an offender.” OAR 213-003-0001(5). A departure sentence comes in one of two forms. It may be a “dispositional departure,” i.e., “a sentence which imposes probation when the presumptive sentence is prison or prison when the presumptive sentence is probation” or straight jail. OAR 213-003-0001(6). Or, it may be a “durational departure,” i.e., “a sentence which is inconsistent with the presumptive sentence as to [the] term of incarceration, [the] term of supervised probation or [the] number of sanction units which may be imposed as a condition of probation.” OAR 213-003-0001(8).

7-1. OAR 213-008-0001 DEPARTURE SENTENCES.

OAR 213-008-0001 authorizes sentencing courts to depart:

Except as provided in OAR 213-005-0006, the sentencing judge shall impose the presumptive sentence provided by the guidelines unless the judge finds substantial and compelling reasons to impose a departure. If the sentencing judge departs from the presumptive sentence, the judge shall state on the record at the time of sentencing the substantial and compelling reasons for the departure.

7-1.1. Intent of Provision.

A trial court may impose a departure sentence, i.e., a sentence, other than optional probation, which differs from that specified by the defendant’s grid block or a special statute, upon finding “substantial and compelling reasons” to do so. The court bases its substantial and compelling finding on what the guidelines call “departure factors.” State v. Upton, 339 Or 673, 679, 125 P3d 713 (2005). As explained in Chapter 1, and as will be further discussed later in this chapter, under the Sixth Amendment principles, absent a jury waiver or the defendant’s admissions, the jury, not the court, is the trier of fact on aggravated departure factors. However, findings that aggravating factors support “substantial and compelling reasons” to depart are not subject to the Sixth Amendment principles. State v. Upton, 339 Or at 681.

As a practical matter, a party’s motion precedes a sentencing court’s departure decision. The state moves for aggravated departure sentences, and defendants move for mitigated departure sentences. But nothing in OAR 213-008-0001 generally “prohibits a court from * * * deciding to impose a departure sentence without the motion of a party.” State v. Swisher, 116 Or App 129, 131, 840 P2d 1339 (1992), rev den, 315 Or 312 (1993). A sentencing court thus may depart sua sponte, so long as the departure decision complies with state and federal constitutional jury trial requirements, and due process requirements. See Chapter 1.

OAR 213-008-0001 is corollary to four other provisions. The first is ORS 137.080(2), stating:

“Notwithstanding any other provision of law, the consideration of aggravating and mitigating circumstances as to felonies committed on or after November 1, 1989, including the maximum sentence that may be imposed because of aggravating circumstances, shall be in accordance with the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.”

Commentary explains the essential purpose of OAR 213-008-0001 and the corollary provisions:

“[OAR 213-008-0001] reiterates the general principle that presumptive sentences should be applied in most cases. It also introduces the authority of sentencing judges to depart from the presumptive guidelines sentence in unusual cases. That authority * * * was provided by [Or Laws 1987, ch 619, § 6(1)]: ‘The court may impose a sentence outside the presumptive sentence or sentence range . . . for a specific offense if it finds, considering the purposes of this Act, there are substantial and compelling reasons justifying a [departure] from the presumptive sentence.’”

Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 123-24 (1989) (hereafter Implementation Manual).

Similarly, ORS 137.671 states:

“(1) The court may impose a sentence outside the * * * range made presumptive under ORS 137.669 * * * if it finds * * * there are substantial and compelling reasons justifying a [departure] from the presumptive sentence.

“(2) Whenever the court imposes a sentence outside the presumptive sentence [range] it shall set forth the reasons for its decision in the manner required by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.”

Next, OAR 213-002-0001(3)(d) states:

“Subject to the discretion of the sentencing judge to deviate and impose a different sentence in recognition of aggravating and mitigating circumstances, the appropriate punishment for a felony conviction should depend on the seriousness of the crime of conviction when compared to all other crimes and the offender’s criminal history.”

Finally, the 2005 Legislature’s Senate Bill 528 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2005, chapter 463), which took effect on July 7, 2005, regulates jury trials on what the bill generically calls “enhancement facts,” ORS 136.760(2), which term includes aggravating factors. SB 528’s requirements are discussed later in this chapter, and in Chapter 1.

In State v. Wilson, 111 Or App 147, 826 P2d 1010 (1992)—the first case in which the Court of Appeals reversed a departure sentence—the court essentially approved commentary to OAR 213-008-0001 quoted above. The court said,

“[A] departure must further the purposes of the guidelines. The purposes are primarily to punish offenders appropriately and to ensure the security of the public. The premise underlying the guidelines is that the grid block sentence presumptively accomplishes the purposes. It follows that substantial and compelling reasons must be exceptional circumstances that overcome that presumption.”

111 Or App at 149-50 (citations and footnote omitted). See also State v. Parsons, 135 Or App 188, 191, 897 P2d 1197, rev den, 322 Or 168 (1995) (the guidelines create a presumption that a defendant is to be sentenced within his grid block). Thus, the moving party must demonstrate that the departure factors establish “substantial and compelling reasons” to “overcome” the “presumption” that the defendant is to be sentenced within his grid block. Wilson, 111 Or App at 149-50.

7-1.1.1. Types of Departures.

As previously discussed, a departure sentence comes in one of two forms. First, it may be a durational departure, i.e., “a sentence which is inconsistent with the presumptive sentence as to [the] term of incarceration, [the] term of supervised probation or [the] number of sanction units which may be imposed as a condition of probation.” OAR 213-003-0001(8).

With respect to durational departures as to the term of incarceration, the court may impose a sentence which is greater than or less than the presumptive range stated in the defendant’s grid block. Thus, the court may durationally depart to the defendant’s detriment by imposing an exceptionally long incarceration or probationary term, or exceptionally more sanction units. Requirements for exceptionally long terms or exceptionally more units are discussed later in this chapter. But it should be noted that in murder cases in which courts are required to impose mandatory minimum 25-year sentences under ORS 163.115, and ORS 137.700 (for adults) or ORS 137.707 (for juveniles), trials courts are prohibited from imposing aggravated-departure sentences that exceed the 25-year minimum. State v. Ambill, 282 Or App 821, 833, 385 P3d 1110 (2016), rev den, 361 Or 439, 361 Or 524 (2017).

Conversely, the court may depart to the defendant’s benefit by imposing an exceptionally short incarceration or probationary term, or exceptionally fewer sanction units. With few exceptions, there are no limitations on the duration of exceptionally short incarceration or probationary terms. But see State v. Watkins-McKenzie, 286 Or App 569, 400 P3d 1012 (2017), rev dismissed, 363 Or 224 (2018) (where a defendant is sentenced in a single proceeding for multiple repeat property offender (RPO) convictions, and the trial court imposes a mitigated departure on one of the convictions, ORS 137.717(6)(b) prohibits the court from imposing mitigated departures on any other of the RPO convictions). Defendants who are not subject to any of the state’s mandatory prison sentence laws discussed in Chapter 4 may incur mitigated departure sentences that amount to no incarceration or no probation. Chapter 5 discusses the limitations on durational departures as to the duration of probation, or as to the number of sanction units that may be imposed as part of a probationary sentence.

The second type of departure sentence is a dispositional departure, i.e., “a sentence which imposes probation when the presumptive sentence is prison or [which imposes] prison when the presumptive sentence is probation.” OAR 213-003-0001(6). The court may dispositionally depart to the defendant’s detriment by imposing imprisonment when the defendant’s grid-block specifies presumptive sentence/straight jail. Requirements for imposing such sentences are discussed later in this chapter. Conversely, the court may dispositionally depart to the defendant’s benefit by imposing probation or straight jail, when the defendant’s grid-block specifies presumptive imprisonment.

7-1.2. Departure Findings Requirements.

The sentencing court must state on the record its «substantial and compelling reasons» for imposing a departure. ORS 137.671(2); OAR 213-008-0001. This requirement should limit the frequency of unauthorized departures and thus should vindicate the guidelines’ policy that presumptive sentences should be imposed in most cases. Furthermore, to the extent the requirement applies to aggravated departures, it brings the guidelines into compliance with the due process requirement that there be a sufficient record “to make meaningful any appeal that is allowed.” Specht v. Patterson, 386 US 605, 608-10, 87 S Ct 1209, 18 L Ed 2d 326 (1967). See also Chapter 1 (discussing constitutional requirement that in the absence of a jury waiver or defendant’s admissions when pleading to indictment, aggravated departures must be based on jury findings using reasonable-doubt standard).

Under the guidelines, appellate

“review is limited to whether the reasons are substantial and compelling. That is, [the appellate court reviews] the explanation of why the circumstances are so exceptional that imposition of the presumptive sentence would not accomplish the purposes of the guidelines. If the explanation does not demonstrate that, we must remand for resentencing. If it does, we will not disturb the trial court’s exercise of discretion.”

Wilson, 111 Or App at 151 (citation omitted). See also State v. Davilla, 280 Or App 43, 69, 380 P3d 1003 (2016) (trial court erred in relying on Aggravating Factor E, use of a weapon, to impose an aggravated departure on a murder conviction, because the court’s explanation for its reliance on that factor was “insufficient to demonstrate why defendant’s use of a dangerous weapon in this case created circumstances so exceptional that the imposition of a presumptive sentence would not accomplish the purposes of the guidelines”); State v. Morales, 192 Or App 355, 84 P3d 1127 (2004) (where court based departure on fact that defendant was “two-state offender,” in that he had prior conviction from separate state, court erred by failing to explain why having multiple convictions in separate states is qualitatively different from having multiple convictions in same state); State v. Mitchell, 136 Or App 99, 103, 900 P2d 1083 (1995) (court erred by failing to explain how aggravating factor warranted departure); State v. Zavala-Ramos, 116 Or App 220, 224, 840 P2d 1314 (1992) (where court’s findings did “not permit [the] review” required by Wilson, case remanded for resentencing). See also State v. Coronado, 115 Or App 386, 387, 838 P2d 641 (1992) (departure sentence reversed where court failed to make findings but “simply acknowledged that it had listened to the arguments from both sides and then adopted the state’s recommendations”). But see State v. Fults, 343 Or 515, 523, 173 P3d 822 (2007) (holding that where defendant expressly and actively did not object to trial court’s imposition of departure without citing any departure factors, Court of Appeals erred in reviewing unpreserved challenge and in reversing imposition of departure).

In State v. Woodin, 131 Or App 171, 175-76, 883 P2d 1332 (1994), the court explained that “[u]nder the guidelines, the maximum sentence is the presumptive sentence, unless the court finds substantial and compelling reasons to impose a departure”; furthermore, “the court [is] required to make departure findings to justify a sentence in excess of the presumptive sentence.” Although that statement was conventionally accepted as legally accurate when the court decided Woodin, as explained in Chapter 1, under the Sixth Amendment principles first announced in Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 US 466, 120 S Ct 2348, 147 L Ed 2d 435 (2000), and clarified in Blakely v. Washington, 542 US 296, 124 S Ct 2531, 159 L Ed 2d 403 (2004), absent a jury waiver or the defendant’s admissions, the jury, not the court, is the trier of fact on aggravated departure factors.

Furthermore, the fact that aggravating factors were found in compliance with constitutional requirements does not automatically mean that they may be used to depart. As explained, the sentencing court still must make “a determination of whether the factors so proved provide a substantial and compelling reason that justifies imposing a sentence beyond the presumptive range.” Upton, 339 Or at 679. See also ORS 136.785(5) (“[n]otwithstanding the findings made by a jury relating to an enhancement fact, the court is not required to impose an enhanced sentence”). As mentioned in Section 7-1.1, the state, as the party against whom the presumptive-sentence concept is directed, cf. OEC 307(1), has the burden of persuading the court that the aggravating factors make a defendant’s case so “exceptional” that they “overcome” the “presumption” in favor of presumptive sentences. Wilson, 111 Or App at 149-50.

PRACTICE TIP: Aggravating factors are just as susceptible to pretrial challenges, such as demurrers, and to trial challenges, such as motions for judgment of acquittal, as are any other matters on which the jury would be instructed. A substantial body of pre-Blakely case law recognizes various limitations, discussed later in this chapter, on basing aggravated departures on factors found by the appropriate trier of fact—then, the trial court—on the ground that they did not provide substantial and compelling reasons to depart. Those limitations apply post-Blakely every bit as much as they did pre-Blakely, so defense counsel should not be shy about using the limitations to try to fend off aggravated-departure sentences.

Finally, a court may apply the same departure factor to multiple crimes sentenced in the same hearing, but only if the factor properly applies to each crime to which it is applied. See State v. Petrie, 139 Or App 474, 477-78, 912 P2d 913 (1996) (where court relied on multiple factors to impose departure sentences on multiple crimes, but one factor did not apply to one of the crimes, the invalid application of the one departure factor required resentencing).

7-1.3. Procedural Requirements in Imposing Departure Sentences.

Except for the rules of privilege and in the determination of enhancement facts, the evidence code, does not apply in sentencing hearings. OEC 101(3); OEC 101(4)(d). But it also may be that the relevancy requirement of OEC 401 applies in sentencing hearings. See State v. Stevens, 319 Or 573, 580, 879 P2d 162 (1994) (OEC 401 applies in penalty phase of capital cases). Furthermore, an assortment of other statutory requirements do apply.

7-1.3.1. Statutory Requirements in Departure Sentencing.

A court must follow three procedural statutes in imposing a departure sentence. The first is ORS 137.080(1), stating:

“After a plea or verdict of guilty, or after a verdict against the defendant on a plea of former conviction or acquittal, in a case where discretion is conferred upon the court as to the extent of the punishment to be inflicted, the court, upon the suggestion of either party that there are circumstances which may be properly considered in aggravation or mitigation or punishment, may, in its discretion, hear the same summarily at a specified time and upon such notice to the adverse party as it may direct.”

The second procedural statute is ORS 137.090, stating:

“(1) In determining aggravation or mitigation, the court shall consider:

“(a) Any evidence received during the proceeding;

“(b) The presentence report, where one is available; and

“(c) Any other evidence relevant to aggravation or mitigation that the court finds trustworthy and reliable.

“(2) In determining mitigation, the court may consider:

“(a) Evidence regarding the defendant’s status as a servicemember as defined in ORS 135.881.

“(b) Whether the defendant committed the crime while under duress, compulsion, direction or pressure from another person who has:

“(A) Committed acts of domestic violence, as defined in ORS 135.230, against the defendant;

“(B) Committed acts of abuse as a family or household member of the defendant, as those terms are defined in ORS 107.705, against the defendant; or

“(C) Used force, intimidation, fraud or coercion to cause the defendant to engage, or attempt to engage, in a commercial sex act.

“(3) When a witness is so sick or infirm as to be unable to attend, the deposition of the witness may be taken out of court at such time and place, and upon such notice to the adverse party, and before such person authorized to take depositions, as the court directs.”

This statute’s most substantial limitation is its subsection (1)(c). Under it, evidence that would be used to depart is admissible only if the court finds that it is “trustworthy and reliable.” Id. As explained in Section 1-7.7, this appears to be a codification of constitutional confrontation clause requirements. Although the evidence code’s hearsay provisions do not apply in sentencing hearings, OEC 101(4)(d), state or federal constitutional confrontation requirements (or both) may apply.

The next procedural statute is ORS 137.100, stating:

“If the defendant consents thereto, the defendant may be examined as a witness in relation to the circumstances which are alleged to justify aggravation or mitigation of the punishment; but if the defendant gives testimony at the request of the defendant, then the defendant must submit to be examined generally by the adverse party.”

Finally, as explained in Chapter 1, aggravated-departure factors generally must be submitted to the jury using the reasonable-doubt standard. Also as explained in that section, it is possible that mitigated-departure factors may be submitted the jury (although the reasonable-doubt standard does not apply to proof of mitigating factors).

As an historical matter, before the decision in Blakely v. Washington, trial courts found aggravating factors on their own, and without receiving valid jury waivers. In doing so, trial courts frequently based their findings of aggravating factors on uncharged and unproven crimes, notwithstanding that

“[i]n the guidelines system, the seriousness of criminal conduct is determined by the crime of conviction. Consequently, a departure sentence is not appropriate for elements of alleged offender behavior not within the definition of the offense of conviction. * * *

“For example, if an offender is convicted of ORS 164.405 Robbery II, the sentence should not be aggravated beyond the upper limit of the presumptive sentence range because the offender was actually armed with a deadly weapon at the time of the robbery. This aspect of the crime was not captured in the crime of conviction since it is an element of ORS 164.415 Robbery I. Since this aspect of the crime was not captured in the conviction, it should not later be used to impose an aggravated sentence for the conviction of the lesser-included offense.”

Implementation Manual at 125 (emphasis added).

This commentary explains that if a defendant is convicted only of a lesser crime, the sentencing court may not depart because the defendant actually committed a greater crime. This is consistent with pre-guidelines case law. See State v. Brown, 44 Or App 597, 602 n 4, 606 P2d 678 (1980) (in banc) (“we have serious reservations about the propriety of allowing a prosecutor in a summary sentencing proceeding to put on a case implicating a defendant in a serious criminal activity for which the defendant has never been charged or tried”). Cf. State v. Plourd, 125 Or App 238, 242-43, 864 P2d 1367 (1993) (a conviction may not be used in a criminal history score calculation until after the defendant has been sentenced for the crime). It also is consistent with “real-facts doctrine” found in other state’s guidelines systems on which Oregon’s was based, e.g., Washington’s, which generally prohibit basing aggravated-departure factors on “[f]acts that establish the elements of a more serious crime or additional crimes[.]” Rev Code Wash § 9.94A.370(2).

§ 7-1.3.2. Constitutional Requirements in Departure Sentencing—Notice.

As explained in Chapter 1, due process requires that where “a new finding of fact that was not an ingredient of the offense charged” would serve as the basis for a more severe sentence, the defendant “‘is entitled to the full panoply of the relevant protections which due process guarantees in state criminal proceedings,’” notice included. Specht, 386 US at 608-09 (quoting Gerchman v. Maroney, 355 F2d 302, 312 (3d Cir 1966)). See State v. Frinell, 290 Or App 296, 304, 414 P3d 430 (2018) (trial court violated ORS 136.765 by imposing aggravated departure in absence of state’s failure to give notice of intent to rely on enhancement facts). The test for adequacy of notice is whether the notice was “‘reasonably calculated, under all the circumstances, to apprise interested parties of the pendency of the action and afford them an opportunity to present their objections.’” Dusenbery v. United States, 534 US 161, 168, 122 S Ct 694, 151 L Ed 2d 597 (2002) (quoting Mullane v. Central Hanover Bank & Trust Co., 339 US 306, 314, 70 S Ct 652, 95 L Ed 865 (1950)). See Lankford v. Idaho, 500 US 110, 111 S Ct 1723, 114 L Ed 2d 173 (1991) (reversing trial court’s imposition of aggravated sentence (of death) on defendant who had not been given notice that such sentence might be imposed); United States v. Jackson, 32 F3d 1101 (7th Cir 1994) (defendant has right to advance notice of sentencing enhancements that may be applied to him); State v. Hardy, 489 A2d 508, 512 (Me 1985) (the purpose of the notice “requirement is to provide the defendant with an opportunity to dispute inaccuracies” in the evidence offered against him); Supplement Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 20 (1992) (Advisory #3, Sept. 25, 1991).

State v. Dearmitt, 321 Or App 628, 517 P3d 368 (2022), the prosecution had properly alleged and proved a single aggravating factor (i.e., Aggravating Factor D). The sentencing court relied on that factor, plus additional factors that the prosecution had not alleged. The Court of Appeals “conclude[d] that the resentencing court may have improperly relied on enhancement facts that were not pleaded by the state to support its finding for an upward departure, and therefore remand[ed] for resentencing.” 321 Or App at 629. The court explained:

“It is not clear whether the court found that the single appropriately pleaded enhancement fact of persistent involvement itself amounted to a substantial and compelling reason to depart. Accordingly, we cannot address whether a court may consider unpleaded facts in determining the extent of the departure after it makes the necessary finding that persistent involvement alone amounts to a substantial and compelling reason to depart, because it is unclear whether the court in this case did, in fact, make such a finding.”

Id. at 632.

State v. Dearmitt, 321 Or App 628, 517 P3d 368 (2022), the prosecution had properly alleged and proved a single aggravating factor (i.e., Aggravating Factor D). The sentencing court relied on that factor, plus additional factors that the prosecution had not alleged. An open question is whether notice of enhancement facts, such as aggravated-departure factors, must be given either by indictment or by a district attorney’s information followed by a preliminary hearing. That question is discussed in Chapter 1. Otherwise, ORS 136.765(2) and 136.790 authorize the state to give notice more informally, through a document called a “notice of intent to prove enhancement facts.”

7-1.3.3. Constitutional Requirements in Departure Sentencing—Jury Trial.

The due process requirements announced in Specht must be read in conjunction with the state constitutional jury trial requirements in State v. Wedge, 293 Or 598, 652 P2d 773 (1982), and with the federal constitutional jury trial requirements announced in Apprendi v. New Jersey. Those requirements apply to aggravating factors, for they fit ORS 136.760(2)’s definition of “enhancement fact” (although the Wedge requirements apply only to offense-specific enhancement facts). Chapter 1 discusses the requirements.

7-1.3.4. Constitutional Requirements in Departure Sentencing—Mental State.

Defense counsel should argue that certain aggravating factors carry mental state requirements, and, therefore, that the prosecution must prove the factor’s mental state requirements. For further information about this, see the PRACTICE TIP found 13 paragraphs into Section 1-7.2 of Chapter 1.

7-2. OAR 213-013-0010 MINIMUM CONTENTS OF PRESENTENCE REPORTS.

PSIs frequently recommend departures, so often serve as the starting point for trial court findings that aggravated-departure factors constitute substantial and compelling reasons to support a court’s departure decision. OAR 213-013-0010 states the content requirements of PSIs:

Except as provided by section (9), each presentence report prepared for an offender to be sentenced for one or more felonies committed on or after November 1, 1989, shall at a minimum include the following information:

(1) A summary of the factual circumstances of the crime or crimes of conviction and an appropriate classification of each crime of conviction on the Crime Seriousness Scale (Division 17). If the crime of conviction is subclassified in Division 18 or 19, the presentence report shall state the factual circumstances that justify the proposed subclassification.

(2) A listing of all prior adult felony and Class A misdemeanor convictions and all prior juvenile adjudications and an assessment of the appropriate classification of the criminal history on the Criminal History Scale pursuant to OAR 213-004-0006 to 213-004-0013.

(3) An analysis of the disposition that is most likely to reduce the defendant’s criminal conduct and why such disposition would have the desired effect.

(4) An assessment of the availability to the defendant of any relevant programs or treatment, both in and out of custody, whether provided by the Department or another entity.

(5) A proposed grid block classification for each crime of conviction and the presumptive sentence for each crime of conviction.

(a) If the proposed grid block classification is a grid block above the dispositional line, the presentence report shall state the presumptive prison term range and the presumptive duration of post-prison supervision;

(b) If the proposed grid block classification is Grid Block 8-G, 8-H or 8-I, the presentence report shall state whether the offender is eligible for an optional probationary sentence. If the offender is eligible, the presentence report may include a recommendation that an optional probationary sentence be imposed with a further recommendation for the appropriate conditions of probation designed to reduce future criminal conduct.

(c) If the proposed grid block classification is a grid block below the dispositional line, the presentence report shall provide the following information:

(A) The presumptive term of probation;

(B) The maximum number of sanction units that may be imposed and the number of sanction units that may be used to impose jail time as part of the probationary sentence;

(C) A recommendation for the appropriate conditions of probation including both custody and non-custody conditions; and

(D) Any other information relevant to the imposition of a presumptive sentence as provided by these rules.

(6) A victim statement as required by ORS 137.530(2).

(7) A recommendation as to whether a departure from the guidelines is appropriate. If a recommendation is made, the presentence report shall indicate the aggravating or mitigating factors upon which the departure recommendation is made. Such recommendations shall be consistent with the requirements for departures as defined by OAR 213-008-0001 to 213-008-0007.

(8) Any additional information as provided upon request of the sentencing judge.

(9) The sentencing judge may waive the requirement for any information necessary to establish the presumptive sentence if that information has been made part of an accepted plea agreement.

7-2.1. Intent of Provision.

Whereas OAR 213-013-0010 sets out the minimum-content requirements of PSI›s, ORS 144.791, which describes the circumstances in which sentencing courts order PSI›s, which states:

“(1) When a person is convicted of a felony, including a felony sexual offense, the sentencing court may order a presentence report upon its own motion or upon the request of the district attorney or the defendant.

“(2) The sentencing court shall order a presentence report if the defendant is convicted of a felony sexual offense unless:

“(a) The defendant, as part of the same prosecution, is convicted of aggravated murder;

“(b) The felony sexual offense requires the imposition of a mandatory minimum prison sentence and no departure is sought by the court, district attorney or defendant; or

“(c) The felony sexual offense requires imposition of a presumptive prison sentence and no departure is sought by the court, district attorney or defendant.

“(3) The Department of Corrections shall:

“(a) Require that a presentence report provide an analysis of what disposition is most likely to reduce the offender’s criminal conduct, explain why that disposition would have that effect and provide an assessment of the availability to the offender of any relevant programs or treatment in or out of custody, whether provided by the department or another entity;

“(b) Determine what additional information must be included in the presentence report; and

“(c) Establish a uniform presentence report form.”

Id.

ORS 144.791(3) was created in 2005. Or Laws 2005, ch 473. ORS 144.791(3)(a) should change Department of Corrections (DOC) “culture” in the creation of PSI’s. No longer could PSI’s serve as little more than instruments in support of the imposition of the longest sentences possible. Instead they would be used to meet the evidence-based program requirements of ORS 181.515 and 181.525, for they would be used to assure “the availability” of “relevant programs or treatment” that would be “most likely to reduce the offender’s criminal conduct[.]” ORS 144.791(3)(a). Indeed, to the extent a PSI fails to comply with ORS 144.791(3) requirements, defense counsel could move to strike it. ORS 137.079(5); ORS 144.791(3)(a).

In State v. Nygaard, 303 Or App 793, 466 P3d 692 (2020), the defendant was convicted of two felony sexual offense counts, one carrying a mandatory minimum sentence, the other a Guidelines sentence. The trial court imposed an aggravated-departure sentence on the count carrying the mandatory minimum—which was error, 303 Or App at 801-02—and imposed a presumptive sentence on the count sentenced under the Guidelines. Id. at 795. The trial court did not order a presentence investigation report (PSI). Id.

On appeal, the defendant raised the unpreserved claim that the trial court erred by failing to order a PSI. The defendant was right. The PSI statute states that the court “shall order a presentence report,” ORS 144.791(2), unless the crime of conviction carries a mandatory minimum or a presumptive prison sentence “and no departure is sought by the court, district attorney or defendant.” ORS 144.791(2)(b) and (c) (emphasis added). Although no one sought a departure on the count sentenced under the Guidelines, the trial court imposed a departure without first ordering a PSI.

The state confessed error, which the Court of Appeals accepted. Nygaard, 303 Or App at 802. On remand, the court said, “The [trial] court will have an opportunity to order such a report upon remand either upon motion (under ORS 144.791(1)) or if a departure sentence is sought on any count that would trigger the mandatory requirement for a report under ORS 144.791(2).” Id. at 802-03 (emphasis added).

In State v. Phillips, 312 Or App 149, 152, 490 P3d 180 (2021), the defendant sought “a downward departure,” “meaning that the statute * * * obligate[d] preparation of a PSI[.]” A PSI was ordered but was not received in time for sentencing. Rather than set over sentencing to await receipt of the PSI, the court sentenced the defendant anyway. Relying on the decades old case of State v. Biles, 287 Or 63, 597 P2d 808 (1979), the Court of Appeals remanded for resentencing. The court explained:

“The Biles court’s decision hinged upon the legislature’s recognition of a societal interest in the protection of ‘responsible sentencing.’ That conclusion stemmed from the interaction of three statutes that set forth the trial court’s discretion to deviate from a plea recommendation or deviate from a PSI recommendation, and the requirement that the trial court place the reasons for the sentence on record. * * * We are thus bound by the Supreme Court’s decision in Biles, and we therefore conclude that the trial court erred in imposing a sentence without a PSI.”

Phillips, 312 Or App at 152 (emphasis added; quoting Biles, 287 Or at 69).

Moreover, although the defendant plausibly failed to object to the lack of a PSI, the Phillips court concluded that “neither the state [sic] nor defendant’s failure to sufficiently object can dispense with the need to comply with a statutory requirement that protects a societal interest. Rather, as Biles instructs, a defendant cannot waive or even negotiate that societal interest away.” 312 Or App at 152 n 2.

7-2.2. Victim Participation in Preparation of PSIs.

ORS 137.530(2), which requires that the victim of the crime be given the opportunity to participate in the PSI’s preparation, states:

“Whenever a presentence report is made, the preparer of the report shall make a reasonable effort to contact the victim and obtain a statement describing the effect of the defendant’s offense upon the victim. If the victim is under 18 years of age, the preparer shall obtain the consent of the victim’s parent or guardian before contacting victim. The preparer of the report shall include the statement of the victim in the presentence investigation report. If the preparer is unable to contact the victim or if the victim declines to make a statement, the preparer shall report that the preparer was unable to contact the victim after making reasonable efforts to do so, or, if contact was made with the victim, that the victim declined to make a statement for purposes of this section. Before taking a statement from the victim, the preparer of the report shall inform the victim that the statement will be made available to the defendant and the defendant’s attorney prior to sentencing as required under ORS 137.079.”

Id.

§ 7-2.3. Accuracy Demands for PSIs.

PSI’s must be accurate, not only because the criminal code says so, ORS 137.079(5), but also because due process demands that a defendant’s sentence be based on accurate information. United States v. Columbus, 881 F2d 785, 787 (9th Cir 1989) (“[v]iolations of a defendant’s due process rights occur when a court relies on materially false or unreliable information in sentencing”). ORS 137.079(1) requires that the PSI be served on the parties at least five judicial days before sentencing. If after reviewing the PSI defense counsel determines it contains inaccuracies, counsel should object to the inaccuracy in writing. If the sentencing court refuses to correct the PSI, ORS 137.079(5)(d), the refusal may be the subject of an appeal. See Section 9-6.

PRACTICE TIP: With some frequency, PSI writers violate the five-judicial-day requirement of ORS 137.079(1). Depending on its egregiousness, such a statutory violation may violate the due process requirement of notice. See Section 1-7.4. In appropriate cases where the state violates the five-day requirement—e.g., where the writer serves the PSI on the day of sentencing—counsel should move to strike the PSI. Alternatively, counsel should move to set over sentencing to comply with the five-day requirement. As the Seventh Circuit explained in United States v. Jackson, the trial court’s denial of these alternative motions could be reversible error.

When aggravating factors would be based on facts found in a PSI, Apprendi principles apply to the facts if their use as aggravating factors “requires further inferences[.]” State v. Spencer, 203 Or App 463, 465, 125 P3d 849 (2005). This is true even if the defendant requested the PSI and otherwise did not object to its contents. 203 Or at 465-66.

7-3. OAR 213-013-0011 FORMAT FOR PRESENTENCE REPORTS.

The format for all presentence reports for offenders convicted of a felony committed on or after November 1, 1989, shall be as provided by the Department.

7-3.1. Purpose of Provision.

The guidelines’ commentary explains: “The specific format for presentence reports will continue to be established by the Department of Corrections. The State Sentencing Guidelines Board adopted rule OAR [213-013-0011] to ensure that no other rule of the Guidelines Board is construed to restrict or modify the Department’s authority in this area.” Implementation Manual at 78.

7-4. OAR 213-008-0002 DEPARTURE FACTORS.

As previously discussed, a sentencing court may depart from the presumptive sentence only if it has “substantial and compelling reasons” to do so. Davis, 315 Or at 487-88; ORS 137.671(1); OAR 213-008-0001. OAR 213-008-0002 sets out a purportedly non-exclusive list of departure factors on which a court may rely, and specifies limitations on the use of all departure factors:

(1) Subject to the provisions of sections (2) and (3) of this rule, the following nonexclusive list of mitigating and aggravating factors may be considered in determining whether substantial and compelling reasons for a departure exist:

(a) Mitigating factors:

(A) The victim was an aggressor or participant in the criminal conduct associated with the crime of conviction.

(B) The defendant acted under duress or compulsion (not sufficient as a complete defense).

(C) The defendant’s mental capacity was diminished (excluding diminished capacity due to voluntary drug or alcohol abuse).

(D) The offense was principally accomplished by another and the defendant exhibited extreme caution or concern for the victim.

(E) The offender played a minor or passive role in the crime.

(F) The offender cooperated with the state with respect to the current crime of conviction or any other criminal conduct by the offender or other person. The offender’s refusal to cooperate with the state shall not be considered an aggravating factor.

(G) The degree of harm or loss attributed to the current crime of conviction was significantly less than typical for such an offense.

(H) The offender’s criminal history indicates that the offender lived conviction-free within the community for a significant period of time preceding his or her current crime of conviction.

(I) The offender is amenable to treatment and an appropriate treatment program is available to which the offender can be admitted within a reasonable period of time; the treatment program is likely to be more effective than the presumptive prison term in reducing the risk of offender recidivism; and the probation sentence will serve community safety interests by promoting offender reformation.

(J) The offender’s status as a servicemember as defined in ORS 135.881.

(b) Aggravating factors:

(A) Deliberate cruelty to victim.

(B) The offender knew or had reason to know of the victim’s particular vulnerability, such as the extreme youth, age, disability or ill health of victim, which increased the harm or threat of harm caused by the criminal conduct.

(C) Threat of or actual violence toward a witness or victim.

(D) Persistent involvement in similar offenses or repetitive assaults. This factor may be cited when consecutive sentences are imposed only if the persistent involvement in similar offenses or repetitive assaults is unrelated to the current offense.

(E) Use of a weapon in the commission of the offense.

(F) The offense involved a violation of public trust or professional responsibility.

(G) The offense involved multiple victims or incidents. This factor may not be cited when it is captured in a consecutive sentence.

(H) The crime was part of an organized criminal operation.

(I) The offense resulted in a permanent injury to the victim.

(J) The degree of harm or loss attributed to the current crime of conviction was significantly greater than typical for such an offense.

(K) The offense was motivated entirely or in part by the race, color, religion, ethnicity, national origin or sexual orientation of the victim.

(L) Disproportionate impact (for Theft I under ORS 164.055, and Aggravated Theft I under ORS 164.057).

(2) If a factual aspect of a crime is a statutory element of the crime or is used to subclassify the crime on the Crime Seriousness Scale, that aspect of the current crime of conviction may be used as an aggravating or mitigating factor only if the criminal conduct constituting that aspect of the current crime of conviction is significantly different from the usual criminal conduct captured by the aspect of the crime.

(3) Any aspect of the current crime of conviction which serves as a necessary element of a statutory mandatory sentence may not be used as an aggravating factor if that aspect is also used to impose the mandatory sentence.

(4) As used in this rule, “disproportionate impact” means:

(a) The offender caused damage to property during the commission of the theft and the cost to restore the damaged property to the condition the property was in immediately before the theft is more than three times the value of the property that was the subject of the theft; or

(b) The theft of the property creates a hazard to public health or safety or the environment.

7-4.1. Introduction to Provision.

OAR 213-008-0001 says, “If the sentencing judge departs from the presumptive sentence, the judge shall state on the record at the time of sentencing the substantial and compelling reasons for the departure.” The lists of mitigating and, purportedly, aggravating factors are not exclusive, so a court may rely on nonenumerated factors to impose a departure. See, e.g., State v. Orsi, 108 Or App 176, 180, 813 P2d 82 (1991) (“The sentencing court has the discretion to decide to depart on the basis of mitigating or aggravating factors other than those set out in OAR [213-008-0002]. That rule provides a ‘nonexclusive’ list of mitigating and aggravating factors.”).

Although the Supreme Court has upheld the validity of nonenumerated departure factors as against separation of powers challenges. State v. Speedis, 350 Or 424, 432-33, 256 P3d 1061 (2011). Moreover, owing to the legislature’s approval of the guidelines, see, e.g., State v. Langdon, 330 Or 72, 74, 999 P2d 1127 (2000); Or Laws 2003, ch 453, § 2, and to legislative control over guidelines amendments, ORS 137.667(2), the creation of non-enumerated factors is not subject to the APA’s rule-making requirements. Accord State v. McFerrin, 289 Or App 96, 99-100 n 3, 408 P3d 263 (2017), rev dismissed, 365 Or 194 (2019).

the validity of nonenumerated aggravating factors remains subject to certain other constitutional attacks. See Section 7-4.3.13. 1The challenges are based on defendants’ constitutional “fair warning” rights, which rights the state does not enjoy. Consequently, the state is prohibited from making fair-warning objections the nonenumerated mitigating factors.

In any event, use of a nonenumerated factor that merely duplicates the guidelines’ purposes and principles (discussed in Chapter 1) is prohibited. State v Clark, 113 Or App 692, 695, 833 P2d 1314 (1992).

“If the court relies on a factor listed in OAR 213-008-0002(1), its reasons for applying the rule must show that the case before it [substantially and compellingly justifies the departure]. The court must provide the same kind of explanation if it relies on factors not expressed in OAR 213-008-0002.” Wilson, 111 Or App at 150-51. As explained in Section 7-1.1, the state, as the party against whom the presumptive-sentence concept is directed, cf. OEC 307(1), has the burden of persuading the court that the aggravating factors make a defendant’s case so “exceptional” that they “overcome” the “presumption” in favor of presumptive sentences. Wilson, 111 Or App at 149-50. A court needs only one valid factor to depart. Id. at 151 n 4. A court may apply the same departure factor to multiple crimes sentenced in the same hearing, but only if the factor properly applies to each crime to which it is applied. Petrie, 139 Or App at 477-78.

This page shows most aggravating factors in OAR 213-008-0002(1)(b). The 2021 Legislature’s Senate Bill 186 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2021, chapter 76) made permanent a temporary rule the Criminal Justice Commission approved in 2020, which amended Aggravating Factor K. The factor now states:''

(K) The offense was motivated entirely or in part by the race, color, religion, ethnicity, national origin, gender identity (as defined in ORS 166.155), or sexual orientation of the victim.

OAR 213-008-0002(2) and (3) limit the use of departure factors in certain cases. Those limitations are examined later in this chapter.

On the other hand, defense counsel who provides adequate and effective assistance investigates mitigating evidence in effort to limit the length of the defendant’s sentence. For example, in Maxfield v. Cain, 322 Or App 405, 520 P3d 890 (2022), the Court of Appeals explained that competent counsel should

“have used the information about [Maxfield’s] mental impairments to contextualize [his] problems in the juvenile system, which was an aggravating fact emphasized by the state at the original sentencing hearing.

“The original sentencing court heard nothing about [Maxfield’s] childhood difficulties and the impact of his early drug exposure on his mental health and behavioral regulation. That unpresented mitigation information, combined with [his] youth and relatively minimal criminal history up until his month-long spree of robberies, when weighed against the aggravating circumstances in this case—petitioner’s five armed robberies where nobody was ultimately physically injured and [his] poor record in the juvenile system—creates more than a mere possibility that counsel’s deficient performance affected the outcome.

“Reversed and remanded with instructions to grant post-conviction relief on [Maxfield’s] claim that trial counsel was inadequate under Article I, section 11, for failing to adequately investigate and present mitigation evidence[.]”

322 Or App at 416 (citation omitted).

7-4.2. Mitigating Factors Generally.

Most reported departure-sentence decisions involve the use of aggravating factors rather than mitigating factors. Indeed, reported aggravated-sentence cases outnumber reported mitigated-sentence cases by at least a 10-1 ratio.

Mitigating factors do not affect a defendant’s due process and jury trial rights. Apprendi, 530 US at 490 n 16; Gratzer v. Mahoney, 397 F3d 686 (9th Cir 2005). Accord State v. Crescencio-Paz, 196 Or App 655, 103 P3d 666 (2004) (trial court erred in ruling that under state constitutional principles, state must disprove the criteria that “lock” the ORS 137.712 “exit door,” which allows a defendant to avoid ORS 137.700 or ORS 137.707 sentencing). Therefore, the jury trial and pleading requirements discussed in Chapter 1 do not apply to mitigating factors.

Owing to the dearth of reported mitigated-departure cases, whenever possible this manual discusses guidelines’ commentary to explain the mitigating factors. Also, many of the enumerated mitigating factors are derived from the Washington and Minnesota guidelines. Owing to that, “Washington and Minnesota guidelines sentencing appellate decisions function as guidance in interpreting the Oregon provision,” Wilson, 111 Or App at 151 n 4, so defense counsel may wish to consult those states’ case law for guidance in applying the corollary Oregon provisions.

Practitioners should be aware that for a defendant subject to the RPO statute, ORS 137.717, and who committed his crime or crimes on or after January 1, 2012, the court may impose a mitigated departure of less than the presumptive RPO sentence only if either the parties stipulate to it, ORS 137.717(6), or, in addition to finding one or more mitigating factors, the court makes all six of the findings specified by ORS 137.717(6)(a)-(d).2 The presumptive sentences specified by ORS 475.925 and 475.930 are mandatory minimums in that under no circumstances may courts impose mitigated departures of less than the prescribed presumptive sentences. The same is true for defendants subject to the repeat controlled-substance offender sentencing provisions of ORS 475.933.

Practitioners also should use mitigating factors, particularly when prosecution offers aggravating factors in the sentencing hearing. In Bacon v. Cain, 327 Or App 673, ___ P3d ___ (2023), the petitioner, Bacon, was convicted of drug conspiracy crimes, and in the open hearing, the prosecution offered and the trial court accepted aggravating factors. Unfortunately, Bacon's trial attorney offered no mitigating factors. On post-conviction, Bacon's post-conviction attorney claimed that trial counsel's failure to offer mitigating factors provided inadequate assistance. Or Const, Art I, § 11. To prove this, Bacon's post-conviction attorney presented the missing mitigating evidence. The Court of Appeals accepted that the post-conviction attorney's use of mitigating evidence established "substantial and compelling reasons," ORS 137.750(1), "allowing petitioner eligibility for alternative incarceration programs." Cain, 327 Or App at 678. The court "[r]eversed and remanded with instructions to grant post-conviction relief on petitioner’s claim that trial counsel was inadequate under Article I, section 11, for failing to present mitigating evidence at sentencing[.]" Id. at 679.

7-4.2.1. Mitigating Factor A: Victim Was the Aggressor.

Commentary does not provide much guidance about the use of this factor. It says only that the factor may be used if “[t]he victim was aggressor or participant.” Implementation Manual at 128. But presumably it could be used in cases where the defendant and victim engaged in “combat by agreement,” during which the defendant used more force than was reasonably necessary to win the fight. ORS 161.215(3). Similarly, it could be used in cases where the defendant, although acting in self-defense, illegally used deadly force. ORS 161.219.

7-4.2.2. Mitigating Factor B: Defendant Under Duress.

Commentary says this factor applies where “[t]he defendant acted under duress or compulsion under circumstances which do not constitute a complete defense.” Implementation Manual at 128. It explains the factor with the following example:

“Two of three partners in drug distribution operation agree to kill an individual whom they believe is a police informant. The two accomplices threaten the third partner’s wife and children if he does not participate in the murder. This form of coercion may not be sufficient to establish a defense under ORS 161.200 (Choice of Evils) or other legal defense. It may, however, be cited by the sentencing court as a mitigating factor.”

Implementation Manual at 128.

7-4.2.3. Mitigating Factor C: Defendant’s Mental Capacity Was Diminished.

Commentary explains that a sentencing court may use this factor if the defendant’s “mental capacity was diminished by other than [voluntary] drug or alcohol abuse. * * * The Guidelines Board explicitly excluded diminished capacity due to drug or alcohol abuse as a mitigating factor when the offender makes the voluntary decision to consume the substance leading to his or her incapacitation.” Implementation Manual at 128. See State v. Hopkins, 112 Or App 458, 460-61, 829 P2d 97 (1992) (defendant’s voluntary intoxication did not support a mitigated sentence).

For example, consider a defendant suffering from fetal alcohol syndrome. His suffering from the syndrome constitutes involuntary alcohol abuse, because it is the product of his mother’s alcohol abuse, rather than his own. If the syndrome impairs the defendant’s mental state, but not so severely as to support, for example, a guilty except insane verdict, the sentencing court could rely on this factor to mitigate the defendant’s sentence.

Or consider a servicemember who suffers from military service-connected post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and who is charged with alcohol-related vehicular assault. It is well established that alcohol abuse is a symptom of PTSD. See, e.g., William B. Brown, From War Zones to Jail: Veteran Reintegration Problems, Justice Policy Journal, Spring 2011, at 2 (Abstract). This fact supports the argument that the veteran defendant’s alcohol abuse actually was involuntary and, therefore, that Mitigating Factor C applies in his sentencing. See also Section 7-4.2.10 (discussing Porter v. McCollum, 558 US 30, 130 S Ct 447, 175 L Ed 2d 398 (2009)).

7-4.2.4. Mitigating Factor D: Crime Principally Accomplished by Another.

Commentary says this factor applies where “[t]he offense was principally accomplished by another and the defendant exhibited extra caution or concern for the victim.” Implementation Manual at 128. It explains the factor with the following example:

“Two offenders plan to burglarize a home. They both agree not to carry any weapons or to use physical force on the homeowner. During the commission of the crime, however, they are surprised by the homeowner and the offender’s accomplice pulls out a concealed knife and stabs the homeowner. While the accomplice flees, the offender applies the necessary first-aid to save the victim’s life. Such concern for the safety of the victim may be cited as a mitigating factor when sentencing under these guidelines.”

Id.

7-4.2.5. Mitigating Factor E: Defendant Played a Minor Role.

Commentary explains this factor:

“The offender was paid fifty dollars to deliver a package to a third party. The offender believes that the package contained one pound of marijuana and had never before participated in any such activity. In fact, the package contained a pound of heroin[;] he sentencing court may conclude that the offender’s role in the drug distribution scheme was minor and therefore, a mitigated sentence might properly be imposed.”

Id.

7-4.2.6. Mitigating Factor F: Defendant Cooperative.

The court may use this factor where “[t]he offender cooperated with the state as to [the] current crime of conviction or other criminal conduct by [the] offender or [another] person.” Id. It typically would be used where, as part of a plea agreement, the defendant agrees to testify against a co-defendant. See also State v. Hays, 155 Or App 41, 50, 964 P2d 1042, rev den, 328 Or 40 (1998), cert den, 527 US 1006 (1999) (in criminally negligent homicide prosecution, trial court’s reliance on Factor F affirmed where defendant “cooperated with the police after [his son’s] death”). But because a court may not aggravate a defendant’s sentence for refusing to cooperate in his prosecution, see, e.g., State v. Fitzgibbon, 114 Or App 581, 586-87, 836 P2d 154 (1992), “[t]he offender’s refusal to cooperate * * * shall not be considered an aggravating factor.” OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(F).

7-4.2.7. Mitigating Factor G: Harm or Loss Significantly Lower than Usual.

Commentary explains Mitigating Factor G—”[t]he degree of harm or loss attributed to the current crime of conviction was significantly less than typical for such an offense”—with two examples. The first states:

“If an unarmed offender is convicted of Burglary I for stealing a bicycle tire from an unlocked garage which is attached to a residence, the offender will be classified in Crime Category 8 if the residence was occupied at the time of the offense. The sentencing judge may determine that the offender’s conduct was significantly less serious than the usual Crime Category 8 (Burglary I) in which the offender breaks into the victim’s actual living quarters to steal much more valuable property or to commit a physical assault. In such a case, the sentencing judge could properly depart from the guidelines.”

Implementation Manual at 129.

The second example states:

“An offender is convicted of Arson I for burning a yellow ribbon as a protest at a public forum. The burning material caused only minor damage to several floor tiles and the overall threat to human life and safety was minimal. The sentencing court may determine that a departure would be appropriate based on this mitigating factor.”

Id.

In State v. Rhoades, 210 Or App 280, 149 P3d 1259 (2006), the defendant was convicted of a third felony sex offense carrying a presumptive sentence of life imprisonment under ORS 137.719. The trial court relied on two mitigating factors to impose a mitigated departure of 60 months’ imprisonment. One was Mitigating Factor G. Its factual basis was that the 15-year-old victim of the crimes—statutory rape and sodomy—actually consented. The Court of Appeals held that because ORS 163.315(1) declares that persons under the age of 18 are “incapable” of consent, the statute declares that, as a matter of public policy, a “consent” given by a person under 18 has no legal effect. The trial court’s factual basis for using Mitigating Factor G was invalid as a matter of law. To the extent the court based imposition of the mitigated departure on Factor G, it erred. Rhoades, 210 Or App at 285-88.

7-4.2.8. Mitigating Factor H: Significant Crime-Free Period.

This factor has a somewhat unusual origin. As originally drafted, the guidelines contained a “decay” provision “by which certain prior convictions were removed from a criminal history when followed by a specified period free of further conviction. The Guidelines Board repealed this provision to ensure that a defendant’s criminal history classification reflected the actual criminal record.” Implementation Manual at 129. Nevertheless, the Board decided “that a significant period without criminal activity [would] be an appropriate mitigating factor.” Id. It explained the use of the factor as follows:

“The offender has a record of prior person felony convictions for offenses committed during his early twenties. At the age of 62, the offender is convicted of ORS 165.065 Negotiating Bad Checks for $12,000 (a Crime Category 5 offense). The sentencing judge may properly cite the 40-year period of apparent law abiding conduct as a mitigating factor. If this factor is cited to support a mitigated sentence, the degree of mitigation should be limited to a reduction in the sentence enhancement which would otherwise be attributed to the offender’s full criminal history.”

Implementation Manual at 129.

State v. Lucas, 113 Or App 12, 830 P2d 601, rev den, 314 Or 176 (1992) discusses Mitigating Factor H. There, the defendant’s criminal history score was I. The state argued that the “factor was intended to apply only to a person with previous criminal convictions who had apparently rehabilitated himself.” 113 Or App at 15 (citing Implementation Manual at 129). The Court of Appeals rejected that argument. It said a sentencing court may use the factor if it explains why the defendant’s commendable criminal history “is so exceptional that imposition of the presumptive sentence, which is based in part on the criminal history, would not accomplish the guidelines’ purpose.” Lucas, 113 Or App at 15. See also Hays, 155 Or App at 50-51 (affirming trial court’s reliance on Factor H, where the defendant’s criminal history score was I).

7-4.2.9. Mitigating Factor I: Defendant Amenable to Treatment.

This factor was modeled after OAR 213-005-0006(1), which is part of the test for determining whether a defendant in grid block 8-G, 8-H, or 8-I is eligible for optional probation. See Chapter 1. Because the factor was modeled after the optional-probation test, optional probation case law presumably applies to Mitigating Factor I. Thus, the phrase “appropriate treatment program” does not mean something “superficial” such as the defendant’s “brain and society at large[.]” State v. Schuh, 112 Or App 362, 366, 829 P2d 1040, rev den, 314 Or 176 (1992).

In one key respect, the mitigating factor is different from its optional probation counterpart. Except in situations such as Schuh, sentencing court’s decision to impose optional probation generally is not reviewable on appeal. ORS 138.115(6)(a)(B). Conversely, because the Mitigating Factor I is used for dispositional departure purposes, the state may appeal a sentencing court’s decision to use it. ORS 138.115(6)(b).

Mitigating Factor I benefits defendants who are in the three optional probation grid blocks, but who otherwise are legally ineligible for optional probation. OAR 213-005-0006(2). The mitigating factor will facilitate such a defendant’s chances at avoiding imprisonment and securing a probationary sentence.

In State v. Waage, 160 Or App 156, 981 P2d 333 (1999), the Court of Appeals affirmed the sentencing court’s reliance on Mitigating Factor I to impose dispositional departure terms of probation on convictions for using a child in a display of sexually explicit conduct, and for attempted second-degree rape. The court explained that the factor’s requirement that “‘an appropriate treatment program * * * to which the offender can be admitted within a reasonable period of time’ * * * in this context carries a connotation of possibility, or at the most, probability; it does not carry a connotation of certainty.” 160 Or App at 165.

Section 7-4.2.10. Mitigating Factor J: Military Service.

The 2013 Legislature unanimously approved Senate Bill 124 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2013, chapter 331), which explicitly establishes military service as a mitigating factor in criminal sentencings. The legislature did so by amending ORS 137.090, which now reads in pertinent part:

“(2) In determining mitigation, the court may consider:

“(a) Evidence regarding the defendant’s status as a servicemember as defined in ORS 135.881.”

(Emphasis added.)

ORS 135.881 defines “servicemember” as follows:

“(4) ‘Servicemember’ means a person who:

“(a) Is a member of the Armed Forces of the United States, the reserve components of the Armed Forces of the United States or the National Guard; or

“(b)(A) Served as a member of the Armed Forces of the United States, the reserve components of the Armed Forces of the United States or the National Guard; and

“(B) Received an honorable discharge, a general discharge under honorable conditions or a discharge under other than honorable conditions.

The governor signed SB 124 on June 6, 2013. By virtue of the bill’s emergency clause, Or Laws 2013, ch 331, § 3, the bill took effect on that date. SB 124’s substantive amendments “apply to sentences imposed on or after the effective date of this 2013 Act for crimes committed before, on or after the effective date of this 2013 Act.” Or Laws 2013, ch 331, § 2.

Acting under its statutory mandate, ORS 137.667, the Criminal Justice Commission amended OAR 213-008-0002 to expressly include the SB 124-created mitigating factor. That provision now states in pertinent part “The offender’s status as a servicemember as defined in ORS 135.881.” OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(J).

Case law confirms that such special treatment under the law is constitutional. For example, in Regan v. Taxation with Representation, 461 US 540, 550-51, 103 S Ct 1997, 76 L Ed 2d 129 (1983), the Court explained:

“Veterans have been obliged to drop their own affairs to take up the burdens of the nation, subjecting themselves to the mental and physical hazards as well as the economic and family detriments which are peculiar to military service and which do not exist in normal civil life. Our country has a longstanding policy of compensating veterans for their past contributions by providing them with numerous advantages. This policy has always been deemed to be legitimate.” (Internal quotations and footnote omitted.)

Similarly, in MacPherson v. Dept. of Admin. Services, 340 Or 117, 130 P3d 308 (2006), the court refuted the plaintiff’s claim that the legislature created a “closed class” in violation of Article I, section 20, of the Oregon Constitution. The court explained “that the foregoing refutation of plaintiffs’ theory must be correct. Were it not, plaintiffs’ theory would mean that the legislature would be precluded from enacting a law benefitting, for example, Vietnam veterans or Gulf War veterans, both closed classes.” 340 Or App 131.

Military service as a mitigating factor is particularly appropriate when the defendant suffers from a service-connected mental condition. Melissa Hamilton, Reinvigorating Actus Reus: The Case for Involuntary Actions by Veterans with Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, 16 Berkeley J Crim L 340 (2011). For example, in Porter v. McCollum, 558 US 30, 130 S Ct 447, 175 L Ed 2d 398 (2009) (per curiam), the Supreme Court concluded that trial counsel provided ineffective assistance of counsel in his client’s capital trial by failing to present mitigating evidence of, among other things, his military service. Echoing the principle stated in Regan, the Court explained:

“Our Nation has a long tradition of according leniency to veterans in recognition of their service, especially for those who fought on the front lines as [petitioner] did. Moreover, the relevance of [petitioner’s] extensive combat experience is not only that he served honorably under extreme hardship and gruesome conditions, but also that the jury might find mitigating the intense stress and mental and emotional toll that combat took on [petitioner].”

588 US at 43-44 (internal citations omitted). See also Kimbrough v. United States, 552 US 85, 110, 128 S Ct 558, 169 L Ed 2d 481 (2007) (affirming trial court’s imposition of a sentence more lenient than the guidelines range, which trial court based in part on fact that defendant “served in combat during Operation Desert Storm and received an honorable discharge from the Marine Corps”); United States v. Canova, 412 F3d 331 (2d Cir 2005) (defendant convicted of offenses involving multi-million-dollar Medicare fraud; imposition of downward departure sentence based, in part, on six-year military service, not error); United States v. Pipich, 688 F Supp 191 (D Md 1988) (where defendant was convicted of mail theft, his extraordinary military record warranted departure to probation). Cf. United States v. McCaleb, 908 F2d 176 (7th Cir 1990) (departure for military service might be warranted under some circumstances); United States v. Neil, 903 F2d 564 (8th Cir 1990) (same).

Consistent with the decisions in cases such as Kimbrough, Canova, Pipich, McCaleb, and Neil, defense counsel may seek to base mitigation on the so-called “good soldier defense,” which is found in the Uniform Code of Military Justice’s Military Rules of Evidence (MRE). The MRE’s “very closely follow the federal rules of evidence,” but diverge in that they allow “a defendant to put on good military character evidence * * * to include traits like law-abidingness, that they’re well liked, dependable, trustworthy, hard working, respectful, [etc.].” Ore. Legislative Information System, Senate Committee on Judiciary, SB 124, Mar. 6, 2013 (statement of Amy M. Lammers, member, Military & Veterans Law Section, Ore. State Bar). This evidence is admissible both in “the case in chief,” and “for sentencing purposes.” Id.

Without explicitly citing the “good soldier defense,” the Court of Appeals effectively applied it—as well as Mitigating Factor J—in the post-conviction case of Pike v. Cain, 303 Or App 624, 465 P3d 277, rev den, 367 Or 75 (2020).

There, the petitioner (now deceased) was an active duty soldier stationed at Joint Base Lewis/McChord in Washington State, but he was off duty and spending time in Oregon. He got into an altercation, which ended with him pleading guilty to second-degree assault. He faced a 70-month “Measure 11” prison sentence. But he was eligible for application of the “exit door,” see Chapter 4, which would allow him to be sentenced to a lesser, Guidelines term, possibly including a probationary sentence. At the sentencing hearing, trial counsel called petitioner’s platoon sergeant who “testified in support of petitioner’s case for probation. The sergeant stated that petitioner was a ‘good soldier,’ who ‘excelled above his peers,’ and who, prior to the assault, was up for a promotion to sergeant.” 303 Or App at 629 (emphasis added).

Unfortunately, trial counsel otherwise failed to investigate and present evidence of petitioner’s “good soldier” service in the military. Relatedly, counsel failed to rely on Mitigating Factor J to seek a mitigated departure from Measure 11 sentencing. Owing to those failures, the petitioner sought post-conviction relief. The post-conviction court denied relief, but the Court of Appeals reversed. It

“conclude[d] that * * * petitioner is entitled to a new sentencing hearing because his trial counsel’s decision not to further develop the evidence of petitioner’s military service, and his failure to argue for a [mitigated] departure under * * * OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(J) based on petitioner’s servicemember status, did not comport with constitutional standards.”

Pike, 303 Or App at 627.

The court then “reverse[d] and remand[ed] with instructions to grant post-conviction relief on petitioner’s claim that trial counsel was inadequate and ineffective for failing to investigate and develop further evidence pertaining to petitioner’s military service.” Id.

Terri Wood & James Gardner, Still At War: A Guide for Defenders, Prosecutors, & Judges Dealing with Oregon’s Veteran Defendant Crisis 12-17 (Jesse Wm. Barton ed., OCDLA/VDRC 2017) provide additional information about ORS 137.090(2) and Mitigating Factor J.

Finally, defense counsel should be aware of the possibility of representing veteran defendants discharge were received bad conduct or dishonorable discharges, or who served in the militaries of other nations. Those veteran defendants would be ineligible for application of ORS 137.090(2) and Mitigating Factor J, because by definition, they are not “servicemembers.” ORS 135.881(4).

Nevertheless, case law, such as that discussed above, does not limit its application to “servicemembers.” Consequently, in cases involving defendants who are veterans, but are not “servicemembers,” defense counsel may rely on the above case law as authority for mitigation.

Section 7-4.2.12. Mitigating Factor: Crime Committed Under Duress, Compulsion, Direction, or Pressure by Defendant’s Domestic Abuser or Pimp.

The 2017 Legislature’s House Bill 3176 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2017, chapter 123) created a new mitigating factor, by amending ORS 137.090 as follows:

“(2) In determining mitigation, the court may consider:

“ * * * * *

“(b) Whether the defendant committed the crime while under duress, compulsion, direction or pressure from another person who has:

§ 7-4.2.13. Nonenumerated Mitigating Factors.

Two reported Oregon decisions involve the use of judicially created (nonenumerated) mitigating factors. The most recent of them is State v. Rhoades. The trial court relied on two mitigating factors to impose a 60-month departure for crimes carrying a presumptive sentence of life. One of them was Mitigating Factor G. As explained in Section 7-4.2.7, the Court of Appeals deemed invalid the trial court’s use of that factor.

The other factor was judicially created: “that two of the previous sentences on which the presumptive life sentence was based arose from a single course of criminal sexual conduct with the same victim, within a short time frame, and within a limited geographical area.” Rhoades, 210 Or App at 284. The Court of Appeals held that under the circumstances of the instant case, the trial court validly relied on this judicially created factor. Id. at 288-89.

The other case involving a judicially created mitigating factor is State v. Hopkins. There, the victims admitted the defendant into their dwelling. Shortly afterwards the defendant became abusive, and the victims ordered him to leave. Instead he remained unlawfully and assaulted the victims. The jury convicted the defendant of a level 9 first-degree burglary (by physical injury). See Section 2-1.4. The sentencing court departed for various reasons, including “that the incident arose from a domestic dispute and that it was not a common-law burglary.” Hopkins, 112 Or App at 460. Because the court did not explain “why those factors were so exceptional,” the Court of Appeals reversed and remanded for resentencing. Id. at 461.

Other more recently created mitigating factors include: “immigration consequences to defendant.” State v. Arevalo, 301 Or App 328, 330, 456 P3d 671 (2019). Moreover, in the context of state constitutional proportionality challenges, see Chapter 1, the court must consider the defendant’s background, including his behavioral impairments. State v. Ryan, 361 Or 602, 621, 396 P3d 1012 (2017). See also OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(C) (diminished mental capacity). Depending on the resolution of an open question, the court also may be required to consider the subjective experiences of the defendant while incarcerated—a qualitative assessment. State v. Cook, 297 Or App 862, 872-74, 445 P3d 343 (2019). In cases where the factual record would support these proportionality considerations, defense counsel should feel to assert them as mitigating factors.

Owing to the dearth of reported Oregon decisions involving judicially created mitigating factors, defense counsel may wish to review out-of-state case law, including federal case law, for assistance in fashioning judicially created mitigating factors. For example, in Pepper v. United States, 562 US 476, 480-81, 131 S Ct 1229, 1236, 179 L Ed 2d 196 (2011), the defendant was resentenced following a successful appeal. The Court affirmed his post-sentence rehabilitation as a proper mitigation factor.

In Gall v. In United States v. Briggs, 623 F3d 724 (9th Cir 2010), the defendant sold drugs to an undercover federal agent. The agent convinced the defendant to rob a fictional “stash house” that the agent insisted contained vast quantities of drugs. Id. at 726. The defendant pled guilty. At sentencing, he raised entrapment as a type of mitigating factor. He claimed that the agent convinced him to commit a crime carrying greater penalties than he would have otherwise committed. Id. at 729. The trial court rejected the defendant’s mitigating factor and sentenced defendant “at the bottom of his guidelines range.” Id. at 727. On appeal, the defendant alleged governmental misconduct in that the government inflated the drug quantity supposedly in the “stash-house” to increase his sentence. Id. at 729-30. Although the Ninth Circuit expressed concern about the circumstances, it concluded that when the defendant pled guilty to the charge, he acquiesced to any alleged governmental misconduct by admitting to possessing the quantity of drugs alleged. The court also noted that at sentencing the defendant did not present any evidence that he was “incapable of consummating” the robbery. Id. at 730. The court affirmed the trial court’s decision (and remanded the case for findings regarding the defendant’s alleged possession of a deadly weapon in the course of the conspiracy), id. at 731; nevertheless, the court left open the possibility that defendants who did not admit to the charges could use this entrapment-based mitigating factor. Id. at 730.

In United States v. Leon, 341 F3d 928, 931-33 (9th Cir 2003), the appellate court affirmed the trial court’s imposition of a mitigated departure based on the fact that the defendant was the only available caregiver for his wife who was in poor health, was unable to entirely provide for herself, and was a suicide risk if separated from her husband. See also United States v. Gray, 453 F3d 1323 (11th Cir 2006) (sentence of less than half the bottom of guidelines range upheld as reasonable in child pornography case where mitigation was based on defendant’s age, prior minimal criminal record, and medical condition); United States v. Baker, 445 F3d 987 (8th Cir 2006) (upholding lower non-guidelines sentence in child pornography case based on defendant’s youth, lack of criminal history, religious background, and history of employment and higher education).

Finally, Michael R. Levine, “171 Easy Mitigating Factors” (Jan. 1, 2019)3 provides an extensive review of federal case law approving mitigating factors.

Moreover, it is important to note that by passage of House Bill 2462 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2019, chapter 86), the legislature has expressly prohibited a certain type of aggravating factor. The bill’s section 1(4), which is codified at ORS 135.985(4), states: “The fact that a defendant is a servicemember may not be used as an aggravating factor in determining the defendant’s sentence.” (Emphasis added.)

HB 2462 had no special effective date, so it took effect on January 1, 2020. ORS 171.022. Consequently, this express prohibition applies in all types of sentencing hearings held after the bill’s effective date.

It is important to note that HB 2462’s section 1(1), which is codified at ORS 135.985(1), more broadly defines the term “servicemember” than does ORS 135.881(4), which is used by the military service mitigating factor (codified at ORS 137.090(2)(a) and at OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(J)). ORS 135.881(4) excludes from its definition veterans who incurred bad conduct or dishonorable discharges. But the HB 2462 definition states: “As used in this section, ‘servicemember’ means a person who is a member, or who served as a member, of the Armed Forces of the United States, the reserve components of the Armed Forces of the United States or the National Guard.” ORS 135.985(1).

As seen, regardless of discharge type, the ORS 135.985(1) definition excludes no veterans. Therefore, the definition includes all veterans, including those with bad conduct and dishonorable discharges.

As mentioned, the definition of “servicemember” in ORS 135.881(4) applies when a defendant relies on Mitigating Factor J, whereas the definition of “servicemember” in ORS 135.985(1) applies when the prosecution seeks and the sentencing court imposes sentence aggravation. The opening phrase of ORS 135.985(1)’s definition—“[a]s used in this section”4—expressly establishes that its “servicemember” definition applies to all of the bill’s components. Therefore, the ORS 135.985(1) definition controls in determining the application of ORS 135.985(4). While ORS 135.881(4) prohibits a veteran who incurred a bad conduct or dishonorable discharge from using the military service mitigating factor, ORS 135.985(4) prohibits the prosecution, and the sentencing court, from using that same veteran’s military service as an aggravating factor at sentencing. This subsection is important for two reasons.

First, the military service mitigating factor (codified at ORS 137.090(2)(a) and OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(J)) was created by Senate Bill 124 (2013). In its original form—Senate Bill 124 (2013) (introduced)—the bill could have been construed as allowing a court to treat a defendant’s military service as an aggravating factor. House amendments to the bill eliminated that possibility. See Owens v. Maass, 323 Or 430, 442-44, 918 P2d 808 (1996) (history of bill drafts are persuasive in determining legislative intent). Owing to those amendments, SB 124 prohibits using a veteran defendant’s military service as an aggravating factor, if he were a “servicemember” under ORS 135.881(4)’s narrower definition of the term. On the other hand, if a veteran defendant’s discharge type excluded him from the ORS 135.881(4) definition, SB 124 might have allowed using his military service as an aggravating factor.

But that possibility ended when HB 2462 took effect on January 1, 2020. Owing to the bill’s broader definition of “servicemember,” ORS 135.985(1), which includes veterans of all discharge types, ORS 135.985(4) universally prohibits using a veteran defendant’s military service as an aggravating factor.

The second reason ORS 135.985(4) is important stems from the fact that some prosecutors have sought to use servicemember status of an aggravating factor, or at least to oppose mitigation. For example, in petitioner Pike’s criminal prosecution, State v. Pike, Marion County Case No. 14C4403502 (the precursor case to Pike v. Cain), the prosecutor—without objection from defense counsel—effectively opposed mitigation by relying on Pike’s training and experience in the military culture and total institution.5 The prosecutor argued:

“What is of concern to the State is he’s also not a regular civilian. He’s been trained and, based on his actions against the victim, used that training to assault and almost kill a civilian at a public family function.

“ * * * * *

“ * * * What I’m saying is that in the situation he found himself in, he used that training and experience that he has to almost hurt someone to the point where they could have been killed. And to me that is significant.”

(Emphasis added.)6

ORS 135.985(4) should make clear that in cases sentenced on or after HB 2462’s effective date, this type of argument is forbidden.

7-4.3. Aggravating Factors Generally.

Most of the enumerated aggravating factors are discussed in one or more appellate decisions. Also, sentencing courts have used—often successfully—aggravating factors of their own creation, i.e., nonenumerated aggravating factors. The constitutional validity of nonenumerated aggravating factors is discussed in Section 7-4.3.13. Moreover, it is important to reiterate that state and federal constitutions, discussed in Chapter 1, significantly limit the use of aggravating factors, including nonenumerated aggravating factors.

§ 7-4.3.1. Aggravating Factor A: Deliberate Cruelty.

Various cases address this factor. See, e.g., State v. Gunter, 227 Or App 152, 205 P3d 84, on recons, 229 Or App 714, 215 P3d 875 (2009). Moreover, commentary explains that it was modeled after the State of Washington’s guidelines’ deliberate cruelty factor. Implementation Manual at 130. For guidance on the use of the Aggravating Factor A, defense counsel may wish to review cases applying the Washington factor.

Also, in State v. Smitherman, 200 Or App 383, 114 P3d 540 (2005) (per curiam), the court held that the factor is subject to the Apprendi rule. Therefore, the Court of Appeals held, the trial court committed reversible error by basing a departure on a non-juried Aggravating Factor A.

7-4.3.2. Aggravating Factor B: Victim’s Particular Vulnerability—History of Factor.'''

Aggravating factor B was modeled after a parole matrix factor. Implementation Manual at 130. The commentary says, “This factor should only be cited * * * when the court determines that the offender’s knowledge of or disregard for the victim’s vulnerability increased the potential harm attributed to the offender’s criminal conduct.” Implementation Manual at 130. The commentary explains this limitation with an example of an “offender [who] embezzles $58,000 from his employer who has been confined to a wheelchair for the last twenty years.” Id. Under these circumstances, the commentary says, “The victim’s disability should not be cited as an aggravating factor if it did not play a role in the commission of the offense.” Id.

Aggravating Factor B is similar to ORS 137.085, which states:

“When a court sentences a defendant convicted of any crime involving a physical or sexual assault, the court shall give consideration to a victim’s particular vulnerability to injury in such case, due to the victim’s youth, advanced age or physical disability. Such particular vulnerability of the victim is a fact enhancing the seriousness of any assault, and the court shall consider it as such in imposing the sentence within the limits otherwise provided by law.”

7-4.3.2.1. Aggravating Factor B: Use of the Factor.

Various appellate decisions address this factor’s use. In State v. Enemesio, 233 Or App 156, 225 P3d 115 (2010), rev den, 348 Or 414 (2011), the defendant was convicted of coercion. The trial court relied on Aggravating Factor B to depart, because, as a victim of a prior rape, the victim was a member of a particularly vulnerable class. The Court of Appeals reversed. There was no evidence that the specific case’s victim was particularly vulnerable. There only was evidence that persons of the victim’s class are particularly vulnerable, which is not enough.

In State v. Lykins, 357 Or 145, 163, 348 P3d 231 (2015), a witness-tampering case, the court relied on text, context, and legislative history to construe Aggravating Factor B, and to conclude that the case’s witness is not a “victim” for purposes of the factor. Consequently, the court concluded “that the trial court erred in imposing a departure sentence for defendant’s conviction of that offense based on OAR 213-008-0002(1)(b)(B).” 357 Or at 163.

In State v. Fitzgerald, 117 Or App 152, 843 P2d 964 (1992), the defendant was convicted of first-degree burglary of an occupied dwelling. The sentencing court held that the victim was particularly vulnerable, because he was asleep in the dwelling. The Court of Appeals rejected use of the factor, because the sentencing court did not explain how the victim’s sleeping presence made him particularly vulnerable. 117 Or App at 155.

In Boxberger v. Board of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 123 Or App 339, 858 P2d 1356, rev den, 318 Or 97 (1993), the defendant was convicted of various counts of first-degree sodomy under the victim-under-12 version of that crime. ORS 163.405(1)(b). The board used the parole matrix’s particular vulnerability factor to aggravate the defendant’s sentence on grounds that the victims were under five years of age. The defendant argued that the factor could not be used, because the victims’ age was an element of the crimes.7 In rejecting that argument the Court of Appeals said, “[T]he victims were not simply under 12 years of age, they were substantially younger than 12.” Boxberger, 123 Or App at 341.

In State v. Ambrose, 117 Or App 298, 844 P2d 227 (1992), the defendant was convicted of the first-degree sexual abuse of a victim who was both mentally retarded and paraplegic. The defendant was a trusted friend of the victim, and he abused her in her own home. Under these facts, the Court of Appeals held, the factor was properly used. 117 Or App at 301.

In State v. Newman, 113 Or App 102, 832 P2d 47, rev den, 314 Or 176 (1992), a first-degree sodomy case, the court upheld use of this factor. The court reasoned that the victim “was particularly vulnerable because he was the defendant’s adopted child and had previously been sexually abused and because defendant had physically threatened him.” 113 Or App at 105.

7-4.3.2.2. Aggravating Factor B: Proof and Vicarious Use.

Aggravating Factor B says, “The offender knew or had reason to know of the victim’s particular vulnerability[.]” OAR 213-008-0020(1)(b)(B) (emphasis added). Thus, the factor has an offender-specific knowledge component. Because the knowledge component is offender-specific, its use is not subject to the constitutional requirements of Wedge. But its use is subject to the Sixth Amendment requirements discussed in Chapter 1. Moreover, because it is subject to those requirements and is offender-specific, it may be tried only in the “sentencing” phase of a defendant’s trial (if there is one). ORS 136.773.

Moreover, there is vicarious liability for an enhancement fact is allowed only when the fact is offense specific; conversely, if the fact is offender specific, vicarious liability is prohibited. Wedge, 293 Or at 604. Owing to Aggravating Factor B’s offender-specific knowledge component, defense counsel should object to using it on a vicarious-liability basis.

EXAMPLE: Defendants Able and Baker are convicted of robbing a convenience store. Able entered the store while Baker remained in the car as the “wheelman.” When Able entered the store, he learned that the store clerk is blind and in some manner took advantage of that knowledge. Baker knew nothing about the clerk’s vulnerability until days after the fact, and otherwise did nothing to take advantage of his tardy knowledge. Because Able knew of and took advantage of the clerk’s vulnerability, this factor could be used to aggravate his sentence. But the factor could not be used vicariously, to aggravate Baker’s sentence.

7-4.3.3. Aggravating Factor C: Threat of or Actual Violence.

Commentary says this factor may be used “when the sentencing court determines that the offender threatened or actually caused physical harm to the victim or any other witness as a means to keep the individual from testifying or to force the individual to commit perjury.” Implementation Manual at 130. However, the factor’s use is not limited to situations where, after the crime of conviction, the defendant committed witness tampering or attempted to suborn perjury. It may also be used in situations where, during the current crime of conviction, the defendant threatened or committed violence. Wilson, 111 Or App at 153 n 7 (where defendant was charged with promoting prostitution, factor properly used where he threatened victim in order to induce her to return to prostitution).

In State v. Lee, 110 Or App 528, 823 P2d 445, rev den, 313 Or 211 (1992), the defendant initially intended to shoplift, but the crime escalated into a second-degree robbery. The Court of Appeals held that because the defendant’s “use of force” was “significantly different from the usual conduct that the presumptive sentence for robbery II is intended to punish,” this factor was properly used. 110 Or App at 531.

Finally, the Apprendi rule applies to Aggravating Factor C. State v. Robertson, 203 Or App 18, 20, 125 P3d 20 (2005). A court errs if, without a valid jury waiver or the defendant’s admission, it bases a departure on a non-juried Aggravating Factor C.

7-4.3.4. Aggravating Factor D: Persistent Involvement in Similar Offenses or Repetitive Assaults.

Aggravating Factor D—”[p]ersistent involvement in similar offenses or repetitive assaults”—may be the most frequently used aggravating factor. It certainly is the most complex of the enumerated factors.

7-4.3.4.1. Aggravating Factor D: Persistence and Similarity.

The defendant’s criminal record serves as part of the factual basis for this factor. The sentencing court may base the factor both on the defendant’s prior convictions, as well as on his record of unproven crimes if he has admitted them. State v. Barrett, 134 Or App 162, 165, 894 P2d 1183, rev den, 321 Or 340 (1995); Wilson, 111 Or App at 153. Indeed, the Apprendi rule, discussed in Chapter 1, prohibits basing this factor on unproven crimes that the defendant has not admitted.

WARNING: Established case law holds that in order for Aggravating Factor D to apply, the defendant must have at least two prior convictions or admissions to unproven crimes. But in State v. Cam, 255 Or App 1, 11, 296 P3d 578, adh’d to as modified on recons, 265 Or App 146, 300 P3d 208, rev den, 354 Or 148 (2013), the Court of Appeals refused to consider a plain-error claim that the trial court erred in failing to instruct the jury that in considering Aggravating Factor D, “it could not consider uncharged misconduct.”8 More recently, in a rather curious opinion, the Court of Appeals relied on State v. Cam to reject the defendant’s claim that the trial court erred in basing Aggravating Factor D on one prior conviction, and on one unproven criminal allegation the defendant had not admitted. State v. Summerlyn, 316 Or App 230, 231, 501 P3d 1076 (2021). Then, near the end of the opinion, the court acknowledged, “It may be that the Supreme Court will one day adopt * * * defendant’s view that proof of multiple prior convictions is required to establish [Aggravating Factor D] would ultimately be proved correct.” 316 Or App at 234.

Under its present formulation, the Apprendi rule does not apply to the criminal-record component of this factor. Moreover, State v. Bray, 342 Or 711, 160 P3d 983 (2007) explains that the prior convictions to which the current crime would be compared are offender specific, so the Wedge rule does not apply to this factor. But the factor’s “similar offenses” and “repetitive assaults” components are characteristics of the defendant’s criminal record, and the Apprendi rule does apply to the characteristic. 342 Or at 724. But because the characteristics pertain to the defendant’s criminal record, the characteristics are offender-specific and may be tried only in the “sentencing” phase of a defendant’s trial (if there is one). ORS 136.773.

“To determine whether a defendant has shown ‘persistent involvement in similar offenses or repetitive assaults * * * unrelated to the current offense,’ the [trier of fact] may consider similar offenses committed after the current offense for which sentence is being imposed.” State v. Ceballos, 162 Or App 477, 481, 986 P2d 680 (1999), rev den, 330 Or 252 (2000) (citation omitted). Although the appellate courts have not completely explained what constitutes persistence, several cases say that a single prior conviction does not demonstrate persistence. Ambrose, 117 Or App at 300 (for sexual abuse conviction, one prior conviction for public indecency did not demonstrate persistence); State v. Rodriguez, 113 Or App 696, 699, 833 P2d 1343 (1992) (in sentencing of a sex abuse conviction, one prior sex abuse conviction did not establish persistence); Clark, 113 Or App 692, 694, 833 P2d 1314 (1992) (for multiple convictions stemming from “crime spree,” one set of convictions from similar spree did not demonstrate persistence). Furthermore, only convictions unrelated to the current crime of conviction may be used in the persistence determination. State v. Nelson, 119 Or App 84, 87-88, 849 P2d 1147 (1993).

In State v. Rodriguez, 122 Or App 117, 856 P2d 339 (1993) (in banc), the court held that although there was nearly an eight and one-half-year hiatus between the defendant’s current crime of conviction and his most recent similar crime of conviction, the sentencing court properly used the factor. This was because the defendant was incarcerated for four and one-half years’ worth of the hiatus period. 122 Or App at 119. By reverse implication, Rodriguez may hold that a lengthy period of crime-free years in the community prohibits use of Aggravating Factor D.

In State v. Lennon, 348 Or 148, 229 P 3d 589 (2010), the Supreme Court put to rest the “separate malevolent quality” requirement for use of Aggravating Factor D. The Court of Appeals had created that requirement nearly 20 years beforehand, as a means of avoiding the anomaly of using a defendant’s criminal record, which already was used to set his presumptive sentence, then being used a second time to depart above his presumptive sentence. State v. Kennedy, 113 Or App 134, 831 P2d 712 (1992). The Lennon court explained, “In the wake of Bray, the Court of Appeals recognized that requiring a ‘separate malevolent quality’ to be inferred from a defendant’s criminal history placed a ‘gloss’ on the operative departure standard that had no place in the analysis.” Lennon, 348 Or at 156–57 (citing inter alia State v. Schenewerk, 217 Or App 243, 247, 174 P 3d 1117 (2007), rev den, 344 Or 671 (2008)). The Supreme Court concluded that it “agree[d] with the Court of Appeals’ reconsideration of its former ‘separate malevolent quality’ analysis in the context of the ‘persistent involvement in similar offenses’ ground for a departure sentence.” Id. at 157.

In State v. Williams, 238 Or App 9, 12-15, 241 P3d 1170 (2010), the Court of Appeals relied on Lennon to affirm the trial court’s use of the Aggravating Factor D in sentencing the defendant for first-degree robbery. The factor was based on the defendant’s record of robbery and felon in possession of a weapon convictions incurred 10 years before the instant offense. During that interim, the defendant had two probation violations, two parole violations, and three arrests. In view of this record, the Court of Appeals explained, it found “no legitimate debate” that Aggravating Factor D applied. 238 Or App at 14-15. See also State v. Skaggs, 239 Or App 13, 17, 244 P3d 380 (2010) (upholding use of Aggravating Factor D, despite 14-year gap between theft convictions).

Despite the courts’ abandonment of the “separate malevolent quality” requirement, use of Aggravating Factor D still requires something more than a merely toting up the number of “similar” convictions in a defendant’s criminal record. The nonenumerated “failure to deter” factor, which is discussed in Section 7-4.3.13.8, was at issue in Lennon. With respect to Aggravating Factor D, three aspects of Lennon are significant. First, the defendant’s criminal record was “much more extensive” than his criminal-history score reflected, 348 Or at 150; thus, the anomaly that the Court of Appeals sought to avoid, when in Kennedy it created the “separate malevolent quality” requirement, was not really at issue. Second, the court’s prior decision addressing Aggravating Factor D, State v. Bray, played a large role in the court’s analysis of the failure-to-deter factor, and for good reason: the failure-to-deter factor is significantly redundant to Aggravating Factor D. Finally, with respect to the failure-to-deter factor, the court held, “A finding that those past criminal sanctions have not deterred a defendant from committing further crimes thus requires something beyond a conclusion that a defendant has one or more criminal convictions in his past.” Lennon, 348 Or at 157 (boldface added; court’s italics).

Because the failure-to-deter factor draws its force from and is heavily redundant to Aggravating Factor D, any limitations on the use of the failure-to-deter factor also should apply to the use of Aggravating Factor D.

In State v. Cornelius, 112 Or App 98, 827 P2d 937, rev den, 314 Or 176 (1992), the court attempted to explain the meaning of the term “similar.” The sentencing court imposed a departure sentence on a conviction for possession of a weapon by an inmate, “which arose in the context of an escape[.]” 112 Or App at 101. The defendant’s criminal history “included escape and failure to appear convictions.” Id. The Court of Appeals rejected the defendant’s argument that his prior convictions were not similar, “because they did not involve weapons.” Id. The prior convictions were similar, the court explained, because like the current crime of conviction, the priors were “‘governmental types of offenses’” establishing “a pattern of committing crimes to evade criminal sanctions.” Id. See also State v. Britt, 136 Or App 398, 401-02, 901 P2d 960, rev den, 322 Or 360 (1995) (where defendant was convicted of drug possession and was intoxicated when arrested, sentencing court properly used defendant’s prior drunk driving convictions as basis for Aggravating Factor D).

§ 7-4.3.4.3. Aggravating Factor D: Consecutive Sentences.

Aggravating Factor D contains an express limitation on its use: “This factor may be cited when consecutive sentences are imposed only if the persistent involvement in similar offenses or repetitive assaults is unrelated to the current offense.” OAR 213-008-0002(1)(b)(D). This limitation seems to say that when the court imposes consecutive sentences, it may not use current crimes of conviction as its factual basis for Aggravating Factor D. Nelson, 119 Or App at 88 (dictum) provides a somewhat cryptic discussion of this limitation.

7-4.3.5. Aggravating Factor E: Use of Weapon.

Use of this factor was at issue in State v. Hudson, 115 Or App 301, 303, 839 P2d 721 (1992), rev den, 315 Or 442 (1993). There, the Court of Appeals held that where the use of a weapon was neither a factual aspect of an element of the crime of conviction, nor a factual aspect of a statutory mandatory sentence, the sentencing court properly used the factor. 115 Or App at 304-005. See also Sections 7-4.4.1 (discussing OAR 213-008-0002(2)).

But this factor may not be used when the defendant used the weapon for a non-assaultive or non-threatening purpose. In State v. Perez, 186 Or App 122, 129, 61 P3d 945 (2003), the defendant “used a steak knife to cut methamphetamine.” The court held that basing a departure on the defendant’s use of the knife was error, for the legislature would not have “intended to authorize departure sentences for ‘use’ of a weapon as something other than a weapon.” 186 Or App at 130.

§ 7-4.3.6. Aggravating Factor F: Violation of Public Trust or Professional Responsibility.

Commentary explains the application of this factor:

“A local government employee who has been appointed to serve as a trustee on behalf of a number of elderly clients embezzles funds from the trust accounts. This offender has violated not only this trust relationship with the victims, but has also abused the authority vested in him as a public servant. Consequently, an aggravated sentence would be appropriate.”

Implementation Manual at 131.

Thus, the factor contemplates situations where the defendant has violated a trust conferred upon him by virtue of his position as a public employee or official. Similarly, it contemplates situations where the defendant has violated a trust conferred upon him by virtue of his membership in an organization regulated by rules of professional responsibility (e.g., the state bar’s Rules of Professional Conduct).

The Apprendi rule applies to Aggravating Factor F. Bray, 197 Or App at 19–20; Sawatzky, 195 Or App at 162. A trial court commits reversible error if, without a valid jury waiver or the defendant’s admission, it bases this factor on non-juried findings.

Although Aggravating Factor F does not authorize using a private trust violation as grounds for imposing an aggravated departure, it is possible for a sentencing court to expand its scope into non-traditional areas. But the trier of fact must be instructed in a manner that “define[s] the scope of the ‘trust’ and explain[s] why it is properly viewed as ‘public’ rather than private. State v. Reid, 140 Or App 293, 296, 915 P2d 453 (1996). “In all events a” trier of fact “cannot simply denominate as a ‘public trust’ the expectations that society has about how its members should behave toward each other.” Reid, 140 Or App at 296.

For example, in Perez the defendant

“had agreed to assist law enforcement officials in the performance of law enforcement activities and that, after engaging in those endeavors for some time, defendant violated the trust that those officials had come to place in him by lying about the purported assassination attempt. In turn, that deception led to defendant’s placement in a motel room at public expense, where he engaged in [the manufacture of methamphetamine].”

186 Or App at 132.

The Court of Appeals explained that the trier of fact “relied on defendant’s having engaged in that conduct despite, and contrary to, his agreement to act as the agent of the police officers and his doing so, moreover, at public expense.” Id. at 133. Based on that reliance, the Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court’s use of the factor. It “conclude[d] that the trial court adequately defined the scope of the trust at issue in this case and explained why it is properly viewed as public rather than private.” Id.

The federal guidelines contain a factor similar to Aggravating Factor F, called “abuse of position of trust.” In United States v. Contreras, 581 F3d 1163 (9th Cir 2009), the district court relied on the factor to enhance the sentence of a cook in a federal prison who was convicted of smuggling drugs into the prison. The Ninth Circuit reversed. It held that for there to be a “position of trust,” the defendant’s position must carry “professional or managerial discretion.” Id. at 1164. The defendant’s position as a cook carried no such discretion, so the aggravating factor did not apply.

In State v. Fox, 262 Or App 473, 324 P3d 608 (2014), the defendant was a recruiter for the Oregon Army National Guard. He stood convicted of coercion under ORS 163.275(1)(g), which applies when a “public servant” unlawfully coerces a victim. The trial court relied on Aggravating Factor F to depart. At sentencing, the defendant objected to basing an aggravated departure on Factor F, because (i) that factor’s factual basis already was used to prove the “public servant” element of the coercion crime, and (ii) OAR 213-008-0002(2) generally prohibits basing departure factors on facts that previously were used to prove elements of the underlying crime. The Court of Appeals held that the trial court did so err (but it affirmed on the ground that the error did not affect the defendant’s actual period of imprisonment, so the error lacked adverse consequence (i.e., was harmless)).

7-4.3.7. Aggravating Factor G: Multiple Victims or Incidents.

Commentary explains that “[t]his fact[or] will not support a departure sentence for any offense for which consecutive sentences are imposed if the basis for the consecutive sentences is the fact that multiple victims were involved.” Implementation Manual at 131. In other words, if the court imposes consecutive sentences because the multiple crimes involved multiple victims, ORS 137.123(5)(b),9 the court may not base a departure on Aggravating Factor G. State v. Teeters, 278 Or App 812, 814, 379 P3d 839 (2016) (per curiam) (trial court erred in relying on Aggravating Factor G to depart on count whose sentence the court made consecutive, because “that enhancement fact can be used to impose a departure sentence only when it is not ‘captured in a consecutive sentence.’ OAR 213-008-0002(1)(b)(G)”). Conversely, if the court imposes concurrent sentences on multiple crimes which involved multiple victims, the court may use this aggravating factor.

PRACTICE TIP: Defense counsel should be aware of the possibility that after prevailing on the court to impose concurrent terms for multiple convictions, the court might be able to impose a departure term whose duration is greater than the maximum consecutive presumptive term the court otherwise might have imposed. Owing to this risk, counsel should make a diligent effort to determine which is worse—consecutive presumptive terms or concurrent departure terms—and advocate accordingly.

The Apprendi rule applies to Aggravating Factor G. Ramirez, 209 Or App at 239; Bray, 197 Or App at 19–20. A trial court commits reversible error if, without a valid jury waiver or the defendant’s admission, it bases this factor on non-juried findings.

In State v. Texeira, 259 Or App 184, 313 P3d 351 (2013), the Court of Appeals held that the trial court erred in relying on Aggravating Factor G to depart on a burglary sentence. In that case, the defendant stole seven different occupants’ property during a single burglary. The court explained that the factor

“is ‘phrased in the past tense’ and has a ‘distinctly retrospective and completed-act focus.’ The ‘same is true of the provision’s surrounding context.’ Thus, the ‘multiple victims’ of the ‘offense’ must be persons who are directly injured by the ‘harm that inheres in the crime’s commission or that forms the rationale for criminalizing the conduct.’”

259 Or App at 197 (quoting inter alia State v. Allred, 165 Or App 226, 233, 995 P2d 1210 (2000)).

The court then held:

“[F]or purposes of that factor, a ‘victim’ is a person who is directly, immediately, and exclusively injured by the commission of the crime—not persons injured only by subsequent, additional criminal conduct. Here, the theft victims were injured only when defendant took possession of and appropriated their property, not when defendant entered the residence and thereby committed the burglary. Defendant’s appropriations occurred after, and were distinct from, the commission of the burglary. Thus, the theft victims were not directly, immediately, and exclusively injured by the burglary. The trial court erred in applying OAR 213-008-0002(1)(b)(G) to include the theft victims.”

Texeira, 259 Or App at 199.

7-4.3.8. Aggravating Factor H: Organized Criminal Operation.

Commentary explains that this factor may be used where “two or more individuals cooperate in a formal or informal organization to commit illegal acts for financial benefits or as a means to further the objectives of the organization or its members.” Implementation Manual at 131. In State v. Farias-Estrada, 203 Or App 474, 476, 124 P3d 1289 (2005), the Court of Appeals held that the Apprendi rule applies to Aggravating Factor H. A court commits reversible error if without a valid jury waiver or defendant’s admission, it bases this factor on non-juried findings.

In State v. Rodriguez, the Court of Appeals relied on the commentary quoted above in passing on the sentencing court’s use of Aggravating Factor H. The court said the commentary “demonstrates that, whether the organization is ‘formal’ or ‘informal,’ the aggravating factor was intended to capture an entity that has some structure or identity apart from the individual participants.” 122 Or App at 120. To determine whether these requirements are met, the trier of fact must consider whether the “defendant was ‘united,’ ‘systematically united’ or ‘organized’ with the other persons.” Id. at 121.

Applying this test, the Rodriguez court rejected use of the factor. It explained:

“All the evidence [showed was] that three persons, including defendant, together broke into a store and took some clothes and that defendant was caught wearing one of the stolen items. Not under the rule, nor under any common-sense understanding of the term, can it be said that evidence shows the existence of an ‘organized criminal operation.’”

Id.

7-4.3.9. Aggravating Factor I: Permanent Injury.

Commentary states, “This aggravating factor is particularly appropriate when the victim’s injury is clearly debilitating and incapacitates the victim for a considerable period of time. The victim’s injury should significantly hinder the victim’s efforts to pursue his or her prior lifestyle.” Implementation Manual at 131.

In State v. Wilson, the Court of Appeals relied on this commentary. There, the defendant was sentenced for promoting a juvenile’s prostitution. The trier of fact based its finding of Aggravating Factor I on “a general statement of the injury that results to juveniles, their families and society when juveniles are involved in prostitution.” 111 Or App at 152. The Court of Appeals reversed, explaining: “There is nothing that shows why or how this victim was injured. * * * Even though the [sentencing court’s basis for the factor] may be correct sociology, reliance on the injury factor for a departure must relate to the individual victim.” Id. (court’s emphasis).

Similarly, in State v. Fitzgerald the defendant was sentenced for first-degree burglary of an occupied dwelling. The trier of fact found this factor based on a presumed psychological effect of the crime on a typical burglary victim, rather than on the effect the defendant’s burglary actually had on the crime victim. 117 Or App at 155-56. The Court of Appeals rejected the trial court’s use of the factor to depart.

In State v. Wolff, 174 Or App 367, 27 P3d 145 (2001), the defendant was convicted of hindering prosecution of a homicide. The Court of Appeals rejected the trial court’s use of Aggravating Factor I. The court held that because the homicide victim was dead, the defendant’s criminal conduct could not have inflicted on the victim any type of permanent injury.

In State v. Hudson, the Court of Appeals upheld the lower court’s use of the factor. There, the defendant fired a pistol into the victim, leaving him paralyzed. The defendant was convicted of attempted murder and first-degree assault with a firearm. The court said, “Defendant’s arguments that the court erred in applying [Aggravating Factor I] do not require discussion.” 115 Or App at 305.

7-4.3.10. Aggravating Factor J: Significantly Greater Harm or Loss.

Commentary explains that this factor may be used whenever “[t]he degree of harm attributed to the current crime of conviction was significantly greater than typical for such an offense.” Implementation Manual at 132. Commentary also explains that like Aggravating Factor I, physical injury to the victim authorizes use of this aggravating factor. Id. But unlike Aggravating Factor I, this factor may be used even if the victim’s injuries are not permanent. Id.

Several cases involve use of this factor. For example, in Sawatzky, 195 Or App at 162-63, the Court of Appeals held that the Apprendi rule applies to the factor. A court commits reversible error if without a valid jury waiver or the defendant’s admission, it bases this factor on non-juried findings.

In State v. Allred, the defendant aided a murderer in his flight from the authorities and pled guilty to hindering prosecution. The trier of fact based its finding of the factor on the theory that although the fleeing murderer committed no further crimes before he was apprehended, the defendant created a situation where the murderer could “‘potentially commit a similar crime or the same type of crime, and so * * * put the public at great risk[.]’” 165 Or App at 230. The Court of Appeals reversed, because the defendant’s conducted merely risked causing greater harm than usual. The court said it was “unwilling to interpret the guidelines to authorize a” trier of fact to find the factor “based on predictions of someone else’s future dangerousness without an unequivocal expression of the legislature’s intent to do so.” Id. at 231 n 4.

In State v. Mitchell the Court of Appeals held that the sentencing court erred in basing a departure on the factor, for it did not explain how the harm or loss was significantly different than usual. 136 Or App at 103.

In State v. Reid the trier of fact partially based its finding of the factor on the defendant’s decision to go to trial, rather than plead guilty. The Court of Appeals held that to the extent the finding was based on the defendant’s decision to exercise his constitutional rights to go to trial, the sentencing court erred in relying on the factor to depart. 140 Or App at 299. See also OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(F) (Mitigating Factor F—”[t]he offender’s refusal to cooperate with the state shall not be considered an aggravating factor”).

See also State v. Gassner, 310 Or App 38, 483 P3d 1207 (2021) (trial court erred in imposing harsher sentence because defendant went to trial).

In State v. Ambrose the defendant was convicted of sexually abusing a mentally retarded paraplegic woman. The sentencing court based a departure on, inter alia, Aggravating Factor J, because the victim’s loss “was significantly greater than typical[.]” 117 Or App at 300. The Court of Appeals reversed, because the victim’s loss “flowed only from [the defendant’s] act incidentally.” Id. at 301.

In State v. Nelson the defendant was convicted of unauthorized use of a vehicle. The defendant extensively damaged the vehicle while he used it. The Court of Appeals held that if the damages were extraordinary, the sentencing court could use Aggravating Factor J. 119 Or App at 88-89.

In State v. Hudson the defendant emptied a pistol into the victim, leaving him a paraplegic. The defendant was convicted of attempted murder and first-degree assault with a firearm. The Court of Appeals said, “Defendant’s arguments that the court erred in applying [Aggravating Factor J] do not require discussion.” 115 Or App at 305.

In , 146 Or App 338, 932 P2d 107, rev den, 325 Or 438 (1997), the defendant was tried for robbery and felony murder after her accomplice killed the robbery victim. The jury convicted the defendant of robbery but acquitted her of felony murder. The sentencing court departed on the robbery based on the finding that the victim’s death established greater harm than usual. The Court of Appeals affirmed, notwithstanding that the finding was based on jury-rejected facts. 146 Or App at 343. But Watkins is a pre-Blakely case. Under the constitutional principles discussed in Chapter 1, basing a departure on jury-rejected facts should be indefensible in the Blakely-era. See also Section 7-1.3.1 (discussing commentary found in Implementation Manual at 125).

7-4.3.11. Aggravating Factor K: Hate Crime.

Commentary states: “This factor was added * * * to allow departure sentences as a matter of public policy when an offense arises from various forms of bigotry.” Implementation Manual at 132. Commentary further provides the following example:

“An offender is convicted of an Assault II (ORS 163.175) for an attack on a victim who belongs to a racial minority. The sentencing court determines that the attack was related to the offender’s membership in a white supremacist gang. This fact may be cited as an aggravating factor if the attack was motivated by the offender’s desire to further the gang’s objective of racial bigotry.”

Implementation Manual at 132.

In Wisconsin v. Mitchell, 508 US 476, 113 S Ct 2194, 124 L Ed 2d 436 (1993), the defendant’s sentence was enhanced pursuant to a hate-crime statute. The Supreme Court held that this penalty enhancer did not violate the defendant’s free speech rights. Accord State v. Hendrix, 314 Or 170, 838 P2d 566 (1992) (upholding constitutionality of first-degree intimidation statute, ORS 166.165).10

In State v. Dilts II, 337 Or 645, 651-52, 103 P3d 95 (2004), the court held that the Apprendi rule applies to Aggravating Factor K. A court commits reversible error if, without a valid jury waiver or the defendant’s admission, it bases the factor on non-juried findings.

The 2021 Legislature’s Senate Bill 186 (Oregon Laws 2021, Chapter 76) made permanent a temporary rule the Criminal Justice Commission approved in 2020, which amended Aggravating Factor K. The factor now includes “gender identity (as defined in ORS 166.155)[.]”

§ 7-4.3.12 Aggravating Factor L: Disproportionate Impact (For Theft I under ORS 164.055, and Aggravated Theft I under ORS 164.057).

Pursuant to the directives of Senate Bill 570 (2009), the Criminal Justice Commission created and defined a new Aggravating Factor L that applies solely to the crimes of ordinary and aggravated theft in the first degree. For purposes of that aggravating factor, “disproportionate impact” means:

“(a) The offender caused damage to property during the commission of the theft and the cost to restore the damaged property to the condition the property was in immediately before the theft is more than three times the value of the property that was the subject of the theft; or

“(b) The theft of the property creates a hazard to public health or safety or the environment.” OAR 213-008-0002(4).

Aggravating Factor L is subject to constitutional attack on two grounds. The first ground is that SB 570’s the title of the bill—“[r]elating to commerce in metal property”—does not embrace the factor. This renders the factor void under Article IV, section 20 of the Oregon Constitution, which states:

‘Every Act shall embrace but one subject, and matters properly connected therewith, which subject shall be expressed in the title. But if any subject shall be embraced in an Act which shall not be expressed in the title, such Act shall be void only as to so much thereof as shall not be expressed in the title.”

The second ground for constitutional attack on SB 570 is that it was enacted in violation of the “full text” requirement of Article IV, section 22 of the Oregon Constitution, because it amended a guidelines provision without setting forth the full text of the provision.

Jesse Wm. Barton, Effectively Using Article IV, section 22-Based Challenges, The Oregon Defense Attorney 6 (July-August 2010) provides a full discussion of these challenges to Aggravating Factor L.

§ 7-4.3.12.1. Definition for Aggravating Factor L.

For some reason, the definition of the phrase “disproportionate impact” was placed in OAR 213-008-0002, rather than in the guidelines’ definitional provision, OAR 213-003-0001. The definition is provided later, in Section 7-4.5.

§ 7-4.3.13. Nonenumerated Aggravating Factors and Challenges to their Constitutional Validity.

As previously discussed, the legislature intended that the enumerated list of departure factors, including the enumerated list of aggravating factors, to be “nonexclusive[.]” OAR 213-008-0002(1). Under ORS 136.765(1)-(2) and ORS 136.790, the fact that the list is nonexclusive impliedly delegates to grand juries and prosecutors the authority to create additional factors that trial courts may use as the basis to impose aggravated departures.

This differs from the pre-SB 528 era, when trial courts, rather than juries, were the finders of aggravating factors. The authority to create nonenumerated factors was delegated to the trial courts, who were not shy about exercising that authority. In several published opinions, the appellate courts (primarily the Court of Appeals) approved trial courts’ use—but not necessarily the manner of creation—of several nonenumerated factors discussed later in this chapter. But in the post-SB 528 era, trial courts are forbidden from applying aggravating factors other than those that the state or the grand jury alleged. Speedis, 350 Or at 436. Consequently, trial courts also are forbidden from creating nonenumerated factors on their own.

Also in the post-SB 528 era, various cases have rejected trial court use of nonenumerated aggravating factors on the ground that they are subject to the Apprendi rule discussed in Chapter 1, but were found in violation of that rule. See, e.g., State v. Munguia, 209 Or App 240, 147 P3d 350 (2006) (per curiam) (defendant had an extensive criminal history); State v. Williams, 207 Or App 645, 142 P3d 552, rev den, 342 Or 117 (2006) (per curiam) (prior sanctions proved ineffective); State v. Courtney, 201 Or App 399, 400, 118 P3d 858 (2005) (per curiam), former disposition withdrawn, 207 Or 148, 139 P3d 1010 (2006) (judicially created factor of “lack of respect for the law”); State v. Phipps, 201 Or App 397, 398, 118 P3d 850 (2005) (per curiam), former disposition withdrawn, 207 Or 147, 139 P3d 1009 (2006) (judicially created factor of “extreme disregard for the value of human life”). These cases hold only that absent a jury waiver or a defendant’s admission, defendants are entitled to jury trials using the reasonable-doubt standard on the factors. Apart from that jury-trial right, there are separate questions about the validity of the manner in which the nonenumerated factors were or would be created.

As previously mentioned, in State v. Speedis the court rejected claims that OAR 213-008-0002(1)’s implied delegation violates the separation of powers and state constitutional vagueness principles. Speedis, 350 Or at 432-33, 438. But all of the nonenumerated factors at issue in Speedis were offender-specific. When the court rejected the defendant’s state constitutional vagueness claim, it limited its holding as follows:

“Our state constitutional vagueness holding is limited to sentencing and, within that context, to sentencing factors that bear on a defendant’s character. We have no occasion to consider whether greater specificity would be required either for a statute defining the elements of an offense or for sentencing factors that relate to the offense rather than the offender.”

350 Or at 438 n 14 (emphasis added).

The court either declined to address, or was not asked to address, other claims against nonenumerated factors. Because the court did not decide those claims, it left them open. The court’s decision is not stare decisis against these claims. See Stranahan v. Fred Meyer, Inc., 331 Or 38, 54, 11 P3d 228 (2000) (only ruling on matters necessary to court’s decision are stare decisis).

The first of these other claims is one that the court left open in Speedis: Whether offense-specific nonenumerated factors violate state constitutional vagueness principles.

Another of the defendant’s claims was that the nonenumerated factors violated his federal due process right of “fair warning.” But all of the nonenumerated factors at issue in Speedis were approved—“sanctified”—in reported appellate court decisions issued before the date of the defendant’s crimes. When the Supreme Court rejected his federal constitutional “fair warning” claims, it held, “Even if the sentencing guidelines, standing alone, would not provide sufficient notice that those factors would justify an enhanced sentence,[11] appellate decisions did and, in doing so, satisfied due process.”12 Speedis, 350 Or at 439 (citing United States v. Lanier, 520 US 259, 266, 117 S Ct 1219, 137 L Ed 2d 432 (1997)). See also State v. Gallegos, 217 Or App 248, 256-57, 174 P3d 1086 (2007), rev den, 344 Or 670 (2008) (consistent with Speedis).

The court thus left open questions about the validity of nonenumerated aggravating factors that were not sanctified by reported appellate decisions issued before the date of a defendant’s crimes. Consequently, any such factor that is alleged in another defendant’s case is subject to fair warning-based attacks. These attacks are based on:

• The “general purpose[]” of the criminal code of “giv[ing] fair warning of the nature of the conduct declared to constitute an offense and of the sentences authorized upon conviction.” ORS 161.025(1)(c). This “general purpose” is a statutory codification of the “due-process function of putting persons on notice of the law’s demands,” by being “sufficiently explicit to inform those who are subject to it what conduct on their part will render them liable to its penalties.” State v. Hodges, 254 Or 21, 27, 457 P2d 491 (1969).

• The prohibition on ex post facto laws, US Const Art I, § 10; Or Const Art I, § 21, for the legislature may not enact laws that would increase the punishment for acts that already have been committed. See Peugh v. United States, 569 US 530, 133 S Ct 2072, 186 L Ed 2d 84 (2013) (use of advisory presumptive range, created after date of defendant’s crime and that was greater than range in effect at time of crime, violated ex post facto prohibition); Miller, 482 US at 429-30; State v. Wille, 317 Or 487, 502, 858 P2d 128 (1993).

• The prohibition on bills of attainder, US Const Art I, § 10, for the legislature may not “singl[e] out disfavored persons and met[e] out summary punishment for past conduct.” Landgraf v. USI Film Products, 511 US 244, 266, 114 S Ct 1483, 128 L Ed 2d 229 (1994). See also United States v. Lovett, 328 US 303, 66 S Ct 1073, 90 L Ed 1252 (1946) (legislation denying money to pay specified officials’ salaries is an invalid bill of attainder).

The state may argue that OAR 213-008-0002(1)’s statement that its list of factors is “nonexclusive” gives a defendant fair warning. But that rule merely notifies a person that executive-branch (the district attorney’s) action or judicial-branch (the grand jury’s) action may expand the enumerated list of factors. That rule does not meet the fair-warning requirement for non-sanctified nonenumerated aggravating factors. To rule otherwise would conflict with the Supreme Court decision in Miller, that “[t]he constitutional prohibition against ex post facto laws cannot be avoided merely by adding to [OAR 213-008-0002(1)] notice that it might be changed” by executive- or judicial-branch expansion. Miller, 482 US at 431.

PRACTICE TIP: As the proponent of a nonenumerated aggravating factor, the burden is on the prosecution to cite a reported decision, issued before the date of the defendant’s crime, which expressly sanctifies the factor. Opinions that discuss the factor, but do not expressly approve its use, should not count. If the prosecution does not or cannot cite a preexisting, sanctifying opinion, the factor may not be used. Consequently, fair warning principles should prohibit the creation of new, nonenumerated aggravating factors—because there will be no sanctifying decisions the prosecution could cite.

With respect to non-sanctified, offense-specific nonenumerated aggravating factors, Section 7-4.3.13.2 discusses State v. Bennett, 249 Or App 379, 277 P3d 586 (2012), rev den, 353 Or 203 (2013). That case involved a nonenumerated aggravating factor—specifically, a factor called “additional crimes committed while evading criminal sanctions.” Unfortunately, both the defendant’s counsel failed to make fair-warning and state constitutional vagueness claims against the factor. In its opinion, the Court of Appeals sanctified the factor. Under Speedis, the factor now may be used in any case involving crimes committed on or after Bennett’s April 18, 2012 date of decision.

Similarly, in State v. Kindler, 277 Or App 242, 370 P3d 909 (2016), the defendant failed to appear for trial. The state then gave notice of its intent to prove as an enhancement fact that “defendant willfully failed to appear for a mandatory proceeding in connection with this matter.” 277 Or App at 248. The defendant’s counsel failed to make fair-warning and state constitutional vagueness claims against the factor. But fortunately for the defendant, the Court of Appeals reversed on the ground that during the sentencing trial, the trial court failed to instruct the jury “in accordance with OEC 201(g), that it ‘may, but is not required to, accept as conclusive any fact judicially noticed in favor of the prosecution.’” Id. at 253. Thus, on remand, Kindler would have had the opportunity to pose constitutional objections to the nonenumerated factor.

Finally, the following sections discuss nonenumerated aggravating factors that trial courts created through the years—however unconstitutionally. As will be seen, in previous opinions the appellate courts approved the use of most but not all of the factors. But it should be noted that to the extent these nonenumerated factors are offender specific, the Wedge rule should prohibit applying them on a vicarious liability basis, and they must be tried in the case’s penalty phase (if there is one). ORS 136.773.

§ 7-4.3.13.1. Additional Crimes Committed to Evade Criminal Sanctions.

This factor’s origin is State v. Cornelius. There, the Court of Appeals upheld using Aggravating Factor D to depart on a conviction for possession of a weapon by an inmate. The defendant’s criminal history included convictions for escape and failure to appear. The Court of Appeals held that the prior convictions were similar. It explained that like the current crime of conviction, the priors were “‘governmental types of offenses’” establishing “a pattern of committing crimes to evade criminal sanctions.” Cornelius, 112 Or App at 101.

In State v. Nelson, the defendant committed four acts of failure to appear (FTA) during the pendency of his current prosecution for unauthorized use of a vehicle. The sentencing court used the FTAs as a basis to depart. Relying on its Aggravating Factor D analysis from Cornelius, the Court of Appeals sanctified a new aggravating factor. It said the defendant’s “crimes committed to evade criminal sanctions”—i.e., his FTAs—“may be considered in deciding whether to impose a departure sentence.” Nelson, 119 Or App at 87. Accord Zavala-Ramos, 116 Or App at 223 (“[c]ircumstances that demonstrate a defendant’s unwillingness to conform his conduct to legal requirements, whether or not there are criminal consequences, may be” relevant to a departure decision).

Notwithstanding cases such as Nelson and Zavala-Ramos, aggravating factors based on allegations that the defendant committed additional crimes should be subject to the Wedge and Apprendi rules. For example, in Blakely the Court virtually held that the Apprendi rule applies to aggravating factors based on additional crimes committed to evade criminal sanctions. 542 US at 307 n 11. In Sawatzky the Court of Appeals held that the trial court violated the Apprendi rule by basing a departure on non-juried findings, without a valid jury waiver or defendant’s admissions, regarding the defendant’s release status at the time of her crimes and her intention to flee prosecution to avoid responsibility. 195 Or App at 162-63.

Assuming Apprendi’s dicta purporting to create a prior-conviction exception to its rule is valid law,13 this nonenumerated aggravating factor requires proof of more than that the defendant committed other crimes. It also requires proof that the defendant committed the other crimes “to evade criminal sanctions” for the crimes on which he is being sentenced. Cornelius, 112 Or App at 101. Proof of the “evade criminal sanctions” characteristic of the prior crimes should itself be subject to the Wedge and Apprendi rules. See Bray, 342 Or at 724 (Apprendi rule applies to Aggravating Factor D’s requirement that record of convictions constitutes “persistence”).

Furthermore, the guidelines explicitly require that sentences be proportionate to the offense and to the defendant’s criminal history. OAR 213-002-0001; OAR 213-008-0003(1). These statutory requirements seek to bring the guidelines into compliance with the state constitution’s proportionate sentencing requirements. Or Const, Art I, § 16 (“all penalties shall be proportioned to the offense”). See State v. Shumway, 291 Or 153, 630 P2d 796 (1981) (a sentencing scheme which imposes a longer sentence for a lesser-included crime than it does for the greater crime is disproportionate); State v. McLain, 158 Or App 419, 974 P2d 727 (1999) (same). See also State v. Wheeler, 343 Or 652, 676, 175 P3d 438 (2007) (reiterating principle from Shumway). Even assuming use of the additional-crimes factor generally is statutorily and constitutionally permissible, its unbridled use in specific cases may violate the guidelines’ and the state constitution’s proportionality requirements.

For example, suppose a defendant whose criminal history score is A is convicted of murder. His maximum presumptive sentence is 269 months. As explained in Section 7-5.1, his departure maximum is 538 months. Suppose further that during trial the defendant somehow managed to secure a bail release, but then committed the crime of failure to appear (FTA). FTA is ranked at crime seriousness level 4. If the state were to charge and convict the defendant of the FTA, he would be sentenced in grid block 4-A. Assuming no aggravating factors support a departure, at most the sentencing court could impose a presumptive maximum of 11 months.

But suppose that instead of (or even in addition to) charging the defendant for the FTA, the state uses the FTA as an aggravating factor in the murder sentencing. Based on the FTA the court could impose the departure maximum of 538 months. Wilson, 111 Or App at 151 n 4 (court needs only one valid factor to depart). Thus, the FTA would produce a departure component of 269 months. That term is 258 months longer than the sentence the defendant would have received had he been charged with and convicted of the FTA. An argument may be made that such use of the FTA violates the guidelines’ and the state constitution’s proportionate sentencing requirements.

PRACTICE TIP: To ensure compliance with the statutory and constitutional proportionate-sentencing requirements, defense counsel should argue that to properly use this nonenumerated aggravating factor, the sentencing court must limit the sentence’s departure component to the maximum sentence the court could impose for a separate conviction for the additional crime on which the factor is based. For example, in the hypothetical discussed above, the sentencing court could aggravate the defendant’s murder sentence by up to 11 months—the presumptive maximum for the FTA—for a total sentence of 280 months (269 + 11 = 280).

§ 7-4.3.13.2. Additional Crimes Committed While Evading Criminal Sanctions.

This factor’s origin is State v. Bennett. There, the defendant had escaped from custody, and while on escape status committed a burglary. After the defendant later was convicted of second-degree escape, the court imposed an aggravated departure based on the nonenumerated “factor that [the defendant] had committed [the new crime] while he was on escape status.” 249 Or App at 380.14 The defendant relied on the “ejusdem generis principle of construction to assert that a court may not rely on post offense conduct when imposing a departure sentence.” Id. at 381.

The Court of Appeals rejected that claim. It explained:

“In Ceballos [see Section 7-4.3.4.1], we held that, ‘[t]o determine whether a defendant has shown persistent involvement, * * * the sentencing court may consider similar offenses committed after the current offense for which sentence is being imposed.’ 162 Or App at 481 (internal quotation marks and citation omitted; emphasis added); see also State v. Reeves, 134 Or App 38, 894 P2d 1170, rev den, 321 Or 284 (1995) (affirming a finding of persistent involvement where the defendant committed assaults while incarcerated pending trial). Necessarily, then, the list of factors in OAR 213-008-0002(1) cannot be read to prohibit consideration of a defendant’s post-offense conduct.”

Id. at 382.

§ 7-4.3.13.3. Defendant’s Immigration Status.

This judicially created factor was first discussed in State v. Zavala-Ramos. That case involved a defendant who was illegally in the United States for the third time. He stood convicted of delivery of a controlled substance, and his presumptive sentence was probation. The sentencing court imposed a dispositional departure term of incarceration. Apparently, the court partially based its decision on the defendant’s prior illegal entries.

In explaining the proper use of this aggravating factor, the Court of Appeals said, “We agree that a defendant’s current illegal immigration status cannot, per se, be considered to be an aggravating factor. For the same reason, we also agree that consideration of a history of deportations, standing alone, would not justify a departure sentence.” 116 Or App at 222. But the court concluded that

“circumstances that demonstrate a defendant’s unwillingness to conform his conduct to legal requirements, whether or not there are criminal consequences, may be. Defendant had been illegally in the United States at least twice. The [trier of fact] could consider that pattern of conduct in determining whether it is likely that a probationary sentence would serve the purposes of the guidelines to protect the public and punish the offender.”

Id. at 223 (footnote omitted). See also State v. Morales-Aguilar, 121 Or App 456, 458-59, 855 P2d 646 (1993) (factor properly used, because defendant’s repeated illegal entries “demonstrated an absolute disregard for any of the laws”).

§ 7-4.3.13.4. Violating Preexisting Supervisory Term.

This factor applies when the defendant is on parole, probation, or post-prison supervision when he commits his current crime of conviction. Besides Speedis, various cases uphold its use. See, e.g., Gallegos, 217 Or App at 256; State v. Williams, 133 Or App 191, 195 n 2, 891 P2d 3 (dicta), rev den, 321 Or 512 (1995); State v. Berg, 115 Or App 254, 256, 838 P2d 73 (1992), rev den, 315 P2d 312 (1993); State v. Mitchell, 113 Or App 632, 634, 833 P2d 1324 (1992); State v. Hill, 112 Or App 213, 214, 823 P2d 951 (1992). However, the Court of Appeals has held that use of the factor requires proof of more than just that the defendant was on supervision when he committed his new crime. It also requires proof that the defendant possessed a separate malevolent quality and an inclination to commit increasingly serious crimes; moreover, the Apprendi rule applies to this additional requirement, and thus to the factor itself. See State v. Burns, 213 Or App 38, 47, 159 P3d 1208 (2007) rev dismissed, 345 Or 302 (2008) (reaffirming decision in State v. Allen, 198 Or App 392, 108 P3d 651, adh’d to on recons, 202 Or App 565, 123 P3d 331 (2005), former disposition withdrawn, 207 Or 295, 140 P3d 1135, rev den, 342 Or 46 (2006)). See also State v. Perez, 196 Or App 364, 371-73, 102 P3d 705 (2004) (guidelines generally prohibit using record of prior convictions already used in criminal-history scoring as ground for aggravating factor), rev’d on other grounds, 340 Or 310, 130 P3d 780 (2006). Thus, a trial court commits reversible error if without a valid jury waiver or defendant’s admission, it bases the factor on non-juried findings.

7-4.3.13.5. Defendant’s Substance Abuse Problems.

Current case law upholds using a defendant’s substance abuse problems as an aggravating factor. But see OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(C) (defendant’s voluntary substance abuse problem may not be used as a mitigating factor). For example, in State v. Kennedy the trier of fact found “that defendant’s alcohol problem was leading to increasingly serious crimes and that his chance for rehabilitation depends on controlling that problem.” 113 Or App at 139. “Even after passage of the guidelines,” the Court of Appeals explained, “reformation remains a goal of sentencing. Or Const, Art I, § 15.” Kennedy, 113 Or App at 139 (emphasis added).

This aggravating factor’s underlying policy purpose is reformation. See also Alexander, 114 Or App 220, 221, 834 P2d 521 (1992) (per curiam) (where defendant needed extended incarceration period to obtain substance-abuse treatment, departure was proper). That did not change with the adoption of Ballot Measure 26 (1996). Although that measure amended Article I, section 15, it retained reformation as one of the foundational principles of the “[l]aws for the punishment of crime[.]” Id.

7-4.3.13.6. “Just Deserts.”

In State v. Clark, the Court of Appeals rejected the sentencing court’s reliance on “just deserts” as a departure factor. “Just deserts” connotes the concept of imposing appropriate punishment. The Court of Appeals held:

“Punishing an offender appropriately is one of the guidelines’ primary sentencing objectives encompassed by the presumptive sentence. [Implementation Manual at 7.] Although a court might refer to the principle in explaining why a presumptive sentence would not accomplish the purposes of the guidelines, appropriate punishment is not an aggravating factor.”

Clark, 113 Or App at 695.

Thus, the factor may not be used, for it duplicates the guidelines principles and purposes, which are discussed in Chapter 1.

7-4.3.13.7. Lack of Remorse.

A sentencing court may base a departure on a defendant’s lack of remorse over the consequences of his crime. Reid, 140 Or App at 299 (“[w]e agree that a defendant’s empathy—or lack thereof—[for the plight of his victim] is appropriate to a court’s consideration of what sentence should be imposed”). But to the extent the defendant’s decision to go to trial rather than to plead guilty or no contest serves as the factual basis, the factor’s use is invalid. Id. See also OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(F) (Mitigating Factor F—”[t]he offender’s refusal to cooperate with the state shall not be considered an aggravating factor”).

Similarly, suppose the perception of a defendant’s lack of remorse was based on his decision to remain silent at sentencing. In that situation, aggravating his sentence based on his lack of remorse would punish him for exercising his constitutional right to remain silent. Mitchell v. United States, 526 US 314, 328, 119 S Ct 1307, 143 L Ed 2d 424 (1999). Because a court may not aggravate a defendant’s sentence because he exercised his constitutional rights, Reid, 140 Or App at 299, the right against self-incrimination should prohibit aggravating a defendant’s sentence because at sentencing he declined to speak. See Mitchell, 526 US at 330 (leaving open question of whether right against self-incrimination prevents sentencing aggravation where defendant refused to speak). Consequently, the lack of remorse factor should be based only on the defendant actually speaking remorselessly either when his right against self-incrimination did not apply, or after he validly waived the right.

§ 7-4.3.13.8. Failure to Deter.

Yet another nonenumerated aggravating factor is the one the Supreme Court addressed in State v. Lennon: The “defendant’s prior criminal convictions or sanctions should have, but did not, deter the defendant from committing his new offense or offenses[.]” 348 Or at 157.

As previously discussed, post-State v. Bray, the Lennon court approved Court of Appeals decisions that abandoned the requirement that use of Aggravating Factor D requires that “a ‘separate malevolent quality’ * * * be inferred from a defendant’s criminal history[.]” Lennon, 348 Or at 157. The Lennon court held that the same is true about the nonenumerated, failure-to-deter aggravating factor. What is required instead is an “inference that a defendant’s prior criminal convictions or sanctions should have, but did not deter the defendant from committing his new offense or offenses[.]” Id. at 157.

The court further explained:

“Whether that inference follows from the defendant’s prior criminal sanctions will depend on such factors as the number of past convictions or sanctions, when they occurred, and the kind of crimes that the defendant committed. A finding that those past criminal sanctions have not deterred a defendant from committing further crimes thus requires something beyond a conclusion that a defendant has one or more criminal convictions in his past.7 * * * If the record supports the factual inference that a defendant’s prior criminal convictions or sanctions should have, but did not, deter the defendant from committing his new offense or offenses, that factual finding can, in a proper case, support a departure sentence.

____________

7For example, a defendant might have a 15-year-old conviction for an assault that he committed as a young man during an argument over a girlfriend. Years later, he may have become involved with drugs and have a current conviction for a drug offense. The single 15-year-old assault conviction would be a weak basis for a ‘failure to deter’ inference in such a circumstance.”

Id. (boldface added; court’s italics).

The Lennon court concluded that there was “no legitimate debate” over whether the evidence supported such an inference in the defendant’s case. This was because the

“defendant’s criminal history spans two decades, without a significant lapse of time between his criminal activities. He has been convicted of 37 criminal offenses; has received supervision terms in 22 cases, and has violated supervision at least 34 times. His parole and probation record includes over 1,500 entries that detail repeated instances of arrest and incarceration, illegal drug use, restraining order violations, failing to report or keep contact with parole and probation officers, and failing to undergo or complete court-ordered drug treatment. What is more, defendant committed the drug offense in this case while awaiting sentencing on a drug-related conviction for which he had been tried only about two weeks earlier.”

Id. at 158. See also Williams, 238 Or App at 15 (relying on State v. Lennon to conclude that where the defendant’s criminal record disclosed that 10 years prior to his current crime of robbery the defendant committed another robbery and a felon in possession of a weapon, and that in the interim he had two probation violations, two parole violations, and three arrests, there was “no legitimate debate” that these prior sanctions failed to deter the defendant’s new criminal conduct).

7-4.4. Explicit Limitations on the Use of Departure Factors.

OAR 213-008-0002(2) and (3) supplement the guidelines’ principle that “a departure sentence is not appropriate for elements of alleged offender behavior not within the definition of the offense of conviction.” Implementation Manual at 125. Likewise, they supplement the guidelines’ ban on judicially created factors whose use would duplicate the guidelines’ purposes and principles discussed in Chapter 1.

7-4.4.1. Factor Duplicates Statutory Element or Subclassification Factor of the Crime.

According to commentary, “If a given fact is a statutory element of the crime, or if it is used under OAR 213-004-0002(2) to subclassify the crime on the Crime Seriousness Scale, that fact generally may not be used as an aggravating or mitigating fact for departure purposes.” Implementation Manual at 132.

Commentary explains this limitation’s general principle with two examples. The first example states:

“The defendant is convicted of Burglary I ranked at Crime Category 8 because the dwelling was occupied at the time of the burglary. Because the fact of occupancy elevates Burglary I from Crime Category 7 to Crime Category 8, the fact of occupancy may not be relied on as an aggravating factor to support a departure sentence.”

Id. at 132-33.

The second example states:

“Defendant is convicted of Burglary I ranked at Crime Category 7 because the dwelling was unoccupied. The fact that the dwelling was unoccupied is already recognized in ranking the crime as a Crime Category 7 instead of Crime Category 9 offense, [so] that fact cannot also be used as a mitigating factor to support a departure sentence.”

Id. at 133.

There is an exception to this subsection’s limitation on duplicative aggravating factors (colloquially called the ban on “double dipping”). A fact that establishes an element or subclassification of the crime “may be used to support a departure only if it makes the crime of conviction significantly different from the usual criminal conduct which the presumptive sentence is intended to punish.” Id. at 132. The exception applies only “[i]n very rare cases,” Implementation Manual at 133, and its application requires explicit findings. See, e.g., Mitchell, 136 Or App at 103.

Because the state is the party against whom the presumptive-sentence concept is directed, cf. OEC 307(1), the state has the burden of proving the double-dipping prohibition’s “significantly different from the usual” exception. That means the state, through a thorough review of prior cases, must establish what exactly is “the usual” conduct captured by the applicable crime elements and subclassification factors. After establishing “the usual,” the state then must establish a coherent definition of the phrase “significantly different.” After establishing those two things, the state then must establish that the facts of a specific crimes are “significantly different” from “the usual” facts that underlie the commission of the pertinent crime element and subclassification factor.

Commentary explains the significantly different exception by example:

“An offender is convicted of Assault I ORS 163.185 for the torture and permanent disfigurement of a victim. While serious physical injury is an element of the offense, the degree of harm actually inflicted in this case far exceeds the usual damage caused by such an offense. The sentencing judge may cite the deliberate nature of the defendant’s act (Aggravating Factor J) and the permanent nature of the victim’s injury (Aggravating Factor I) to impose a departure sentence.”

Implementation Manual at 132.

In Davilla, 280 Or App at 45, the defendant stood convicted of murder, having decapitated his victim using “a small, dull ‘Boy Scout knife’ during an attempted rape.” The defendant challenged the court’s use of relied on Aggravating Factor E, “use of a weapon,” to impose an aggravated departure, arguing “that OAR 213-008-0002(2) precludes consideration of an aggravating fact that is integral to the commission of the offense to impose a departure sentence,” and “that the use of a dangerous weapon is ‘integral’ to the offense of murder.” Id. at 51. The Court of Appeals rejected this construction of OAR 213-008-0002(2). It held instead:

“[T]he plain text of OAR 213- 008-0002(2) applies only to statutory elements of the crime or a fact used to subclassify the crime on the crime seriousness scale. Under ORS 163.115 (1989), murder does not contain ‘use of a weapon’ as a statutory element. Further, there are no subclassifications for murder on the crime seriousness scale.”

Davilla, 280 Or App at 54 (footnote omitted).

The appellate courts have applied OAR 213-008-0002(2) in various cases. For example, in State v. Guthrie, 112 Or App 102, 107, 828 P2d 462 (1992), the defendant was convicted of various assaultive crimes including attempted murder. The Court of Appeals accepted the state’s concession that the sentencing court erred in using the Aggravating Factors C, E, and I. 112 Or App at 106. The court explained:

“Under OAR [213-008-0002(2)], a factual aspect of a crime may not be used as an aggravating factor unless the aspect is ‘significantly different from the usual criminal conduct captured by the aspect of the crime.’ The court did not explain any significant difference and, in fact, indicated that the aggravation on which it was relying was, in part, ‘incorporated in the crime itself.’”

Guthrie, 112 Or App at 106. See also State v. Pedro, 242 Or App 366, 256 P3d 153 (2011) (where defendant was convicted of unlawful use of a weapon, trial court erred in basing departure on “use of a weapon” aggravating factor); State v. Torres, 182 Or App 156, 165, 48 P3d 170 (2002) (where defendant was convicted of second-degree assault with a firearm, trial court erred in basing departure on defendant’s use of a firearm); Mitchell, 136 Or App at 103 (reversed, because the sentencing court did not explain how the harm or loss was significantly greater than usual).

In State v. Lee, the defendant initially intended to shoplift. The crime escalated into second-degree robbery. Based on the defendant’s acts of violence against the victim (Aggravating Factor C), the sentencing court departed. Although violence, or at least the threat of violence, is an element of robbery, ORS 164.405(1)(a), the Court of Appeals upheld use of the aggravating factor, because the sentencing court determined that the defendant’s “use of force” was “significantly different from the usual conduct that the presumptive sentence for robbery II is intended to punish.” Lee, 110 Or App at 531.

Finally, in Boxberger v. Board of Parole, the defendant was convicted of various counts of first-degree sodomy under the victim-under-12 version of that crime. ORS 163.405(1)(b). The board used the parole matrix’s particular vulnerability factor to aggravate the defendant’s sentence on grounds that the victims were under five years of age. That factor is essentially the same as guidelines’ Aggravating Factor B. The defendant argued that the factor could not be used, because the victims’ age was an element of the crimes. In rejecting that argument, the Court of Appeals said, “[T]he victims were not simply under 12 years of age, they were substantially younger than 12.” Boxberger, 123 Or App at 341.

Defense counsel should recognize that because this limitation on the duplicative use of aggravating factors involves factual aspects of elements or subclassification factors of a crime, the factual aspects will have been found by the jury beyond a reasonable doubt, thereby satisfying the Apprendi rule. But the jury very well may not have found how the factual aspect is “significantly different from the usual,” and the significant difference is what permits the duplicative use. In the post-Blakely era, a “significantly different from the usual criminal conduct” finding is subject to the Apprendi rule.

State v. Fox confirms that OAR 213-008-0002(2)’s “significantly different from the usual” exception to basing aggravating factors on facts that previously were used to prove crime elements is subject to the Apprendi rule. There, the defendant stood convicted of coercion under ORS 163.275(1)(g). That statute applies when a “public servant” unlawfully coerces a victim. Over the defendant’s objections, the trial court relied on Aggravating Factor F to depart. The basis for the defendant’s objection was that the facts underlying Aggravating Factor F already were captured by the “public servant” element of the coercion crime, and, contrary to the requirements of the Apprendi rule, the defendant’s jury did not find beyond a reasonable doubt OAR 213-008-0002(2)’s “significantly different from the usual” exception to the duplicative use of facts that previously were used to prove crime elements, to prove departure factors. The Court of Appeals agreed that the trial court did so err. It explained:

“[O]ne of the factual aspects of that crime also constitutes a violation of the public trust and of standards of professional responsibility. Therefore, OAR 213-008-0002(2) requires a finding that defendant’s criminal conduct was ‘significantly different’ from the usual criminal conduct that constitutes coercion through the use or abuse of a person’s position as a public servant. As the state rightly concedes, ‘[t]he jury did not make such a finding here.’”

Fox, 262 Or App at 484-85 (emphasis added).

Sawatzky also supports the claim that a “significantly different from the usual criminal conduct” finding is subject to the Apprendi rule.. There the trial court based a departure on Aggravating Factor J, “degree of harm or loss attributed to the current crime of conviction was significantly greater than typical for such an offense.” OAR 213-008-0002(1)(b)(J) (emphasis added). The Sawatzky court held that Factor J is subject to the Apprendi rule. Sawatzky, 195 Or App at 171-72. There is no appreciable difference between Factor J’s “significantly greater” requirement and the significantly-different exception of OAR 213-008-0002(2). Because Sawatzky holds that the Apprendi rule applies to Factor J, which holding necessarily should include the factor’s “significantly greater” requirement, the rule should apply to the significantly-different exception of OAR 213-008-0002(2).

§ 7-4.4.1.1. Factor Duplicates Uncharged or Unproven Element or Subclassification Factor of the Crime.

By its terms, OAR 213-008-0002(2) does not seem to preclude basing departures on factual aspects of the crime that would prove an element or a subclassification of an uncharged or unproven crime. If the state fails to charge an enhancing factor, it could secure a greater sentence for a lesser crime.

For example, suppose a defendant with a criminal-history score of A burglarizes an occupied dwelling. Chapter 2 explains that the burglary of an occupied dwelling is classified at level 8. The state could charge the defendant with a level 8 burglary, but could not rely on Aggravating Factor J, significantly greater harm or loss, because the factor’s factual basis would duplicate the factual aspect of the level 8 subclassification factor. OAR 213-008-0002(2). Assuming there are no departure factors, the maximum sentence the court could impose is grid block 8-A’s presumptive maximum of 45 months’ incarceration.

But suppose the state indicted the defendant for a level 7 burglary, and gave notice of intent to prove Aggravating Factor J based on the fact that the dwelling was occupied. Because the factor’s factual basis does not duplicate a factual aspect of the elements of the burglary charge, nor of any subclassification factors, the state could prove Factor J without also having to prove OAR 213-008-0002(2)’s “significantly different from the usual” requirement.

For the burglary charge, the defendant’s presumptive maximum would be 36 months. But if the state also proved Factor J, and the court found that the factor warranted “substantial and compelling reasons” to depart, then as explained in Section 7-5, the court could impose an aggravated departure of 72 months. See State v. Lark, 113 Or App 458, 464, 833 P2d 1286 (1992) (dictum: suggesting that where court may not use subclassification factor to increase crime’s seriousness level, it may use underlying facts to depart), rev’d on other grounds, 316 Or 317, 851 P2d 1114 (1993).

This means that by securing convictions for the less egregious level 7 burglary along with Aggravating Factor J, the state could secure a sentence 27 months longer than authorized for the more egregious level 8 burglary (72 - 45 = 27). This anomalous result seems particularly unfair, because it is asymmetrical to the principle that the non-existence of a subclassification factor does not constitute a valid basis for a mitigated departure. Parsons, 135 Or App at 191. It also appears inconsistent with the statutory requirements for aggravated-departure sentencing. See Section 7-1.3.1.

Defense counsel should argue that the maximum sentence that may be imposed for the higher-ranked crime—in the above hypothetical, 45 months—sets the departure maximum on the lower-ranked crime. This limitation would be consistent with Article I, section 16’s proportionality principle, discussed in Chapter 1, which prohibits a greater punishment for a lesser-included offense than is allowed for the greater crime.

7-4.4.2. Factor Duplicates Mandatory Sentence.

OAR 213-008-0002(3) absolutely prohibits basing a departure on an aspect of the crime which is used to impose a mandatory sentence. The following commentary explains this absolute ban on double dipping:

“Defendant is convicted of robbery with use of a firearm. The court imposes [a] minimum mandatory sentence specified by ORS 161.610 for a crime based on use of a firearm. The weapon use may not be cited as an aggravating factor to impose a departure sentence which exceeds the mandatory sentence.”

Implementation Manual at 133.

A case addressing this limitation is State v. Hudson, 115 Or App 301, 839 P2d 721 (1992). There, the defendant received concurrent sentences for convictions for attempted murder and first-degree assault with a firearm. The sentencing court used Aggravating Factor E—use of a weapon—to depart on the attempted murder conviction. It imposed a gun minimum on only the assault conviction. The Court of Appeals “reject[ed] defendant’s argument that, because his sentences are concurrent, imposing a gun minimum on one sentence is tantamount to imposing the minimum on both sentences. Separate sentences for separate convictions are treated separately.” Hudson, 115 Or App at 305.

PRACTICE TIP: Defense counsel must recognize that OAR 213-008-0002(3) does not contain the “significantly different” exception found in OAR 213-008-0002(2). Thus, even if the aspect of the crime that is used to impose a statutory maximum sentence is significantly different than usual, OAR 213-008-0002(3) still would prohibit basing an aggravating factor on that aspect. For example, if a defendant used a bazooka in a drive-by shooting and received a mandatory, repeat gun-minimum sentence, his use of the bazooka (rather than, say, a pistol) could not be used to depart.

Practitioners should recognize that this absolute ban on double dipping may apply to facts used to prove the element of a crime that itself carries a mandatory minimum, e.g., a term under ORS 137.000 or ORS 137.707.

EXAMPLE: Based on the victim’s physical handicaps, a defendant is convicted of first-degree rape under the theory that the victim was incapable of consent owing to physical helplessness. ORS 163.375(1)(d). Also based on the victim’s physical handicaps, the defendant is convicted of Aggravating Factor B—“[t]he offender knew or had reason to know of the victim’s particular vulnerability, such as the * * * disability or ill health of victim, which increased the harm or threat of harm caused by the criminal conduct.” OAR 213-008-0002(1)(b)(B). Even if a “significantly different” finding properly could be made, to overcome the general ban on double dipping, see Section 7-4.4.1, because the victim’s physical handicaps were the basis for proving an element that results in “a statutory mandatory sentence,” OAR 213-008-0002(3), defense counsel may argue that the absolute ban on double dipping prohibits using Aggravating Factor B to depart.

§ 7-4.5. Definition for Aggravating Factor L.

Aggravating Factor L, discussed in Section 7.4.3.12, applies to “disproportionate impact” first-degree theft. For some reason the definition of the phrase “disproportionate impact” was placed in OAR 213-008-0002, rather than in the guidelines’ definitional provision, OAR 213-003-0001.

§ 7-5. OAR 213-008-0003 DURATION OF DEPARTURES.

OAR 213-008-0003 sets upper limits on the length of the duration of aggravated-departure sentences. It states:

(1) When a sentencing judge departs in setting the duration of a prison term, the judge shall consider the purposes and principles of these guidelines as described in OAR 213-002-0001 to impose a sentence which is proportionate to the seriousness of the crime of conviction and the offender’s criminal history.

(2) A durational departure from a presumptive prison term shall not total more than double the maximum duration of the presumptive prison term. In no case may the sentence exceed the statutory maximum indeterminate sentence described in ORS 161.605.

(3) The limit on durational departures established by section (2) of this rule does not apply to the indeterminate sentence imposed on a dangerous offender ORS 161.725 and 161.737.

(4) Durational departure sentences of 12 months or less shall be served at the direction of the supervisory authority. Durational departure sentences greater than 12 months shall be served in the legal and physical custody of the Department.

(5) Notwithstanding section (4) of this rule, terms of incarceration 12 months or less imposed pursuant to ORS 166.070(2) shall be served in the legal and physical custody of the Department.

7-5.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-008-0003 sets the upper limit on durational departures for a conviction carrying a presumptive sentence of imprisonment. See OAR 213-003-0001(8) (defining “‘[d]urational departure’ [as] a sentence which is inconsistent with the presumptive sentence as to [the] term of incarceration”). OAR 213-008-0003(2) creates the so-called “doubling limit.” Under it, the departure maximum is double the defendant’s presumptive maximum. If the sentencing court’s departure factors are valid, the appellate court has no authority to review the length of the departure sentence, so long as it is within the doubling limit. State v. Barber, 113 Or App 603, 605-06, 832 P2d 51 (1992).

OAR 213-003-0001(16) defines the term “presumptive sentence.” It states: “‘Presumptive sentence’ means the sentence provided in a grid block for an offender classified in that grid block by the combined effect of the crime seriousness ranking of the current crime of conviction and the offender’s criminal history or a sentence designated as a presumptive sentence by statute.” Id. Thus, presumptive sentences generally are found in the guidelines grid, but sometimes they are found in special statutes (discussed in Chapter 4). The doubling limit applies to the sentence found in the grid-block, or to the sentence the special statute defines as presumptive, e.g., an RPO sentence under ORS 137.717. State v. Hicks, 249 Or App 196, 275 P3d 195 (2012). The doubling limit does not apply to other sorts of sentences, e.g., mandatory minimum sentences under ORS 137.700. See State ex rel Huddleston v. Sawyer, 324 Or 597, 603, 932 P2d 1145, cert den, 522 US 994 (1997) (“[f]rom the relevant statutes and from the Commission’s rules, it is clear that a ‘presumptive sentence’ does not mean a statutorily mandated term of imprisonment that does or may apply to a particular conviction” (emphasis added)).

For example, in State v. Nygaard, the defendant was convicted of a level 9 crime, his criminal-history score was H, so his grid-block was 9-H. That grid-block carries a maximum presumptive prison sentence of 38 months, and a maximum departure prison sentence of 76 months. But the defendant also was subject to a 100-month mandatory minimum prison sentence. The trial court departed. But instead of using the presumptive maximum as the “springboard” to depart, the court used the mandatory minimum. Consequently, the court imposed a 200-month departure sentence. Trial counsel did not object, but appellate counsel raised the claim on appeal, as plain error. The state confessed error. The Court of Appeals accepted the state’s confession and reversed and remanded for resentencing. 303 Or App at 801-02.

EXAMPLE 1: A defendant’s grid block is 10-I, carrying a presumptive sentence of 58-60 months, but he is subject to a 100 mandatory minimum under ORS 137.700. Under the doubling limit, his aggravated-departure maximum is double the 60-month presumptive maximum, i.e., 120 months.

EXAMPLE 2: A defendant’s grid block is 3-I, carrying a presumptive sentence of 30 days’ straight jail, but he is subject to a 18-month presumptive sentence under ORS 137.717. Under the doubling limit, his aggravated-departure maximum is double the 18-month presumptive sentence under ORS 137.717, so is 36 months.

Although OAR 213-008-0003 specifies the maximum length of aggravated durational departures, it says nothing about the minimum length of mitigated durational departures. With few exceptions, there is no limit on how far below the presumptive minimum a court may impose a mitigated durational departure.

§ 7-5.2. Statutory Maximums and Departure Sentences.

The 1993 Legislature created OAR 213-008-0003(2) to overturn the portion of State v. Little, 116 Or App 322, 842 P2d 414 (1992), which held that the statutory maximums of ORS 161.605 do not limit departure maximums. Little, 116 Or App at 328 & n 6.

For example, suppose a defendant is in grid block 8-A. That grid block has a presumptive maximum of 45 months and a departure maximum of 90 months. Further suppose that the defendant’s crime of conviction is a Class C felony. Such a crime has a maximum sentence of 60 months (five years). ORS 161.605(3). Under OAR 213-008-0003(2), the departure maximum is 60 months. State v. Umtuch, 144 Or App 366, 368, 927 P2d 142 (1996), rev den, 324 Or 564 (1997).

§ 7-5.3. Departure Maximums for Murder Convictions.

As originally adopted, the guidelines exempted murder sentences from the doubling limit. Former OAR 253-08-004(2). A sentencing court could impose a practical life term on a murder defendant (e.g., 1,200 months). However, ORS 161.620 prohibits life sentences for remanded juveniles; hence, a sentencing court could not give a remanded juvenile a practical life term. State v. Davilla, 157 Or App 639, 643, 972 P2d 902 (1998), rev den, 334 Or 76 (2002) (invalidating 1,394-month departure sentence imposed on remanded juvenile).

Under current OAR 213-008-0003(3), murder sentences are subject to the doubling limit. But relying on the decision in State v. McLain, 158 Or App 419, 974 P2d 727 (1999), in State v. Ambill, 282 Or App 821, 385 P3d 1110 (2016), rev den, 361 Or 524 (2017), the court held that nothing more than the 25-year minimum specified in ORS 161.115; see also ORS 137.700 (for adults); ORS 137.707 (for juveniles) could be imposed as the determinate component of the defendant’s sentence. The court explained:

“If a sentencing court were authorized to impose a minimum term of confinement greater than the 25-year term in ORS 163.115(5)(b), the risk that those convicted of aggravated murder would be more favorably treated than those convicted of murder would remain. Again, in the light of the legislature’s express objective to solve the particular [proportionality] problem identified by McLain, we decline to construe the statute so as to render that solution incomplete in the absence of any other textual or contextual indicators compelling such a conclusion.

“For the foregoing reasons, we are persuaded from the text, context, and legislative history of ORS 163.115 that the legislature intended an offender convicted of murder to be sentenced to an indeterminate life sentence with the 25-year minimum term of incarceration specified in ORS 163.115(5)(b), and that the legislature did not intend for a sentencing court to impose a different minimum term of incarceration under the guidelines or any other source of law. We therefore affirm the judgment of the trial court.”

Ambill, 282 Or App at 833.

Thus, the net result of Ambill is that in murder cases, trial courts must impose the indeterminate life term specified by ORS 163.115(5)(a), and the determinate 25-year minimum specified by ORS 161.115(5)(b), ORS 137.700 (for adults), and ORS 137.707 (for juveniles), but nothing more. No departures as to the minimum terms are allowed.

Practitioners also should know that the 2019 Legislature’s Senate Bill 1013 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2019, chapter 635) applies in any sentencing proceeding held on or after September 29, 2019.15 The bill bifurcated the crime of murder into (i) first-degree murder, ORS 163.107; and (ii) second-degree murder. ORS 163.115.

As explained in the Chapter 2 Update, both first- and second-degree murder are unranked, and trial courts are prohibited from assigning seriousness rankings to those crimes. OAR 213-004-0003; OAR 213-004-0004; OAR 213-017-0001. Chapter 4 explains the purported sentencing scheme for first-degree murder. That chapter also explains the sentencing scheme for second-degree murder. For second-degree murder, the only applicable sentence is life with a 25-year minimum. ORS 163.115(5)(a) and (b).

7-5.4. Departure Maximums for Dangerous Offenders.

Under ORS 161.737(1), a dangerous-offender sentence is a guidelines departure sentence. Davis, 315 Or at 490. The dangerous-offender findings required by ORS 161.725 to 161.735 constitute grounds on which the trial court may find substantial and compelling reasons to impose the dangerous-offender departure. ORS 161.737(1).16

“[A] dangerous offender sentence has two parts: a determinate part and an indeterminate part.” Davis, 315 Or at 494 (emphasis added). The determinate term serves as the dangerous-offender sentence’s minimum. Id. at 495. Under amendments adopted since the date of decision in Davis, the determinate term may take the form of a presumptive sentence or a standard (as opposed to a dangerous-offender) departure sentence. ORS 161.737(2). Or, the determinate term may be a mandatory determinate sentence, such as a “Ballot Measure 11” sentence. See Chapter 4. The indeterminate term may be as long as 30 years. ORS 161.725(1). Because the term is indeterminate, the Parole Board may reduce it to as short as the sentence’s determinate minimum. ORS 144.232(1). See also Chapter 1 (explaining distinctions between determinate and indeterminate sentences).

The 1993 Legislature’s House Bill 2478 amended former OAR 253-08-004(2), which has since been recodified as OAR 213-008-0003(3). HB 2478 overturned that portion of Davis, which said that in consecutive-sentence cases, the indeterminate (maximum) portion of a dangerous offender sentence is limited by the doubling limit for departure terms as set out in former OAR 253-08-004(1) (now codified at OAR 213-008-0003(2)). Davis, 315 Or at 496. ORS 161.737(2). As mentioned above, the maximum dangerous offender sentence a person may receive now is the 30-year term specified in ORS 161.725.

Similarly, HB 2478 overruled that portion of Davis, which said the determinate (minimum) portion of a dangerous offender sentence is limited by the presumptive maximum for the offense. 315 Or at 494-95. Now the minimum portion may be as much as

“twice the maximum presumptive incarceration term. If the presumptive sentence for the offense is probation, the required incarceration term shall be no less than the maximum incarceration term provided by the rule of the [guidelines] rule * * * that establishes incarceration terms for dispositional departures and no more than twice that amount.”

ORS 161.737(2).

HB 2478 contained an emergency clause. Or Laws 1993, ch 334, § 12. Its amendments apply to crimes committed on or after July 13, 1993. 2 Or Laws 1993, p A-3. For crimes committed before that date, State v. Davis is still the law. Thus, for crimes committed before July 13, 1993, the presumptive maximum sets the limit for the determinate (minimum) portion of the sentence. 315 Or at 494-95. State v. Coburn, 146 Or App 653, 655-56, 934 P2d 579 (1997); State v. Reese, 128 Or App 323, 326, 876 P2d 317 (1994).

In State v. Parkerson, 319 Or App 477, 511 P3d 25 (2022), the defendant was convicted of first-degree assault, which is a Class A felony. The trial court sentenced the defendant under the dangerous offender scheme. The court imposed the indeterminate 30-year term and a determinate minimum of 260-months. Defendant argued that the 260-month determinate minimum was illegal, because it exceeded by 20 months the 20-year statutory maximum under ORS 161.605(1). The Court of Appeals rejected the defendant’s argument, stating that “the 260-month portion of the 30-year dangerous offender sentence imposed, was within the court’s discretion under the applicable statutes. See ORS 161.737(2) (indeterminate 30-year dangerous offender sentence imposed pursuant to ORS 161.725 includes a ‘required incarceration term’ that is ‘no more than twice the maximum [guidelines] incarceration term’).” 319 Or App at 479. In State v. Parkerson, 319 Or App 477, 511 P3d 25 (2022), rev allowed, 370 P3d 822 (2023), the defendant was convicted of first-degree assault, which is a Class A felony. The trial court sentenced the defendant under the Dangerous Offender scheme. The court imposed the indeterminate 30-year term and a determinate minimum of 260-months. Defendant argued that the 260-month determinate minimum was illegal, because it exceeded by 20 months the 20-year statutory maximum under ORS 161.605(1). The Court of Appeals rejected the defendant’s argument, stating that “the 260-month portion of the 30-year dangerous offender sentence imposed, was within the court’s discretion under the applicable statutes. See ORS 161.737(2) (indeterminate 30-year dangerous offender sentence imposed pursuant to ORS 161.725 includes a ‘required incarceration term’ that is ‘no more than twice the maximum [guidelines] incarceration term’).” Parkerson, 319 Or App at 479.

WARNING: The Court of Appeals’ decision could well be wrong, and, pending the Supreme Court’s decision in Parkerson, defense counsel should continue to argue that the Dangerous Offender scheme’s determinate component cannot exceed the statutory maximum under ORS 161.605.

The Court of Appeals relied on ORS 161.737(2), which states that the Dangerous Offender scheme’s indeterminate component “is not subject to any guideline[s] rule establishing limitations on the duration of departures.” Because ORS 161.737(2) specifically exempts only the indeterminate component from ORS 161.605, the court could have and likely should have concluded that the determinate component is subject to ORS 161.605.

The 1993 Legislature amended ORS 137.737. But that legislature also amended former OAR 253-08-003(2) (later renumbered as OAR 213-008-0003(2), which imposed ORS 161.605 statutory maxima on the lengths of durational aggravated-departure sentences. The Supreme Court has consistently and most recently stated that the Guidelines “have the force of statute.”17 In addition, the 2003 Legislature passed HB 2174 (2003) (enrolled as Or Laws 2003, ch 453), which contained the entire version of the Guidelines, in full text. Therefore, because the legislature adopted the Guidelines in full text, unquestionably they are Oregon Session Laws enacted by the legislature, and they are not administrative rules.18

Because HB 2174 included the full text, it included OAR 213-008-0003(2), which imposed ORS 161.605 statutory maxima to limit the lengths of aggravated-departure sentences. Because the legislature passed the bill in 2003, OAR 213-008-0003(2) is later in time than ORS 161.737(2). OAR 213-008-0003(2), which is an Oregon Law, enacted by the legislature, should be construed as controlling over ORS 161.737(2). Therefore, in the context of the Dangerous Offender sentencing scheme, defense counsel should argue that the determinate component sentence cannot exceed the applicable statutory maximum.

As noted above, the Supreme Court allowed review of the lower courts’ decisions, 370 P3d 822—including whether the determinate component could not exceed the statutory maximum, as well as two other matters. In its Media Release, the Supreme Court stated:

“On review, the issues are:

“(1) In imposing the determinate part of a dangerous offender sentence, is the trial court limited by the statutory maximum sentences provided in ORS 161.605?

“(2) Does a trial court err in imposing a dangerous offender sentence, if the presentence investigation report and psychological evaluation required by ORS 161.735 is based on a report and evaluation from a prior case?

“(3) Does a trial court err in admitting reports and testimony derived from a dangerous offender evaluation that was conducted in another case, including statements made by the defendant, in light of ORS 161.735(4), which provides that no statement by a defendant while participating in a dangerous offender evaluation shall be used against the defendant in any other criminal proceeding?”

In State v. Parkerson 1, 319 Or App 477, 511 P3d 25 (2022), aff'd, 371 Or 716, ___ P3d ___ (2023), the defendant was convicted of first-degree assault, which is a Class A felony. The trial court sentenced the defendant under the Dangerous Offender Law. The court imposed the indeterminate 30-year term and a determinate minimum of 260-months. Defendant argued that the 260-month determinate minimum was illegal, because it exceeded by 20 months the 20-year statutory maximum under ORS 161.605(1). The Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals decision, allowing the 260-month determinate minimum. The Supreme Court "conclude[d] that the maximum determinate part of a dangerous offender sentence that may be imposed is twice the presumptive sentence under the sentencing guidelines, that is, 260 months in this case." Parkerson, 371 Or at 736.

7-5.4.1. Jury Trial Rights in Dangerous-Offender Sentencing.

SB 528 explicitly amended the dangerous-offender scheme to try to bring it into compliance with the Apprendi rule. ORS 161.735(9). The 2007 Legislature made further, technical amendments to the dangerous-offender scheme to ensure its compliance with the Apprendi rule. Those amendments are codified at ORS 161.735(6).

But before the effective date of the 2005 amendments, the dangerous-offender statutes expressly mandated bench trials on dangerous-offender findings. For example, the Supreme Court explained that in order to impose a dangerous-offender sentence under former ORS 161.725:

“The court * * * must make findings whether (1) the defendant is dangerous, (2) because of the dangerousness of the defendant an extended period of confinement is required for the protection of the public, and (3) the defendant is suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity.”

Person v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 327 Or App 332, 340, ___ P3d ___ (2023) (the board's order lacked substantial reason, in that the board found that petitioner has "a mental or emotional disturbance, deficiency, condition, or disorder" predisposing him to certain crimes and concluded "therefore" that the condition that made him dangerous (i.e., a severe personality disorder predisposing him to dangerous crimes) and was not in remission, without adequately explaining how that finding leads to that conclusion). But see Guzek v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 325 Or App 795, 530 P3d 510 (2023) (the Court of Appeals affirmed the board’s decision that defendant, who was sentenced in the pre-guidelines era under the Dangerous Offender Law, could not be paroled because he still suffers from “severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity,” ORS 161.735(3)).

State v. Huntley, 302 Or 418, 437-38, 730 P2d 1234 (1986) (emphasis added).

   Guzek v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 325 Or App 795, ___ P3d ___ (2023) (the Court of Appeals affirmed the board’s decision that defendant, who was sentenced in the pre-guidelines era under the Dangerous Offender scheme, could not be paroled because he still suffers from “severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity,” ORS 161.735(3)).

This is clearly demonstrated by former ORS 161.725, which stated:

“(1) Subject to the provisions of ORS 161.737, the maximum term of an indeterminate sentence of imprisonment for a dangerous offender is 30 years, if the court finds that because of the dangerousness of the defendant an extended period of confined correctional treatment or custody is required for the protection of the public and if it [the court] further finds, as provided in ORS 161.735, that one or more of the following grounds exist:

“(a) The defendant is being sentenced for a Class A felony, and the court finds that the defendant is suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another.

“(b) The defendant is being sentenced for a felony that seriously endangered the life or safety of another, the defendant has been previously convicted of a felony not related to the instant crime as a single criminal episode and the court finds that the defendant is suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another.

“(c) The defendant is being sentenced for a felony that seriously endangered the life or safety of another, the defendant has previously engaged in unlawful conduct not related to the instant crime as a single criminal episode that seriously endangered the life or safety of another and the court finds that the defendant is suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another.”

(Emphasis added.)

A recently developed legal theory calls into serious question a key holding in Huntley. There, the Supreme Court held that even “when the only psychiatric report considered by the court * * * concludes that the defendant does not suffer from a severe personality disorder with a propensity toward criminal activity,” 302 Or 422 (emphasis added), the trier of fact—in Huntley, the sentencing court—could “reject the psychiatrist’s conclusion and,” in effect, return its own diagnosis that the defendant “‘suffer[s] from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity.’” Id. at 436. In this post-Blakely era, where the Apprendi rule applies and requires juries, using the reasonable-doubt standard, to serve as the trier of fact on such matters, reconsideration of this aspect of Huntley is demanded.19

Regarding dangerous-offender sentencing generally, Huntley established a still-applicable four-step process for imposing dangerous-offender sentences. But because Huntley specifies “the judge” as the trier of fact during a mere “hearing,” the process Huntley specifies would be unconstitutional. Consequently, Huntley must be read in context with and modified by Apprendi. See also ORS 161.735(9) (establishing that dangerous-offender factors must be considered by the jury during trial, and where both the rules of evidence and the reasonable-doubt standard apply).

As modified—and with the modifications shown in brackets—Huntley’s four-step dangerous-offender process is as follows:

1. “[T]he [trier of fact] must first declare that [it] has reason to believe that because of the dangerousness of the defendant an extended period of confinement is required for the protection of the public and make appropriate findings on the record to justify that belief.” 302 Or at 437.

2. “The [trier of fact] must also find the defendant is being sentenced for a Class A felony or a felony that seriously endangered the life or safety of another and has been previously convicted of a felony not related to the instant crime as a single criminal episode.” Id.

3. “After the [trier of fact] has made these findings, the judge must order a presentence report and psychiatric examination of the defendant. The judge should not merely make a general referral; rather, the judge should specify whatever clues or questions he or she has. If some element in the crime or some facet of the defendant’s history puzzles the judge, he or she should make his query specific. Details of the crime should be made known to the psychiatrist, and whatever denial of the facts is made by the defendant should also be presented.” Id.

4. “After receiving these reports, the judge then must conduct a [penalty-phase trial] unless waived by the defendant. At the [trial] the [trier of fact] must consider the presentence report, the psychiatric report, and the evidence in the case or evidence presented at the presentence hearing. The [trier of fact] then must make findings whether (1) the defendant is dangerous, (2) because of the dangerousness of the defendant an extended period of confinement is required for the protection of the public, and (3) the defendant is suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity.” Id. at 437-38.

Cases involving Class B and C felony convictions require one more modification of Huntley. Where a defendant stands convicted of a Class B or C felony, ORS 161.725(1)(b) or (c) must be used to impose sentence. Unlike ORS 161.725(1)(a), which is used only in Class A felony cases, ORS 161.725(1)(b) and (c) require proof of an entirely different finding—that the defendant’s Class B or C felony “seriously endangered the life or safety of another.” Unlike all Class A felony findings, this “serious-endangerment” finding is offense specific, for it pertains to the factual nature of the crime. Because the serious-endangerment finding is offense specific, it is subject to the rule from State v. Quinn, 290 Or 383, 623 P2d 630 (1981) and State v. Wedge, 293 Or 598, 652 P2d 773 (1982). See State v. Mitchell, 84 Or App 452, 456-67, 734 P2d 379 (1987) (so holding).

The Quinn/Wedge rule states that under the jury-trial clause of Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution, “facts which go to the criminal acts for which a defendant is to be punished must be proved to a jury’s satisfaction unless admitted or waived.” Wedge, 293 Or at 607 (citing Quinn, 290 Or at 406). Thus, unless the defendant admits to the serious-endangerment finding, or waives jury trial on it, he is entitled to a jury trial, using the reasonable-doubt standard, on the finding.20

As seen, one year after Huntley but 13 years before Apprendi, the Court of Appeals held that state constitutional law requires that unless the defendant waives jury or admits to it, the jury serves as the trier of fact on the serious-endangerment factor. In Mitchell, the Court of Appeals reversed the imposition of a dangerous-offender sentence on a Class B felony, because the serious-endangerment finding was made in violation of the Quinn/Wedge rule. In State v. Tanner, 192 Or App 670, 87 P3d 688, rev den, 337 Or 160 (2004), the court reversed the imposition of dangerous-offender sentences on Class B felonies, because the serious-endangerment finding was made in violation of Mitchell (and, by extension, in violation of the Quinn/Wedge rule). Perhaps most dramatically, in Lovelace v. Zenon, 159 Or App 158, 976 P2d 575 (1999), rev den, 329 Or 589 (2000) (De Muniz, P.J.), the defendant was sentenced after the Supreme Court had decided Quinn and Wedge, but before the Court of Appeals had decided Mitchell. The Lovelace court held that trial counsel provided inadequate assistance by failing to raise a Quinn/Wedge objection to the imposition of a dangerous-offender sentence on Class B felony. Lovelace, 159 Or App at 161-62. Cf. Richardson v. Belleque, 277 Or App 615, 620, 628, 373 P3d 1113 (2016) (where prosecution expert based his opinion that criminal defendant suffered from a severe personality disorder, indicating a propensity toward criminal activity, on his conclusion that defendant suffered from a “conduct disorder before the age of 15,” defense counsel provided constitutionally deficient assistance by failing to consult an expert, and thereby position himself to cross-examine the prosecution expert about the possibility “that petitioner might actually have suffered from an adjustment disorder,” rather than a conduct disorder).

In State v. Huntley, the defendant stood convicted of Class A felonies, so the serious-endangerment factor and the constitutional rule from Quinn and Wedge were not at issue. The Court of Appeals decided State v. Mitchell a year later. For cases involving Class B and C felonies, Mitchell’s constitutionally based rule changed the four-step, dangerous-offender process. For Class B and C felony cases, Huntley’s step 2—“the [trier of fact] must also find the defendant is being sentenced for * * * a felony that seriously endangered the life or safety of another”—is step 1 in the process.

Consequent to changing Huntley’s step 2 into step 1 for cases involving Class B and C felonies, Huntley’s step 1 becomes step 2. Huntley’s steps 3 and 4 remain the same.

When the state seeks a dangerous-offender sentence for a defendant convicted of a Class B or C felony, so the state must prove that the defendant’s felony “endangered the life or safety of the victim,” ORS 161.725(1)(b) or (c) (emphasis added), defense counsel should recognize that although the definition of the “life * * * of the victim” alternative is clear, the definition of the “safety of the victim” alternative is not. But given the similarity between the phrase “life or safety of another,” and the definition of “serious physical injury”—i.e., an “injury which creates a substantial risk of death or which causes serious and protracted disfigurement, protracted impairment of health or protracted loss or impairment of the function of any bodily organ,” ORS 161.015(8)—the serious-physical-injury definition should be context in construing the dangerous-offender statute’s phrase “endangered the life or safety of the victim.” That construction would restrict the state’s ability to prove the serious-endangerment factor.21

As modified by Mitchell for cases involving Class B and C felonies, if all of the requirements of Huntley’s four-step process are met, the court then has discretion to impose a dangerous-offender sentence. The court decides whether to impose a dangerous-offender sentence at all and, if so, of what length. See ORS 161.725(1) (“the maximum term of an indeterminate sentence of imprisonment for a dangerous offender is 30 years”); ORS 161.735(6) (if findings are properly made in the course of steps 1-4, “the court may sentence the defendant as a dangerous offender” (emphasis added)).

Finally, it should be noted that for a time, Sixth Amendment principles did not apply to aggravating factors used to impose aggravated-departure sentences, if those sentences served as minimum terms in conjunction with greater, indeterminate dangerous offender terms. Harris v. United States, 536 US 545, 122 S Ct 2406, 153 L Ed 2d 524 (2002). In State v. Stephens, 223 Or App 644, 198 P3d 423 (2008), rev den, 346 Or 11 (2009), the Court of Appeals applied Harris. There, the trial court imposed 30-year indeterminate dangerous offender sentences on multiple counts. In conjunction with each of those sentences, the court imposed aggravated-departure sentences as minimum terms. The court relied on non-juried aggravating factors to impose the minimums. The defendant argued that the trial court’s reliance on the non-juried factors violated Sixth Amendment principles. Relying on Harris, the Court of Appeals rejected that argument. The court held that Sixth Amendment principles did not apply to the aggravating factors, because the departure sentences, as minimums, did not “exceed the prescribed [30-year] statutory maximum sentence for [each] crime.” Stephens, 223 Or App at 651.

But four years after the decision in Stephens, the United States Supreme Court decided Alleyne v. United States, 570 US 99,133 S Ct 2151, 186 L Ed 2d 314 (2013). There, the trial court had imposed a life sentence. The law further required a presumptive minimum term of five years, but allowed an enhanced, seven-year minimum. The sentencing court relied on non-juried findings to impose the enhanced, seven-year minimum. The Court reversed, and did so by expressly overruling Harris. The Court stated:

“Harris drew a distinction between facts that increase the statutory maximum and facts that increase only the mandatory minimum. We conclude that this distinction is inconsistent with our decision in Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 US 466, 120 S Ct 2348, 147 L Ed 2d 435 (2000), and with the original meaning of the Sixth Amendment. Any fact that, by law, increases the penalty for a crime is an ‘element’ that must be submitted to the jury and found beyond a reasonable doubt. See id., at 483, n. 10, 490. Mandatory minimum sentences increase the penalty for a crime. It follows, then, that any fact that increases the mandatory minimum is an ‘element’ that must be submitted to the jury. Accordingly, Harris is overruled.”

Alleyne, 570 US at 103.

There can be no question that Alleyne overruled Stephens sub silentio. That means that under Alleyne, the aggravating factors on which a trial court might rely, to impose an aggravated departure as the minimum component of a longer, dangerous offender sentence, are subject to Sixth Amendment principles. Therefore, the factors used to impose an aggravated departure, as the minimum component, are subject to the Apprendi rule.

7-5.5. Place of Incarceration.

The 1997 Legislature ordered that OAR 213-008-0003 be amended to reflect the place-of-incarceration requirements of the 1995 Legislature’s Senate Bill 1145. The 1997 amendments are contained in OAR 213-008-0003(4). It states that if a defendant’s sentence is greater than one year, he serves it in the DOC. If his sentence is one year or less, he serves it in county jail, with two exceptions. First, a sentence of one year or less that runs consecutively with a sentence of greater than a year is served in the DOC. ORS 137.124(2)(b). Second, a sentence of any length imposed on a conviction for third-degree assault under ORS 166.070(2) is served in the DOC. OAR 213-008-0003(5).

§ 7-6. OAR 213-008-0005 DISPOSITIONAL DEPARTURE LIMITATIONS.

OAR 213-008-0005 sets the upper limits on aggravated-departure sentences, when the court imposes incarceration instead of probation. It provides:

(1) When a sentencing judge imposes a prison term as a dispositional departure, the term of incarceration shall be:

(a) Up to six months for offenses classified in Crime Categories 1 and 2, or grid blocks 3-G, 3-H and 3-I;

(b) Up to twelve months for offenses classified in grid blocks 3-A through 3-F, 4-C through 4-I, and 5-G through 5-I; and

(c) Up to eighteen months for offenses classified in grid blocks 5-F, 6-F through 6-I, and 7-F through 7-I.

(2) When a sentencing judge imposes a prison term as a dispositional departure, the term of post-prison supervision shall be determined by the crime seriousness category of the most serious current crime of conviction as required by OAR 213-005-0002.

(3) Any sentence inconsistent with the provisions of this rule shall constitute an additional departure and shall require substantial and compelling reasons independent of the reasons given for the dispositional departure. Such a sentence shall not exceed double the maximum duration set forth in section (1) of this rule.

(4) Any sentence imposed pursuant to this section that is 12 months or less shall be served at the direction of the supervisory authority. Any sentence imposed pursuant to this section that is greater than 12 months shall be served in the legal and physical custody of the Department.

(5) Notwithstanding section (4) of this rule, terms of incarceration 12 months or less imposed pursuant to ORS 166.070(2) shall be served in the legal and physical custody of the Department.

7-6.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-008-0005 applies where the sentencing court dispositionally departs by imposing “prison when the presumptive sentence is probation.” OAR 213-003-0001(6). OAR 213-008-0005(1) limits the duration of such departures. Dispositional departure maximums are six, 12, or 18 months, depending on the crime’s grid block. OAR 213-008-0005(1)(a)-(c). There is no requirement that the court impose more than a 12-month sentence. State v. Pemberton, 226 Or App 285, 203 P3d 326 (2009). As will be discussed below, OAR 213-008-0005(3) allows a court, after departing dispositionally, then to depart durationally (i.e., impose a “double-barreled” departure).

OAR 213-008-0005(2) makes clear that when a court imposes a dispositional departure sentence of imprisonment, the court must also impose a term of post-prison supervision (PPS). See Chapter 4 (discussing PPS terms). Like the findings on which a durational departure is based, the findings on which a dispositional departure is based are subject to the Apprendi rule. State v. Buehler, 206 Or App 167, 136 P3d 64 (2006).

§ 7-6.2. Dispositional-Durational Departures.

“OAR [213-008-0005(3)] authorizes a sentence in excess of [an] initial term of dispositional departure, so long as the additional departure is supported by aggravating circumstances that are substantial and compelling and that are different from the factors used to support the initial departure.” State v. Tracy, 116 Or App 329, 332, 840 P2d 1380 (1992) (emphasis added). Thus, OAR 213-008-0005(3) authorizes the sentencing court to impose a sentence for a single crime containing both dispositional and durational departure components. But in imposing these dispositional-durational departures, or “double-barreled” departures, the court must make clear that the factors on which it bases the durational component are different from those on which it bases the dispositional component. See State v. Gibson, 183 Or App 25, 35, 51 P3d 619 (2002) (trial court committed reversible error by imposing double-barreled departure without clearly explaining that it was relying on independent aggravating factor to impose durational component of sentence); State v. Ferrell, 146 Or App 638, 933 P2d 973 (1997) (court committed reversible error when it relied on same four departure factors both to depart dispositionally and durationally).

The double-barreled departure maximum for a given crime is double its dispositional departure maximum. Hence, double-barreled departure maximums are 12, 24, and 36 months, depending on the crime’s grid block. OAR 213-008-0005(1)(a)-(c) and (3). But those double-barreled maximums are, or arguably are, limited by the constitutional principles discussed immediately below.

§ 7-6.2.1. Proportionality and Cruel and Unusual Problems Attendant to Dispositional-Durational Departures.

Owing to the sentencing court’s authority to impose double-barreled departures, persons in certain presumptive-probation grid blocks may incur longer incarceration sentences than they could incur if they were in presumptive-incarceration grid blocks. In State v. Koch, 169 Or App 223, 7 P3d 769 (2000), the court held that such a result violates the proportionate sentencing requirements of Article I, section 16, of the Oregon Constitution.

Simplified, in Koch the defendant was convicted of first-degree forgery. His grid block was 3-B, for which OAR 213-008-0005(3) would authorize a double-barreled departure maximum of 24 months’ imprisonment. If his forgery had been subclassified at the higher level of 4, his grid block would have been 4-B, which carries a presumptive maximum of nine months’ imprisonment, and a departure maximum of 18 months. This means that if the defendant had been convicted of a forgery with the higher, level 4 subclassification, i.e., if he had committed a worse crime, his maximum sentence would have been six months less. The court declared that absurd result a proportionality violation, and held that to avoid it, the 18-month durational departure maximum for the 4-B grid block set the double-barreled departure maximum for the defendant’s 3-B grid block. Koch, 169 Or App at 230.

The Koch limitation on the maximum lengths of double-barreled departure sentences applies to the following grid blocks: 3-A, 3-B, 3-D, 3-E, 4-D, and 4-E.

Defense counsel also may argue that a similar limitation applies when the defendant’s criminal-history score subjects him to a maximum sentence that is greater than what he could incur with a higher criminal-history score. For example, for a defendant in presumptive probation grid blocks 4-I to 4-C, OAR 213-008-0005(3) would authorize a double-barreled departure sentence of 24 months’ incarceration. But if that defendant were in presumptive incarceration grid block 4-B, which has a presumptive maximum of nine months, his departure maximum would be 18 months. OAR 213-008-0003(2). That term is six months less than the 24-month double-barreled departure the defendant could receive if he were in one of the lower presumptive probation grid blocks.

Thus, OAR 213-008-0005(3) yields this anomaly: Relative to defendants who committed the same crime but have more serious criminal history scores, defendants may get more severe sentences if they have lower criminal history scores so are in lower grid blocks. Defense counsel should argue that subjecting the defendant to a greater sentence, effectively because he has a lesser criminal record, violates the cruel and unusual punishment clause, as well as the proportionality clause of Article I, section 16. Cf. Wheeler, 343 Or at 674–75 (the court’s case law applying the constitutional prohibitions on disproportionate sentences and cruel and unusual punishments, Or Const, Art I, § 16, “permit the legislature to [specify] enhanced sentences on recidivists, even if those sentences would be disproportionate when applied to a defendant without prior convictions”).22

7-6.3. Place of Incarceration.

Like OAR 213-008-0003, the 1997 Legislature ordered that OAR 213-008-0005 be amended to reflect the place-of-incarceration requirements of the 1995 Legislature’s Senate Bill 1145. The 1997 amendments are contained in OAR 213-008-0005(4). It states that if a defendant’s sentence is greater than one year, he serves it in the DOC. If his sentence is one year or less, he serves it in county jail, with two exceptions. First, a sentence of one year or less that runs consecutively with a sentence of greater than a year is served in the DOC. ORS 137.124(2)(b). Second, a sentence of any length imposed on a conviction for third-degree assault under ORS 166.070(2)is served in the DOC. OAR 213-008-0005(5).

§ 7-7. OAR 213-008-0006 DEPARTURE LIMITATIONS ON THE USE OF SANCTION UNITS.

In cases where a court imposes probation, so also may impose sanction units, OAR 213-008-0006 sets the upper limit on aggravated departures as to the number of sanction units. The rule provides:

(1) A departure on the number of sanction units imposed as part of a probationary sentence shall not total more than double the maximum number of sanction units permitted as part of the sentence as described in OAR 213-005-0011(2) and (3).

(2) Notwithstanding the provisions of section (1) of this rule, the maximum number of sanction units that may be used to impose a jail term as part of a probationary sentence shall be limited to the maximum number of sanction units included in the sentence as provided by OAR 213-005-0011(2) and (3).

7-7.1. Intent of Provision.

This provision authorizes sentencing courts to depart as to the number of sanction units imposed as part of a probationary sentence, and to depart as to the number of sanction units the court may order be satisfied by jail incarceration. Sanction units are generally discussed in Chapter 5.

7-7.2. Sanction Unit Departures.

According to guidelines commentary, OAR 213-008-0006(1) “sets a departure limitation on the imposition of custody units comparable to the limitations on durational departures described in OAR [213-008-0004]. The basis for both limitations is the same: to reduce the potential for sentence disparity and to maintain effective control over the use of available correctional resources.” Implementation Manual at 139. Commentary explains the limitation with the following example: “The maximum number of custody units allowed for an offender classified in Grid Block 6-G is 180 units. The maximum use of custody units for a departure under this rule is a total of 360 custody units.” Id.

7-7.3. Jail Term Departures.

According to guidelines commentary, OAR 213-008-0006(2) “limits the use of jail as part of a departure sentence. The number of [sanction] units which may be used to impose a jail term for the departure sentence may not exceed the total number of [sanction] units which could be imposed as part of the presumptive sentence.” Implementation Manual at 139. Commentary explains the subsection with the following example:

“An offender is classified in grid block 2-D for violations of wildlife laws. The number of [sanction] units that may be imposed as part of the presumptive sentence is 90. Thirty of these units may be used to impose a jail term. If the sentencing judge finds grounds for departure to increase the jail term imposed, the maximum departure permitted by this rule would be a jail term of no more than 90 days.”

Id.

§ 7-8. OAR 213-008-0007 DEPARTURE LIMITATIONS IN CONSECUTIVE SENTENCES.

In cases where the court imposes durational aggravated-departure terms of incarceration, OAR 213-008-0007 limits the extent to which the court may run the terms consecutively. It provides:

(1) The court may depart from the limits established by OAR 213-012-0002 for consecutive sentences only if the judge finds substantial and compelling reasons to impose a departure sentence for any individual offense being sentenced consecutively.

(2) Except as provided by section (3) of this rule, the sentencing judge shall comply with the provisions of OAR 213-008-0001 to 213-008-0006 when a departure sentence is imposed for an offense sentenced consecutively.

(3) When a departure sentence is imposed for any individual offense sentenced consecutively, the incarceration term of that departure sentence shall not exceed twice the maximum incarceration term that may be imposed for that offense as provided in OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a). This limit on the duration of a departure sentence does not apply to any indeterminate sentence imposed on a dangerous offender under ORS 161.725 and 161.737 nor to consecutive sentences imposed for crimes that have different victims.

7-8.1. Intent of Provision.

This provision works in conjunction with OAR 213-012-0002(2). Together, OAR 213-008-0007 and OAR 213-012-0002(2) create the so-called “400 percent” rule. That rule applies in extremely limited situations. When it applies, it limits the total consecutive departure sentence for a string of crimes to four times (400 percent of) the maximum presumptive term for the defendant’s primary offense (i.e., the offense which has the highest crime seriousness ranking). For further discussion of the 400 percent rule, see Chapter 8.
