It is easy to confuse the concepts of merger and consecutive sentences. Merger occurs when two or more convictions combine or are subsumed into one conviction. The result when two or more convictions merge is that there is only one conviction. It follows that, because there is only one conviction, there can be only one sentence. Practitioners and judges often conceive of merger as purely an issue of sentencing, in that convictions “merge for sentencing purposes.” That is incorrect, as explained below.

Sentences for distinct convictions that do not merge may be run concurrently or consecutively. If they are run concurrently, they are still separate sentences (i.e., they have not “merged,” just because the sentences are run concurrently).

This chapter first discusses situations in which a sentencing court must merge multiple convictions. This chapter then discusses situations in which a court may or may not impose consecutive sentences when a defendant incurs multiple convictions. Finally, this chapter discusses the guidelines’ limitations on the total lengths of consecutive sentences.

§ 8-1. MERGER OF CONVICTIONS.

In general, the merger doctrine permits multiple convictions for a single criminal course of conduct only when the legislature has “made its intent” to allow multiple convictions “crystal clear.” Missouri v. Hunter, 459 US 359, 368, 103 S Ct 673, 74 L Ed 2d 535 (1983). This doctrine is based on a double jeopardy theory that prohibits multiple convictions for a defendant’s single act. See Arthur W. Campbell, Law of Sentencing, § 9:21 at 422-23 (3d ed. 2004). See also United States v. Davenport, 519 F3d 940, 943 (9th Cir 2008) (in the absence of clear legislative intent to punish a defendant for multiple convictions stemming from same conduct, double jeopardy requires merger).

Oregon’s main statute regulating merger is ORS 161.067. It substantially replaced case law that regulated merger decisions. See, e.g., State v. Crotsley, 308 Or 272, 779 P2d 600 (1989), for background about merger case law.

§ 8-1.1. The “Anti-Merger” Statute.

ORS 161.067, the “anti-merger” statute, describes three circumstances in which convictions stemming from the same criminal episode nonetheless may result in separately punishable convictions: (i) when they violate different laws, each with distinct elements; (ii) when they involve multiple victims; or (iii) when they violate the same law multiple times against the same victim, and the violations are separated by a pause sufficient to afford the defendant an opportunity to renounce the criminal intent.

ORS 161.067 provides:

“(1) When the same conduct or criminal episode violates two or more statutory provisions and each provision requires proof of an element that the others do not, there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are separate statutory violations.

“(2) When the same conduct or criminal episode, though violating only one statutory provision involves two or more victims, there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are victims. However, two or more persons owning joint interests in real or personal property shall be considered a single victim for purposes of determining the number of separately punishable offenses if the property is the subject of one of the following crimes:

“(a) Theft as defined in ORS 164.015.

“(b) Unauthorized use of a vehicle as defined in ORS 164.135.

“(c) Criminal possession of rented or leased personal property as defined in ORS 164.140.

“(d) Criminal possession of a rented or leased motor vehicle as defined in ORS 164.138.

“(e) Burglary as defined in ORS 164.215 or 164.225.

“(f) Criminal trespass as defined in ORS 164.243, 164.245, 164.255, 164.265 or 164.278.

“(g) Arson and related offenses as defined in ORS 164.315, 164.325 or 164.335.

“(h) Forgery and related offenses as defined in ORS 165.002 to 165.070.

“(3) When the same conduct or criminal episode violates only one statutory provision and involves only one victim, but nevertheless involves repeated violations of the same statutory provision against the same victim, there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are violations, except that each violation, to be separately punishable under this subsection, must be separated from other such violations by a sufficient pause in the defendant’s criminal conduct to afford the defendant an opportunity to renounce the criminal intent. Each method of engaging in deviate sexual intercourse as defined in ORS 163.305, and each method of engaging in unlawful sexual penetration as defined in ORS 163.408 and 163.411 shall constitute separate violations of their respective statutory provisions for purposes of determining the number of statutory violations.”

For background on the general application of this statute, see State v. Parkins, 346 Or 333, 211 P3d 262 (2009) (multiple sex abuse convictions based on alternative statutory theories merge); State v. White, 346 Or 275, 211 P3d 248 (2009) (multiple robbery convictions based on alternative statutory theories merge); State v. Brown, 276 Or App 578, 368 P3d 79 (2016) (where defendant was convicted of a burglary, during which he stole a safe containing a firearm and some coins, and also was convicted of two counts of theft, one involving the firearm and the other involving the coins, Court of Appeals held the trial court erred in relying on ORS 167.067(1) to decline to merge the theft convictions, but remanded for determination of whether anti-merger provision of ORS 167.067(2) applies); State v. Ascencio-Galindo, 220 Or App 600, 188 P3d 392, rev den, 345 Or 175 (2008) (trial court erred in failing to merge convictions for commercial drug offense and substantial quantity deliveries of controlled substance, where convictions stemmed from single delivery of a controlled substance); State v. Rodvelt, 187 Or App 128, 130, 66 P3d 577, rev den, 336 Or 17 (2003) (trial court erred in failing to merge first-degree criminal mischief into convictions for contemporaneous fourth-degree assaults).

§ 8-1.1.1. Different Elements.

The first provision of the statute—that there is no merger if the charged statues each have an element the others do not, ORS 161.067(1)—“is controlled by the elements in the criminal statute and not by factual circumstances.” State v. Sparks, 150 Or App 293, 296, 946 P2d 314 (1997), rev den, 326 Or 389 (1998) (citing State v. Atkinson, 98 Or App 48, 777 P2d 1010 (1989)). In other words, if the crimes’ statutory elements allow some conceivable way of committing each crime without also committing the other crime, the crimes do not merge even if the manner in which the defendant committed the crimes could establish that the defendant did not intend to commit one of the crimes without committing the other. Compare Sparks, 150 Or App at 296 (during course of single trespass, where defendant committed the two separate crimes of theft and criminal mischief and was convicted of two burglaries, convictions merged because they “do not require proof of separate statutory elements”), with State v. Luers, 211 Or App 34, 70, 153 P3d 688, on recons, 213 Or App 389, 160 P3d 1013 (2007) (conviction for possession of a destructive device does not merge into related conviction for manufacture of destructive device), and State v. Brown, 106 Or App 291, 296-97, 807 P2d 316, rev den, 311 Or 427 (1991) (conviction for possession of drugs does not merge into conviction for manufacture of same drugs).

But if the charged crimes may be committed in alternative ways, only the elements actually charged are analyzed. See State v. Reiland, 153 Or App 601, 604, 958 P2d 900 (1998) (“[i]f a statute defining a crime contains alternate elements that constitute the crime, we look at the combination of elements that constitutes the crimes for which the defendant was convicted”); State v. Mezick, 109 Or App 563, 565, 820 P2d 849 (1991). Crime subclassification factors (discussed in Chapter 2) are not used in determining which convictions merge. State v. Baker, 265 Or App 500, 336 P3d 547 (2014), rev den, 356 Or 685 (2015). But cf. State v. Flores, 259 Or App 141, 313 P3d 378 (2013), rev den, 354 Or 735 (2014) (holding that “with a firearm” allegation that supported the gun minimum counted as an element for purposes of merger).

Furthermore, if a single statute defining a single crime contains alternative means of committing the crime, convictions for multiple alternatives merge if the statute demonstrates that the legislature intended to address a single concern as opposed to separate and distinct concerns. Compare State v. Barrett, 331 Or 27, 10 P3d 901 (2000) (holding that multiple convictions for aggravated murder merge, when convictions were based on single murder with multiple aggravating theories; but also holding that separate convictions for crimes forming bases for aggravating theories do not merge into aggravated murder conviction, and separate convictions for crimes underlying felony murder conviction do not merge into felony murder), State v. Tucker, 315 Or 321, 331, 845 P2d 904 (1993) (conviction for predicate felonies underlying conviction for aggravated felony murder merge into conviction for aggravated felony murder), State v. Slatton, 268 Or App 556, 343 P3d 253 (2015) (convictions for multiple theories of first-degree theft merge into single conviction), State v. Miller, 309 Or 362, 369, 788 P2d 974 (1990) (convictions for multiple theories of DUII merge into single conviction), State v. Kizer, 308 Or 238, 242-44, 779 P2d 604 (1989) (convictions for multiple theories of forgery merge into single conviction), and Luers, 211 Or App at 67 (multiple counts of arson, each alleging danger of physical injury to a separate person, merge into one conviction where the damaged property belongs to one owner), with Crotsley, 308 Or 272 (two convictions for first- and third-degree rape and two convictions for first- and third-degree sodomy arising from a single incident do not merge into single convictions for first degree rape and sodomy, as each statutory alternative addresses a separate and distinct legislative concern), and State v. Johnson, 174 Or App 27, 25 P3d 353 (2001) (two convictions for a single incident that violated two separate provisions of first-degree robbery statute do not merge).

In Martinez v. Cain, 366 Or 136, 458 P3d 670 (2020), the defendant was convicted of attempted aggravated murder and first-degree robbery, with the robbery being the attempted aggravated murder charge’s predicate felony. The defendant argued on appeal, and then on post-conviction relief, that under ORS 161.067(1), the sentencing court should have merged the two convictions. He relied on State v. Tucker, 315 Or 321, 331, 845 P2d 904 (1993), which held that convictions for predicate felonies, underlying a conviction for aggravated felony murder, merge into the conviction for aggravated felony murder. The post-conviction court held that Tucker did not control, because State v. Barrett, 331 Or 27, 10 P3d 901 (2000) had overruled Tucker. The Court of Appeals affirmed.

Although dictum in Barrett could be read as overruling Tucker, in Martinez the Supreme Court disavowed that dictum. The court explained that Barrett’s “erroneous premise was the assertion * * * that the predicate felonies of aggravated felony murder were alternative ways to prove ‘aggravation.’” Martinez, 366 Or at 149. But, the court explained, “A predicate felony was not a method of showing ‘aggravation,’ but a necessary element of the lesser offense of felony murder[.]” Id.

Thus, Martinez makes clear that lesser-included offenses merge into their associated greater offenses. After all, lesser-included offenses have all the elements of their associated greater offenses, and nothing more, so ORS 161.067(1) requires they merge into the greater offense. Martinez further makes clear that Tucker is still good law. Therefore, a predicate felony in a felony murder charge is a lesser-included offense of the felony murder charge. So if the defendant is convicted of both charges, the conviction of the lesser-included predicate felony merges into greater felony murder conviction. See also State v. Lugo, 322 Or App 477, 482, 520 P3d 917 (2022) (where defendant was convicted of both first-degree and third-degree sexual abuse, the trial court erred in failing to merge the lesser-included offense into the greater offense); State v. Postlewait, 312 Or App 467, 493 P3d 35 (2021) (relying on Martinez v. Cain, the Court of Appeals accepted the state’s concession that the trial court plainly erred in failing to merge defendant’s convictions for second-degree robbery and third-degree theft).

In State v. Breshears, 281 Or App 552, 554, 383 P3d 345 (2016), the defendant was convicted of one count each of second-degree sexual abuse and third-degree rape, for having sexual intercourse with a 13-year-old when he was 22 years old. In addressing the defendant’s claim that his convictions merge, the Court of Appeals explained that “merger is controlled by the elements of the crimes that are alleged—not by the particular facts that the state might prove to establish those elements.” 281 Or App at 558. The relevant element of both charges was lack of consent; therefore, proof of the rape charge necessarily proved the sexual abuse charge. “Because proof of the elements of third-degree rape will necessarily prove the elements of second-degree sexual abuse as alleged here, defendant’s guilt determinations must merge under ORS 161.067(1).” Id. at 559.

Consistent with the double jeopardy theory that prohibits multiple convictions for a single act if the legislature had not expressed a “crystal clear” intent to allow multiple convictions, Hunter, 459 US at 368, “[d]oubts about [such] legislative intent are to be resolved in favor of the defendant.” State v. Linthwaite, 52 Or App 511, 523 n 12, 628 P2d 1250 (1981), modified, 295 Or 162, 665 P2d 863 (1983). Accord Whalen v. United States, 445 US 684, 694, 100 S Ct 1432, 63 L. Ed. 2d 715 (1980). The fact that multiple counts arise out of the same statutory subsection “weighs in favor of merger.” Luers, 211 Or App at 62.

§ 8-1.1.2. Multiple Victims.

The statute’s second anti-merger provision is that if there are multiple victims, there may be as many convictions as there are victims. ORS 161.067(2). The determination of who is a victim of a crime, and how many victims there are in any course of conduct can be complicated. Where the statute defining a crime does not expressly identify the person who qualifies as a “victim,” the court examines the statute to identify the gravamen of the crime and to determine the class of persons whom the legislature intended to directly protect. See State v. Glaspey, 337 Or 558, 100 P3d 730 (2005) (child who witnesses domestic assault is not an independent victim of felony fourth-degree assault, ORS 163.160(3); therefore, trial court erroneously imposed separate convictions for crimes under ORS 161.067(2)); Luers, 211 Or App at 64 (multiple counts of arson, each alleging danger of physical injury to a separate person, merge into one conviction where the damaged property belongs to one owner).

State v. Martin, 322 Or App 266, 273, 519 P3d 132 (2022) (the trial court did not err in refusing to merge defendant’s five convictions of public indecency, because the offenses were separate criminal episodes based on different times, places, and circumstances);

But see State v. Moncada, 241 Or App 202, 212, 250 P3d 31 (2011), rev den, 351 Or 546 (2012) (trial court properly declined to merge hit and run convictions that had two separate victims). State v. Williams, 229 Or App 79, 209 P3d 842 (2009) (trial court properly imposed two robbery convictions, for one convenience store robbery, when he used or threatened force against a clerk and a customer, as each use or threat of force had a separate victim).

Finally, in State v. Jenkins, 280 Or App 691, 699, 383 P3d 395 (2016), the defendant stood convicted of four counts of witness tampering, stemming from a single letter he sent to his mother asking her and his brother to fail to appear or change their stories before the grand jury. The Court of Appeals held that these convictions all were based on a single act (sending the letter), and had only a single victim (the state). Therefore, the trial court erred in failing to merge the convictions.

See also State v. Paye, 310 Or App 408, 486 P3d 808 (2021) (multiple theories of promoting prostitution charges merge, because the crimes have only one victim, that being the state).

§ 8-1.1.3. Sufficient Pause.

The third anti-merger provision dictates that there is no merger when there is a “sufficient pause” between repeated violations of the same crime involving the same victim. ORS 161.067(3). It is an open question whether the sufficient-pause anti-merger provision is subject to the jury-trial requirements discussed in Chapter 1. Cf. State v. Kayfes, 213 Or App 543, 558, 162 P3d 308, rev den, 343 Or 690 (2007).

“Sufficient pause” has been defined as a “temporary or brief cessation of a defendant’s criminal conduct that occurs between repeated violations and is so marked in scope or quality that it affords a defendant the opportunity to renounce his * * * criminal intent.” State v. McConville, 243 Or App 275, 281, 259 P3d 947 (2011) (citing State v. Huffman, 234 Or App 177, 184, 227 P3d 1206 (2010)). The Court of Appeals has also explained it as requiring evidence that “‘something of significance’ occurred between the defendant’s sequential acts.” State v. Nelson, 282 Or App 427, 446, 386 P3d 73 (2016). Cf. State v. Avila, 283 Or App 262, 268-69, 388 P3d 383 (2016) (holding that two counts of sexual abuse merged when the record lacked “any evidence as to the duration of time (if any) that elapsed between the two offenses, or whether the offenses were separated by some intervening event”).

In State v. White, 341 Or 624, 636, 147 P3d 313 (2006), the court abrogated the “quasi-merger” principle it had announced four years earlier in State v. Barnum, 333 Or 297, 39 P3d 178 (2002). The White court held that where the defendant was convicted of two counts of burglary for committing crimes of assault and menacing during a single trespass, and there was not “a sufficient pause” between the burglaries, his separate burglary convictions must merge into single conviction. White, 341 Or at 640-41. See also State v. Sanders, 185 Or App 125, 130, 57 P3d 963 (2002), on recons, 189 Or App 107, 74 P3d 1105 (2003), rev den, 336 Or 657 (2004) (second-degree assault conviction for beating victim with rod over torso and legs, and first-degree assault conviction for fracturing victim’s wrist as she attempted to ward off blow to head, arose from same criminal episode and required merger of convictions).

For additional cases reversing trial courts’ erroneous reliance on ORS 167.067(3)’s “sufficient pause” anti-merger provision, see

State v. Howard, 325 Or App 696, 716-17, 529 P3d 247 (2023) (because the two counts of second-degree abuse of corpse involved neither separate episodes nor a “sufficient pause,” the trial court erred in merger the convictions); State v. Moscote-Saavedra, 320 Or App 682, 700, 514 P3d 1169 (2022) (defendant’s intervening sexual conduct did not establish a “sufficient pause” between his acts of sexual abuse because there was an insufficient level of detail about that intervening conduct from which to infer that the intervening conduct was temporally or qualitatively distinct from his acts of sexual abuse); State v. Moore, 319 Or App 136, 147-48, 510 P3d 907 (2022) (the trial court erred by failing to merge two counts of first-degree rape, because there was not a sufficient pause as defined by ORS 161.067(3); State v. Coats, 312 Or App 213, 218, 496 P3d 89 (2021) (trial court erred in failing to merge defendant’s convictions for taking without permission a trailer and an ATV from one business during the same criminal episode, because the “record does not support an inference that defendant’s unlawful use of the trailer is separated from his unlawful use of the ATV by a sufficient pause within the meaning of ORS 161.067(3)”); State v. Lasheki, 309 Or App 140, 481 P3d 966 (2021) (three counts of sexual abuse based on three acts of touching different body parts were not separated by a sufficient pause as to preclude merger);

State v. Dugan, 282 Or App 768, 387 P3d 439 (2016) (where sexual abuse convictions were based on touching two of victim’s body parts in a single incident, trial court erred in failing to merge convictions), and Nelson, 282 Or App 427 (where defendant’s conduct involved sexual contact with three different parts of victim’s body, and happened in one location without pause, trial court erred in failing to merge convictions).

§ 8-1.1.3.1. Plea Negotiations.

Although not stated in ORS 161.067, the parties may avoid statutory merger principles through plea negotiations. In State v. Young, 188 Or App 247, 71 P3d 119, rev den, 336 Or 125 (2003), the defendant faced six charges, all of which would merge into a single crime of conviction. Pursuant to a plea agreement, he agreed to incur three convictions and have the other three charges dismissed. If he breached his obligations under the agreement, he could be tried, convicted, and sentenced on all six charges, including the three previously dismissed charges.

Young was convicted of the first three charges, and the court dismissed the other three charges. But after he breached his obligations under the agreement, he was convicted and sentenced on the previously dismissed charges. On appeal, he claimed that the sentencing court lacked jurisdiction over the other three convictions, arguing that they should merge into the first three convictions. The Court of Appeals rejected that claim. It explained that the defendant “gave up his right to seek merger when he agreed that” if he breached the agreement, “he could be tried and sentenced on the” other three charges. Young, 188 Or App at 253.

For judgments or orders entered by the trial court on or after January 1, 2018, Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 28, this limitation on appellate review is codified. Under ORS 138.105(5)(b), the appellate court may not review a non-merger decision that resulted from an agreement between the state and the defendant. Conversely, under ORS 138.115(4)(b), the appellate court may not review a merger decision that resulted from an agreement between the state and the defendant).

§ 8-1.2. Crimes Against the State (Victimless Crimes) and the Public At Large.

In some cases involving crimes against the state—so-called “victimless” crimes—the Court of Appeals has applied case law predating ORS 161.067 to trial courts’ merger decisions. Under that case law, “‘merger’ occurs ‘when the completion of one offense necessarily includes commission of acts sufficient to constitute violation [sic] of another statute.’” State v. Eastman, 112 Or App 256, 259, 828 P2d 484 (1992) (quoting State v. Cloutier, 286 Or 579, 586, 596 P2d 1278 (1979)).

In Eastman, the defendant was convicted of four counts of failure to appear (FTA), for violating four separate release agreements that all required his appearance on one specific day for sentencing. The court held that because this scenario did not fit within the merger statutes, pre-statute case law governed. Under that case law, Eastman could incur multiple FTA convictions, because “the gravamen” of each offense was the violation of a separate release agreement; however, the Eastman court concluded that there could only be one sentence, for the legislature did not intend for multiple punishments for one act (not showing up for court). 112 Or App at 259-60.

In contrast, in State v. Camarena-Velasco, 207 Or App 19, 22, 139 P3d 979 (2006), the court held that a defendant who had signed only one release agreement in connection with two different cases should only incur one conviction. Relying on Eastman and the principle that “the state is not a ‘victim’ for purposes of” the merger statutes, the court held that the two FTA convictions imposed by the trial court should merge. 207 Or App at 22-23.

However, it is likely that ORS 161.067 should control merger analyses in all cases, including crimes against the state. In State v. Torres, 249 Or App 571, 277 P3d 641 (2012), the defendant was found guilty of 21 counts of felon in possession of a firearm. ORS 166.270. Under ORS 161.067(3), the trial court merged all of the convictions into a single conviction. The state appealed and argued that ORS 161.067 did not authorize merger because the offenses were “victimless crimes.” The Court of Appeals rejected that argument, overruled its prior cases that had held that the state was not a “victim” under ORS 161.067, and concluded that “the public” was the victim of felon in possession. Because there was no proof of a sufficient pause, the trial court properly merged the convictions under ORS 161.067(3). Torres, 249 Or App at 578.

See also

Paye, 310 Or App 408 (multiple theories of promoting prostitution charges merge, because the crimes have only one victim, that being the state);

State v. Anderson, 264 Or App 183, 331 P3d 1052, adh’d to as modified on recons, 265 Or App 222, 333 P3d 1250, rev den, 356 Or 575 (2014) (applying ORS 161.067(3) to require merger of possession of controlled substance charges). Cf. State v. Davis, 265 Or App 425, 335 P3d 322 (2014), rev den, 365 Or 837 (2015) (affirming non-merger of felon in possession charges under ORS 161.067(3), because evidence supported an inference of a sufficient pause between the defendant’s acquisition of the guns).

PRACTICE TIP: Torres arguably overruled the holding in Eastman that ORS 161.067 does not govern merger of FTA charges based on multiple release agreements. Because ORS 161.067 governs merger even for crimes against the state, violations of multiple release agreements on a single date should merge into a single conviction—the violations all involve the same statutory provision, ORS 161.067(1), the same victim, ORS 161.067(2), and the same conduct, ORS 161.067(3). Consequently, defense counsel should object to multiple FTA convictions based on violations of multiple release agreements on a single date, and argue that Eastman is no longer good law.

§ 8-1.3. Inchoate Crimes.

Oregon’s other merger statute provides in pertinent part:

“(2) A person shall not be convicted of more than one offense defined by ORS 161.405 [attempt], 161.435 [solicitation] and 161.450 [conspiracy] for conduct designed to commit or to culminate in commission of the same crime.

“(3) A person shall not be convicted on the basis of the same course of conduct of both the actual commission of an offense and an attempt to commit that offense or solicitation of that offense or conspiracy to commit that offense.

“(4) Nothing in this section shall be construed to bar inclusion of multiple counts charging violation of the substantive crime and ORS 161.405, 161.435 and 161.450 in a single indictment or information, provided the penal conviction is consistent with subsections (2) and (3) of this section.”

ORS 161.485.

ORS 161.485(2) requires merger where the defendant was convicted of multiple inchoate versions of the same crime. State v. Habibullah, 278 Or App 239, 373 P3d 1259 (2016) (trial court plainly erred in not merging solicitation and conspiracy to commit murder; however, court did not plainly err in failing to merge solicitation and conspiracy to commit murder into attempted aggravated murder that was committed on a different date); State v. Rickards, 133 Or App 592, 593, 891 P2d 1383 (1995) (per curiam) (attempted and conspiracy to commit first-degree assault merge). ORS 161.485(3) requires merger where the defendant was convicted of inchoate and completed versions of a crime. State v. Scott, 135 Or App 319, 323, 899 P2d 697, rev den, 321 Or 560 (1995) (conspiracy to commit witness tampering merges with completed witness tampering).

§ 8-1.4. Sentencing Issues when Convictions are Merged.

When crimes merge, the court imposes one conviction and one sentence for the crime carrying the greatest sentence of the merged convictions. See State v. Seaman, 115 Or App 180, 182, 836 P2d 1379 (1992) (where Class B and C felonies merge, but under guidelines C felony had greater crime seriousness ranking than B felony, court properly merged B felony into C felony and imposed conviction and sentence on C felony). The court does not impose conviction and sentence on the crime(s) that have merged into the primary offense. This is because there is no such thing as “merger for sentencing.” State v. Lopez-Lorenzo, 226 Or App 269, 203 P3d 299 (2009) (per curiam) (where parties agreed that convictions merged, trial court erred in executing judgment saying convictions “‘shall merge for sentencing purposes’” (emphasis added)). See also State v. Green, 145 Or App 175, 177, 929 P2d 1057 (1996) (court erred in imposing convictions on crimes that merged, and then ordering them “merged * * * ‘for sentencing’”). When no crime carries a greater sentence than the other(s), the court chooses the primary conviction into which the other conviction(s) will merge. Rickards, 133 Or App at 593. See also State v. Wright, 150 Or App 159, 162-63, 945 P2d 1083 (1997) (convictions for multiple subclassification factors for same underlying crime merge into single conviction). If multiple convictions merge, because they are based on multiple aggravating theories, the “judgment separately [sh]ould enumerate each of the existing aggravating factors.” Barrett, 331 Or at 37.

§ 8-1.5. Appellate Issues Regarding Merger.

A trial court’s decision on whether to merge convictions is a sentencing issue, so it is reviewable on appeal. See, e.g., State v. Davis, 265 Or App 425, 335 P3d 322 (2014), rev den, 356 Or 837 (2015). For judgments or orders entered by the trial court on or after January 1, 2018, Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 28, ORS 138.105(7) reaffirms that principle by providing that “the appellate court has authority to review any sentence to determine whether the trial court failed to comply with requirements of law in imposing or failing to impose a sentence.”

For judgments or orders entered by the trial court on or after January 1, 2018, Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 28, ORS 138.105(5) further shows that even in guilty and no contest plea cases, merger issues may be reviewable on appeal. ORS 138.105 states in relevant part:

“(5) The appellate court has no authority to review the validity of the defendant’s plea of guilty or no contest, or a conviction based on the defendant’s plea of guilty or no contest, except that:

“ * * * * *

“(b) The appellate court has authority to review whether the trial court erred by not merging determinations of guilt of two or more offenses, unless the entry of separate convictions results from an agreement between the state and the defendant.”

The law has been that when crimes are merged and the remaining crime of conviction is later reversed on appeal, the reversal “has the effect of ‘unmerging’ those crimes.” State v. Cockrell, 170 Or App 29, 31, 10 P3d 960 (2000); State v. Wilson, 161 Or App 314, 318-19, 985 P2d 840 (1999), rev den, 330 Or 71 (2000). On remand, the trial court may conduct further appropriate proceedings on the un-merged crimes if the un-merged crimes were not appealed and reversed.

But for judgments or orders entered by the trial court on or after January 1, 2018, Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 28, this may no longer be the case. ORS 138.035(1)(b) provides: “For the purposes of this subsection, if the trial court merges a determination of guilt on one count with a determination of guilt on another count and imposes a sentence on the merged determinations of guilt, the trial court has conclusively disposed of the merged counts.” (Emphasis added.)

Nevertheless, until the scope of ORS 138.035(1)(b) is established, appellate attorneys should be wary of the “unmerging” principle. In State v. Ventris I, 164 Or App 220, 991 P2d 54 (1999), the defendant appealed his convictions for aggravated murder. He also had been convicted of murder and robbery, but the trial court merged those convictions into his aggravated murder convictions. The Court of Appeals reversed the defendant’s aggravated-murder conviction, and his case was remanded for a new trial. The basis for the reversal apparently would have applied equally to his convictions on the lesser-included charges. But because those convictions had merged with his aggravated-murder conviction, on appeal the defendant did not challenge his convictions on the merged charges.

When the Court of Appeals reversed the aggravated murder convictions, it un-merged the murder and robbery convictions and affirmed them. On remand, in lieu of retrying the defendant on the aggravated murder charges, the state sought consecutive sentences on the murder and robbery convictions that the Court of Appeals had un-merged and affirmed. The trial court then treated the murder conviction as a felony murder conviction and merged the robbery conviction into the felony murder conviction. The state appealed. In State v. Ventris II, 183 Or App 99, 50 P3d 1274 (2002), rev’d on other grounds, 337 Or 283, 96 P3d 815 (2004), the Court of Appeals concluded that the defendant had been convicted of intentional murder. The robbery conviction would not merge into the intentional murder conviction, so the court reversed the merger and remanded for resentencing.

The Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals on other grounds. The Supreme Court concluded that the trial court (i) correctly held that the defendant had been convicted of felony murder, and (ii) correctly merged the robbery conviction into the felony murder conviction. The Supreme Court did not address the propriety of the Court of Appeals decision in Ventris I to un-merge the murder and robbery convictions and affirm them.

So until the scope of ORS 138.035(1)(b) is established, the lesson for appellate counsel, from the Ventris cases, is that when a defendant appeals a conviction into which convictions on other charges have been merged, appellate counsel should challenge the merged convictions along with the crime(s) of conviction left standing post-merger. Otherwise, if the appellate court reverses the crime(s) of conviction, it may un-merge the merged convictions and affirm them. On remand, the defendant would be subject to sentencing on the convictions that the appellate court un-merged and affirmed.

For further cases applying merger principles, see the annotations to ORS 161.067 (and its predecessor, ORS 161.062) and to ORS 161.485.

§ 8-2. CONCURRENT AND CONSECUTIVE SENTENCES.

OAR 213-012-0010 identifies a sentencing court’s statutory authority to impose consecutive terms:

When multiple convictions have been entered against a single defendant, the sentencing judge may impose consecutive or concurrent sentences as provided by ORS 137.123 and 137.370.

§ 8-2.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-012-0010 incorporates two statutory limitations on the imposition of consecutive sentences. Both statutes precede the existence of the guidelines and apply in all criminal cases.

ORS 137.370 directs the Department of Corrections’ administration and computation of sentences; it also dictates that sentences are to be run concurrently unless “the court expressly orders otherwise.”

PRACTICE TIP: Because ORS 137.370 does not address the actual imposition of sentences by the court, it is not further discussed in this manual. But defense counsel should be aware that how they, the prosecution, and the trial court believe consecutive sentences will be administered may bear no resemblance to how staff from the Department of Corrections’ Offender Information and Sentence Computation (OISC) unit will administer the sentences. Moreover, counsel may speak with OISC staff for guidance in how to structure consecutive sentences so that their manner of administration will reflect what the parties and the trial court intend. The OISC unit may be reached at 503-570-6900.

The main statute that OAR 213-012-0010 incorporates is ORS 137.123, which provides:

“(1) A sentence imposed by the court may be made concurrent or consecutive to any other sentence which has been previously imposed or is simultaneously imposed upon the same defendant. The court may provide for consecutive sentences only in accordance with the provisions of this section. A sentence shall be deemed to be a concurrent term unless the judgment expressly provides for consecutive sentences.

“(2) If a defendant is simultaneously sentenced for criminal offenses that do not arise from the same continuous and uninterrupted course of conduct, or if the defendant previously was sentenced by any other court within the United States to a sentence which the defendant has not yet completed, the court may impose a sentence concurrent with or consecutive to the other sentence or sentences.

“(3) When a defendant is sentenced for a crime committed while the defendant was incarcerated after sentencing for the commission of a previous crime, the court shall provide that the sentence for the new crime be consecutive to the sentence for the previous crime.

“(4) When a defendant has been found guilty of more than one criminal offense arising out of a continuous and uninterrupted course of conduct, the sentences imposed for each resulting conviction shall be concurrent unless the court complies with the procedures set forth in subsection (5) of this section.

“(5) The court has discretion to impose consecutive terms of imprisonment for separate convictions arising out of a continuous and uninterrupted course of conduct only if the court finds:

“(a) That the criminal offense for which a consecutive sentence is contemplated was not merely an incidental violation of a separate statutory provision in the course of the commission of a more serious crime but rather was an indication of defendant’s willingness to commit more than one criminal offense; or

“(b) The criminal offense for which a consecutive sentence is contemplated caused or created a risk of causing greater or qualitatively different loss, injury or harm to the victim or caused or created a risk of causing loss, injury or harm to a different victim than was caused or threatened by the other offense or offenses committed during a continuous and uninterrupted course of conduct.”

Before the adoption of ORS 137.123, no statutes regulated the imposition of consecutive sentences. The courts concluded that they had an implied delegated authority to impose such sentences, and created a body of “common law” regulating their imposition. See, e.g., State v. Linthwaite.

With the adoption of the consecutive-sentence statutes, the legislature abolished the judiciary’s implied consecutive-sentence authority and nullified the body of common law the courts previously had created. ORS 137.123 provides courts their sole authority for imposing consecutive sentences. If ORS 137.123 does not authorize consecutive sentences, courts may not impose them. State v. Trice I, 146 Or App 15, 21, 933 P2d 345, rev den, 325 Or 280 (1997).

Moreover, “[i]t is well-established Oregon law that ‘[t]he record must affirmatively show that the court sentenced the defendant solely upon the facts of the case and his personal history, and not as punishment for his refusal to plead guilty.’” State v. Moore, 305 Or App 21, 25, 469 P3d 283 (2020) (quoting State v. Fitzgibbon, 114 Or App 581, 587, 836 P2d 154 (1992)). In Moore, the court based its decision to impose consecutive sentences on three reasons, one of which was “that ‘the victim was reoffended and having to appear in court once again and explain this case.’” 305 Or at 26-27. The Court of Appeals reversed, explaining, that the record “does not reflect that [the] trial court sentenced a defendant based solely on the facts of the case and personal history, and not on the defendant’s exercise of constitutional rights, [which] risks undermining public confidence in the judicial system.” Id. at 28.

With one exception (discussed below in Section 8-2.2), ORS 137.123 “makes concurrent sentences the norm and consecutive sentences the exception.” State v. Nail, 304 Or 359, 366, 745 P2d 415 (1987). Thus, a court errs in imposing consecutive sentences solely because the individual counts carry mandatory sentences. State v. Moore, 290 Or App 306, 414 P3d 915 (2018). Moreover, doubts about whether applicable statutes authorize consecutive sentences should be resolved in favor of leniency. Linthwaite, 295 Or at 179 (citing State v. Welch, 264 Or 388, 505 P2d 910 (1973)).

A court may not run a sentence consecutive to a sentence that has not yet been imposed. State v. Benedict, 95 Or App 750, 770 P2d 973 (1989) (per curiam). As ORS 137.123(1) states: “A sentence imposed by the court may be made concurrent or consecutive to any other sentence which has been previously imposed or is simultaneously imposed upon the same defendant.” (Emphasis added.) Moreover, ORS 137.123 authorizes the imposition only of consecutive sentences. A juvenile court’s disposition in a delinquency case is not a sentence; consequently, a court may not run an adult sentence consecutive to a juvenile court disposition. Trice I, 146 Or App at 20-21. Similarly, the disposition in a contempt case is a sanction, not a sentence, so ORS 137.123 would not allow a court to make a criminal sentence consecutive to a contempt sanction (or vice versa). See State v. Clardy, 292 Or App 890, 422 P3d 434 (2018) (per curiam) (contempt results in a sanction, not a sentence). But cf. State v. Brooks, 187 Or App 388, 67 P3d 426, rev den, 335 Or 578 (2003) (to the same extent that ORS 137.123 authorizes consecutive sentences, the trial court may impose consecutive terms of commitment to the Psychiatric Security Review Board). In cases where ORS 137.123 authorizes consecutive sentences, courts are not limited to making sentences either wholly consecutive or wholly concurrent; courts may make sentences partially concurrent and partially consecutive. Perez v. Persson, 258 Or App 684, 311 P3d 901 (2013), rev den, 355 Or 317 (2014); State v. Trice II, 159 Or App 1, 4-5, 976 P2d 569, rev den, 329 Or 61 (1999).

For further information about cases applying ORS 137.123, see the annotations to ORS 137.123 and its predecessor, ORS 137.122.

§ 8-2.2. Sentences Imposed Consecutive to a Prior Sentence or for Convictions Stemming from Separate Criminal Episodes.

Under ORS 137.123(1)-(2), a court may impose a sentence consecutive to any sentence already imposed. As State v. Duran, 108 Or App 282, 284, 814 P2d 182 (1991) explains, if the court imposes multiple sentences in the same proceeding, it has discretion to run them consecutively if the offenses “d[id] not arise from the same continuous and uninterrupted course of conduct,” ORS 137.123(2), i.e., did not arise from the same criminal episode. Chapter 1 sets out the tests for determining whether crimes stem from separate criminal episodes, or from the same episode. If the court imposes consecutive terms under ORS 137.123(1)-(2), it need not state its reasons for doing so. Duran, 108 Or App at 284.

ORS 137.123(3) differs from ORS 137.123(2). Subsection (3) mandates consecutive sentences for crimes committed while a defendant is serving a sentence for a prior conviction (so long as the defendant is still serving the prior conviction’s sentence). State v. Sumerlin, 139 Or App 579, 588 n 8, 913 P2d 340 (1996).

There is an exception to this, however. In State v. Haugen, 349 Or 174, 243 P3d 31 (2010), the Supreme Court held that the trial court did not err by declining to run a death sentence for an aggravated murder the defendant committed while in prison, consecutive to the life sentence the defendant already was serving when he committed the aggravated murder. Despite ORS 137.123(3)’s mandatory language, the court determined that the legislature’s decision to make imposition of the death penalty possible for murders committed in prison demonstrated that it did not intend to have imposition of a death penalty sentence delayed until the prisoner had died a natural death. “Pursuant to the legislature’s intent, the period of imprisonment for defendant’s earlier crime runs until such time as the sentence of death for aggravated murder is carried out according to the schedule determined by statute.” Haugen, 349 Or at 204-05.

§ 8-2.3. Sentences Imposed on Convictions Stemming from the Same Criminal Episode.

ORS 137.123(4) limits the court’s discretion to impose consecutive sentences for crimes stemming from the same criminal episode. In order to run such sentences consecutively, the court must base the sentences on findings made under ORS 137.123(5).

The facts must show that the defendant:

• through his conduct demonstrated a willingness to commit more than one crime, and that his crimes were not merely incidental to one another, ORS 137.123(5)(a); or

• caused or risked causing qualitatively different harms to the victim, ORS 137.123(5)(b); or

• harmed or risked harming different victims. Id.

As a general principle, for crimes arising from the same criminal episode, the court must make findings of fact on the record, to support the imposition of consecutive sentences. State v. Loftin, 218 Or App 160, 169, 178 P3d 312 (2008), modified, 228 Or App 96, 206 P3d 1208 (2009); State v. Racicot, 106 Or App 557, 561-62, 809 P2d 726 (1991) (en banc). If the court fails to do so, that failure is reviewable on appeal if the defendant objected below. State v. Bucholz, 317 Or 309, 321, 855 P2d 1100 (1993); Sumerlin, 139 Or App at 588-89. If the defendant does not object to the failure to make findings, on appeal the defendant may claim only that the facts would not have supported consecutive sentence findings under any circumstances. Id. at 589. But see State v. Welsh, 267 Or App 8, 340 P3d 132 (2014) (trial court committed plain error by imposing consecutive sentences without making requisite findings under ORS 137.123(5)).

A court errs if it relies on criteria other than those specified in ORS 137.123(5) to impose consecutive sentences. For example, in Nail, 304 Or at 366, the Supreme Court held that the trial court erroneously relied on parole-matrix system’s aggravating factors to impose consecutive sentences. Similarly, in State v. Heiller, 106 Or App 770, 809 P2d 730 (1991) (per curiam), the sentencing court imposed consecutive terms, because the defendant would have been convicted of even more crimes if certain witnesses had not been too timid to testify. The Court of Appeals held that this reason is not enumerated in ORS 137.123(5), so was an improper basis for the consecutive terms. 106 Or App at 771. But see State v. Davis, 113 Or App 118, 120, 830 P2d 620 (1992), aff’d on other grounds, 315 Or 484, 847 P2d 834 (1993) (where sentencing court’s stated reasons why convictions did not merge could also suffice as consecutive-sentence findings, court’s failure to denominate reasons as consecutive-sentence findings did not warrant reversal).

§ 8-2.3.1. Willingness to Commit More Than One Crime.

In State v. Anderson, 208 Or App 409, 145 P3d 245 (2006), the Court of Appeals analyzed the legislative intent of ORS 137.123(5)(a). The court concluded that the trial court did not err in relying on the statute to impose consecutive sentences for a robbery conviction and for an assault committed in the course of leaving the scene of that robbery.

See also State v. Russell, 309 Or App 554, 482 P3d 799 (2021) (the trial court validly imposed consecutive sentences for burglary and robbery, when defendant committed the robbery after learning he had burgled the wrong house).

It is important to recognize an aspect of ORS 137.123(5)(a) the Anderson court did not address. The statute speaks in terms of the “defendant’s willingness to commit more than one criminal offense.” (Emphasis added.) This is an offender-specific finding. State v. Ice, 343 Or 248, 260-61, 170 P3d 1049 (2007), rev’d on other grounds sub nom Oregon v. Ice, 555 US 160, 129 S Ct 711, 172 L Ed 2d 517 (2009). Because it is offender-specific, if an accomplice aids in the principal’s commission of two crimes, but the facts reflect that only the principal’s conduct demonstrated a willingness to commit more than one crime, ORS 137.123(5)(a) should not authorize consecutive sentences on the accomplice. See Chapter 2 (explaining limitation on basing sentence enhancement on vicarious-liability theory).

In comparison to Anderson, in State v. Norris, 281 Or App 512, 513, 383 P3d 944 (2016), the defendant stood convicted of five counts of unauthorized use of a vehicle (UUV), for simultaneously stealing five all-terrain vehicles off a trailer. Relying on ORS 137.123(5)(a), the trial court imposed consecutive sentences on Counts 2 and 3. Norris, 281 Or App at 514. But the defendant “argued that his conduct was akin to stealing a pack of gift cards whereas the trial court thought defendant’s conduct was more akin to stealing multiple items from a store.” Id. at 515.

The Court of Appeals reversed. It explained that the trial court’s theory

“does not support an inference that defendant intended to commit a separate offense. Because the record lacks any discrete facts evincing defendant’s willingness to commit more than one criminal offense, the trial court erred in relying on ORS 137.123(5)(a) to impose a consecutive term of imprisonment for Counts 2 and 3.”

Id.

See also State v. Sanchez-Chavez, 312 Or App 701, 495 P3d 197 (2021) (relying on Norris, the Court of Appeals held that owing to the lack of evidence that defendant’s sexual abuse conviction was temporally or qualitatively distinct from his rape conviction, the trial court erred in relying on ORS 137.123(5)(a) to impose consecutive sentences for those convictions).

In State v. Provancha, 293 Or App 169, 428 P3d 916 (2018), the defendant was charged with second-degree assault and attempted murder for a single attack on the victim. The trial court erred in relying on ORS 137.123(5)(a) to impose consecutive sentences, because “[n]othing in the record * * * support[ed] an inference that defendant’s commission of second-degree assault was ‘not merely’ ‘incidental’ to his commission of attempted murder.” Id. at 175.

In State v. Edwards, 286 Or App 99, 399 P3d 463, rev den, 362 Or 175 (2017), the defendant fired two shots at a pursuing police officer, with the second shot hitting the officer. The defendant pleaded guilty to attempted aggravated murder and first-degree assault. The trial court relied on ORS 137.123(5)(a) to impose consecutive sentences. However, “because the facts [did] not permit a finding that defendant’s conduct in committing first-degree assault was anything but incidental to his conduct in committing attempted murder, the trial court was not authorized to impose consecutive sentences under ORS 137.123(5)(a).” Edwards, 286 Or App at 106.

See also State v. Porter, 313 Or App 565, 494 P3d 988 (2021) (relying on Edwards, the Court of Appeals held that because there was no evidence to support an inference that defendant acted with a willingness to commit multiple offenses, the trial court erred in relying on ORS 137.123(5)(a) to impose consecutive sentences on defendant’s convictions for felony driving under the influence of intoxicants and felony driving while suspended or revoked). Accord State v. Larson, 314 Or App 309, 497 P3d 818 (2021) (relying on Porter to hold the trial court erred in relying on ORS 137.123(5)(a) to impose consecutive sentences on convictions of driving under the influence of intoxicants and driving while suspended).

Finally, State v. Byam, 284 Or App 402, 393 P3d 252 (2017), the trial court relied on ORS 137.123(5)(a) to impose consecutive sentences on robbery and unauthorized use of a vehicle (UUV) convictions, which the defendant committed concurrently (by using a dangerous weapon to take possession of the victim’s vehicle through a “carjacking”). The Court of Appeals reversed, explaining:

“[I]f the conditions [ORS 137.123(5)(a)] prescribes for the imposition of consecutive sentences are to have any meaningful practical application, they must, in the absence of explicit evidence of multiple intents, preclude the imposition of consecutive sentences where, as here, the same act (obtaining the victim’s keys through the use of a dangerous weapon), undertaken to achieve the same end (the exercise of control over the victim’s car), concurrently violated both criminal statutes. In such circumstances, the two criminal offenses are so inextricably intertwined that the consecutively sentenced offense (here, the UUV) is, necessarily, ‘incidental’ to the ‘more serious crime’ (here, the first-degree robbery) and cannot be deemed ‘an indication of defendant’s willingness to commit more than one criminal offense.’ ORS 137.123(5)(a).”

Byam, 284 Or App at 405.

§ 8-2.3.2. Greater or Qualitatively Different Harm.

In State v. Rettmann, 218 Or App 179, 185, 178 P3d 333 (2008), the court announced a four-part test to determine the applicability of ORS 137.123(5)(b):

“To determine whether an offense ‘caused or created a risk of causing greater or qualitatively different loss, injury or harm’ than another offense under the statute, a court must (1) determine which offense is the offense for which a consecutive sentence is contemplated; (2) compare the harms—real or potential—that arose from that offense with those that arose from the offense to which it will be sentenced consecutively2 (3) determine whether the offense for which a consecutive sentence is contemplated caused or risked causing any harm that the other did not; and, if so, (4) determine whether the harm that is unique to that offense is greater than or qualitatively different from the harms caused or threatened by the other.

_______________

“2 By ‘potential’ harms, we mean harms that were risked, though not realized, by the conduct that actually occurred. We do not mean to suggest that it is appropriate to consider theoretical harms that an offense—as defined by statute but not as actually committed—could have caused, as the prosecutor appears to have suggested at the sentencing hearing. See 218 Or App at 183 (quoting the prosecutor as arguing that a second-degree assault ‘could have been anywhere’ and that defendant ‘could have scratched and caused injury to the boy’). 218 Or App at 185-86.

Moreover, the court stated:

“Where two offenses are predicated on the same act, logic will not support the conclusion that one offense caused or created a risk of causing some harm that the other offense did not. * * * In short, ORS 137.123(5)(b) does not authorize consecutive sentences for multiple offenses that arise out of a single act.” Id. at 186.

Porter, 313 Or App at 571-73 (relying on Rettman, the Court of Appeals held that because defendant’s conduct was predicated on a single act, the trial court erred in relying on ORS 137.123(5)(b) to impose consecutive sentences on defendant’s convictions for felony driving under the influence of intoxicants and felony driving while suspended or revoked).

Accord State v. Garcia-Mendoza, 225 Or App 487, 202 P3d 191 (2009).

In State v. Provancha, the defendant was charged with second-degree assault and attempted murder for a single attack on the victim. The trial court erred in relying on ORS 137.123(5)(b) to impose consecutive sentences. Relying on State v. Edwards, 286 Or App 99, 399 P3d 463, rev den, 362 Or 175 (2017), the Provancha court explained:

“[T]he record in this case also does not justify a consecutive sentence under ORS 137.123(5)(b). The trial court contemplated a consecutive sentence for second-degree assault. That assault—a blow with a baseball bat—caused the real harm of physical injury and risked the potential harm of death. That offense was sentenced consecutively to the attempted murder offense, which caused the harm of physical injury and risked the harm of death. Thus, defendant’s conduct, which constituted multiple offenses, caused or risked the same harms to the victim. Moreover, as noted above, nothing in the record suggests that defendant delivered any one blow with a different goal (for example, causing a qualitatively different harm) as compared to that which motivated his other blows to the victim. As a result, the record does not support a determination that defendant’s assault offense caused or risked ‘greater or qualitatively different’ harm to the victim than that caused by the attempted murder offense.”

293 Or App at 176.

In Edwards, the defendant fired two shots at a pursuing police officer, named Buchholz, with the second shot hitting Buchholz. The defendant pled guilty to attempted aggravated murder and first-degree assault. The trial court erred in relying on ORS 137.123(5)(b) to impose consecutive sentences. The Court of Appeals explained,

“The factual basis for defendant’s attempted aggravated murder conviction was that, with the intent to kill, defendant took a substantial step toward causing the death of Buchholz by shooting at Buchholz with a firearm. The harms posed by defendant’s conduct in committing attempted murder included the risk that the bullet would strike Buchholz and cause death or that the bullet would strike Buchholz and cause physical injury. In short, defendant’s conduct underlying both offenses caused or risked precisely the same harms to Buchholz: death and physical injury. It follows that defendant’s assault offense cannot be said to have caused or risked harm that was ‘greater or qualitatively different’ than the harm caused or risked by his conduct in committing attempted aggravated murder. Accordingly, imposition of consecutive sentences was not authorized by ORS 137.123(5)(b).”

Edwards, 286 Or App at 109.

§ 8-2.3.3. Multiple Victims.

Rettman involved a single victim. In State v. Gee, 224 Or App 635, 198 P3d 950 (2008), the court clarified the application of Rettman, and of ORS 137.123(5)(b), in cases involving multiple victims.

Defendant Gee came across two people walking down a street, one of whom Gee thought was a man named Taylor. Gee told his co-defendant to shoot at the man Gee thought was Taylor, but Taylor was several blocks away. The co-defendant fired four shots, hitting a man named White. Gee was convicted of two counts of attempted murder—one count involving Taylor, the other involving White. The trial court ordered that Gee’s sentence for the attempted murder of Taylor be consecutive to his sentence for the attempted murder of White. Gee, 224 Or App at 637.

Gee appealed the consecutive sentence. He argued that the act of having his accomplice shoot at White did not give rise to a separate risk of harm to Taylor. The Court of Appeals concluded that although Gee’s convictions were “predicated on the same” shooting, Rettman, 218 Or App at 186, the shooting created two different risks of harm to two different victims. It was not necessary that Taylor be actually harmed; it was sufficient that a substantial step was taken to cause his death. Therefore, a risk of harm existed as to both victims during the same course of conduct, which authorized consecutive sentences. Gee, 224 Or App at 639-40.

PRACTICE TIP: Practitioners should recognize that ORS 137.123(5)(b)’s separate-victims finding, which authorizes the imposition of consecutive sentences, applies more broadly than does ORS 161.067(2)’s separate-victims, anti-merger provision. This is because ORS 131.007’s broad definition of “victim” applies in determining whether consecutive sentences are authorized, but it does not apply in determining whether convictions merge.

§ 8-2.3.3.1. The State as Victim.

In State v. Gatewood, 300 Or App 21, 22, 452 P3d 1046 (2019), rev den, 366 Or 205 (2020), the trial court relied on the separate-victim authority of ORS 137.123(5)(b) to impose consecutive sentences on the defendant’s convictions for first-degree theft and felon in possession of a firearm (FIP). The defendant challenged the consecutive sentences, arguing that “the state was not a ‘victim’ of the FIP offense, nor does Article I, section 44(3), of the Oregon Constitution support such a proposition.” 300 Or App at 21 (footnote omitted). Without reaching defendant’s argument grounded on Article I, section 44(3), the Court of Appeals rejected his argument. The court declared, “[T]he pertinent ‘victim’ for consecutive-sentencing purposes under ORS 137.123(5)(b) is the ‘victim’ under the substantive statute defining the criminal offense[.]” Gatewood, 300 Or App at 29. The court then held: “[U]nder the substantive statute defining the offense of FIP, the state is the victim.” Id. at 30 (emphasis added).

Without question, Gatewood undermines the utility of the argument in Section 8-2.3.3.1, that Article I, section 44 is void so may not be used to establish consecutive-sentence authority on a separate-victims basis. This is because, under the Gatewood analysis, the state may be identified as a crime victim, without need to rely on section 44(3). See also State v. Brass, 310 Or App 602, 485 P3d 289 (2021) (“the public” constitutes a different victim than an individual for purposes of consecutive sentences).

On the other hand, in Gatewood, the defendant alternatively argued:

“[T]he definition of ‘victim’ set out in Article I, section 44(3), does not support the consecutive sentences imposed here, because the victim of the theft offense * * * was also part of ‘the people of Oregon’ and therefore was not a ‘wholly different’ victim from the victim of the FIP offense, assuming that the FIP victim was the state or the people of Oregon.”

300 Or App at 24.

This alternative argument mirrors the last argument, found in Section 8-2.3.3.1’s PRACTICE TIP, above. That argument maintains that identifying the state as the victim of a crime cannot create consecutive-sentence authority, “because an individual victim is subsumed within the constitutional definition of the state as ‘the people of Oregon.’ Or Const, Art I, § 42(7).” Moreover, although the Gatewood court mentioned the defendant’s alternative argument, the court never addressed it. Consequently, defense counsel still may assert defendant Gatewood’s alternative argument—which repeats the argument found in the PRACTICE TIP—as an objection to treating the state as a victim as authority for imposing consecutive sentences.

Article I, section 44, of the Oregon Constitution provides in pertinent part:

“(1)(a) * * *

“(b) No law shall limit a court’s authority to sentence a criminal defendant consecutively for crimes against different victims.

“* * * * *

“(3) As used in this section, ‘victim’ means any person determined by the prosecuting attorney to have suffered direct financial, psychological or physical harm as a result of a crime and, in the case of a victim who is a minor, the legal guardian of the minor. In the event no person has been determined to be a victim of the crime, the people of Oregon, represented by the prosecuting attorney, are considered to be the victims.”

This portion of section 44 was intended to enhance trial courts’ consecutive-sentence authority, including by allowing the state to assert itself as the victim of otherwise “victimless” crimes. Measure 74 took effect December 2, 1999. It contains no retroactivity clause, so it applies only to crimes committed on or after its effective date. See State v. Lanig, 154 Or App 665, 675, 963 P2d 58 (1998) (in the absence of text clearly indicating that constitutional amendment applies retroactively, the amendment applies prospectively only).

In State v. Lane, 357 Or 619, 355 P3d 914 (2015), the court held that for crimes that have separate victims, Article I, section 44(1)(b) supersedes OAR 213-012-0040(2)’s limitations on consecutive probation-revocation sanctions. See Section 8-5.2. But for the following reasons, section 44 may be void.

First, section 44 was created by the voters’ passage of Ballot Measure 74 (1999), which the legislature referred to the voters when it passed House Joint Resolution 94 (1999). The measure amended the constitution in three ways:

1. By generally prohibiting “set[ting] aside or otherwise not carr[ying] out [sentences], except as authorized by the sentencing court,” Art I, § 44(1); and

2. By prohibiting the imposition of sub-constitutional “limit[ations on] a court’s authority to sentence a criminal defendant consecutively for crimes against different victims,” Art I, § 44 (1)(b); and

3. In “victimless crime” situations, by allowing “the people of Oregon, represented by the prosecuting attorney, [to be] considered * * * the victims,” Art I, § 44(3).

Ballot measures like Measure 74, which include multiple constitutional amendments, violate the separate-vote requirement of Article XVII, section 1, of the Oregon Constitution, Armatta v. Kitzhaber, 327 Or 250, 959 P2d 49 (1998), unless the amendments “are necessary corollaries” to one another, State v. Rogers, 352 Or 510, 523, 288 P3d 544 (2012). Sound arguments may be made that Measure 74’s three amendments “are [not] necessary corollaries” to one another. If so, then the measure is void because it was approved in violation of Article XVII, section 1. Armatta, 327 Or at 285.

A second claim that section 44 is void is grounded on Article IV, section 20, of the Oregon Constitution, which provides in pertinent part:

“Every Act shall embrace but one subject, and matters properly connected therewith, which subject shall be expressed in the title. But if any subject shall be embraced in an Act which shall not be expressed in the title, such Act shall be void only as to so much thereof as shall not be expressed in the title.”

In McIntire v. Forbes, 322 Or 426, 909 P2d 846 (1996), the court declared an act’s title invalid. Subsequently, in what essentially was a companion case, the court held that because the act’s title was invalid, all of its sections were void. Kane v. Forbes, 322 Or 447, 449, 909 P2d 160 (1996) (per curiam). Thus, when the legislature adopts an act in violation of Article IV, section 20’s title requirement, the act is void.

Again, Measure 74 reached the ballot by virtue of a legislative action, HJR 94. But HJR 94 contained no title whatsoever. Consistent with Kane, the resolution’s lack of title arguably violates Article IV, section 20. If so, that violation could render void both the act and the voters’ approval of it. Accord Strunk v. PERB, 338 Or 145, 238, 108 P3d 1058 (2005) (a law adopted “in violation of Article I, section 21” of the Oregon Constitution is “void and of no effect”).

PRACTICE TIP: In same-episode cases, where the state relies on Art I, section 44(3) to assert itself as a victim to try to secure consecutive sentences under a separate-victims theory, defense counsel should argue that section 44 “is void in its entirety,” Kane, 322 Or at 449; Armatta, 327 Or at 285, so the section does not authorize consecutive sentences. Counsel also may argue that there are not separate victims, because an individual victim is subsumed within the constitutional definition of the state as “the people of Oregon.” Or Const, Art I, § 42(7).

§ 8-3. MAXIMUM LENGTH OF CONSECUTIVE SENTENCES.

ORS 137.121 requires courts to follow all applicable guidelines rules when imposing consecutive sentences. State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 847 P2d 834 (1993).

ORS 137.121 states:

“Notwithstanding any other provision of law, but subject to ORS 161.605 [statutory maximums for felonies], the maximum consecutive sentences which may be imposed for felonies committed on or after November 1, 1989, whether as terms of imprisonment, probation or both, shall be as provided by rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.”

OAR 213-012-0020 imposes several limitations on the court’s consecutive sentencing authority:

(1) When the sentencing judge imposes multiple sentences consecutively, the consecutive sentences shall consist of an incarceration term and a supervision term.

(2)(a) Subject to the provisions of subsection (b) of this section, the presumptive incarceration term of the consecutive sentences is the sum of:

(A) The presumptive incarceration term or the prison term defined in OAR 213-008-0005(1) imposed pursuant to a dispositional departure for the primary offense, as defined in 213-003-0001(17); and

(B) Up to the maximum incarceration term indicated in the Criminal History I Column for each additional offense imposed consecutively.

(b) The total incarceration term of the consecutive sentences, including the incarceration term for the primary offense, shall not exceed twice the maximum presumptive incarceration term or the prison term defined in OAR 213-008-0005(1) imposed pursuant to a dispositional departure of the primary sentence except by departure as provided by 213-008-0007.

(c) The incarceration term of any probationary sentence is the maximum jail sentence that could be imposed as provided by these rules as part of the probationary sentence for that offense.

(3)(a) If the court imposes a sentence that includes a term of incarceration that exceeds 12 months and the term is to be served consecutively to a term of incarceration of 12 months or less for a felony that was imposed in a previous proceeding, the defendant shall serve any remaining part of the previously imposed term of incarceration in the legal and physical custody of the Department.

(b) If the court imposes a felony sentence that includes a term of incarceration that is 12 months or less and the term is to be served consecutively to a term of incarceration that exceeds 12 months that was imposed in a previous proceeding or in the same proceeding, the court shall commit the defendant to the legal and physical custody of the Department.

(4) The supervision term of consecutive sentences shall be:

(a) The presumptive post-prison supervision term imposed for the primary offense if the sentence for any offense includes a prison term; or

(b) The presumptive probation term of each offense if no sentence includes a prison term. All presumptive probation terms imposed as provided by this subsection shall run concurrently.

(5) Sections (1), (2), and (3) of this rule shall not apply to any sentence imposed on a dangerous offender under ORS 161.725 and 161.737, nor shall sections (2) and (3) apply to consecutive sentences imposed for crimes that have different victims.

(6) Notwithstanding section (3)(b) of this rule, terms of incarceration 12 months or less imposed pursuant to ORS 166.070(2) shall be served in the legal and physical custody of the Department.

§ 8-3.1. Intent of Provision.

Although OAR 213-012-0020 was intended to substantially limit trial court impositions of lengthy consecutive sentences, there are a number of restrictions on the rule’s application. For example, the limitations do not apply to misdemeanor sentences run consecutive to guidelines sentences. State v. Miller, 114 Or App 235, 237, 835 P2d 131 (1992).

The most significant restriction is explained in State v. Miller, 317 Or 297, 855 P2d 1093 (1993). In contravention of established principles of statutory construction, as well as the guidelines’ “economy” principle (both discussed in Chapter 1), Miller holds that the guidelines’ consecutive-sentence limitations apply only to crimes stemming from the same criminal episode. Miller, 317 Or at 303-06. See also State v. Flower, 128 Or App 83, 86-87, 874 P2d 1359, rev den, 319 Or 572 (1994) (consistent with Miller)).

In other words, crimes that stem from separate criminal episodes are not subject to OAR 213-012-0020’s limitations on the length of consecutive sentences. See Chapter 1 (explaining tests for determining whether crimes stem from separate episodes or from the same episode). But see Jesse Wm. Barton, Flaws in Miller & Bucholz, The Oregon Defense Attorney 14 (Jan/Feb/Mar 2015), which details arguments that State v. Miller should be overruled. Cf. State v. Cuevas, 358 Or 147, 361 P3d 581 (2015) (Walters, J., dissenting) (writing for a three-member dissent, insisting that the companion case to Miller, which authorizes criminal-history score reconstitution based on separate-episode findings, State v. Bucholz, 317 Or 309, 855 P2d 1100 (1993), was wrongly decided and should be overruled). See also Williamson v. Schiedler, 196 Or App 302, 308, 101 P3d 364 (2004) (the “requirement” that “convictions must have been part of a single criminal episode” “is not mentioned in” OAR 213-012-0020).

Another exception to the consecutive-sentence limitations is found in OAR 213-012-0020(5). The 1993 Legislature created it to overturn the holding in State v. Davis, that the indeterminate portion of a dangerous offender sentence is subject to the limits of the 400 percent rule (which is discussed below). 315 Or at 494-96. Now the indeterminate portion is excluded from the 400 percent rule calculation. All that is included is the dangerous offender sentence’s determinate portion. State v. Worth, 274 Or App 1, 39-40, 360 P3d 536 (2015). Furthermore, if the presumptive sentence for the offense

“is a prison sentence, the required incarceration term [i.e., its determinate portion] shall be no less than the presumptive incarceration term and no more than twice the maximum presumptive incarceration term. If the presumptive sentence for the offense is probation, the required incarceration term shall be no less than the maximum [dispositional departure] incarceration term * * * and no more than twice that amount.”

ORS 161.737(2).

OAR 213-012-0020(5) applies to crimes committed on or after October 15, 1993. For crimes committed before that date, Davis is still the law.

The last exception to OAR 213-012-0020’s limitations also is found in OAR 213-012-0020(5). It exempts “crimes that have different victims.” Id. The 1997 Legislature created this single-victim limitation by passing Senate Bill 936, with the intent of implementing Section (1)(k) of Ballot Measure 40 (1996). Although in Armatta v. Kitzhaber the Supreme Court declared Measure 40 invalid in its entirety, SB 936 and, hence, OAR 213-012-0020(5)’s single-victim limitation, survived Measure 40’s invalidation. State v. Fugate, 332 Or 195, 26 P3d 802 (2001).

Nevertheless, application of the single-victim limitation may be challenged on ex post facto grounds in some cases. US Const Art I, § 10; Or Const, Art I, § 21. SB 936 did not take effect until the governor signed it on June 12, 1997. The bill purports to “apply to all criminal actions pending or commenced on or after December 5, 1996.” Or Laws 1997, ch 313, § 38. December 5, 1996 would have been Measure 40’s effective date. Without question, applying the single-victim limitation to crimes committed before Measure 40’s effective date would be ex post facto. Accord Miller v. Florida, 482 US 423, 107 S Ct 2446, 96 L Ed 2d 351 (1987) (retroactive application of amendment increasing presumptive sentencing range is ex post facto).

Finally, the state constitutional provision that “[n]o law shall limit a court’s authority to sentence a criminal defendant consecutively for crimes against different victims,” Or Const, Art I, § 44(1)(b), may make OAR 213-012-0020(5)’s single-victim limitation constitutionally mandatory. But if the state would rely on Article I, section 44(3) to try to assert itself as a victim to avoid application of the guidelines consecutive-sentence limitations, defense counsel should object, by asserting the arguments discussed in Section 8-2.3.3.1.

§ 8-3.2. Application of the Limitations.

In cases where OAR 213-012-0020’s sentencing limitations apply, the first step is determining the defendant’s “primary offense,” i.e., the offense with the highest crime seriousness ranking. OAR 213-003-0001(17). If two or more crimes have the same seriousness ranking, the court may designate any one of the equally ranked crimes as the primary offense. Id.

§ 8-3.2.1. The “Shift-to-I” Rule.

The next step in applying the consecutive-sentence limitations is determining each crimes’ criminal-history score. The defendant’s true criminal history score is used in assessing the grid block for only his primary offense (and any additional offenses whose sentences are made concurrent). OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a)(A). For additional offenses whose sentences run consecutively, the court does not use the defendant’s true criminal history score; instead, the court uses criminal history score I. OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a)(B). This is called the “shift-to-I” rule. See State v. Taylor, 293 Or App 460, 428 P3d 939 (2018) (trial court erred in declining to follow the shift-to-I rule in sentencing three counts of coercion and one count of domestic violence fourth-degree assault, because contrary to trial court’s ruling, the counts stemmed from the same episode); State v. Deslaurier, 277 Or App 288, 371 P3d 505 (2016) (for counts stemming from single criminal episode and having the same victim, trial court violated shift-to-I rule when imposing consecutive sentences); Rodvelt, 187 Or App at 132-33 (using hypotheticals to explain shift-to-I rule); State v. Kautz, 179 Or App 458, 465-67, 39 P3d 937, rev den, 334 Or 327 (2002) (burglary and robbery were part of same criminal episode where defendant, in course of committing burglary, threatened victim with a gun; court erred in declining to apply shift-to-I rule in sentencing additional offense); McNeil, 170 Or App at 413 (where trial court did not find that crimes had separate victims, Court of Appeals remanded for resentencing because trial court failed to follow “shift-to-I” rule); State v. Scott, 126 Or App 176, 178-79, 867 P2d 563 (1994) (reversing and remanding because of failure to apply “shift-to-I” rule). See also State v. Thomas, 133 Or App 754, 757-59, 894 P2d 496 (1995) (where defendant’s true criminal history score places defendant’s additional offense in presumptive incarceration grid block, but the shift-to-I rule moves the additional offense to a presumptive probation grid block, all limitations of OAR 213-012-0020 apply). But see State v. Powell, 253 Or App 185, 194, 288 P3d 999 (2012), rev den, 353 Or 714 (2013) (rejecting defendant’s pro se supplemental claim that first conviction sentenced is “primary offense,” and that the court violated shift-to-I rule in sentencing subsequent conviction; conviction subsequently sentenced had higher seriousness ranking than first conviction sentenced, thus establishing the subsequently sentenced conviction as the primary offense).

If the sentence involves a “statutorily mandated sentence,” which is greater than the sentence specified by the grid block in column I, then the shift-to-I rule does not require using the grid block sentence. Instead, the statutorily mandated sentence applies. State v. Monro, 256 Or App 493, 301 P3d 435, rev den, 354 Or 148 (2013); State v. Hicks, 249 Or App 196, 202, 275 P3d 195 (2012).

PRACTICE TIP: In Hicks, the Court of Appeals held that the shift-to-I rule did not apply to repeat property offender sentences under ORS 137.717, because they were statutorily mandated sentences rather than presumptive sentences. However, Hicks has arguably been overruled by State v. Orcutt, 280 Or App 439, 444, 380 P3d 1105 (2016), rev den, 361 Or 525 (2017), and State v. Webster, 280 Or App 217, 222-24, 380 P3d 1165 (2016), which held that repeat property offender (RPO) sentences are presumptive sentences that are subject to the sentencing guidelines. Consequently, when multiple property offenses involve the same criminal episode and victim, defense counsel should argue that any consecutive sentences would be limited by the shift-to-I rule to the sentence identified in the grid block in column I, rather than the sentence identified in ORS 137.717.

§ 8-3.2.1.1. Ballot Measure 4 (“Denny Smith”) Limitations on Shift-to-I Rule.

As is explained in Chapter 4, if a defendant is subject to ORS 137.635(1) and his grid block is 7-F to 7-I, the court does not impose the sentence called for by those grid blocks. Instead, the court must use the special sentencing ranges of imprisonment found in OAR 213-009-0001(2)(a)-(d).

If a defendant is subject to ORS 137.635 and receives a consecutive sentence for an additional offense having a “true” sentencing grid block of 7-A to 7-E, the court must shift to grid block 7-I. OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a)(B). But in consecutive sentence cases, the court does not use the special sentencing range for ORS 137.635 defendants in grid block 7-I, specified by OAR 213-009-0001(2)(a). Instead, the court uses the sentencing limitations of the standard grid block 7-I. State v. Nicholas, 118 Or App 232, 235-36, 846 P2d 1181 (1993).

§ 8-3.2.2. The “200 Percent” Rule.

Having determined the grid blocks for the defendant’s primary and additional offenses within the consecutive “string,” the court then imposes the terms of incarceration. Under OAR 213-012-0020(2)(b), “the total incarceration term for all of the convictions combined may not exceed twice the maximum presumptive incarceration term for the primary offense, except by departure.” Davis, 315 Or at 492. This is called the “200 percent” rule.

State v. Decleve, 299 Or App 528, 532-33, 450 P3d 999 (2019) (where trial court applied the shift-to-I rule, court erred in failing also to apply the 200 percent rule);

See also Williamson, 196 Or App 302 (trial counsel was inadequate by failing to object to consecutive sentence whose total violated 200 percent rule).

For additional offenses that fall within probationary grid blocks, the maximum sentence the court may impose is “the maximum jail sentence that could be imposed” on each such additional offense. OAR 213-012-0020(2)(c) (emphasis added). Because that provision authorizes imposing the maximum jail sentence, the lower number of sanction units shown in the additional offense’s grid block is the presumptive maximum. OAR 213-005-0013(1). Accord State v. Holliday, 110 Or App 426, 431, 824 P2d 1148, rev den, 313 Or 211 (1992). In that situation, the court essentially would impose presumptive maximum “straight jail” sentences, which are discussed in Chapter 5.

First, note that the presumptive maximum for the first-degree theft is the bottom number in grid block 4-I, i.e., 60 days (two months).

The presumptive maximum for the primary offense (the first-degree arson) is 26 months. Under the 200 percent rule, the consecutive presumptive maximum is: 26 x 200% = 52 months. Running all four presumptive maximum sentences consecutively would yield a total of 64 months, which is 12 months more than the 200 percent rule allows. To comply with the rule, the court reduced the sentences by a total of 12 months. It did so by imposing “adjusted” presumptive maximums on the burglary and stalking convictions, reducing each conviction’s sentence by six months.

The 200 percent rule works differently if the defendant’s primary offense has a presumptive sentence of probation. State v. Freeland, 115 Or App 388, 389, 838 P2d 642 (1992) explains that if the court imposes probation, the rule does not limit the total term of incarceration. However, if on the primary offense the court imposes a dispositional departure term of imprisonment, it may impose a total consecutive sentence of no more than double (200 percent of) the dispositional departure term it imposed on the primary offense. OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a)(A) and (2)(b).

§ 8-3.2.3. The “400 Percent” Rule.

Having determined the grid blocks for the defendant’s primary and additional offenses, and having determined the maximum sentences under the 200 percent rule, the court then may double the sentence for any single offense where an aggravated departure is warranted. This is because OAR 213-012-0020(2)(b) incorporates OAR 213-008-0007, which states in relevant part: “When a departure sentence is imposed for any individual offense sentenced consecutively, the incarceration term of that departure sentence shall not exceed twice the maximum incarceration term that may be imposed for that offense as provided in OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a).” Thus, “the maximum incarceration term that may be imposed for all the consecutive sentences together by departure cannot exceed four times the presumptive incarceration term of the primary offense.” Davis, 315 Or at 492. See also State v. Langdon, 330 Or 72, 999 P2d 1127 (2000) (consistent with Davis); State v. Determann, 122 Or App 480, 484, 858 P2d 171, rev den, 318 Or 26 (1993) (same). This rule is called the “400 percent” rule.

For additional offenses falling within probationary grid blocks, the maximum sentence the court may impose is “the maximum jail sentence that could be imposed” on each such additional offense. OAR 213-012-0020(2)(c) (emphasis added). Because this provision authorizes imposition of the maximum jail sentence, the upper number of sanction units shown in the additional offense’s grid block is the departure maximum. OAR 213-008-0006(2). Accord Holliday, 110 Or App at 431.

That is exactly what the 400 percent rule allows in this hypothetical case (26 x 400% = 104).

The 400 percent rule works differently if the defendant’s primary offense has a presumptive sentence of probation and the court imposes a dispositional departure term of incarceration. When that is the case, the total departure term the court may impose is double the duration of the dispositional departure. OAR 213-012-0020(2)(b). See also State v. Morales-Aguilar, 121 Or App 456, 459, 855 P2d 646 (1993) (court may run dispositional departure sentences consecutively).

Davis, 315 Or at 491-94 and the Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 140-42 (1989) (hereafter Implementation Manual) provides further explanations and examples of the application of the 200 and 400 percent rules.

§ 8-3.2.4. Statutory Minimums in Consecutive Sentence Cases.

There are various types of statutory-minimum sentencing schemes. See Chapter 4. The schemes interact with the guidelines consecutive-sentence limitations in different ways.

First, Measure 11 minimums, see ORS 137.700 (for adults); ORS 137.707 (for juveniles), are not presumptive sentences, State ex rel Huddleston v. Sawyer, 324 Or 597, 608, 932 P2d 1145 (1997), cert den, 522 US 994 (1997), so the 200 percent rule does not limit them.

Because Measure 11 minimums are not presumptive sentences, by default they are departure sentences. OAR 213-003-0001(5). But in State v. Langdon the Supreme Court held that Measure 11 minimums also are not subject to the guidelines’ 400 percent rule’s limit on the maximum length of consecutive departure sentences. The court based its decision on the fact that Measure 11 minimums are mandatory. Owing to that, the court said, the measure impliedly overrode the 400 percent rule’s limitation on trial court authority to run departure sentences consecutively. But the court let stand the portion of the Court of Appeals decision that guidelines sentences run consecutively to Measure 11 sentences are subject to the 200 and 400 percent rules. Langdon, 330 Or at 79 n 5. See also State v. Skelton, 153 Or App 580, 957 P2d 585, rev den, 327 Or 448 (1998) (consistent with Langdon).

The Court of Appeals also has attempted to fashion some semblance of rationality in reconciling the guidelines’ consecutive-sentence limitations with Measure 11. In State v. Longenecker, 175 Or App 33, 27 P3d 509, rev den, 332 Or 656 (2001), the trial court imposed a mixture of guidelines and Measure 11 sentences whose consecutive total exceeded the 400 percent rule. The court said,

“Because the consecutive Measure 11 sentences that could be imposed exceed the maximum guidelines sentence that could be imposed, the court must sentence [the] defendant to consecutive sentences solely under Measure 11 if it wishes to impose on [the] defendant the maximum possible sentence * * *. If the sentence imposed by the court includes any sentence under the guidelines that is consecutive to any other sentence in this case, the maximum sentence that can be imposed on [the] defendant is [the 400 percent rule’s limit.]”

Longenecker, 175 Or App at 41-42.

In State v. Monro, the trial court violated the shift-to-I rule when it sentenced one of the defendant’s convictions that also carried a 90-month Measure 11 minimum. On that conviction, the court used grid block 9-A to impose a consecutive, aggravated-departure maximum of 144 months. But the shift-to-I rule applied to that conviction. Under that rule, the conviction’s correct grid block was 9-I, which carries an aggravated-departure maximum of 72 months. The conviction’s 90-month Measure 11 minimum exceeded, so superseded, the aggravated-departure maximum. Consequently, the 90-month minimum was the maximum consecutive sentence the shift-to-I rule authorized, rendering the 144-month sentence the trial court imposed 54 months too long.

Like Measure 11 minimums, gun minimums specified by ORS 161.610 are not presumptive guidelines sentences. Because they are not presumptive sentences, by default they too are departure sentences. OAR 213-003-0001(5). When a string of sentences includes a gun minimum, the total sentence may be subject to the 400 percent rule. See State v. Clark, 114 Or App 631, 632, 836 P2d 183 (1992) (in consecutive sentence cases, where the sentence for the primary offense is a gun minimum term, the shift-to-I rule limits the terms of the additional offenses). Moreover, the gun minimum itself is subject to the 400 percent rule if it is for a first-time gun offense. That is because the first-time gun minimums are discretionary, so they may be reduced to comply with the 400 percent rule’s limitation.

On the other hand, like Measure 11 minimums, repeat gun minimums are mandatory so may not be reduced. Therefore, they are not subject to the 400 percent rule.

Sentences imposed under certain repeat-offender laws, e.g., the RPO law, ORS 137.717, and the repeat methamphetamine offender law, ORS 137.721, sometimes are inartfully referred to as statutory minimums. They are not. By definition, they are presumptive sentences. See OAR 213-003-0001(16) (definition of “presumptive sentence” includes “a sentence designated as a presumptive sentence by statute”). Because they are presumptive sentences, they are subject to the 200 and 400 percent rules. Accord State v. Orcutt, 280 Or App 439, 444, 380 P3d 1105 (2016), rev den, 361 Or 525 (2017) (holding that ORS 137.717 sentences are presumptive sentences); State v. Webster, 280 Or App 217, 222-24, 380 P3d 1165 (2016) (same). But see State v. Hicks, 249 Or App 196, 275 P3d 195 (2012) (holding that ORS 137.717 sentences are not presumptive sentences). The same thing would be true about repeat felony sex offender sentences under ORS 137.719. But because the presumptive sentence under that statute is “true life,” as a practical matter, the 200 and 400 percent rules do not meaningfully limit imposing them consecutively.

§ 8-3.2.5. Supervisory Terms in Consecutive Sentence Cases.

OAR 213-012-0020(4) specifies the supervisory term that must accompany a consecutive sentence. Initially, the rule makes clear that supervision comes in one of two forms: post-prison supervision (PPS) or probation.

In a consecutive sentence case, where one or more convictions call for terms of incarceration (as opposed to probation), the court imposes only one term of supervision, that being the PPS term for the primary offense. OAR 213-012-0020(4)(a). See State v. Dummitt, 115 Or App 487, 490, 839 P2d 246 (1992) (court erred in imposing term of probation in addition to term of PPS); State v. Markham, 114 Or App 5, 7, 836 P2d 1348 (1992) (court erred in imposing multiple terms of PPS). If all of the convictions call for terms of probation, then the court may impose multiple terms of probation; however, the terms must run concurrently. OAR 213-012-0020(4)(b). See State v. Hagan, 140 Or App 454, 457, 916 P2d 317 (1996) (court erred in imposing seven consecutive terms of probation); State v. Leen, 113 Or App 595, 596, 832 P2d 49 (1992). In the case of a probationary or PPS term imposed when the defendant is already serving a prior sentence, OAR 213-012-0030 (discussed below). See also ORS 137.123(5) (in same criminal episode cases, authorizing only consecutive sentences of “imprisonment”).

The hypothetical case charted in Section 8-3.2.3 exemplifies OAR 213-012-0020(4)’s application. There, the primary offense was a level 8 first-degree arson, carrying a three-year PPS term. OAR 213-005-0002(2)(a). Under OAR 213-012-0020(4), that is the only supervisory term the court may impose.

Occasionally a sentencing court will impose consecutive sentences on an assortment of sex and non-sex crimes, and a sex crime will carry, for example, a PPS term under ORS 144.103. Under that statute, the length of the PPS term is equal to the crime’s statutory maximum imprisonment term minus the length of the prison term the court imposes. If the sex crime has a seriousness ranking lower than a non-sex crime, thus making the non-sex crime the primary offense, the PPS term for the primary offense may be shorter than the PPS term for the sex crime. In such a case, the special PPS statute, such as ORS 144.103, supersedes OAR 213-012-0020(3)(a) and requires the court to impose the longer PPS term for the sex crime, instead of the shorter PPS term for the primary offense. State v. Vedder, 206 Or App 424, 430-31, 136 P3d 1128 (2006), rev den, 342 Or 417 (2007) (relying on implied-amendment theory to hold that longer PPS term for sex crime superseded the shorter term for non-sexual, which had a higher seriousness ranking). See also OAR 213-005-0002(2)(b)(C) (requiring PPS term under ORS 144.103 instead of PPS term for most serious offense).

Moreover, when the defendant stands convicted of sex crimes subject to PPS terms under ORS 144.103, the single-supervisory period rule, OAR 213-012-0020(4)(a), does not apply. Delavega v. Board of Parole, 222 Or App 161, 167, 194 P3d 159 (2008) (relying on Vedder, 206 Or App at 429-31).

§ 8-4. SENTENCES IMPOSED CONSECUTIVELY TO A PRIOR REMAINING SENTENCE.

OAR 213-012-0030 applies to cases where, pursuant to ORS 137.123(1)-(3), the court runs a new incarceration sentence consecutively to an incarceration sentence imposed in a prior sentencing proceeding. OAR 213-012-0030 provides:

(1) When a sentence is imposed consecutively to a sentence imposed in a prior proceeding, the incarceration term of the new sentence is added to the remaining incarceration term of the prior sentence. If any sentence includes a prison term, the incarceration term of all sentences shall be served in prison.

(2)(a) Notwithstanding paragraph (1) of this section, if the court imposes a sentence that includes a term of incarceration that exceeds 12 months and the term is to be served consecutively to a term of incarceration of 12 months or less that was imposed in a previous proceeding, the defendant shall serve any remaining part of the previously imposed term of incarceration in the legal and physical custody of the Department.

(b) If the court imposes a felony sentence that includes a term of incarceration that is 12 months or less and the term is to be served consecutively to a term of incarceration that exceeds 12 months that was imposed in a previous proceeding or in the same proceeding, the court shall commit the defendant to the legal and physical custody of the Department.

(3) When a sentence is imposed consecutively to a sentence imposed in a prior proceeding, the supervision term or terms of the new sentence shall be served concurrently with the prior sentence as follows:

(a) If the supervision term of the new sentence is a term of probationary supervision, the supervision shall begin at the date of sentencing.

(b) If the supervision term of the new sentence is a term of post-prison supervision, the supervision term shall begin upon the completion of the incarceration term of the combined sentences, or upon release pursuant to ORS 421.508(3).

(4) Notwithstanding section (2)(b) of this rule, terms of incarceration 12 months or less imposed pursuant to ORS 166.070(2) shall be served in the legal and physical custody of the Department.

§ 8-4.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-012-0030(1) explains that if a court runs a new incarceration sentence consecutively to an incarceration sentence imposed in a prior sentencing proceeding, the new sentence commences after the defendant completes serving the preexisting sentence. OAR 213-012-0030(1) also creates a “one in prison, all in prison” rule stating, “If any sentence includes a prison term, the incarceration term of all sentences shall be served in prison.”

Although felony sentences of 12 months or less ordinarily are served in a local jail, ORS 137.124(2)(a), if the new sentence is more than 12 months and is run consecutively to a preexisting jail sentence of 12 months or less, the preexisting sentence is converted into a state prison sentence and the defendant serves both sentences in prison. OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a). Somewhat similarly, if the new sentence is 12 months or less, and is run consecutively to a preexisting prison sentence of more than 12 months, the defendant serves both sentences in state prison. ORS 137.124(2)(b); OAR 213-012-0020(2)(b).

OAR 213-012-0030(3) specifies how multiple sentences’ supervisory terms are served. In all cases, new probation and PPS terms run concurrently with any prior probation or PPS terms imposed. If the new sentence is probation, the probationary term begins to run immediately upon sentencing, even if the offender is still incarcerated on a prior sentence and has yet to start any PPS term. OAR 213-012-0030(3)(a). Guidelines commentary explains:

“This rule creates the possibility that an offender will be subject to both probation supervision and post-prison supervision during the course of the consecutive sentences. If an offender is serving a prison term when he or she is later sentenced to a probationary term in a subsequent case, the probationary term will begin on the date of sentencing. OAR [213-012-0030(3)(a)].

“After the offender has served the initial prison term, he or she will be released to post-prison supervision which will continue to run concurrently with the remainder of the probationary sentence.”

Implementation Manual at 117.

OAR 213-012-0030(3)(b) is simpler. It says that if the new sentence’s supervisory term is PPS, the defendant begins to serve it “upon the completion of the incarceration term of the combined sentences.” Id.

OAR 213-012-0030(4) applies to the crime of aggravated harassment. ORS 166.070. A person commits that crime by committing harassment against a “public safety officer,” ORS 166.070(3)(a), or a “staff member,” ORS 166.070(3)(b), with “staff member” essentially meaning persons working for the Department of Corrections or the Oregon Youth Authority. Id. (incorporating “staff member” definition from ORS 163.165). Defendants convicted of that crime always serve their incarceration sentence in state prison—even if they are no longer than 12 months.

§ 8-5. MULTIPLE SUPERVISION TERMS.

OAR 213-012-0040 applies in cases where a defendant has received multiple terms of supervision:

(1) If the offender has been sentenced to multiple terms of post-prison supervision, the terms of post-prison supervision shall be served as a single term. The maximum sanction for a post-prison supervision violation in such a case shall be limited as provided by OAR 213-011-0004 for a single term of post-prison supervision.

(2) When an offender is serving multiple terms of probationary supervision, the sentencing judge may impose revocation sanctions for supervision violations as provided by OAR 213-010-0002 for the violation of each separate term of probationary supervision.

(a) If more than one term of probationary supervision is revoked for a single supervision violation, the sentencing judge shall impose the incarceration sanctions concurrently.

(b) If more than one term of probationary supervision is revoked for separate supervision violations, the sentencing judge may impose the incarceration sanctions concurrently or consecutively.

§ 8-5.1. Multiple Post-Prison Supervision Terms.

OAR 213-012-0040(1) applies to situations where a defendant is serving multiple terms of PPS. Commentary explains:

“Whenever an offender is serving more than one term of post-prison supervision at a single time, the terms shall be treated as a single term of supervision. Consequently, the maximum sanction for supervision violations is limited by OAR [213-011-0004]. This limitation on the maximum revocation sanction applies regardless of how many terms of post-prison supervision are being served by the offender. This limitation even applies when the separate terms of post-prison supervision have been imposed in separate cases.”

Implementation Manual at 128. See also State v. Barnum, 154 Or App 24, 26, 959 P2d 1007 (1998) (explaining that multiple PPS terms effectively “merge” into a single term, even if the defendant’s incarceration terms are consecutive). But see Norris v. Bd. of Parole & Post-Prison Supervision, 237 Or App 1, 9, 238 P3d 994 (2010) (explaining that multiple terms of PPS for sex offenses under ORS 144.103 run concurrently upon release from custody).

§ 8-5.2. Revocations of Multiple Probationary Terms.

Under OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a), if the sentencing court revokes multiple probationary terms owing to a single probation violation, the court must run the revocation sanctions concurrently. For example, in State v. Lewis, 257 Or App 641, 307 P3d 560 (2013), the trial court relied on a single probation violation to revoke the defendant’s multiple probationary sentences, and then imposed consecutive sanctions. On appeal, the Court of Appeals held that State v. Miller, and its application of OAR 213-012-0020, had no bearing on the court’s authority to impose consecutive probation-revocation sanctions. Lewis, 257 Or App at 645-46. Only OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a) mattered, the Lewis court held, and it authorized only concurrent sanctions. Because the state proved only one supervision violation, the Court of Appeals reversed and remanded for resentencing. Id. at 646. See also

State v. Stokes, 133 Or App 355, 358-59, 891 P2d 13 (1995) (where court revoked two probationary terms for a single violation, it erred in imposing consecutive incarceration sanctions).

The reason for this is that OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a) is specific, or particular, as to probation revocations, whereas ORS 137.123 is a general statute that applies at the time of original sentencing. “When a general and a particular provision are inconsistent, the latter is paramount to the former[.]” ORS 174.020(2). Thus, OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a) controls, for of the two provisions at issue, it is the one that is particular to probation revocations. See Stokes, 133 Or App at 357-58 (applying concept). See also State v. Newell, 238 Or App 385, 242 P3d 709 (2010) (“trial court was correct in determining that the sole criterion for whether it could impose multiple incarceration sanctions was whether there had been multiple probation violations”).

In State v. Rusen, 369 Or 677, 704, 509 P3d 628 (2022), the Supreme Court affirmed the Court of Appeals’s decision and reaffirmed State v. Stokes. The Court rejected the state’s reliance on ORS 137.123(2) and resolved an apparent conflict between that provision and OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a), which precludes a sentencing judge from imposing consecutive incarceration sanctions upon revoking multiple terms of probation for a single violation. The Court held that ORS 137.123(2) applies only to the initial sentencing following conviction and, therefore, is compatible with OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a), which applies only after a court revokes probation.

On the other hand, OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a) does not apply where multiple probationary sentences were revoked owing a single probation violation, but the underlying crimes involved multiple victims. In that situation, Article I, section 44(1)(b), of the Oregon Constitution controls. It provides: “No law shall limit a court’s authority to sentence a criminal defendant consecutively for crimes against different victims.” Id.

In State v. Lane, 260 Or App 549, 318 P3d 750 (2014), rev’d, 357 Or 619, 355 P3d 914 (2015), the defendant had incurred multiple sentences of probation for convictions involving different victims. Although the state proved only a single probation violation, the trial court imposed consecutive revocation sanctions. Consistent with cases such as Lewis, the Court of Appeals held that under OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a), only concurrent sanction were allowed.

On review, the Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals and affirmed the trial court. First, the Supreme Court held, “[T]he prohibition in Article I, section 44(1)(b)—that no law may limit a court’s authority to impose consecutive sentences for crimes against multiple victims—applies to the imposition of probation violation sanctions.” Lane, 357 Or at 638 (emphasis added). The court then held, “[B]ecause OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a) limited the trial court’s authority to sentence defendant consecutively for his crimes against different victims, Article I, section 44(1)(b), invalidated it.” Id. at 639. Therefore, Article I, section 44(1)(b) applies to crimes having multiple victims. It authorizes (but does not require) consecutive probation-revocation sanctions in cases where courts revoke multiple sentences of probation imposed on convictions having multiple victims. Lane, 357 Or at 638-39.

However, the Supreme Court did not address, because the defendant did not assert, the claims discussed in Section 8-2.3.3.1, that section 44 is void. Because the claims were neither raised nor addressed in Lane, they present open questions. See Stranahan v. Fred Meyer, Inc., 331 Or 38, 54, 11 P3d 228 (2000) (only those matters necessary to court’s decision are stare decisis).

PRACTICE TIP: Defense counsel should assert the claims discussed in Section 8-2.3.3.1, that section 44 is void. If successful, the claims would negate the Supreme Court decision in Lane, and leave OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a) as the sole authority for imposing consecutive probation-revocation sanctions where there is only a single probation violation.

On the other hand, in certain circumstances, the court has discretion to run revocation sanctions consecutively if the defendant committed multiple probation violations. OAR 213-012-0040(2)(b).

EXAMPLE: Able and Baker are each serving two separate probationary sentences. Both of Able’s probations are revoked because he failed a urinalysis. The court must make his revocation sanctions concurrent. OAR 213-012-0040(2)(a). On the other hand, one of Baker’s probations is revoked because he failed a urinalysis, and his other probation is revoked because he failed to report to his probation officer. The court may make Baker’s sanctions consecutive. OAR 213-012-0040(2)(b).

Finally, contrary to what previously has been conventional wisdom within the defense bar, OAR 213-012-0040(2)(b) does not “prescribe [a] one-for-one rule,” State v. McFerrin, 289 Or App 96, 100, 408 P3d 263 (2017), rev dismissed, 365 Or 194 (2019)—that is, “[t]he rule does not limit the sentencing court to imposing only as many consecutive sentences as there are separate supervision violations[.]” 289 Or App at 101. Instead, “[a]s long as the multiple supervision violations are truly ‘separate,’ under [OAR 213-012-0040(2)(b)], the sentencing court may impose consecutive sentences for each term of probationary supervision that is revoked.” Id. (emphasis added). In other words, the state proving two or more “truly separate” supervision violations authorizes the trial court to impose as many consecutive sanctions as it desires. But as noted, the Supreme Court allowed review of McFerrin.1

Note: When the Supreme Court allowed review of McFerrin, it also allowed review of a companion case, State v. Sparks, 364 Or 696, 439 P3d 980 (2019). The court then consolidated the two cases for oral argument. While awaiting oral arguments, the state moved to dismiss McFerrin’s appeal on jurisdictional grounds. The court responded by putting McFerrin into abeyance (and later dismissed the appeal), and holding arguments only in Sparks. In Sparks, the trial court had imposed three consecutive sanctions on what Sparks claimed were fewer than three probation violations. The Supreme Court declined to reach the question of whether OAR 213-012-0040(2)(b) imposed a one-to-one rule, because, the court held, the state alleged and proved that the defendant had committed at least seven probation violations.

PRACTICE TIP: In McFerrin, the Supreme Court may decide between the competing constructions—that of the Court of Appeals in McFerrin, and the one-for-one rule construction the defendant asserts. Unless and until the Supreme Court affirms the Court of Appeals, when applicable, defense counsel should assert that OAR 213-012-0040(2)(b) imposes the one-for-one rule.

OAR 213-012-0040 has a gap. Neither it nor any other provision addresses the situation where a defendant is serving a term of PPS and a concurrent term of probation, violates both supervisory terms, and is revoked from probation and sanctioned on the PPS violation. Commentary addresses this gap, stating:

“Although not expressly addressed in these rules, an offender serving a term of post-prison supervision simultaneously with a probationary term is serving two separate supervisory periods. If the offender violates the conditions of post-prison supervision and the conditions of probation, separate sanctions may be imposed for both violations. The Board of Parole
and Post-Prison Supervision has the authority to sanction the post-prison supervision violation, and the sentencing judge has the authority to sanction the probation violation. If the basis for the revocation for both the post-prison supervision term and the probationary term stem from the same conduct, the separate sanctions shall be served concurrently. If, however, the supervision violations are based on separate incidents, the revocation sanctions may be imposed consecutively.”

Implementation Manual at 118-19.

Commentary would fill the gap by holding that if a defendant commits multiple violative acts, the PPS and probation revocation sanctions may be run consecutively. But if the defendant commits a single violative act, the sanctions must be run concurrently. It is unclear who makes this determination—the probation judge or the parole board.

Note that the commentary openly admits that nothing in OAR 213-012-0040 supports imposing consecutive sanctions in the scenario the commentary describes. Note also that commentary “does not carry the force of law.” State v. Anderson, 111 Or App 294, 297, 826 P2d 66 (1992); State v. Moeller, 105 Or App 434, 440 n 4, 806 P2d 130, rev dismissed, 312 Or 76 (1991). Consequently, in a case where a defendant incurs PPS and probation sanctions owing to multiple violations, defense counsel should argue against the commentary and against interpreting the rule as permitting consecutive sanctions. Instead, counsel should argue that the guidelines themselves authorize only concurrent sanctions.

This argument is supported in two ways. First, cases such as State v. Trice I and State v. Heiller make clear that consecutive terms may be imposed only when they are explicitly authorized by law. OAR 213-012-0040 does not explicitly authorize consecutive sanctions in a case where a defendant was serving a term of PPS and a term of probation. Therefore, holding that consecutive sanctions are authorized in this type of case would impermissibly insert into OAR 213-012-0040 language that the legislature omitted. ORS 174.010.

Second, although the Oregon Criminal Code generally repeals the rule of lenity, ORS 161.025(2), it remains applicable in determining whether statutes authorize consecutive terms. See, e.g., Linthwaite, 295 Or at 179. As between concurrent and consecutive PPS and probationary violation sanctions, concurrent sanctions are the more lenient. So under Linthwaite, only concurrent sanctions are authorized. But cf. State v. Richards, 361 Or 840, 401 P3d 767 (2017) (affirming trial court’s authority to revoke probation for same conduct that resulted in PPS sanction, even though the defendant had already served the PPS sanction).

OAR 213-012-0040 has a second gap. OAR 213-012-0040(2) authorizes making revocation sanctions consecutive with other revocation sanctions. It does not authorize making them consecutive with sentences imposed on new crimes. The fact that at the original sentencing the court could have imposed consecutive sentences under ORS 137.123 is irrelevant. Stokes, 133 Or App at 357-58. Therefore, revocation sanctions must be concurrent with sentences for new crimes.

EXAMPLE: A defendant is on probation in Polk County and commits a new crime in Coos County. He is convicted and sentenced in Coos County and then is brought to Polk County where the court revokes his probation. If the crimes have the same victim, or if Article I, section 44 were declared void, the Polk County court must make the defendant’s probation-revocation sentence concurrent with his sentence in Coos County.

Finally, OAR 213-012-0040(2) has yet another gap. It speaks solely in terms of making felony revocation sanctions consecutive to other felony revocation sanctions. It does not mention, so arguably does not authorize, making felony revocation sanctions consecutive to misdemeanor revocation sanctions. Under that argument, the only way felony and misdemeanor revocation sanctions may be made consecutive is by making the misdemeanor sanction consecutive to the felony. But that order of sentencing is subject to the consecutive-sentence limitations of ORS 137.545(5). A consecutive sanction on the misdemeanor conviction may be imposed only if it is authorized by ORS 137.545(5), or by Article I, section 44(1)(b) if section 44 the constitutional provision is not void).

PRACTICE TIP: Misdemeanor sentences of probation involve suspended execution or imposition of sentence. ORS 137.010(3) and (4). If the court originally suspended execution of sentence, then on revocation it may not make the sanction consecutive to anything unless the original judgment of conviction provides for a consecutive sentence. E.g., State v. Anderson, 149 Or App 506, 508, 945 P2d 594 (1997). If the court originally suspended imposition of sentence, then on revocation it must impose a sentence for the underlying offense but not for the violation of probation. State v. Barajas, 254 Or App 106, 112-13, 292 P3d 636 (2012), rev den, 353 Or 747 (2013). In the latter case, whether the misdemeanor sentence could be made consecutive would depend on the application of ORS 137.123, or Article I, section 44(1)(b).
