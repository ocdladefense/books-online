Plea Agreements

This chapter discusses guidelines provisions and other laws governing plea agreements, the significance of which is of paramount significance. As the United States Supreme Court has explained:

“Ninety-seven percent of federal convictions and ninety-four percent of state convictions are the result of guilty pleas. See Dept. of Justice, Bureau of Justice Statistics, Sourcebook of Criminal Justice Statistics Online, Table 5.22.2009; Dept. of Justice, Bureau of Justice Statistics, S. Rosenmerkel, M. Durose, & D. Farole, Felony Sentences in State Courts, 2006-Statistical Tables, p. 1 (NCJ226846, rev. Nov. 2010); Padilla v. Kentucky, 559 US 356, 130 S Ct 1473, 176 L Ed 2d 284 (2010) (recognizing pleas account for nearly 95% of all criminal convictions). The reality is that plea bargains have become so central to the administration of the criminal justice system that defense counsel have responsibilities in the plea bargain process, responsibilities that must be met to render the adequate assistance of counsel that the Sixth Amendment requires in the criminal process at critical stages. Because ours ‘is for the most part a system of pleas, not a system of trials,’ Lafler v. Cooper, 566 US 156, 132 S Ct 1376, 1381, 182 L Ed 2d 398 (2012), it is insufficient simply to point to the guarantee of a fair trial as a backstop that inoculates any errors in the pretrial process. ‘To a large extent * * * horse trading [between prosecutor and defense counsel] determines who goes to jail and for how long. That is what plea bargaining is. It is not some adjunct to the criminal justice system; it is the criminal justice system.’ Scott & Stuntz, Plea Bargaining as Contract, 101 Yale LJ 1909, 1912 (1992). See also Barkow, Separation of Powers and the Criminal Law, 58 Stan L Rev 989, 1034 (2006) (‘[Defendants] who do take their case to trial and lose receive longer sentences than even Congress or the prosecutor might think appropriate, because the longer sentences exist on the books largely for bargaining purposes. This often results in individuals who accept a plea bargain receiving shorter sentences than other individuals who are less morally culpable but take a chance and go to trial’ (footnote omitted)). In today’s criminal justice system, therefore, the negotiation of a plea bargain, rather than the unfolding of a trial, is almost always the critical point for a defendant.

“To note the prevalence of plea bargaining is not to criticize it. The potential to conserve valuable prosecutorial resources and for defendants to admit their crimes and receive more favorable terms at sentencing means that a plea agreement can benefit both parties. In order that these benefits can be realized, however, criminal defendants require effective counsel during plea negotiations. ‘Anything less * * * might deny a defendant “effective representation by counsel at the only stage when legal aid and advice would help him.”’ Massiah v. United States, 377 US 201, 204, 84 S Ct 1199, 12 L Ed 2d 246 (1964) (quoting Spano v. New York, 360 US 315, 326, 79 S Ct 1202, 3 L Ed 2d 1265 (1959) (Douglas, J., concurring)).”

Missouri v. Frye, 566 US 134, 143-44, 132 S Ct 1399, 182 L Ed 2d 379 (2012).

This chapter concludes with an Appendix that contains a checklist defense counsel should work through in preparing for plea negotiations. Moreover, counsel also should consult Cynthia Alkon, The Right to Defense Discovery in Plea Bargaining Fifty Years After Brady v. Maryland, 38 NYU Rev of Law & Social Change 407 (2014). In her introduction, Prof. Alkon explains, “Fifty years after Brady v. Maryland, defense attorneys around the United States continue to struggle to get basic information from prosecutors. This is even more of an issue in the ninety-four to ninety-seven percent of criminal cases that are resolved by guilty pleas.” Id. (footnote omitted). Prof. Alkon continues:

“[T]his article will first briefly examine how the Brady standard applies and fails to protect defendants in plea bargaining. Next, this article will explain why the recent Supreme Court decisions in Lafler and Frye demand that the Court revisit Brady and consider defense rights to discovery in the specific context of plea negotiations. The article will also offer specific suggestions for defense lawyers to better protect the record on appeal for discovery issues for plea bargaining cases post-Lafler and Frye. Finally, this article will argue for legislative reform that would require open-file discovery as a remedial approach in addition to waiting for the Supreme Court to more fully guarantee defense rights to discovery in plea bargaining. In concluding that legislative action is necessary, this article will use a recent change in Texas law as an example of useful improvements while also illustrating specific problems that can occur when policy-makers and legislators write a discovery law focused on trials and thereby fail to protect defense rights to discovery during the more common process of plea bargaining.”

Id. at 409.

§ 6-1. OAR 213-007-0001 PERMISSIBLE PLEA AGREEMENTS.

OAR 213-007-0001 states:

The sentencing judge shall comply with the rules of this division when accepting a negotiated plea as a plea agreement for any offense committed on or after the effective date of these rules.

§ 6-1.1 Intent of Provision.

The commentary to OAR 213-007-0001 states:

“Judicially approved plea agreements constitute a proper and effective means to dispose of criminal cases without trial. In Oregon under current law, 85 to 95 percent of all criminal charges are disposed of by plea agreement. This rule reflects the Guidelines Board’s judgment that plea agreements must continue to play an important role under sentencing guidelines in Oregon, subject to conditions and controls applied by the sentencing judge.”

Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 72 (1989) (hereafter Implementation Manual).

§ 6-1.2. Right to a Plea Agreement

There is no direct right to a plea agreement. However, there is a right to the use of permissible criteria in forming plea agreements. State v. Savastano, 354 Or 64, 309 P3d 1083 (2013). For example, in State v. McDonnell, 310 Or 98, 794 P2d 780 (1990), the prosecutor declined to extend a plea bargain because the victim’s family opposed one. On appeal, the court held that the prosecutor had erred in viewing the family’s consent as a prerequisite to a plea bargain, and remanded for the prosecutor to decide whether to extend a plea offer based on permissible criteria.

§ 6-1.3. Nature of Plea Agreements—Contract Theory.

Under Oregon law, plea bargains are usually treated as contracts. State v. Heisser, 350 Or 12, 19, 249 P3d 113 (2011) (when “interpreting a plea agreement, contract law generally (but not invariably) controls”); State v. McDonnell, 310 Or 98, 114-16, 794 P2d 780 (1990) (Fadeley, J., concurring in part, dissenting in part) (enumerating jurisdictions which have and have not adopted contract theory). See also United States v. Goroza, 941 F2d 905, 909 (9th Cir 1991) (embracing contract theory); United States v. Novosel, 481 F3d 1288 (10th Cir 2007) (regarding the intersection of plea agreements and contract law.)

Plea agreements have all of the elements of a valid contract: consent, capacity, consideration, and a lawful objective. Kenneth R. Davis III, Contract Law in Oregon, § 6.1 (Oregon State Bar CLE 2003 with 2008 supplement). But see Heisser, 350 Or at 23 (citing United States v. Harvey, 791 F2d 294, 300 (4th Cir 1986) for some of the differences between a plea agreement and an ordinary contract).

A plea agreement is typically a unilateral contract. The defendant accepts the offered agreement by performance, i.e., by entering a plea. A written acceptance by the defendant is unenforceable until he enters his plea; until then, the defendant has the right to refuse to enter a plea. See Taylor v. Multnomah County Deputy Sheriff’s Retirement Bd., 265 Or 445, 510 2d 339 (1973) (discussing unilateral contracts, also called option contracts at that time). See also Mabry v. Johnson, 467 US 504, 507, 81 L Ed 2d 437, 104 S Ct 2543 (1984) (accepted, unexecuted plea offer was unenforceable); United States v. Norris, 486 F3d 1045 (8th Cir 2007) (plea agreement signed by both parties was unenforceable before bargain was accepted by judge under federal procedure).

A plea bargain may become enforceable if the defendant detrimentally relies on it. No reported Oregon case discusses the relationship between plea-bargains and detrimental reliance, also called promissory estoppel. Indeed, few cases discuss detrimental reliance at all. See generally Neiss v. Ehlers, 135 Or App 218, 899 P2d 700 (1995) (Haselton, J, concurring) (discussing promissory estoppel, listing cases). But under federal law, detrimental reliance is a recognized part of plea agreements. E.g., United States v. Savage, 978 F2d 1136, 1138 (9th Cir 1992). Cf. Frye, 566 US at 154 (Scalia, J., dissenting) (“[v]irtually no cases deal with the standards for a prosecutor’s withdrawal from a plea agreement beyond stating the general rule that a prosecutor may withdraw any time prior to, but not after, the entry of a guilty plea or other action constituting detrimental reliance on the defendant’s part”).

State v. King, 361 Or 646, 398 P3d 336 (2017) likely is the case that most profoundly applies contract law principles to plea agreements. There, the defendant pled guilty to assault and robbery charges. His crimes left the victim severely injured, and the victim died six months after the defendant pled guilty. The state knew about the severity of the victim’s injuries, but “there [was] no evidence that defense counsel had the same information.” 361 Or at 664.

In the plea agreement, the state failed to reserve the right to bring murder charges in the event the victim died. Relying on the “default contractual rule,” the court held that the state was barred from filing murder charges against the defendant. The court explained,

“We approve a default rule that places the burden on the state—when it is reasonably foreseeable to the prosecutor that the victim may die and the state intends to reserve the right to reprosecute a defendant for homicide in the event of the victim’s death—to disclose its intention to the defendant as part of the plea deal, either expressly during negotiations or, preferably, as a term of the plea agreement itself.”

Id. at 666.

See also [v. Lobue], 300 Or App 340, 344 n 1, 453 P3d 929 (2019) (Lageson, J., concurring) (plea “agreements with the government should be strictly construed against the government, given its superior bargaining power and potential due process concerns”).

State v. Thomas, 281 Or App 685, 386 P3d 218 (2016) provides another example of applying contract law principles to plea agreements. There, pursuant to an agreement, the defendant pled “no contest to one count of unauthorized use of a vehicle (UUV)[.]” 281 Or App at 686. The detailed terms of the agreement said nothing about restitution. At sentencing, the state sought restitution anyway, which the trial court imposed. In the course of holding that the trial court committed reversible error by imposing restitution in accordance with the state’s breach, the Court of Appeals explained,

“Allowing the state to recommend restitution would introduce a new term that was not expressed anywhere in the printed or handwritten text of the plea agreement. To insert a new term of contract would be contrary to ordinary contract principles. We are reminded by ORS 42.230 that, “[i]n the construction of an instrument,” the court “is simply to ascertain and declare what is, in terms or in substance, contained therein, not to insert what has been omitted.” A court may not read into a contract provisions that simply do not exist.”

Id. at 693 (certain internal quotations omitted).

Finally, consider State v. Anderson, 243 Or App 222, 189 P3d 28 (2011). There, the defendant accepted a plea agreement in which he was convicted of third-degree rape and, using a 6-D grid block, was sentenced to dispositional-departure sentence of probation. A prior assault conviction was the basis of his criminal-history score of “D.” While on probation, the Court of Appeals reversed the prior assault conviction. State v. Anderson, 221 Or App 193, 189 P3d 28 (2008). Subsequent to that, the defendant violated a term of his probation and the trial court revoked his probation. He moved for modification of his criminal-history score from “D” to “G,” and his grid-block to 6-G, by excluding his now reversed assault conviction from his criminal-history assessment. The trial court denied his motion, and the Court of Appeals affirmed.

Critically, the court stated: “Defendant was fully aware that the appeal of his assault charge was pending in this court at the time he entered the plea agreement.” 243 Or App at 229. Parallel to the reasoning in State v. King, the Anderson court’s opinion suggests that the defendant could have and should have, as part of his plea agreement, negotiated a condition subsequent, stating that in the event he won his appeal, he could seek modification of his plea by excluding the assault conviction from his criminal-history assessment.

§ 6-1.3.1 Terms of the Agreement.

Plea agreements come in three forms. In a case involving multiple charges, the agreement may consist only of counts to which the accused will plead guilty or no contest (or agree to a stipulated-facts trial), and the counts the state will move to dismiss. Such an agreement leaves sentencing open and is called a “charge agreement.” Plea Bargaining in Federal Courts, The Champion, Nov. 1991, at 10. An agreement containing one or more sentencing concessions is called a “sentence agreement.” Id. at 10-11. Thus, plea agreements containing both charge and sentencing concessions are called “charge/sentencing” agreements.

The parties also may agree on other terms of the sentence, such as the grid-block, restitution amount, or counts as to which restitution may be imposed. Cf. State v. Miller, 116 Or App 432, 435, 841 P2d 678 (1992) (a criminal defendant may waive the right to object to restitution by so stating in the plea agreement). Section 6-2 discusses limitations on the agreement, regarding the defendant’s criminal history.

Plea agreements are between the parties. Accordingly, the defendant is not directly entitled to receive the sentence to which the parties have agreed; the defendant is merely entitled to have the prosecutor act in accordance with the agreement. Unless the judge agrees to accept the agreement under ORS 135.432 (discussed below), the judge remains free to impose any lawful sentence. There is no breach of the plea agreement if the judge declines to follow the prosecutor’s recommendation.

A judge other than the assigned trial judge may participate in plea negotiations at the request of both parties. ORS 135.432(1)(b). A judge may also tentatively agree to be bound by a plea agreement, but the judge retains the power to refuse to impose the agreed-upon sentence in which case the defendant is entitled to withdraw the plea. ORS 135.432(2). Such a plea is often called a “contract plea.”

Although the judge is not bound by a typical plea agreement, the state as a party to a plea agreement, is bound by it. Arguably, other government entities also may be bound by the agreement. Although Rise v. Board of Parole, 304 Or 385, 393, 745 P2d 1210 (1987) does not resolve that question, it cites federal authorities on the topic, which suggests that the plea agreement may bind other government entities, as well. Cf. United States v. Cook, 668 F2d 317 (7th Cir 1982) (prosecutor breached agreement where he agreed not to assist in seeking sentencing aggravation, but then allowed probation officer access to prosecutor’s file so officer could see unfavorable information and then use that information to persuade court to impose aggravated sentence).

PRACTICE TIP: Plea bargains involving multiple government entities, such as multiple jurisdictions or non-prosecution agencies, are hard to negotiate but can confer significant advantages for the client. An experienced prosecutor is unlikely to be willing to make concessions that bind other government entities unless, at a minimum, representatives of those entities are included in the discussions.

Some authorities hold that ambiguities in the plea bargain are construed against the state. McDonnell, 310 Or at 114-16 (Fadeley, J., concurring in part, dissenting in part) (listing cases). Ambiguous written instruments are generally construed against the drafter. Transamerica Insurance Co. v. United States Nat’l Bank, 276 Or 945, 951, 558 P2d 328 (1976). That principal also could be applied to vague terms in a written plea offer, but no reported Oregon decision so holds.

Oregon law provides that plea agreements are enforced according to their express terms. For example, in Heisser, 350 Or at 19, the defendant pleaded guilty and agreed to open sentencing. At sentencing, he objected to an aggravated-departure sentence, see Chapter 7, on the theory that the state’s notice of enhancement facts was inadequate. The trial court disagreed, ruling that the defendant had waived his right to make that objection. The Supreme Court reversed. The court explained that the plea bargain permitted the defendant to raise objections to the sentence, because the agreement provided for open sentencing. Heisser expressly holds that what the terms of the agreement are is a “legal” question. Id. at 20. The court rejected the state’s argument that there had been no “meeting of the minds” (consent), because the parties did not subjectively intend the same bargain; the bargain is based on the parties communications, not on their intent. See also State v. Dinsmore, 342 Or 1, 147 P3d 1146 (2006) (similar).

That sort of precise reading of the plea deal does not always benefit the defendant. In State v. Young, 188 Or App 247, 71 P3d 119 (2003), the defendant faced six charges, all of which would merge into a single crime of conviction. Pursuant to a plea agreement, the defendant agreed to incur three convictions and the other three charges would be dismissed. He also agreed that if he breached his obligations under the agreement, he could be tried, convicted, and sentenced on the three charges that otherwise would be dismissed.

Pursuant to the agreement, Young was convicted of the first three charges. But he breached his obligations under the agreement and consequently was convicted of and sentenced on all six charges, including the three that previously were dismissed. On appeal he claimed the last three convictions should have been merged into the first three. The court rejected that claim. It explained that the defendant “gave up his right to seek merger when he agreed that” if he breached the agreement, “he could be tried and sentenced on the” other three charges. 188 Or App at 253.

PRACTICE TIP: Defense counsel must pay attention to all the terms of the bargain, even those that, at the time of the plea, do not seem important. It is common for the indictment and plea offer, and then the plea petition and colloquy, to leave significant questions unanswered, such as the dates of offenses, the relationship between multiple offenses, or factual details that may affect grid-block assessments or probation-revocation sentences, or, in theory, later double-jeopardy protections. Counsel must decide whether to clarify those details, or, if they are likely to be clarified against the defendant, including by jeopardizing the agreement, leave them obscure for later argument.

Cases such as Heisser and Dinsmore permit a reviewing court to determine the terms of the agreement and to enforce them. Heisser expressly holds that what the terms of the agreement are is a “legal” question. Heisser, 350 Or at 20. Cf. Brock v. Wright¸ 98 Or App 323, 325, 778 P2d 999 (1989) (whether there is a meeting of the minds (consent) is a question of fact.)

At least one federal case approves a unilateral, wrong-looking decision by the prosecutor that a plea agreement had been breached. In Ricketts v. Adamson, 483 US 1, 107 S Ct 2680, 97 L Ed 2d 1 (1987), the defendant agreed to testify against co-defendants in exchange for the dismissal of charges. The co-defendants were convicted, but the convictions were reversed on appeal. Prior to re-trial, the defendant’s attorney told the prosecutor that his obligations under the plea bargain had been performed, and sought additional concessions in exchange for additional testimony. The prosecutor treated the plea agreement as breached, and re-indicted the defendant for the dismissed charges. The Court upheld the prosecutor’s actions. 483 US at 11-12.

Brock v. Wright¸ 98 Or App 323, 778 P2d 999 (1989) is similar. There, the defendant had pleaded guilty pursuant to a plea bargain, which provided that the defendant would “cooperate” with the state. The defendant believed that “cooperation” meant providing information, and the prosecutor believed it meant participating in a controlled buy. Because the defendant did not participate in the controlled buy, the prosecutor believed the plea agreement had been breached and did not make the agreed-upon recommendations at sentencing. The post-conviction court rejected the argument that counsel had been ineffective in failing to enforce the agreement, and the Court of Appeals agreed. The courts reasoned that because there was no meeting of the minds, there was no plea-bargain to enforce.

But this reasoning may not survive Heisser. Even if it does, in that case it was clear that the defendant entered a plea, believing that there was a bargain. If the defendant pleaded guilty believing there was a bargain when in fact there was not, the standard contract remedy would be to rescind the contract and return each party to its prior position. See, e.g, State Farm Fire & Casualty Co. v. Sevier, 272 Or 278, 288–289, 537 P2d 88 (1975) (regarding rescission).

PRACTICE TIP: Assuming that Brock survives cases such as Heisser and Dinsmore, it presents a substantial trap for the defense. Counsel should be very wary of agreeing to terms in the plea bargain that will generally be left to the state to construe and apply. Counsel should demand clarity about the required conduct and who decides if the agreement has been satisfied, ask for a ruling from the judge in the event of a dispute, and understand the possible remedies, such as rescission, reformation, and specific performance.

In the context of “charge agreements,” the parties occasionally agree that the state may or may not use uncharged conduct as the factual basis for aggravating factors for imposing departure sentences. See Chapter 1 (discussing using uncharged and unproven conduct to impose departure sentences). If the agreement allows the state to use uncharged conduct to aggravate the current crime’s sentence, double jeopardy may not prohibit the state from later filing the uncharged conduct as a new crime. Witte v. United States, 515 US 389, 115 S Ct 2199, 132 L Ed 2d 351 (1995).

PRACTICE TIP: In situations such as those just described, it is crucial that counsel ensure that the plea petition clearly state that the uncharged conduct will not later be charged as a new crime. If the agreement is unusually complex, it is prudent to attach and incorporate into the petition any writings describing the terms of the parties’ agreement.

§ 6-1.3.2. Remedies for Breaches of Plea Agreements.

Various state courts have held that if the defendant or his attorney made no misrepresentations during plea negotiations, the state’s “[f]ull and wholehearted compliance is required” at sentencing. State v. Hall, 104 Wash 2d 486, 706 P2d 1074, 1076 (1985). See also State v. Carreno-Maldonado, 135 Wash App 77, 143 P3d 343, 347 (2006) (consistent with Hall). The federal rule is similar. The government’s sentencing recommendation must be “reasonably consistent” with the sentence contained in the plea agreement. United States v. Canada, 960 F2d 263, 271 (1st Cir 1992). The government may not “inject [into the proceedings] material reservations about the agreement to which [it] has committed itself.” Id. at 270. Cf. Chilcote v. Zenon, 105 Or App 393, 395, 804 P2d 521 (1991). As the Third Circuit has said, “The doctrine that the government must adhere to its bargain in the plea agreement is so fundamental that ‘even though the government’s breach is inadvertent and the breach probably did not influence the judge in the sentence imposed,’ due process and equity require that the sentence be vacated.” United State v. Hayes, 946 F2d 230, 233 (3d Cir 1991) (quoting United States v. Martin, 788 F2d 184, 187 (3d Cir 1986)).

In State v. Lynch, 305 Or App 122, 469 P3d 800 (2020), the plea agreement authorized the state to seek restitution within 90 days of judgment entry. The state did so. But a few months after the 90-day deadline, the state sought, and the trial court granted, additional restitution. The defendant argued that imposing restitution after the plea agreement’s 90-day deadline violated his due process rights. The state argued that Article I, section 42, of the Oregon Constitution, authorizing crime victims to seek prompt restitution, rendered the 90-day deadline unenforceable.

In its decision, the Court of Appeals relied on Article I, section 42(2), which in pertinent part states: “Nothing in this section reduces a criminal defendant’s rights under the Constitution of the United States.” The court held:

“[A] victim may not be granted restitution if that would result in a reduction of the criminal defendant’s [due process] rights afforded by the federal constitution. Here, as explained above, even if the term at issue might conflict with Article I, section 42(1)(d), in some circumstances, the resolution of the charges against defendant under the terms of the plea bargain gave defendant a protected right under the federal constitution to the benefit of his bargain. Under the plain terms of Article I, section 42(2) (and likely the Supremacy Clause as well), that federal constitutional right supersedes any conflicting right or interest under the terms of Article I, section 42(2), itself.”

Lynch, 305 Or App at 130.

PRACTICE TIP: Lynch should be compared with State v. Rieker, 302 Or App 613, 461 P3d 1083 (2020).1 In Lynch, the defense negotiated for the inclusion of the 90-day deadline as part of the plea agreement. In Rieker, the plea agreement did not contain the deadline. Defense counsel should follow the lead in Lynch, by negotiating for the inclusion of the 90-day deadline. The defendant then would have a due process right to compliance with the 90-day deadline. Under the federal Supremacy Clause, US Const, Art VI, his due process right then would supersede the victim’s rights under Article I, section 42.

In an ordinary contract dispute, damages are the typical remedy. By contrast, specific performance is discretionary, governed by equitable principles of justice and fairness and the weighing of the parties’ responsibilities. Combs v. Loebner, 315 Or 444, 450, 846 P2d 401 (1993).

By contrast, in the event of a breach of a plea agreement, remedies at law, such as damages, are not available. What are available instead are the equitable remedies of specific performance and rescission. See Heisser, 350 Or at 19 (ordering specific performance with minimal discussion as to the remedy); Rise, 304 Or at 392-03 (recognizing rescission as an available remedy for failed plea agreement). See also State v. Johnson, 288 Or App 220, 225-26, 406 P3d 139 (2017) (where tentative plea agreement allowed defendant to be considered for early release programs under ORS 137.750, trial court erred in imposing sentence that denied early-release program eligibility without first giving defendant an opportunity to rescind).

Santobello v. New York, 404 US 257, 92 S Ct 495, 30 L Ed 2d 427 (1971) is the lead federal case discussing remedies for the state’s breach of an agreement. “Santobello prohibits not only ‘explicit repudiation of the government’s assurances, but must in the interests of fairness be read to forbid end-runs around them.’” Canada, 960 F2d at 279 (quoting United States v. Voccola, 600 F Supp 1534, 1537 (D.R.I. 1985); emphasis added). In the event of a breach, a court may order one of two mutually exclusive remedies. It may order a new sentencing hearing before a different judge where the state must specifically perform its contractual obligations. Santobello, 404 US at 263. See also State v. Schaupp, 111 Wash 2d 34, 757 P2d 970, 971-72 (1988) (consistent with Santobello). Alternatively, the court may order rescission. Schaupp, 757 P2d at 973.

United States v. Heredia, 768 F3d 1220 (9th Cir 2014) provides an example of the government making a “forbidden end-run around” the assurances it made during plea negotiations. There, in exchange for the government’s recommendation of a six-month sentence, the defendant pled guilty to a count of illegal reentry and waived certain constitutional rights. The government recommended six months, but in its sentencing memorandum presented a lengthy discussion of the defendant’s criminal history. At the sentencing hearing, the defendant did not withdraw his plea when given the option. The court imposed a 21-month term of imprisonment.

On appeal, the Ninth Circuit held that when the government commits to a plea agreement, it may not use prejudicial or inflammatory language to implicitly argue for a sentence harsher than the agreed upon sentence. The panel reasoned that the purpose of a plea agreement is to provide a unified front before the court, but here, the government abandoned the front by providing irrelevant and prejudicial details. The court vacated and remanded for further proceedings before a different trial court judge.

PRACTICE TIP: Article I, section 42(a), of the Oregon Constitution confers on crime victims the right to speak at sentencing. Defense counsel should be wary of prosecutors relying on that provision to enlist the crime victim as its surrogate, to provide the sort of information at sentencing that would be a “forbidden end-run” if directly provided by the prosecution. Moreover, the law forbidding such an “end run” is protected by the defendant’s federal due process rights. The defendant’s federal constitutional rights supersede the victim’s state constitutional rights. Therefore, defense counsel should rely on the defendant’s federal due process rights to object to any “end-run-by-surrogate” that the prosecution might try to execute.

Santobello leaves the decision between specific performance and rescission to the trial court’s discretion, but provides minimal guidance as to when one or the other may be appropriate. The defendant in Santobello sought to withdraw his plea, and the opinion implies that specific performance more likely is the appropriate remedy. 404 US at 263. Cf. Chilcote, 105 Or App at 395 (when prosecutor violated agreement not to seek incarceration and then withdrew that recommendation, counsel was inadequate in failing to seek specific performance, i.e., resentencing in front of a different judge.)

Nevertheless, federal courts have held that the court makes the choice between the two remedies. Canada, 960 F2d at 271. Conversely, some state courts have held that the defendant chooses the remedy (absent compelling reasons to allow otherwise). State v. Harrison, 148 Wash 2d 550, 61 P3d 1104, 1107 (2003); Schaupp, 757 P2d at 971-72; State v. Miller, 110 Wash 2d 528, 756 P2d 122, 126 (1988). See also State v. Munoz, 305 Mont 139, 23 P3d 922, 927 (2001) (agreeing with Miller, and holding that unless state shows by clear and convincing evidence that defendant’s choice of remedy would result in miscarriage of justice, defendant’s choice of remedy controls). Indeed, in Washington the defendant must be given the choice of specific performance even if the stipulated sentence is unauthorized. Disallowing the choice violates his due process rights. Schaupp, 757 P2d at 973; Miller, 756 P2d at 124-25.

Consider, for example, State v. Thomas. There, the trial court imposed restitution in accordance with the state’s request. But the request was a breach, because the plea agreement did not allow the state to seek restitution. The defendant objected. 281 Or App at 688. After the trial court overruled his objection, the court scheduled a restitution hearing. Id. At that hearing, the defendant again objected and demanded specific performance. Id. at 689.

On appeal, the state defended its earlier actions by arguing “that because defendant did not ask to withdraw his plea but asked instead for a restitution hearing, defendant’s conduct shows that ‘the parties had not agreed to no restitution.’” Id. at 690. The Court of Appeals rejected that claim. In doing so, it relied on Stewart v. Cupp, 12 Or App 167, 507 P2d 503 (1973), which holds:

“In contract cases the victim of a breach frequently has a choice of remedies; he can affirm the contract and seek damages, or disaffirm it and seek to be reinstated to the position he was in before it was consummated. As applied to plea bargaining, ‘affirming the contract’ would, in effect, result in specific performance of the prosecutor’s promise that induced the defendant to plead guilty, and ‘disaffirming the contract’ would enable the defendant to withdraw his guilty plea, or pleas, thereby restoring all parties to the [s]tatus quo ante.”

12 Or App at 173 (internal citation omitted) (citing Santobello, 404 US at 262).

Because the trial court failed to remedy the state’s breach in any manner, the Court of Appeals “reverse[d] and remand[ed] for further proceedings under a correct understanding of the law.” Thomas, 281 Or App at 695.

Rescission and specific performance must be sought in separate proceedings. On direct appeal, an appellate court may order specific performance, ORS 138.105(7), so defendants may use that proceeding to seek that remedy. But on direct appeal, an appellate court may not order the remedy of rescission; such a remedy must be sought in post-conviction. See, e.g., State v. Clevenger, 297 Or 234, 683 P2d 1360 (1984).

Thus, a post-conviction court may order rescission. But it not be able to order specific performance, because that remedy could have been sought on direct appeal. See ORS 138.550(2) (precluding post-conviction petitioner from raising claims he did or could have raised on direct appeal). See also Palmer v. State, 318 Or 352, 867 P2d 1368 (1994) (regarding the scope of post-conviction review). Accord State v. Arnold, 214 Or App 201, 212, 164 P3d 334 (2007) (discussing reviewability of sentencing errors).2

Situations arise where the state seeks to withdraw from a plea agreement the court previously accepted. If the state seeks such withdrawal because of a change in the law, the withdrawal should be denied.

For example, in Aragon v. Wilkinson ex rel. County of Maricopa, 97 P3d 886 (Ariz App 2004), the defendant’s presumptive sentence was 10 years, but the plea agreement allowed the state to advocate for a sentence of up to 21 years. Not long before the scheduled sentencing hearing, the United States Supreme Court issued its decision in Blakely v. Washington, 542 US 526, 124 S Ct 2531, 159 L Ed 2d 403 (2004). Blakely clarified that Sixth Amendment principles restricting the imposition of a sentence of greater than the presumptive term, see Chapter 1 (discussing principles), which principles forbade the state from seeking the 21-year sentence. Based on the Blakely clarification, the trial court allowed the state to withdraw from the plea agreement. The appellate court reversed, stating: “A change in the law between the time a plea is accepted and sentencing is not a sufficient reason for withdrawal.” Aragon, 97 P3d at 890.

Somewhat similarly, in State v. Heisser, 232 Or App 320, 222 P3d 719 (2009), aff’d, 350 Or 12, 249 P3d 113 (2011), the defendant pled guilty pursuant to the parties’ plea agreement. At sentencing, the defendant voiced a procedural objection to the state’s request for an aggravated departure. The state then moved to vacate the plea, and the court allowed the motion. The defendant was tried to a jury, was convicted, and was sentenced to a prison term that exceeded the sentence contemplated in the plea agreement.

On appeal, the Court of Appeals held that no statute authorized the trial court to allow the state’s motion to set aside the plea. 232 Or App at 328. Moreover, the trial court did not purport to exercise some sort of inherent authority when it allowed the state’s motion. Id. at 329. Consequently, the Court of Appeals held, the trial court erred in allowing the motion. The court reversed and remanded with instructions to vacate the judgment, to reinstate the defendant’s guilty plea, and for resentencing. The Supreme Court affirmed. 350 Or at 27-28.

PRACTICE TIP: “In Oregon,” it is well settled that “the common law in criminal cases was abolished with the adoption of the Deady code in 1864.” State v. Nussbaum, 261 Or 87, 91, 491 P2d 1013 (1971). Moreover, none of the criminal law principles found in the common law “has * * * been resurrected.” State ex rel Juv. Dept. v. Merritt, 83 Or App 378, 380, 732 P2d 46 (1987). Finally, “the legislature generally has plenary authority to enact laws governing matters of criminal procedure and the sentences for criminal offenses, subject to applicable constitutional restrictions.” State v. McDonnell, 329 Or 375, 383, 987 P2d 486 (1999). Defense counsel should rely on these principles and, in addition, should rely on the principle that in construing statutes the court is “not to insert what has been omitted,” ORS 174.010, to argue that owing to the lack of statutory authority, trial courts have no inherent authority to vacate guilty pleas on the state’s motion. Cf. State v. Person, 316 Or 585, 592, 853 P2d 813 (1993) (notwithstanding that former statute did not provide trial court authority to grant continuances sua sponte, state claimed that “the trial court retains the inherent authority to continue a case on its own motion”; Supreme Court rejected that claim when, citing ORS 174.010, it “decline[d] the state’s * * * invitation to add to the statute what the legislature did not include”).

Occasionally, the state, as part of its consideration, makes agreements that turn out to be contrary to law. Various cases hold that the state’s mistake of law does not excuse it from fulfilling its contractual obligations under plea agreements. See, e.g., United States v. Manzo, 675 F3d 1204, 1210-11 (9th Cir 2012); United States v. Transfiguracion, 442 F3d 1222, 1227 (9th Cir 2006); United States v. De la Fuente, 8 F3d 1333, 1335 (9th Cir 1993).

§ 6-1.4. Plea Agreements and Advice of Counsel.

Part of the Sixth Amendment right to the adequate assistance of counsel is the right to adequate assistance in deciding whether to accept or reject plea offers. Cf. Frye, 566 US at 145 (counsel was ineffective in failing to convey a favorable plea offer). The Oregon rule is similar. E.g., Hartzog v. Keeney, 304 Or 57, 64, 742 P2d 600 (1987).

Various pre-guidelines cases hold that if, in advising a client to accept a plea offer, counsel neglects to inform the client of a minimum-sentence possibility, and if the court imposes such a minimum, the attorney will have rendered inadequate assistance if the failure to advise affected the client’s decision to accept the offer. E.g., Moen v. Peterson, 312 Or 503, 513, 824 P2d 404 (1991); Hartzog 304 Or 57.

“Boilerplate” clauses in plea petitions, specifying ORS 161.605 maximums of five, 10, or 20 years, can create similar problems. These maximums rarely apply in guidelines cases. But if they induce defendants to plead guilty, the defendants’ pleas could be declared invalid.

Consider a defendant whose criminal-history score is E, and who is charged with a level 6 delivery of a Schedule I controlled substance (other than marijuana) that is classified as a Class A felony, ORS 475.840(1)(a), and no “enhancement facts” (as are discussed in Chapter 1). Under the guidelines his grid block would be 6-E, which carries a presumptive sentencing range of 10-12 months’ incarceration. The defendant’s maximum sentence would be his grid block’s presumptive maximum of 12 months. However, the alleged Class A felony has a statutory maximum sentence of 20 years’ imprisonment. ORS 161.605(1). That maximum is 20 times greater than the guidelines presumptive maximum, but the 12-month presumptive maximum controls. See, e.g., State v. Sawatzky, 195 Or App 159, 172, 96 P3d 1288 (2004) (under the Apprendi rule, see Chapter 1, “the relevant ‘prescribed statutory maximum’ under the Oregon sentencing guidelines is the presumptive sentence that the court determines based on the offender’s criminal history and crime seriousness score”).

Suppose that in the above hypothetical, the state offered to recommend a presumptive minimum sentence of 10 months if the defendant pleads guilty, and the defendant takes the plea. If defense counsel advised that the maximum exposure by going to trial was 12 months’ imprisonment, counsel will have given accurate advice. But if boilerplate in the plea petition stated the defendant’s maximum exposure was 20 years, and counsel did not advise the defendant that 20 years was legally impossible, and that 12 months was the maximum, counsel will have allowed the defendant to believe that his maximum exposure was 20 times greater than what it truly is. Under Moen and Hartzog, that failure to advise very well could constitute inadequate assistance of counsel, and grounds for vacating the plea. See Hinton v. Hill, 342 Or 222, 149 P3d 1205 (2006) (plea petition vacated where defendant was informed that maximum possible sentence was 226 months, but it actually was 496 months, and court imposed 366-month sentence).

PRACTICE TIP: Because many judges wish to inform the defendant of the maximum penalty ORS 161.605—even in cases where that maximum has no application—a compromise position is to note both the statutory maximum and the guidelines maximum on the plea petition. In addition, counsel should clearly inform the client that if the guidelines maximum is the lesser of the two—as it almost always will be—the shorter, guidelines maximum controls.

§ 6-1.5. Appellate Review in Plea Agreement Cases, Including Conditional Plea Agreement Cases.

Appellate review in guilty or no contest plea cases is sharply limited. These limitations are found in ORS 138.105, which states in relevant part:

“(5) The appellate court has no authority to review the validity of the defendant’s plea of guilty or no contest, or a conviction based on the defendant’s plea of guilty or no contest, except that:

“(a) The appellate court has authority to review the trial court’s adverse determination of a pretrial motion reserved in a conditional plea of guilty or no contest under ORS 135.335.

“(b) The appellate court has authority to review whether the trial court erred by not merging determinations of guilt of two or more offenses, unless the entry of separate convictions results from an agreement between the state and the defendant.

“ * * * * *

“(9) The appellate court has no authority to review any part of a sentence resulting from a stipulated sentencing agreement between the state and the defendant.”

(Emphasis added.)

Thus, the validity of an ordinary plea agreement is beyond appellate review, ORS 138.105(5); instead, and as previously explained, State v. Clevenger establishes that such agreements are reviewable only on post-conviction relief. Similarly, in sentencing agreement cases, components of the sentence to which the parties stipulated are beyond appellate review. ORS 138.105(5)(b) and (9).3

Thus, in State v. Wheeler, 320 Or App 273, 511 P3d 1142 (2022) (per curiam), the

“[d]efendant stipulated with the state to specific grid block classifications. Defendant [then] challenge[d] his total sentence, including the time he served on probation, as unconstitutional. But defendant stipulated with the state to that total, including to the potential consequence that he may violate probation within the probationary period and then still have to serve the stipulated prison and PPS sentence. Because defendant therefore is challenging a sentence ‘that result[ed] from a stipulated sentencing agreement’ with the state, ORS 138.105(9), we cannot review the issue that defendant presents to us.”

320 Or App at 276.

The limitations on appellate review should be considered in plea-bargaining. If the defendant has a good issue for appeal, involving an adverse ruling on a pretrial motion (e.g., a motion to suppress evidence), that issue will not be reviewable on appeal if the defendant enters an ordinary guilty or no contest plea.

But ORS 135.335(3) authorizes the defendant to enter a “conditional plea,” i.e., to enter a guilty or no contest that is conditioned on his preserving for appeal a “specified pretrial motion.” “[A] pretrial motion reserved in a conditional plea of guilty or no contest under ORS 135.335” is reviewable on appeal. ORS 138.105(5)(a). This procedure was intended as an alternative to the clumsy process of using “stipulated facts” bench trials to preserve adverse pretrial rulings for appeal. To be valid, a conditional plea must be done with the “consent of the court and the state” and must be reduced to writing. ORS 135.335(3). If the defendant “prevails on appeal [he] may withdraw” the guilty or no contest plea. Id.

Defense counsel should be wary of the requirement that the defendant “reserv[e], in writing, the right, on appeal from the judgment, to a review of an adverse determination of any specified pretrial motion.” Id. (emphasis added). In City of Lake Oswego v. Albright, 222 Or App 117, 193 P3d 988 (2008), defendant’s trial counsel prepared a written conditional plea that failed to specify the pretrial motion his client wished to reserve for appeal. The Court of Appeals held that owing to that lack of specification, it could not review the defendant’s claim.

Moreover, defense counsel should be wary of using a conditional plea to preserve a speedy trial claim. In State v. Ralston, 310 Or App 470, 496, 486 P3d 822 (2021), the Court of Appeals explained:

“Because this case was resolved via guilty plea, the record does not contain evidence about the officer’s testimony. Defendants who raise a speedy trial motion pre-trial, then resolve the case with a plea, add an additional complexity to their already difficult task of establishing prejudice—showing the materiality of evidence never seen, to a trial that never happened.

“Similarly, because materiality can sometimes only truly be known in the context of a tried case, not every speedy trial motion can be resolved conclusively pretrial. Certainly, in some instances, all the factors can be shown pretrial, but, in other circumstances, a court may not be able to assess materiality until after the close of the state’s case. Accordingly, it may be necessary for a trial court to defer ruling on a speedy trial motion, or for prudent defense counsel to reraise the motion at the close of the case in chief.”

§ 6-2. OAR 213-007-0002 CRIMINAL HISTORY.

OAR 213-007-0002 regulates the parties’ ability to negotiate a defendant’s criminal history score:

(1) An offender’s criminal history classification shall be accurately represented to the sentencing judge in the plea agreement.

(2) If a controversy exists as to the inclusion of a prior conviction or juvenile adjudication in an offender’s criminal history or as to the classification of a prior conviction or juvenile adjudication, the district attorney and defense may stipulate to the inclusion, exclusion or classification of the conviction or juvenile adjudication as part of a plea agreement subject to the approval of the sentencing judge.

§ 6-2.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-007-0002 is corollary to ORS 135.407(1). The statute states:

“Whenever a plea agreement is presented to the sentencing judge, the defendant’s criminal history classification, as set forth in the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission, shall be accurately represented to the trial judge in the plea agreement. If a controversy exists as to whether a prior conviction or juvenile adjudication should be included in the defendant’s criminal history, or as to its classification under rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission, the district attorney and the defendant may stipulate to the inclusion, exclusion or classification of the conviction or adjudication as part of the plea agreement subject to approval of the court.”

Id. (emphasis added).

As the Implementation Manual explains, the guidelines “prohibits any misrepresentation of the criminal history to the sentencing judge in the context of a plea agreement. This prohibition includes the intentional non-disclosure of a prior conviction or juvenile adjudication by the state.” Id. at 72.

Accordingly, the Court of Appeals has explained: “Under ORS 135.407(1), a plea agreement must include the defendant’s criminal history classification.” State v. Guyton, 126 Or App 143, 146, 868 P2d 1335, rev den, 319 Or 36 (1994), overruled on other grounds, State v. Hoffmeister, 164 Or App 192, 990 P2d 910 (1999). The Guyton court explained: “Including the grid block in the agreement gives the court information from which it can decide to accept or reject the parties’ agreement.” 126 Or App at 146.

But the provisions requiring that the trial court be provided the defendant’s criminal history must be reconciled with the constitutional principle that unless the defendant has validly waived his rights against self-incrimination, those rights extend through sentencing Mitchell v. United States, 526 US 314, 325, 119 S Ct 1307, 143 L Ed 2d 424 (1999). See also State v. Barone, 329 Or 210, 231, 986 P2d 5 (1999) (right against self-incrimination continues until after person has “been convicted and [his appeal or] his time for appeal [has] run”). A defendant providing the court damaging criminal-history information would conflict with his penal interests. Consequently, requiring him to provide that information could violate his rights against self-incrimination.

The guidelines actually address this problem. As explained in Chapter 3, the state bears the burden of proving a defendant’s criminal history. This burden should be strictly applied to protect the defendant’s constitutional rights. Thus, ORS 135.407(1) should be read as applying only to the state. See, e.g., Jones v. United States, 526 US 227, 239, 119 S Ct 1215, 143 L Ed 2d 311 (1999) (when narrowing construction would save statute from fatal constitutional defect, statute should be so construed); State v. Kitzman, 323 Or 589, 602, 920 P2d 134 (1996) (same).

PRACTICE TIP: Counsel should be cautious in this situation and seek advice from other practitioners regarding the ethical tension between the client’s interests and counsel’s obligation to speak truthfully to the court. Counsel may ethically stipulate to criminal history proffered by the state, but counsel should not affirmatively misrepresent the defendant’s criminal history. See also Chapter 3.

§ 6-2.2. Plea Agreements Containing Stipulated Criminal History Scores.

ORS 135.407(1)-(2) and OAR 213-007-0002(2) recognize that a defendant’s criminal history score may be in dispute. For example, the parties may disagree as “to the treatment of out-of-state convictions and juvenile adjudications under OAR 213-004-0011, and the classification of Burglary I convictions or juvenile adjudications under OAR 213-004-0010.” Implementation Manual at 73. In that situation, the parties may “resolve such disagreements by stipulation subject to the sentencing judge’s approval.” Id. The stipulation would be part of the agreement. Once accepted, it would be binding.

§ 6-2.3. Use of Stipulated Criminal History Scores in Subsequent Sentencing Hearings.

Commentary to OAR 213-007-0002(1) says that stipulated criminal history scores “may not be used as evidence of prior convictions in any other proceeding unrelated to the current sentencing matter.” Implementation Manual at 72. In other words, the commentary says, a stipulated, judicially approved criminal history score may not be given collateral estoppel effect in a subsequent sentencing hearing for a different crime.4

§ 6-3. OAR 213-007-0003. STIPULATED GRID BLOCK.

OAR 213-007-0003 regulates the parties’ ability to negotiate a defendant’s grid block:

(1) Subject to the provisions of OAR 213-007-0002 and the approval of the sentencing judge, the district attorney and defense may stipulate to the grid block classification within the Sentencing Guidelines Grid which will provide the presumptive sentence for the offender.

(2) If the sentencing judge accepts the stipulated grid block classification and imposes a sentence other than the presumptive sentence for the stipulated grid block, the sentence is a departure.

§ 6-3.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-007-0003 is corollary to provisions in ORS 135.407. In pertinent part, the statute provides:

“(2) The district attorney and the defendant may stipulate to the grid block classification within the sentencing guidelines grid established by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission that will provide the presumptive sentence range for the offender. The sentencing judge may accept the stipulated classification and impose the presumptive sentence provided in the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission for that grid block.

“(3) If the district attorney and the defendant stipulate to a grid block classification within the sentencing guidelines grid, and the sentencing judge accepts the stipulated classification but imposes a sentence other than the presumptive sentence provided by rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission, the sentence is a departure sentence and is subject to rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission related to departures.”

Id.

Commentary to ORS 135.407(3) states:

“The parties may stipulate to an appropriate grid block classification for an offender without also agreeing to the actual sentence to be imposed. If this situation occurs, the sentencing judge is free to impose the presumptive sentence or to depart from the guidelines. If a departure sentence is imposed, it must be supported by substantial and compelling reasons as required by OAR [213-008-0001]. It is not, however, appealable as a departure sentence pursuant to [ORS 138.105(8)(b),] because such appeal is precluded as a plea-agreement sentence. See [ORS 138.105(9)].”

Implementation Manual at 71.

But the commentary is patently incorrect. Appellate review of sentences can be intricate, but generally, only the terms to which the appealing party stipulated are not reviewable. ORS 138.105(9). If the parties stipulate to a grid-block, they stipulate only to a presumptive range. Certainly a sentence imposed within that range is not reviewable. ORS 138.105(8)(a)(A). But a sentence imposed outside that range—a “departure sentence,” see Chapter 7—is reviewable. See State v. Kephart (explaining scope of ORS 138.105(9)’s predecessor, former ORS 138.222(2)(d).

§ 6-4. OAR 213-007-0004. STIPULATED PRESUMPTIVE SENTENCE.

OAR 213-007-0004 regulates the parties’ ability to negotiate for a sentence within the presumptive range of the defendant’s grid block:

(1) The district attorney and the defense may stipulate to a specific sentence within the presumptive sentence range for the stipulated grid block classification.

(2) If the sentencing judge accepts the plea agreement, the judge shall impose the stipulated sentence.

§ 6-4.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-007-0004 is corollary to ORS 135.407(4). The statute states: “The district attorney and defendant may stipulate to a specific sentence within the presumptive range provided by provisions of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission for the stipulated offender classification. If the sentencing judge accepts the plea agreement, the judge shall impose the stipulated sentence.” Id.

Moreover, the commentary to the statute states: “If the parties stipulate to an appropriate grid block classification for an offender, they may stipulate to a specific sentence within the presumptive sentence range. If the agreement is accepted, the sentencing judge shall impose the stipulated sentence.” Implementation Manual at 71 (emphasis added). See also Kephart, 320 Or at 442 (dicta: if under ORS 135.407(4) the parties “stipulate to a specific sentence within the presumptive range for the stipulated offender classification * * * [and] the judge accepts the plea agreement, the judge must impose the stipulated sentence”).

In other words, ORS 135.407(4) and OAR 213-007-0004(2) say that once a court accepts a stipulated, presumptive sentencing agreement, it must impose the stipulated sentence. The court lacks authority to impose any other sentence.

Unfortunately, commentary to OAR 213-007-0004(2) states: “If the court imposes any other sentence” than the one the previously approved agreement calls for, “the state or defendant may withdraw [from] the plea agreement.” Implementation Manual at 74. This commentary essentially mirrors ORS 135.432(3), which states:

“If the trial judge concurs[, i.e., accepts a stipulated sentencing agreement], but later decides that the final disposition of the case should not include the sentence concessions contemplated by the plea agreement, the trial judge shall so advise the defendant and allow the defendant a reasonable period of time in which to either affirm or withdraw a plea of guilty or no contest.”

Thus, both guidelines commentary and ORS 135.432(3) would authorize a sentencing court to decline to impose the stipulated, presumptive sentence the court previously accepted as part of a plea petition. The commentary and statute go on to say that if the court refuses to impose the sentence, it must allow the defendant to withdraw his plea. If the defendant affirms his plea, the court may impose whatever lawful sentence it deems fit.

Insofar as stipulated, presumptive-sentence agreements are concerned, the commentary on page 74 of the Implementation Manual conflicts with the commentary on page 71. Moreover, both the page 74 commentary and ORS 135.432(3) conflict with the texts of ORS 135.407(4) and OAR 213-007-0004(2). The question then is which set of authority controls: the authority allowing courts to impose whatever lawful sentence they deem fit, after first giving the defendant the opportunity to rescind the presumptive-sentence agreement), or the authority absolutely requiring the court to impose the previously accepted presumptive-sentence agreement.

Principles of statutory construction resolve the conflict. Those principles’ purpose is to effectuate the legislative intent underlying the provisions. The provisions’ text, context, and legislative history are the primary focus in discerning that intent. State v. Gaines, 346 Or 160, 171-72, 206 P3d 1043 (2009); ORS 174.020(1)(a).

ORS 135.432(3) is a general statute. On the other hand, ORS 135.407(4) and OAR 213-007-0004(2) specifically apply to guidelines cases and, even more specifically, to presumptive-sentence guidelines cases. Thus, the provisions specifically applying to presumptive-sentence guidelines cases—ORS 135.407(4) and OAR 213-007-0004(2)—should control over the general statute, ORS 135.432(3). See ORS 174.020(2) (“[w]hen a general and particular provision are in conflict, the latter is paramount to the former”).

Furthermore, ORS 135.432(3) was created in 1973. Or Laws 1973, ch 836, § 173. The legislature never has substantively amended the statute, Or Laws 1997, ch 313, § 4; Or Laws 1987, ch 202, § 1, so ORS 135.432(3) effectively is a 1973 statute. See Corp. of Sisters of Mercy v. Lane County, 123 Or 144, 164, 261 P 694 (1927) (where statute “has been re-enacted from time to time, but always in the same language,” “such re- enactment is considered as a continuation of the language so repeated, and not as a new enactment of law”). On the other hand, ORS 135.407 and OAR 213-007-0004(2) were created later in time, in 1989. Or Laws 1989, ch 790, §§ 2(4) and 87.

The pertinent portions of ORS 135.407(4) and OAR 213-007-0004(2) irreconcilably conflict with ORS 135.432(3). The provisions created later in time—ORS 135.407(4) and OAR 213-007-0004(2)—should control and apply in guidelines cases involving judicially accepted presumptive sentencing agreements. See, e.g., State v. Shumway, 291 Or 153, 160, 630 P2d 796 (1981) (where statutes irreconcilably conflict, statute created later in time controls); State v. Vedder, 206 Or App 424, 430-31, 136 P3d 1128 (2006), rev den, 342 Or 417 (2007) (same); State v. Lawler, 144 Or App 456, 463, 927 P2d 99 (1996) (same).

Finally, ORS 174.020(3) requires the court to “give the weight to [a statute’s] legislative history that the court considers to be appropriate.” See also Gaines, 346 Or at 171-72 (applying ORS 174.020(3)). The commentary is the only possibly useful legislative history. As the Court of Appeals has stated: “Although commentary is not authoritative, [it is persuasive if] it accurately reflects the legislative intent.” State v. Seals, 113 Or App 700, 703, 833 P2d 1344 (1992) (emphasis added).

For judicially approved presumptive sentencing agreements, the page 74 commentary finds support in the text of ORS 135.432(3). But as previously explained, that is a general statute. It does not specifically apply to guidelines cases, much less to presumptive-sentence guidelines cases. Conversely, the page 74 commentary finds absolutely no support in, and in fact conflicts with the texts of ORS 135.407(4) and OAR 213-007-0004(2). For these reasons, under ORS 174.020(3), courts should accord the page 74 commentary no weight. Cf. State v. Anderson, 111 Or App 294, 297, 826 P2d 66 (1992) (rejecting commentary’s interpretation of a guidelines provision).

In sum, dicta in State v. Kephart state that ORS 135.407(4) and OAR 213-007-0004(2) require the sentencing court to impose the previously accepted, stipulated presumptive sentence. 320 Or at 442. Although this claim was presented in State v. George, 127 Or App 581, 873 P2d 468 (1994), the Court of Appeals did not reach it. The court instead held that under former ORS 138.222(2)(d), it could not review the claim. George, 127 Or App at 584. Interestingly, State v. Kephart overruled George’s reviewability holding sub silentio. Thus, no court has rejected the claim raised in George, and in Kephart, the Supreme Court embraced the claim in dicta. The claim that ORS 135.407(4) and OAR 213-007-0004(2) require the sentencing court to impose the previously accepted, stipulated presumptive sentence is viable. Counsel should make it in appropriate cases.

§ 6-5. OAR 213-007-0005 STIPULATED NON-PRESUMPTIVE SENTENCES.

OAR 213-007-0005 regulates the parties’ ability to stipulate to departure sentences, and to optional probationary sentences:

(1) The district attorney and defense may stipulate to a sentence outside the presumptive sentence range for a stipulated grid block classification.

(2) If the parties stipulate to an optional probationary sentence, the sentencing judge may accept the plea agreement only after making the findings as required by OAR 213-005-0006.

(3) If the parties stipulate to a departure sentence, the sentencing judge may accept the plea agreement if the judge finds on the record substantial and compelling reasons for the departure.

§ 6-5.1. Intent of Provision.

This provision is corollary to ORS 135.407(5), which states: “The district attorney and the defendant may stipulate to a sentence outside the presumptive sentence range for a stipulated grid block classification. The sentencing judge may accept an agreement for an optional probationary sentence or a departure sentence as provided in rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.”

Commentary to ORS 135.407(5) explains both the statute and OAR 213-007-0005:

“If the parties stipulate to an appropriate grid block classification for an offender, they may also stipulate to a specific sentence outside the presumptive sentence range. If the sentencing judge accepts the agreement, the sentence must be imposed in a manner consistent with the rules for departures or optional probationary sentences. Because the sentence results from a plea agreement, it will not be appealable. See [ORS 138.105(9)].”

Implementation Manual at 71-72.

State v. Adams, 315 Or 359, 847 P2d 397 (1993) implicitly applied ORS 135.407(5) and OAR 213-007-0005. There, the defendant was charged with murder but pleaded guilty to first-degree manslaughter. His correct grid block was 10-G, which has a presumptive-sentence range of 66 to 70 months’ imprisonment. The parties stipulated to using an incorrect grid block of 10-E, which has a presumptive-sentence range of 81 to 90 months’ imprisonment, and they further stipulated to a sentence of 84 months. The sentencing court accepted the stipulations and imposed the 84-month sentence. Adams, 315 Or at 363-64.

The defendant appealed. He claimed the 84-month sentence was invalid, because it exceeded the presumptive maximum of his correct grid block of 10-G. Supreme Court ruled that because the parties stipulated to the sentence, it was not reviewable on appeal. Id. at 366-67.
