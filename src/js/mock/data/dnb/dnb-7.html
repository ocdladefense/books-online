I. General Considerations

A motion is a request that the court do something. Trial courts have inherent power to grant any relief required to provide a fair trial. State ex rel Ricco v. Biggs, 198 Or 413, 428-429, 255 P2d 1055 (1953). Motions serve myriad purposes: to obtain a bargaining, trial, or sentencing advantage by winning the motion, to secure a favorable plea-bargain with the threat of winning the motion or making the prosecutor work, to preserve issues for subsequent review, or to favorably dispose the court toward your client or you. Good motion practice is essential to good lawyering.

A. Statutes, Court Rules, and Standard Practices

You should be familiar with the Uniform Trial Court Rules and Supplemental Local Rules. Many courts have local practices that are not really consistent with the UTCRs and SLRs, and it will be easier to follow the local practice than to insist that the judge follow the SLR.

Some motion practice is also driven by statute. See, e.g., ORS 14.210 et seq. (regarding motions for change of judge, setting both substantive and procedural requirements).

If you want to file a motion in violation of the applicable rules then do so, cite to the rule and explain why it doesn’t apply or will deprive your client of a fair trial, and make a record. If the basis for a motion to suppress only became apparent when the prosecutor provided the fourth batch of discovery, file a motion to suppress, explain why it is late, offer evidence if any is required (such as an affidavit about when you got the discovery and why it matters) and ask the court to consider the late motion. The trial court has discretion to permit the late filing.

If you are filing a motion to challenge the validity of the prior convictions then you must send notice of intent to challenge at least seven days before the first trial setting. ORS 813.328. Compare the elements of any out of state prior conviction under State v. Guzman, 366 Or 18 (2019).

B. Style and Format

A written motion is not a mere formal requirement so you can get a hearing and make a record. Rather, a written motion is an attempt to persuade the judge to do something. It should be clear about what you want the judge to do, why the law permits or requires the judge to do what you want, and why justice and fairness are on your side. Keep it as short as you can.

Read UTCR Ch. 2 on standards for pleadings; judges know those rules and will notice if you don’t follow them. If the motion is longer than ten pages or so, consider including tables of contents and authorities. Most word processors will do some or all of that automatically.

C. Content

You can’t file a good motion if you don’t know the applicable law and who has the burdens of production and persuasion. Making a minimal showing because you don’t have the burden of proof may be sufficient in the case of, e.g., a motion to suppress a warrantless search. See, e.g., State v. Taylor, 43 Or App 589, 603 P2d 1218 (1979); UTCR 4.060 (regarding the requirements for suppression motions). But a minimal showing is probably not the most persuasive way to make your argument, and hiding the ball from opposing counsel is not a tactic that often succeeds. It’s better to take your opportunity to frame the argument and make a persuasive written showing.

II. Initial Motions

A. Release

Provisions for release are contained in ORS 135.230-135.290 and apply to all major traffic offenses. ORS 135.295. These are the same release provisions for a person charged with any other crime. ORS 136.290 requires that a defendant who has been held for more than 60 days prior to trial shall be released from custody unless the defendant has consented to a continuance or the judge finds good cause to deny release.

Release is different in every county. Know the local practice.

B. Bail

Some bail amounts are set by statute, and those amounts tend to affect bail, but a judge always has the authority to reduce bail for a specific defendant (except those charged with murder or treason). State v. Sutherland, 329 Or 359, 362, 987 P2d 501, 502 (1999). Like release, bail varies widely from county to county, and in some counties very specific bail amounts are coded information to guide later release decisions.

A judge may accept a driver’s license or AAA or other automobile membership club card in lieu of bail. ORS 810.300 et seq. It is arguable that bail is only a guarantee of future trial attendance, and therefore bail cannot be used to coerce the defendant or be seized following the defendant’s failure to comply with non-flight release conditions. See Priest v. Pearce, 314 Or 411, 413-14, 840 P2d 65, 66 (1992), on the right to bail. But, in practice, a defendant paying bail into court should expect that the bail will be connected to release agreements and will be seized to pay mandatory fines, restitution, even an unrelated child support. See State v. Baker, 165 Or App 565, 568, 998 P2d 700 (2000) (applying rule.) In Baker, the defendant had signed a release agreement permitting the seizure. It is doubtful whether a judge would order release on bail with no release agreement, but arguably the release-agreement requirement is inconsistent with the constitutional right to bail.

C. Jurisdictional Issues

State, municipal and justice courts have concurrent jurisdiction over misdemeanor traffic offenses such as DUII (ORS 51.050(1), 51.070; 221.339(1)), although neither municipal courts (ORS 221.339(3)) nor justice courts (ORS 51.050(3)) have jurisdiction to hear any felony. Some justice and municipal courts are not courts of record, and in those courts the defendant possesses a right to remove his or her case to circuit court and must be notified of the option to remove. The motion to remove must be made within ten days of entering a plea in order to be valid. ORS 51.050(2); Henderson v. Smith, 282 Or 109, 577 P2d 504 (1978).

An appeal from not-of-record justice and municipal courts is an appeal de novo to circuit court. ORS 157.010-157.020; 221.359. In other words, the defendant gets a second try at an acquittal or other favorable result.

If the justice or municipal court is a court of record, then an appeal proceeds as provided in chapter 138, as for an appeal in any criminal matter.

III. Motions Against Charging Instruments: Demurrers and Motions in Arrest

A demurrer is a facial attack on the adequacy of the charging instrument. A motion in arrest of judgment is a post-trial demurrer, with fewer grounds available and a stricter standard. ORS 135.630 (regarding demurrers); ORS 136.500 (regarding motions in arrest of judgment). Demurrers and motions in arrest are based solely on the charging instrument. State v. Davis, 1 Or App 285, 290, 462 P2d 448, 451 (1969). But see State v. Shadley/Spencer/Rowe, 16 Or App 113, 517 P2d 324 (1973) (discovery can clarify vague terms in the charging instrument). If you think that the discovery creates a demurrable issue, attach the discovery to your demurrer and explain how you think the discovery modifies the charging instrument.

A. Formal or Technical Issues

The formal requirements for a complaint appear in ORS 133.015; for a citation, in ORS 133.069; and for an indictment, in ORS 132.510-132.580; 135.711-135.740. Technical errors can lead to dismissal, so it’s worth knowing the requirements and demurring if they are not met. If the DUII charging instrument does not explicitly state controlled substances and it is a DUII for controlled substances or combination, you don’t want to raise this pretrial.

B. Substantive Issues

In addition to the formal requirements, a charging instrument may be subject to demurrer if it does not allege all the elements, or if it alleges multiple unrelated offenses.

It is hard to win an argument that an accusatory instrument is indefinite. See, e.g., State v. Molver, 233 Or App 239, 247, 225 P3d 136 (2010) (listing authorities). Nonetheless, the defendant is entitled to sufficient information to make a defense. Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution provides that “in all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall have the right to demand the nature and cause of the accusation against him.” The Due Process Clause contains a similar guarantee. State v. Smith, 182 Or 497, 500-01, 188 P2d 998 (1948); Russell v. United States, 369 US 749, 82 S Ct 1038, 8 L Ed 2d 240 (1962).

Whether multiple offenses can be joined in a single accusatory instrument is a complex, fact-dependent question. See State v. Campbell, review dismissed as improvidently granted, 354 Or 375, 377, 312 P3d 511 (2013) (Justice Walters, concurring) (discussing issue, citing authorities). When a DUII investigation leads to the discovery of other, logically-unrelated offenses, and all the offenses are combined in a single accusatory instrument, consider a demurrer for misjoinder. See also Joinder and Severance, below.

Venue is now a matter to attack through pretrial motion, rather than demurrer or MJOA. State v. Mills, 354 Or 350, 312 P3d 515 (2013).

C. Effect of Demurrer

The granting of a demurrer results in the dismissal of the case and is a bar to recharging the defendant without leave of the court. If the court grants leave to refile, it must be done within 30 days of the order. Ordinarily, leave to refile is granted if it appears that the defect is easily correctable. ORS 135.670-135.690. But see State v. Eberhardt, 225 Or App. 275, 280-81, 201 P3d 915, 918 (2009) (failure to grant demurrer was harmless).

IV. Motions to Dismiss

A. Former Jeopardy

Former jeopardy bars prosecution for offenses based upon the same criminal episode reasonably known to the prosecutor and properly tried in the same court. ORS 131.505–131.535. Oregon Constitution, Article I, section 12.

Jeopardy will attach and will act as a bar to a second prosecution where multiple traffic crimes arise out of the same “criminal episode,” each can be tried in the same court, the charges are adjudicated in separate proceedings and the defendant is acquitted or convicted of the first criminal charge. State v. Hagey, 38 Or App 487, 590 P2d 753 (1979). The term “criminal episode” may have a different meaning under the Oregon Constitution from that under ORS 131.515(2). Thus, for constitutional purposes, DWS and DUII are committed during a single episode, State v. Farley, 301 Or 668, 375 P2d 359 (1986), since they arise from one single act of driving, while DUII and False Name to a Police Officer are not. State v. Ellison, 301 Or 676, 725 P2d 363 (1986). Normally, a defendant’s objection to consolidating the offenses will waive double jeopardy rights, State v. Shields, 280 Or 471, 571 P2d 892 (1977), and dismissal for purposes of consolidation does not trigger double jeopardy rights. ORS 135.753(3).

The former jeopardy statutes provide greater protection than the Oregon Constitution. See Custer v. Baldwin, 163 Or App 60, 66, 986 P2d 1203 (1999), rev den, 329 Or 589:

Subsection (2) [ORS 131.515] … provides greater protection for criminal defendants because it bars consecutive prosecutions not only for the same offense but also for all known charges arising out of the same criminal episode.

Jeopardy attaches either when a jury is impaneled and sworn or when the first witness in a court trial is sworn. Jeopardy also attaches, for constitutional purposes, upon the entry of a guilty plea to one offense when two or more offenses arise from a single episode. ORS 131.505(5); State v. Farley, supra. Thus, to the extent that ORS 131.525(2) authorizes the withdrawal of a guilty plea to prevent jeopardy from attaching, that statute is unconstitutional. State v. Farley, 301 Or 668.

Fifth Amendment analysis is less favorable, and probably only applies in the case of true greater- and lesser-included offenses. See Rutledge v. United States, 517 US 292, 116 S Ct 1241, 134 LEd2d 419 (1996).

Jeopardy only attaches in criminal prosecutions. Thus, a trial of a companion violation will not bar trial of the DUII. ORS 153.108(1) provides that “if a person commits both a crime and a violation as part of the same criminal episode, the prosecution for one offense shall not bar the subsequent prosecution for the other.” See State v. Roeder, 209 Or App 199, 147 P3d 363 (2006) (applying the four-part Selness/Miller test to violation of refusing a breath test, and holding that offense is not “criminal in nature for purposes of Article I, section 12” of the Oregon Constitution). See also State v. McGilchrist, 294 Or 473, 657 P2d 681 (1983) (subsequent vehicular assault charge barred by prior DUII prosecution). But see Collateral Estoppel discussion below.

Similarly, the administrative suspension of a defendant’s driver’s license for refusal to take a breath test does not bar subsequent criminal prosecution for driving under the influence. State v. Burgess, 145 Or App 334, 930 P2d 869, rev den, 325 Or 80 (1996). Nor does the civil forfeiture of the vehicle in a collateral proceeding. United States v. Ursery, 518 US 267, 116 S Ct 2135, 135 L Ed 2d 549 (1996); State v. Vettrus, 142 Or App 359, 922 P2d 673 (1996).

If a prosecutor causes a mistrial by conduct that the prosecutor knows was improper and prejudicial, and either intends or is indifferent to a mistrial, retrial is barred under Article I, section 12, Oregon Constitution. State v. Kennedy, 295 Or 260, 276, 666 P2d 1316 (1983). See Mistrial Section for more on this.

B. Collateral Estoppel

Collateral estoppel (ORS 43.160) is available for use by a criminal defendant in Oregon. State v. George, 253 Or 458, 455 P2d 609 (1969); Ashe v. Swenson, 397 US 436, 445, 90 S Ct 1189, 1195, 25 L Ed 2d 469 (1970). The prior proceeding may be a probation violation hearing. State v. Donovan, 305 Or 332, 751 P2d 1109 (1988). So, if the defendant is accused of violating probation by committing a new criminal offense, and wins the probation-violation hearing, move to dismiss the criminal prosecution on former-jeopardy grounds.

V. Motions to Suppress

The subject of illegally seized evidence is broad, complex, and constantly changing. It would not be possible to have an exhaustive discussion in the context of this manual, see the OCDLA Search and Seizure in Oregon manual and libraryofdefense.org for that. This chapter will contain, however, a brief discussion of the law regarding statements and a few specific issues regarding DUII defense.

A. Statements and Other Compelled Evidence

1. Advice of Rights

In Miranda v. Arizona, 384 US 436, 86 S Ct 1602, 16 LEd 2d 694 (1966), the U.S. Supreme Court held that prior to “custodial interrogation” the defendant must be advised that he or she has the right to remain silent, that anything he or she says can or will be used against him or her in a court of law, and that he or she has the right to an attorney, and that if he or she cannot afford an attorney, an attorney will be appointed for him or her. Absent a subsequent knowing, intelligent and voluntary waiver of those rights by the defendant, the statements are not admissible. U.S. Constitution, Amendments 5 and 14.

Article I, section 12, of the Oregon Constitution appears to require Miranda-type warnings prior to the interrogation of a defendant who is “in custody” or who is being interrogated in a “setting which judges would or officers should know is compelling.” State v. Magee, 304 Or 261, 256, 744 P2d 250 (1987); cited with approval State v. Smith, 310 Or 1, 791 P2d 836 (1990). (This appears to be a total repudiation of the plurality opinion in State v. Smith, 301 Or 681, 725 P2d 894 (1986), which held to the contrary.)

a. Custodial Status of Defendant

Whether the advice of rights is required depends initially on the defendant’s custodial status. Under the recently emerging state constitutional analysis, Miranda-type warnings are certainly required if the defendant is in “full custody.”

“The concept obviously includes extended official detention in a cell or another enclosure, with or without booking or deprivation of personal belongings. But an enclosure is not essential; one would hardly dispute that a person handcuffed on the street or in his own home is in ‘full custody.’” State v. Magee, supra at 265.

The “compelling” circumstances referred to in Magee are somewhat more elusive and have not been specifically identified by the courts. The lead opinion in State v. Brown, 100 Or App 204, 212, 785 P2d 790 (1990), held that the facts in that case supported the conclusion that the “questioning was not so prolonged, pressured, or police-dominated as to become tantamount to an arrest,” and therefore warnings were not required. Four of the judges, however, would have held that a more elastic standard should be applied: “[T]he truly relevant test is whether there are sufficient, compelling circumstances...to support a conclusion that warnings must have been given in order for the subsequent admissions to be untainted.” Id, 215. The Oregon Supreme Court in State v. Smith, supra, seems to have adopted the latter approach, at least for the time being. See State v. Carlson, 311 Or 201, 808 P2d 1002 (1991).

Under the federal constitution analysis, the standard was articulated in Berkemer v. McCarty, 468 US 420, 441–42 (1984). Warnings are required if “a reasonable person in defendant’s situation would have understood himself to be in custody or under restraints comparable to those associated with a formal arrest.” See Oregon v. Elstad, 470 US 298 (1985); State v. Sadler, 85 Or App 134, 735 P2d 1267, modified on reconsideration, 86 Or App 152, 738 P2d 601 (1987).

Under either analysis, a “routine traffic stop” is neither sufficiently custodial nor compelling as to require Miranda or Miranda-like warnings prior to interrogation; that is, until the subject is placed into custody. But see Justice Linde’s dissent in State v. Roberti, 298 Or 412, 415 (1984).

b. Investigation or Interrogation

Assuming the defendant’s level of restraint is sufficient to warrant them, Miranda-type warnings are required prior to the interrogation of the defendant, but not prior to mere “investigatory” inquiries.

Interrogation means express questioning or its functional equivalent. The analysis appears to be similar under the state and federal constitutions. State v. Bradbury, 80 Or App 613, 723 P2d 1051 (1986); State v. Dorey, 100 Or App 457, 786 P2d 1288 (1990).

“That is to say, the term ‘interrogation’ refers not only to express questioning, but also to any words or actions on the part of the police (other than those normally attendant to arrest and custody) that the police should know are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response from the subject. The latter portion of this definition focuses primarily on the perception of the suspect, rather than the intent of the police. This focus reflects that the Miranda safeguards were designed to vest a suspect in custody with an added measure against coercive police practices without regard to objective proof of the underlying intent of the police. A practice that the police should know is reasonably likely to evoke an incriminating response from a suspect thus amounts to interrogation. But, since the police surely cannot be held accountable for the unforeseeable results of their words or actions, the definition of the word interrogation can extend only to words or actions on the part of the police officers that they should have known were reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response.” Rhode Island v. Innis, 446 US 291, 301–02 (1980); cited with approval, State v. Bradbury, supra, at 616; State v. Dorey, supra, at 462.

On the other hand, statements made in response to mere “investigatory questions” are not the product of “custodial interrogation” and are thus admissible. See, e.g., State v. Cohn, 43 Or App 913, 607 P2d 729 (1979) (asking for name and license); State v. Medenbach, 48 Or App 133, 616 P2d 543 (1980) (statements made in response to questions about defendant’s alcohol consumption in a response to a request to do field sobriety tests); See also State v. Ledbetter, 95 Or App 187, 768 P2d 431 (1989).

In State v. Swan, 363 Or 121 (2018), the court suppressed the breath test result because after the arrested driver asked to speak with a lawyer, the police officer asked interview questions and then asked for consent to a breath test. This violation of counsel led to suppression of the breath test.

2. Waiver

Once Miranda rights have been asserted, there is a “heavy burden” placed on the state to prove that a subsequent waiver is made in a knowing, voluntary and intelligent manner. State v. Singleton, 288 Or 89, 602 P2d 1059 (1979); State v. Mendacino, 288 Or 231, 608 P2d 1247 (1979). Similarly, for an initial waiver of Miranda rights to be valid, it must also be made knowingly, voluntarily and intelligently. State v. McGrew, 38 Or App 493, 590 P2d 755 (1979). Where the police choose not to use available tape recording devices, this weighs against the state when it seeks to prove waiver. See State v. Quinn, 290 Or 383, 389 fn 2, 623 P2d 630 (1981).

Waiver most often becomes an issue when a defendant asserts his or her right to counsel or states that he or she does not wish to answer questions. This should be a complete bar to the introduction of subsequent statements. Law enforcement personnel often institute “interrogation” by allegedly innocuous conversation. Rhode Island v. Innis, supra (1980), prohibits this form of “waiver” if the officer knew he or she was likely to elicit an incriminatory response. Such “interrogations” can be physical or verbal in nature, and the susceptibilities of the subject, such as intoxication, are considered in determining if “interrogation” occurred. See also State v. Crawford, 73 Or App 53, 698 P2d 40 (1985).

The recent case law requires an in-depth look at Miranda violations and also violation of the right to counsel. In State v. Taylor, 296 Or App 278 (2019), the court suppressed the breath test after the arrested driver did not clearly waive her Miranda rights. The court weighs the following factors: (1) the nature of the violation, (2) amount of time between the violation and any later statements, (3) whether the defendant remained in custody before making any later statements, (4) subsequent events that may have dissipated the taint of the earlier violation, and (5) the use that state has made of the statements/evidence.

3. Voluntariness of Statements/Confessions

The common law rule is that inculpatory statements by a defendant are presumed to be involuntary, the burden being on the state to prove voluntariness. See State v. Wintzingerode, 9 Or 153, 161–62 (1881); State v. Foster, 288 Or 649, 607 P2d 173 (1980); State v. Ely, 237 Or 329, 390 P2d 348 (1964); State v. Brewton, 238 Or 590, 395 P2d 374 (1964). Cf., Oregon Constitution, Article I, section 12.

Ordinarily, the advice of Miranda rights, their acknowledgment by the defendant, and waiver thereof will satisfy the state’s burden. However, even if the appropriate warnings are given, the statement may still be shown to be involuntary if it was induced by fear produced by official threats or promises (State v. Foster, 303 Or 518, 525, 739 P2d at 1032 (1987)), or overreaching (State v. Foster, 288 Or 649 (1980)), or otherwise is involuntary.

In cases in which the officer has asserted that the defendant was “dead drunk,” the client’s statements might be suppressed on the grounds that they were involuntary. Johnson v. Zerbst, 304 US 458 (1938); Gladden v. Unsworth, 396 F2d 373 (9th Cir, 1968) (“gross intoxication”); State v. Culley, 25 Or App 387, 549 P2d 1130 (1976); State v. Smith, 4 Or App 130, 134, 476 P2d 802, 25 ALR 4th 419 (1970). (If, in the course of the suppression hearing, the clever officer temporizes, with instances of defendant’s sobriety, in the spirit of “well, he wasn’t that drunk,” such statements should be used at trial to rebut the contention that the client was “drunk” at all.)

For a complete discussion of the history of the voluntariness of confessions in Oregon, see State v. Smith, 301 Or 681, supra (no longer good law but still accurate as history). State v. Lloyd, 22 Or App, 254 (1975), lays out specific facts resulting in a defendant’s inability to waive his Miranda rights due to intoxication. State v. Lowry, 245 Or 565 (1967) holds that a defendant with a .30 BAC is incapable of validly waiving his Miranda rights.

In State v. Adame, 261 Or App 11 (2014), the court held verbal field sobriety tests are admissible so long as the defendant was not under the impression, based upon the officer’s words, that a refusal could be used in court. This is in contrast to physical FSTs, which are not testimonial and can be compelled by an admonishment regarding the admissibility of a refusal.

4. Right to Counsel

In Edwards v. Arizona, 451 US 477, 101 S Ct 1880 (1981), the Supreme Court decided that once an accused asserts his or her right to counsel during custodial interrogation, all questioning must cease until counsel is provided, unless the accused reinitiates the contact with the police. Edwards was followed by Oregon v. Bradshaw, 462 US 1039, 103 S Ct 2830 (1983). Clearly, an accused has greater rights when she asserts a right to counsel then when she tells the police she doesn’t want to talk with them, because in asserting a right to counsel the accused has expressed a desire “to deal with the police only through counsel.” See also State v. Barmon, 67 Or App 369, 679 P2d 888, rev den, 297 Or 227 (1984); State v. Crawford, 73 Or App 53, 698 P2d 40 (1985).

The state constitutional right to counsel has been extended to the breath test situation. In State v. Spencer, 305 Or 59, 750 P2d 147 (1988), the court held that a suspect that requests so must be given a reasonable opportunity to consult with an attorney prior to taking a breath test. If denied that opportunity, the breath test result is suppressed. While not specifically deciding what a “reasonable opportunity” might be, the court noted that 15 minutes on the telephone “will doubtless suffice in most cases.” Id, at 75, n. 5.

In State v. Martinez, 263 Or App 658 (2014), the court held that A DUII suspect who wishes to invoke his 5th Amendment right to counsel must be clear that he is not merely accepting his more limited right to call an attorney prior to the administration of a breath test under Art I, Sec 11 of the Oregon Constitution. The defendant’s statements are viewed in lieu of the totality of the circumstances.

The defendant must be accorded privacy in which to make the phone call, which was impossible when the police were taping the attorney-client phone call even if the officers promised not to listen to it. State v. Riddle, 149 Or App 141, 941 P2d 1079 (1997). The defendant need not specifically request that the officer allow privacy during the consultation with the attorney. State v. Durbin, 335 Or 183, 63 P3d 576 (2003).

In State v. Lile, 267 Or App 712 (2014), the court held that an officer must be out of earshot when the defendant makes a call to his attorney. The right to a reasonable opportunity to consult with counsel before deciding whether to submit to a breath test includes the right to a private consultation. In this case, the defendant was able only to contact his attorney’s receptionist. The court held, “that difference does not lead to a different result. An attorney may not always be available to speak directly with a client. As a result, a receptionist can, and often does, serve as a conduit of confidential communications between a client and the attorney.”

State v. Carlson, 225 Or App 9 (2008). Defendant refused FSTs and asked to talk to a lawyer before taking a breath test. Officer wouldn’t let defendant use his own cell phone. Officer tried to dial inmate phone for defendant and was not sure if he knew how to properly use the phone. He never got a completed call. The state must prove that the officer used the phone properly and that the phone worked in order to use the breath test or the refusal against the defendant. State v. Hunt, 225 Or App 51 (2008). Defendant asked to consult an attorney. Officer pointed to a phone in another room. Defendant said he wanted a lawyer, not a phone. Held: refusal comes in. There was enough evidence to show that the defendant would have had the opportunity to privately talk to the lawyer on the phone. State v. Stone, 229 Or App 708 (2009), affirms Matviyenko. No chance to call a lawyer after asking for one means bac suppressed. State v. Freytag, 230 Or App 694 (2009). Defendant wanted to call a nonlawyer and was refused the opportunity. Held: Defendant did not prove a “minimal factual nexus” between the denial of his “alleged right” to call a non-lawyer and his decision to take the breath test.

The defendant is not entitled to have counsel present at the administration of the breath test. City of Roseberg v. Dykstra, 121 Or App 317, 854 P2d 985 (1993).

Note: Since field sobriety tests are now part of the implied consent law, it might be that the same rule applies prior to the administration of field sobriety tests.

If a defendant initially asks for an attorney but does not follow through with his request, he has not necessarily waived his right to counsel. The Court of Appeals has held a defendant is not obliged to make a futile request for a private consultation with an attorney when, under the circumstances, it was clear that he would not be granted a private consultation with an attorney. The test for determining whether or not a request for an attorney is futile is whether the defendant subjectively believes that he will not be permitted a private consultation with an attorney before deciding to submit to an Intoxilyzer test, and that belief must be objectively reasonable under the totality of the circumstances. State v. Greenwood, 175 Or App 69, 27 P3d 151 (2001).

A citizen not trained in the law does not have to recite specific magic words to invoke a right such as the right to communicate. State v. Trenary, 114 Or App 608, 614 (1992). Petitioner stated the desire for advice before submitting to a breath test under Oregon law which is all that is required to trigger the right to communicate. “[A]n adequate invocation of the right to counsel is any plain reference, however glancing, to a need or desire for representation.” State v. Trenary, supra.

See Swan, 363 Or 121 (2018), for violation of right to counsel by continued questioning after the invocation of counsel.

In State v. Matviyenko, 212 Or App 125, 157 P3d 268 (2007), the court reviewed the relevant case law regarding the right to a reasonable opportunity to consult privately with counsel and arguably expanded its application to the benefit of defendants.

In Matviyenko, the defendant requested an attorney immediately upon arrest and was told that he could call an attorney at the station. Once at the station, the officer took him to the Intoxilyzer room, which had a telephone and phone books, and sat down across the table from the defendant. The defendant asked to call his wife and was told that he could “make any phone calls at that time.” The defendant called his wife and then provided a breath sample. The officer never left the room and testified at the suppression hearing that the defendant would never have been allowed privacy in the Intoxilyzer room due to the sensitive nature of the equipment.

In rejecting the state’s argument that the “practical reality” of the situation required the officer to remain in the room, the court noted that the officer created the situation by taking the defendant—who had requested an attorney—to a room the officer knew would afford no privacy. Id. at 129–130. The court went on to reject the state’s arguments that the defendant waived his right to counsel by calling his wife and not calling an attorney, holding that taking the breath test did not “unambiguously demonstrate that he had changed his mind about wanting to call an attorney.” Id. at 130.

The case law following Matviyenko makes it clear that when an attorney is requested, inherent in that request is the ability to communicate privately with an attorney. If the request for counsel is equivocal, the law requires the police officer to offer a phone call or clarify the reason for the request. State v. Ohm, 224 Or App 390, 395-6 (2008). In Ohm, the court addressed an equivocal request for advice and determined that asking for advice is construed as asking for legal advice in the context of a breath test. The court held,

[i]f a suspect makes an equivocal invocation of the right to counsel under Article I, section 12, police are required to ask follow-up questions to determine what the suspect meant before proceeding with interrogation. State v. Charboneau, 323 Or. 38, 54, 913 P.2d 308 (1996). Similarly, we conclude that if a driver arrested for DUII makes an equivocal invocation of the right to counsel under Article I, section 11, police must ask follow-up questions to determine whether they must inform the driver that, once he or she contacts an attorney, privacy will be afforded. In determining whether a defendant’s statement is an equivocal invocation, we view the statement in light of the totality of the circumstances at and preceding the time that it was made, “to ascertain whether a reasonable officer in the circumstances would have understood that defendant was invoking [her] rights.” State v. Holcomb, 213 Or. App. 168, 176, 159 P.3d 1271, rev. den., 343 Or. 224, 168 P.3d 1155 (2007).

We conclude that, under the circumstances here, a reasonable police officer would regard defendant’s statement that she wanted advice from “someone” as an equivocal invocation of her right to counsel [Emphasis Added]. See Ohm, supra.

It is the officer’s responsibility to inform the suspect of the ability to communicate privately, as a valid waiver cannot occur if suspect is not informed he will not have the ability to privately communicate. State v. Sawyer, 221 Or App 350 (2008). The suspect must also actually be afforded the ability to communicate privately. State v. Carslon, 225 Or App 9 (2008) (officer tried to dial the phone for suspect but was not sure how to use the phone and never got a completed call). As long as the officer effectively communicates to the suspect that privacy will be afforded, however, the Court of Appeals has generally found a subsequent waiver of the right to call an attorney valid. State v. Hunt, 225 Or App 51 (2008) (refusal comes in after a suspect asked to consult an attorney and the officer pointed to a phone in another room).

5. Refusals

A refusal to take a breath test was deemed an exercise of an Article I, section 9 right under State v. Banks and therefore inadmissible as evidence in the criminal case. 364 Or 332, 337 (2019). This likely also applies to field sobriety tests. See State v. Stills, 299 Or App 194 (2019) where the state concedes the FST’s refusal should not have been admitted. This makes sense because a person cannot invoke their right to be free from search (Article I, section 9) without waiving the Article I, section 12 right of showing “consciousness of guilt.” Field sobriety tests are searches under Oregon law (similar to a breath or blood test).

The Oregon Supreme Court, in State v. Fish, 321 Or 48, 893 P2d 1023 (1995), held, inter alia, that the request made of a stopped motorist to take a field sobriety test requires a testimonial response, and the circumstances of such request—being made in an inherently coercive setting, upon threat of using any test results or the refusal to take such test against the motorist in court—are sufficiently “compelling” that the transaction must be analyzed under the self-incrimination clauses of Article I, section 12, and Amendments V and XIV, as discussed above. The court held that the evidence of defendant’s refusal was “compelled ‘testimonial’ evidence” regarding his state of mind, and was therefore inadmissible. That holding has been narrowed by subsequent decisions.

The Oregon Court of Appeals has held that “testimony” is “communication by words or conduct of an individual’s thoughts, beliefs or ‘state of mind.’ Thus, purely verbal answers to purely verbal questions (‘on an intoxication scale of 1 to 10, I think I rank a 2’) are testimony, as are answers by conduct to the same question (holding up two fingers to self-rank intoxication on a 1 to 10 scale).”

State v. Nielsen, 147 Or App 294, 306, 936 P2d 374 (1997). The court went on to hold that a test producing physical evidence of intoxication, without requiring an individual to reveal his or her thoughts, beliefs or state of mind, is generally not testimonial.

However, in State v. Rohrs, 157 Or App 494, 499, 970 P2d 262 (1998), non-verbal FST’s were admissible after an admonishment, but this old case should be re-read in accordance with Banks.

6. Burden of Proof

Where statements are challenged as involuntary, the state has the burden of proving the statements were voluntary, were not made in a custodial setting absent Miranda advice, and, if made in a custodial setting, were made in compliance with Miranda. State v. Wheeler, 43 Or App 875, 880, 604 P2d 449 (1979). It is not just confessions the state must prove were voluntary; admissions must be proved voluntary as well. State v. Rollo, 221 Or 428, 432, 531 P2d 422 (1960).

The Oregon Supreme Court distinguishes between admissions and confessions because a defendant cannot be convicted solely on a confession, ORS 136.425, but could on an admission. For a definition of confession and historical discussion of this issue, read State v. Manzella, 306 Or 303, 759 P2d 1078 (1988).

B. Audio and Video Recordings

The admission of recordings made of field sobriety tests are nontestimonial in character and have been held to be admissible. State v. Medenbach, 48 Or App 133, 616 P2d 543 (1980). The driver must be told he or she is being recorded, however. If a police officer records a conversation or test without the defendant’s knowledge, that recording is inadmissible under ORS 165.540(1)(c) and ORS 41.910(1). State v. Cooney, 36 Or App 217, 584 P2d 329 (1978). ORS 136.432, which limits the authority of the court to exclude relevant evidence for statutory violations, does not apply to ORS 41.910(1) or Cooney, as ORS 136.432 does not repeal statutory rules that expressly provide for evidentiary exclusion. State v. Thompson-Seed, 162 Or App 483, 986 P2d 732 (1999).

A defendant sufficiently intoxicated might successfully argue that his intoxication precluded knowing he was recorded. While the statute only requires one to be “specifically informed,” one could argue that the purpose is to permit a choice not to speak, thus requiring knowledge.

Additionally, an officer may advise the defendant that her statements are not being recorded and not turn off the recorder. The defendant may be placed in the rear of the police vehicle and, even in the absence of the officer, make incriminating statements while unaware the recorder is operating. Such statements may be suppressible.

It has been suggested that suppression or exclusion of a recording may be ordered where the officer records other persons in conjunction with recording the defendant or records radio communications simultaneously. This would appear to violate ORS 165.540(c).

Keep in mind that even though the entire recording may not be excludable or suppressible parts of it may be, such as statements of third persons, conclusions or opinions. If there is a destroyed or lost tape/recording/video, then the defense may be able to suppress parts that would have been captured on the recording if the defense can show it would have been favorable. State v. Zinzli, 156 Or App 245 (1998).

C. Other Compelled Evidence

Even though authority exists to allow recordings, the results of field sobriety tests, blood tests, breath tests, lineups and show-ups into evidence—on the basis that they are not “testimonial”—such evidence may in fact not be admissible because it was obtained in violation of the defendant’s right not to be compelled to furnish evidence against himself under Article I, section 12, of the Oregon Constitution.

In State v. Soriano, 68 Or App 642, fn. 4, 684 P2d 1220, op. adopted, 298 Or 392, 693 P2d 26 (1984), Judge (now Justice) Gillette stated the following:

In most constitutions, the statement of the right is that a person may not “be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself,” United States Constitution, Amendment V, or “be compelled in any criminal prosecution to testify against himself.” Oregon Constitution, Article I, section 12. Some constitutions provide that a person shall not “be compelled to accuse or furnish evidence against himself,” New Hampshire Constitution, Bill of Rights, Art 15th, and some courts hold that the additional language provides a broader right than would otherwise exist. Emery’s Case, 107 Mass 172 (1871); State v. Nowell, 58 NH 314 (1878). The common law privilege included both the right not to be required to testify against oneself in a criminal trial (indeed, at common law, the defendant was generally not a competent witness) and the right not to give self-incriminating testimony in other judicial proceedings. Early decisions held that the constitutional right included both parts of the common law privilege, however the privilege might be phrased. See State v. Quarles, 13 Ark 307, 312 (1853); Wilkins v. Malone, 14 Ind 153 (1860); Cullen v. Commonwealth, 65 Va (24 Gratt) 624 (1873). Some courts holding otherwise did so in order to give a witness broader rights than courts in other states provided. Emery’s Case, supra; State v. Nowell, supra. Other early cases that took a more restrictive view were later limited. Compare People ex rel Hackley v. Kelly, 24 NY 74 (1861) (dictum limiting the right to criminal prosecutions) with People v. Sharp, 107 NY 427, 14 NE 319 (1887) (assuming that the right applied to a witness before a legislative committee). In 1892, the United States Supreme Court declared that all the constitutional provisions were entitled to the same broad construction, and state courts generally followed its lead thereafter. Counselman v. Hitchcock, 142 US 547, 584–85, 12 S Ct 195, 35 LEd 1110 (1892); See People ex rel Lewisohn v. O”Brien, 176 NY 253, 68 NE 353 (1903) (adopting the reasoning in Counselman and overruling People ex rel Hackley v. Kelly, supra); but see Attorney General v. Colleton 387 Mass 790, 444 NE2d 915 (1982) (continuing to give the Massachusetts constitutional language a broader meaning than the Fifth Amendment in order to avoid such recent restrictive United States Supreme Court decisions as Kastigar v. United States, 406 US 441, 92 S Ct 1653, 32 LEd2d 212 (1972)). We see no reason to construe the Oregon Constitution to give protection from testifying but not from furnishing evidence.” (Emphasis added.)

[Case links available in Soriano.]

In State v. Sanchez, 273 Or App 778 (2015), the court held that consent to search may not cure a violation of a defendant’s Article I, section 9, rights. Under the Article I, section 9, exclusionary rule, “[w]henever the state has obtained evidence following the violation of a defendant’s Article I, section 9, rights it is presumed that the evidence was tainted by the violation and must be suppressed.” State v. Miller, 267 Or App 382, 398, 340 P3d 740 (2014). To avoid suppression, the state bears the burden of proving that the evidence at issue “did not derive from the preceding illegality.” Id. (quoting Hall, 339 Or at 25)… Whether a defendant’s consent to a search or consent to speak with officer breaks the connection between a prior illegality and subsequently discovered evidence depends on “the totality of the circumstances, including the temporal proximity between that misconduct and the consent, and the existence of any intervening or mitigating circumstances. We also consider the nature, purpose, and flagrancy of the misconduct.” State v. Unger, 356 Or 59, 88, 333 P3d 1009 (2014). In Sanchez, the court held the officer exploited the Article I, section 9, violation; thus, even with the defendant’s consent, all evidence seized after the unlawful entry of the curtilage should have been suppressed.

In State v. Ritz, 270 Or App 88 (2015), the court held that in order for the police to enter a home absent a warrant, they must establish the evidence would have been completely lost, not just diminished. The court established a standard that, “after first showing how long it would take to obtain a search warrant, the state may prove exigency by further showing that police had an objectively reasonable belief that the circumstances were such that, had they waited for that warrant, the suspect’s blood would have lost all evidentiary value. In this case, the state established, the defendant took almost four hours trying to escape from the police. The officer testified further that it would take up to 90 minutes to obtain a warrant. Based upon normal dissipation rates, had the defendant been a .08 at the time of driving, the evidence may have been completely lost by the time a warrant was obtained and his blood was drawn. However, in State v. Rice, 270 Or App 50 (2015), the court held the state did not make a showing at trial that critical evidence would have been lost, other than it being a case involving driving and likely intoxication.

D. Warrantless Searches of Breath/Blood/Urine

Warrantless blood draws are likely inadmissible under the Fourth Amendment. You might want to cite Birchfield v. North Dakota, 136 S.Ct. 2160 (2016), rather than using Oregon’s laws or constitution.

ORS 813.131 provides in part:

(1) Any person who operates a motor vehicle upon premises open to the public or the highways of this state shall be deemed to have given consent, subject to the Motorist Implied Consent Law, to a chemical test of the person’s urine for the purpose of determining the presence of a controlled substance or an inhalant in the person’s body if the person is arrested for driving while under the influence of intoxicants in violation of ORS 813.010 or of a municipal ordinance and either:

(a) The person takes the breath test described in ORS 813.100 and the test discloses a blood alcohol content of less than .08 percent; or

(b) The person is involved in an accident resulting in injury or property damage. A urine test may be requested under this paragraph regardless of whether a breath test has been requested and regardless of the results of a breath test, if one is taken.

Although the police may request urine tests in the situations outlined above, the admissibility of this type of evidence is still in question. It is possible that a person giving a urine sample that is positive for controlled substances is not being affected by the controlled substance in the urine at the time the sample is given. See State v. Jayne, 173 Or App 533, 540, 24 P3d 920 (2001). The Court of Appeals in Jayne found that urinalysis evidence was not relevant in that case for the charges of first and second degree manslaughter or failing to perform the duties of a driver to an injured person. In so finding the court stated:

Clearly, whether defendant was impaired due to alcohol or drug consumption at the time of the accident was material to the state’s case. However, the trial court correctly accorded the urinalysis evidence little probative value as to that question, because of the conceded lack of direct correlation between the urinalysis test results and impairment at the time of the accident. At most, the evidence could properly be used to demonstrate that defendant had consumed drugs at some point before the accident. It also could be viewed as providing some slight corroboration of the officer’s observation of symptoms, e.g., red eyes and greenish tongue, that the officer associated with drug use. However, given the significant weight that a jury is likely to accord this type of evidence, the potential for prejudice here is high. Specifically, the jury might infer, from the very fact that it is being told that defendant tested “positive” for marijuana and methamphetamine after the accident, that there necessarily must be some correlation between the drugs in her urine and her physical or mental condition at the time of the accident. Alternatively, the jury might assume that because defendant had consumed drugs in the past, she was a reckless person and was likely to have been acting in a reckless manner at the time of the accident. Evidence that may cause “unfair prejudice,” for purposes of OEC 403, is evidence that has “an undue tendency to suggest a decision on an improper basis,” and “describes a situation in which the preferences of the trier of fact are affected by reasons essentially unrelated to the persuasive power of the evidence to establish the fact of consequence.” O’Key, 321 Or at 321. In this case, the trial court correctly concluded that the probative value of this evidence was low and the danger of unfair prejudice was high. The trial court did not err in excluding the urinalysis evidence.

The same argument applies in cases where the state seeks to use a urine test to prove that the defendant was under the influence at the time of driving. Since the state will not be able to produce a witness to testify that the results of the urinalysis have any relationship to the impairment of the defendant at the time the sample is given, the results are not very relevant and are terribly prejudicial. The Court of Appeals has not spoken directly to this issue yet, but it has met with some success at the trial court level. A sample motion and memorandum is attached as an appendix to the chapter.

Furthermore, the lack of foundation for a breath test may lead to the exclusion of urine test results. As noted above, Oregon statute provides that the police may request that a defendant give a urine sample after the defendant has given a breath sample of .08 or less. ORS 813.131(1)(a). What if the police have no basis for requesting a breath test? An implied consent law cannot obviate the unconstitutional seizure of a breath sample. State v. Newton, 291 Or 788, 636 P2d 393 (1981). Ordinarily, the results of a breath test will be allowed in spite of the fact it is compelled by the threat of a license suspension because it is supported by probable cause and exigent circumstances.

There will be times when the police arrest a defendant for driving under the influence of a controlled substance without having any evidence that the defendant has consumed alcohol. If the police don’t have any reason to believe that the defendant has consumed intoxicating liquor, they have no probable cause to seize a breath sample from the defendant. If the breath test is not constitutionally obtained, derivative evidence from the breath test, such as a urine sample, must also be suppressed.

In State v. Tripathi, 226 Or App 552 (2009), the court excluded the results of a urine test as not scientific. The state argued that the results came from an accredited laboratory and therefore were by definition reliable and scientific. The court held that without a confirmatory (not necessarily quantitative) test, the results were not scientific. The legislature amended ORS 813.131 to add (4)(a): At the trial in any civil or criminal action, suit or proceeding arising out of the acts committed by a person driving a motor vehicle while under the influence of intoxicants, a valid chemical analysis of a person’s urine is admissible as evidence and may be used with other evidence, if any, to determine whether the person was driving under the influence of intoxicants. (b) A chemical analysis of a person’s urine is valid under this subsection if analysis is performed in an accredited or licensed toxicology laboratory.

See Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts, 557 US 305 (2009), holding that forensic laboratory reports are subject to the Confrontation Clause.

To admit the results of a warrantless search of a defendant’s breath, blood, or urine into evidence, prosecutors have relied on two exceptions to the warrant requirement: consent and exigency. The Oregon Court of Appeals addressed both of those exceptions in State v. Machuca, 231 Or App 232 (2009) (Machuca I). In Machuca I, the court reversed a DUII conviction after deciding that the reading of the implied consent warnings based on ORS 813.100 rendered the defendant’s subsequent consent involuntary and that the state had not shown sufficient exigency to justify the warrantless search of the defendant’s blood. After taking review, however, the Oregon Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals in State v. Machuca, 347 Or 644 (2010) (Machuca II), holding that under Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution, the “evanescent nature” of blood alcohol evidence created an exigency in all but “the rare case” in which a warrant could have been obtained “significantly faster” than the evidence was actually obtained. The Machuca II court, however, only based their holding on the exigency analysis and seemingly left the Machuca I analysis on consent alone.

A post Machua II Court of Appeals confirmed that the implied consent warnings rendered any subsequent consent involuntary in State v. Moore, 247 Or App 39 (2011), confirming that the threats inherent in the implied consent warnings rendered any subsequent consent granted and citing State v. Newton, 291 Or 788, 636 P2d 393 (1981). Later, however, it extended a similar broad exigency analysis from alcohol to controlled substances in State v. McMullen, 250 Or App 208 (2012). In McMullen the court applied the Machuca II exigency analysis to controlled substances and held that since the state was able to establish the “evanescent nature” of some controlled substances in urine, an exigency existed in all but the “rare case” alluded to in Machuca II. This was expanded soon after by State v. Fuller, 252 Or App 245 (2012), in which the Court of Appeals held that all the state needed to do to establish exigency for a search for controlled substances was to adduce some evidence that some controlled substances are of “evanescent nature,” but did not need to establish specific probable cause regarding which controlled substance may be found in the defendant’s urine.

Then in 2013 the United States Supreme Court issued its opinion in Missouri v. McNeely, 133 S Ct 1552 (2013), and held that a per se exigency was not created by the mere fact that blood alcohol content diminishes over time. The facts of McNeely involved a forcible warrantless blood draw; the holding was narrow and in response to the prosecution argument for a blanket per se exigency rule when evidence of blood alcohol content is sought by police. The Court essentially left alone the previous test for exigency, which was a totality of the circumstances test examining the specific facts of each individual case. It left alone the holding in Schmerber v. California, 384 U.S. 757 (1966), in which the Court upheld a warrantless search for blood alcohol content using an exigency analysis. The holding in both McNeely and Schmerber seemingly meant that, under a Fourth Amendment analysis, the prosecution had to do more than merely point out that blood alcohol evidence was being searched for to establish an exigency. It was unclear, however, how this holding affected DUII practice in Oregon, if at all.

1. Consent

The Oregon Supreme Court issued its opinion in State v. Moore, 354 Or 493 (2013), in December of 2013, reversing the Court of Appeals Machuca I analysis regarding the coercive nature of reading the implied consent warnings and potentially clearing the air regarding how McNeely affects the exigency analysis in Oregon. In doing so, the court relied on State v. Hirsch, 267 Or 613 (1974), in which the court held that if an officer threatens a defendant “only to do what the law permits them to do,” the coercion the threat may produce does not render the consent constitutionally invalid. Since all the officer in Moore did was to accurately read the defendant the legal consequences of refusing to provide blood and urine samples, the court reasoned, Hirsch controlled and the reading of the implied consent warnings did not unconstitutionally coerce the subsequent consent given for the search. The issue of exigency and McNeely was also addressed in a footnote, in which the court indicated it did not consider the holding in McNeely inconsistent from its own holding in Machuca II. So for now, it seems that trial courts are going to consider the issues regarding voluntary consent and the implied consent warnings closed and exigency regarding chemical searches for intoxicants closed.

However, the Oregon Supreme Court did leave itself an escape valve through which it could potentially revisit this issue. As pointed out the by court, the defendant’s argument and briefing in Moore did not rely on the nature of the coercion of the threat of punitive civil penalties for refusing a breath test, but rather that a portion of the warnings given was unconstitutional—namely, the threat that the refusal could be offered against him in court proceedings. Since part of the threat was not legal, the defendant in Moore argued, such a warning rendered the consent unconstitutionally coerced. The court firmly closed the door on that argument but, in a footnote, acknowledged that the idea in Hirsch regarding threats of legally permissible consequences could be problematic. It concluded, however, that since neither party fully briefed the issue, it would be inappropriate to reconsider it. Justice Kistler wrote a concurring opinion in which he pointed out that the precedential value of Hirsch is, in his mind, open for debate. The facts of Hirsch involved a threat by officers to obtain a search warrant if the defendant did not consent to a search, and the reasoning Hirsch uses came from a dissenting opinion in State v. Douglas, 260 Or 60 (1971).

But Moore did not address the issue of the nature of the coercion of the threat of punitive civil penalties for refusing a breath test. The court has since addressed this argument.

In State v. Wieboldt, 260 Or App 583 (2014), the court directly dealt with the argument that advice about civil penalties was coercive. In Wieboldt, the court held, citing State v. Moore, 354 Or 493 (2013), the defendant voluntarily consented to the seizure and search of his urine even though he consented after he had received the statutory implied consent warnings required by ORS 813.100(1), ORS 813.130, ORS 813.131, and ORS 813.132 about the economic harm and loss of privileges that would result if he refused.

In State v. Geren, 263 Or App 716 (2014), the court held that consent to a breath or urine test is not involuntary merely because it follows an officer’s reading of information about defendant’s rights and consequences of refusal. Accurate advice about legal consequences does not render consent unlawful. See State v. Moore II, 354 Or 835 (2014).

In State v. Lopez-Lopez, 271 Or App 817 (2015), the court held that agreeing to a warrantless breath test after being read the implied consent warnings indicates the voluntariness of consent unless there are some signs of police exploitation or threats. The court held, “[a]lthough defendant was under a DUII investigation and her consent to the breath test was obtained at the police department, there is no evidence that physical force was used or threatened, that weapons were displayed, or that the atmosphere surrounding defendant’s consent was antagonistic or oppressive.”

2. Exigency

Warrantless entry into the home for the loss of evidence might be a violation of Article I, section 9. See State v. Ritz, 291 Or App 660 (2018).

Warrantless blood draws should be analyzed under Fourth Amendment. See Birchfield discussion above.

As pointed out earlier, beyond consent, the next issue regarding a warrantless search of breath, blood, or urine is exigency. Exigency is a factual question that depends on procedures in obtaining a warrant and whether the delay caused by obtaining the warrant would lead to a loss of evidence. In State v. Raymond, 274 Or App 409 (2015), the court held that under the Oregon Constitution, the exigency exception to the warrant requirement will justify the warrantless collection of defendant’s urine in DUII investigation if (1) there is probable cause to believe that a controlled substance other than alcohol would be present in defendant’s urine, (2) at least one relevant controlled substance dissipates rapidly in urine after it is consumed, and (3) a warrant cannot be obtained significantly faster than the actual process otherwise used. Here, after completing 11 of the 12 steps of the DRE protocol the officer concluded that defendant was under the influence of a central nervous system stimulant. At trial, the state offered evidence that cocaine, a central nervous system stimulant, has a short detection time and may be eliminated from the urine within several hours of consumption. Finally, the officer collected the urine sample within two and a half hours of the arrest and was conducting the DRE evaluation at the police station during most of that time. It was estimated that it would have taken about two hours to obtain a warrant. Thus, even if the officer could have initiated the warrant process immediately upon arrival at the station, the potential half-hour difference was not significantly faster than the two and half hour process that actually occurred. Accordingly, the state established a constitutionally sufficient exigency for purposes of Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution. 

Also, in State v. Perryman, 275 Or App 631 (2015), the court held that where the time it would take to obtain a search warrant to draw blood would lead to a significant loss of evidence, the exigent circumstances exception applies. Here, defendant was lawfully seized for probable cause of driving under the influence. Defendant was taken to a hospital where he was subjected to a blood test. The officer testified that the normal process to obtain a warrant at two a.m. would have taken approximately two and a half hours, and during the whole process the officer was without much “down time” where the officer could have obtained the warrant. Accordingly, the warrantless blood draw was justified. 

VI. Motions in Limine

A motion in limine is an early evidentiary motion. Although prosecutors will gripe if you file them two days before trial, you are within your rights to file them after trial has started, when the prosecutor offers expert evidence and you raise an objection based on the evidence code. Doing so will annoy the judge, so you should decide if you want to file early and tip your hand, or file late and make it harder for the prosecutor to react.

Be careful about filing a motion in limine before jeopardy has attached, because until jeopardy attaches, the state can appeal an adverse ruling. ORS 138.060. See also State v. Jones, 199 Or App 424, 426, 112 P3d 356, 357 (2005) (listing authorities, applying rule). In the right case, the right time to file the motion would be after the jury is sworn and before opening argument. For example, if in a controlled-substance DUII the state has erroneously alleged the use of alcohol and not a controlled substance, you should move in limine to exclude evidence of drug use after jeopardy has attached. See State v. Stiles, 165 Or App 584, 998 P2d 703 (2000) (controlled-substance theory must be alleged specifically in charging instrument).

VII. Speedy Trial

Effective April 1, 2014, ORS 135.747, the statutory speedy trial provision, has been repealed. Constitutional speedy trial rights remain. Constitutionally, speedy-trial analysis involves a flexible balancing approach if the delay is long enough to trigger an inquiry, but, generally, a court should look at the length of the delay, reason for the delay, and prejudice to the defense if the length of the delay is longer than average. State v. Johnson, 342 Or 596, 606-07, 157 P3d 198, 207 (2007).

Because the factors are interrelated, there is no clear point at which the delay is too long. Compare, e.g., State v. Ivory, 278 Or 499, 564 P2d 1039 (1977) (10-month delay led to dismissal) with Haynes v. Burks, 290 Or 75, 619 P2d 632 (1980) (30-month delay did not lead to dismissal).

Although a long delay may lead to dismissal without a showing of prejudice, State v. Hale, 80 Or App 279, 729 P2d 887 (1986) (three-year delay led to dismissal), usually the defendant must show “a reasonable possibility of prejudice.” State v. Harberts, 331 Or 72, 94, 11 P3d 641 (2000).

The Sixth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution provides similar protection. See Barker v. Wingo, 407 US 514, 92 S Ct 2182, 33 L Ed 2d 101 (1972). Federal analysis adds a fourth factor: the timeliness of the assertion of the right to a speedy trial filed by the defendant. State law rejects that factor, because the Article I, section 10, guarantee is “not a ‘right’ of the accused but a mandatory directive not within the disposal of the parties.” Haynes v. Burks, 290 Or at 80–81. VIII. Miscellaneous Motions

A. Dismissal When State Not Ready to Proceed

ORS 136.120 provides for the dismissal of cases when the defendant is ready for trial but the district attorney is not and does not justify a further postponement. However, if it is not dismissed with prejudice, a DUII charge may be reinstituted subject to the statute of limitations. ORS 136.130, 135.753.

B. Motions to Quash

A summons—a necessary part of the citation—must be personally served on the defendant or it is subject to a motion to quash. ORS 133.055–133.065. Failure to serve the summons on the defendant leads to dismissal. State v. Picard, 37 Or App 483, 587 P2d 514 (1978) (The defendant was not personally served with the new complaint following the allowance of his demurrer on the first complaint. Savvy defense counsel argued the motion in the absence of the client, preserving the service question.)

C. Severance of Charges

Under ORS 132.560, the law favors joinder of charges even from different incidents so long as they are related or similar. Therefore you may be faced with a trial setting charging two or more DUIIs from different dates (ORS 132.560(1)(b)(A)). To obtain severance, you will need to show prejudice by the joinder; for example, the defendant will testify in one case but not the other, or in the case of multiple charges from the same incident, that evidence is admissible as to one charge but not the other, i.e., breath or blood alcohol tests taken in violation of implied consent law admissible on assault charge but not DUII. See State v. Heintz, 286 Or 239, 594 P2d 385(1979) (implied-consent statutes do not apply in manslaughter prosecution). Heintz may no longer be good law in light of ORS 136.432. See also Chartrand v. Coos Bay Tavern, 298 Or 689 (1985) (potential for confusing instruction: .08 percent BAC has nothing to do with level of intoxication). See discussion relating to Demurrers, above.

D. Aliases

ORS 135.065(2) permits, upon motion, the striking of aliases. A defendant may be charged in another name than he gave to the officer at the arrest.

E. Motions to Disqualify Judge

Both parties can remove up to two judges as a matter of right, a process colloquially called “affidaviting” a judge. See ORS 14.250–14.270. The motion must be made at the time of assignment of the case to the judge in judicial districts of 100,000 people or more, or in smaller jurisdictions “within five days after such cause … is at issue upon a question of fact or within 10 days after the assignment … of another judge to preside over such cause, matter or proceeding.”

The motion must also be made before the judge in question has made any rulings in the case (except for rulings on motions to extend time). Only two such motions are allowed. Check local rules for timing and procedures for your jurisdiction. Some counties ask that counsel identify judges to remove before judges are assigned; that process is not required by the statute, and it makes it more likely that an objectionable judge will be assigned. The defense bar should resist this procedure, and counsel should weigh whether to object in a particular case.

No particular basis needs to be alleged in the affidavit of prejudice except that the lawyer or the defendant has a good faith belief that a fair and impartial trial cannot be had before that judge. The judge may challenge the good faith of the affiant.

Of course, a judge may also be disqualified for statutorily enumerated cause. ORS 14.210. Beyond the statutory provisions, a judge who is actually biased against a defendant or counsel must be disqualified as a matter of due process, regardless of any failure to comply with statutory procedures. See State v. Meyer, 31 Or App 775, at 781, 571 P2d 550 (1977); Taylor v. Hayes, 418 US 488, 94 S Ct 2697, 41 LEd2d 897 (1974). If you have such an issue, make a motion and offer evidence of facts tending to show bias.

IX. Day of Trial Motions

A. Exclusion or Sequestration of Witnesses

It is usually a good idea to move to exclude all witnesses, whether at trial or a motions hearing. OEC 615. For similar reasons, do not let your client talk to the officer, and attempt to keep your client from walking in the officer’s presence. The prosecution frequently asks the officer if the defendant talks and walks the same today as she did at the time of the arrest.

B. Motions for Judgment of Acquittal

The motion for judgment of acquittal tests whether the prosecution’s evidence is legally sufficient to prove the elements of the offense. A true judgment of acquittal, whether granted at trial or on appeal or subsequent review, is final and prevents a retrial. ORS 136.445; Oregon Constitution, Article I, section 2; U.S. Constitution, Amendments V and XIV; State v. Harris, 288 Or 703, 609 P2d 798 (1980); Wright v. West, 505 US 277, 112 S Ct 2482, 120 L Ed 2d 225 (1992).

A true motion for judgment of acquittal relates solely to the sufficiency of the state’s proof, i.e., whether the evidence is sufficient to prove the elements of the offense. See, e.g., State v. Lovins, 177 Or App 534, 537, 33 P3d 1060 (2001) (so stating). It is not a basis to attack the indictment or the statute. E.g., State v. Hankins, 342 Or 258, 151 P3d 149 (2007). It may, however, focus on a legal question rather than a factual one. E.g., State v. Kurtz, 233 Or App 573, 228 P3d 583 (2010) (judgment of acquittal should have been granted because a tribal officer is not a police officer for purposes of attempt to elude statute).

If the state’s evidence is sufficient to prove the defendant’s guilt of a different offense than the charged offense, there is a variance between the pleading and the proof. If the variance is “material,” the defendant is entitled to acquittal. See State v. Duvall, 187 Or App 316, 67 P3d 424 (2003) (discussing rule, citing cases). If the variance is not material, i.e., if the defendant is not prejudiced, there is no remedy for the variance. State v. Woodward, 187 Or App 233, 66 P3d 556 (2003). If the defendant is convicted notwithstanding a material variance, the remedy is a new trial. State v. Alben, 139 Or App 236, 911 P2d 1239 (1996).

A ruling on a defense motion for acquittal by reason of a variance may not bar a retrial. ORS 131.535; 136.490; State v. Freeman, 127 Or App 640, 873 P2d 1107 (1994) (trial court dismissed with prejudice accusatory instrument alleging wrong date; Court of Appeals held dismissal was error). But, if the vague terms of the indictment threatens the defendant with a double-jeopardy violation because there are multiple acts as to which jeopardy might attach, defendant should argue that a retrial is barred. See State v. Roper, 34 Or App 273, 277, 578 P2d 479 (1978), reversed on other grounds, 286 Or 621 (1979) (noting that a variance might be prejudicial if it exposed the defendant to a possible double-jeopardy violation). If the accusatory instrument alleges one theory, but the proof is another theory so closely related that the two would have to be joined for trial, retrial may be barred by statutory double jeopardy under ORS 131.535.

A trial court has discretion to grant the state’s motion to reopen the case. ORCP 58B; State v. Agee, 223 Or App 729, 196 P3d 1060 (2008). The court’s discretion might be more limited, and is more likely to be exercised in your favor, if the defense has offered evidence before the motion is made. So, in the right case, it would be worth not making the motion at the close of the states case, putting on evidence (preferably completely unrelated to the gap in the state’s case) and then making the motion. Id. (discussing factors to guide the exercise of discretion). Agee cites Creasey v. Hogan, 292 Or 154, 169, 637 P2d 114 (1981) for the proposition that the court should not reopen evidence once the jury starts deliberating, but a trial court might find the motion untimely once the jury is out. See State v. Brust, 158 Or App 455, 974 P2d 734 (1999) (suggesting that the trial court has discretion to rule on an MJOA at that time, and reviewing on appeal after the trial court rules on the merits.) The court cannot grant an MJOA post-verdict. State v. Metcalfe, 328 Or 309, 974 P2d 1189 (1999).

Evidence produced during the defense case will be considered by the trial court on a renewed motion at the end of the case, and may cure any error in the mid-trial ruling. State v. Nix, 7 Or App 383, 491 P2d 635 (1971); State v. Stothoff, 99 Or App 197, 781 P2d 412 (1989). Defense counsel should object to this procedure on due process and equal protection grounds; the defendant should not be in a worse position than an otherwise identical defendant for whom the trial court rules correctly and grants the motion made at the close of the state’s case. See State v. Moore/State v. Coen, 349 Or 371, 245 P3d 101 (2010) (where defendant takes the stand to refute improperly-admitted evidence, that testimony may not be offered by the state on retrial).

C. Continuance

If you need more time, ask for it. A written motion is required in some counties and in front of some judges, and it is virtually always more persuasive than an oral request at a scheduling or pretrial hearing. Offer evidence of related facts, such as when you got the last batch of discovery or why your expert needs to conduct more tests. A request for a continuance is within the court’s discretion, State v. Wolfer, 241 Or 15, 17, 403 P2d 715 (1965). It is possible to win reversal for denial of a continuance, and if you ask and make a good showing, you’re more likely to get the time you need. Consider whether the state is the one who really needs a continuance; if, for example, the state failed to provide discovery timely, then you want a dismissal for failure to provide discovery, but you don’t object if the state seeks a continuance.

X. Recent Case Law

Motion to suppress—probable cause of traffic violation—failure to drive within a lane.

Failure to drive within a lane statute requires drivers to stay within a single lane unless the driver has some valid reason that makes it impracticable to do so. An officer observed defendant’s Hummer drift back and forth within its lane, briefly cross a lane-dividing line, and cross over the fog line for 20 to 30 feet. The officer stopped defendant for failing to drive within a single lane, ORS 811.370(1)(a), observed evidence that defendant was intoxicated, and arrested defendant for DUII. Defendant moved to suppress, arguing that the officer lacked probable cause to stop him because ORS 811.370 does not prohibit brief and minor instances of driving outside a lane. Court holds that ORS 811.370 requires drivers to stay within a single lane unless the driver has some valid reason that makes it impracticable to do so. Here, because there was no apparent reason for defendant not to keep his vehicle within the lane, the officer had probable cause to stop him for violating ORS 811.370(1)(a).

State v. Rosling, 288 Or App 357 (2017) (Aoyagi, J.).

Search & Seizure—reasonable suspicion—suspicion of duii diversion violation not a lawful basis to stop.

Officer’s reasonable suspicion that defendant had violated her DUII diversion agreement is not a lawful basis for a stop.

Defendant entered diversion following a charge for driving under the influence of intoxicants (DUII). Law enforcement at a victim’s impact panel observed signs that defendant was under the influence of drugs, asked defendant to leave the auditorium, directed her to sit in a chair and blocked her exit, and confronted her about using illegal drugs. Defendant admitted to taking drugs and gave an officer consent to search her purse. The search revealed a firearm and drugs.

The court concludes that because the provisions of Oregon’s statutory DUII diversion program do not authorize officers to arrest a person for failing to comply with the conditions of DUII diversion, they do not implicitly authorize officers to stop a person based on reasonable suspicion that the person has violated those conditions.

Miranda warnings were not required during traffic stop and duii investigation.

State v. Nelson, 285 Or App 345 (2017) (Ortega, P.J.)

The court concludes that defendant was not in compelling circumstances during a DUII investigation that took place on defendant’s driveway and he was not entitled to suppression of his statements. Defendant was stopped for a traffic violation on his own driveway. The officer then developed suspicion that defendant was driving while intoxicated. Defendant made several incriminating statements to the officer. The court rejects defendant’s argument that the stop went beyond a routine investigatory stop into compelling circumstances. In particular, the court notes that defendant was in his own driveway. And, although the officer told defendant he was not free to leave and did not allow him to walk into his house, the restrictions on his movements were not so significant to create compelling circumstances. Further, the duration of the encounter – 32 minutes – was also not so lengthy to create compelling circumstances. Finally, the officer’s statement to defendant that he believed defendant was too impaired to be driving did not convey to defendant that the officer had probable cause to arrest defendant.

Defendant could not invoke right to counsel in non-compelling circumstances.

State v. Anderson, 285 Or App 355 (2017) (Ortega, P.J.)

The court rejects defendant’s argument that she invoked her right to counsel by handing an officer a preprinted care asserting her rights at the beginning of a traffic stop. Additionally, after consenting to performing FSTs, defendant twice asked to talk to her attorney, and the officer said she could talk to her attorney in the event she was arrested. The court rejects without discussion defendant’s argument that the circumstances were compelling. The court then explains that in non-compelling circumstances, officers were permitted to question defendant even after she invoked her rights and her statements were not involuntary.

Miranda warnings – warnings were not rendered inadequate by officer’s subsequent statements and defendant knowingly and voluntarily waived rights.

State v. Fink, 285 Or App 302 (2017) (Garrett, J.)

The court concludes that defendant knowingly and voluntarily waived her Miranda rights. Defendant was arrested for DUII and given Miranda warnings. Defendant told the officer to stop talking to her, but the officer asked her questions related to her hair and eye color and where she lived. Defendant asked for an attorney and the officer said, “Well these aren’t the questions that you can really avoid by using, you would like an attorney.” Defendant continued to engage in off-topic conversation with the officer, eventually saying that she drove drunk. On appeal, defendant conceded that she initiated a discussion of the crime and that the officer’s statement was reasonably referring to “booking questions” that are excepted from Miranda. However, under the totality, she argued that the officer’s comment undermined the Miranda warnings and there was no valid waiver. The court rejects that argument. Defendant’s initial warnings were adequate and it was clear from the record that she understood her rights. Nothing occurred between the warnings and her re-engagement in conversation that would have required the officer to give new warnings.

Search and seizure – defendant did not have a privacy interest in a bac test conducted for medical treatment.

State v. Miller, 284 Or App 818 (2017) (Duncan, P.J.)

ORS 676.260(1) requires hospital staff to notify law enforcement investigating a car accident if the purported driver has a BAC that exceeds the legal amount. Defendant was in the hospital being treated following a car accident. Hospital staff tested his blood as part of his medical treatment and revealed a .333 percent BAC. Staff then notified the officer who was present investigating the accident.

Defendant argued that the hospital’s disclosure of his BAC test result was a search under both Article I, section 9, and the Fourth Amendment. The court first rejects defendant’s argument that he had a privacy interest under Article I, section 9, because it had previously rejected that argument in State v. Gonzalez, 120 Or App 249 (1993) and defendant did not meet the burden of showing that Gonzalez was wrongly decided. Under the Fourth Amendment, defendant relied on Ferguson v. City of Charleston, 532 US 67 (2001), in which the United States Supreme Court struck down as unconstitutional searches of pregnant patient’s urine pursuant to a joint policy between the hospital and law enforcement. Here, the court distinguishes that case because the purpose of the search in Ferguson was to gather incriminating evidence for later prosecution. The Supreme Court did not similarly overrule policies that impose a duty to make disclosures to law enforcement when evidence is discovered in the course of routine treatment, as it was in this case.

Search and seizure – extension of stop justified by reasonable suspicion of DUII.

State v. Craig, 284 Or App 786 (2017) (Flynn, J., pro tem)

The court concludes that police were justified in extending a traffic stop of defendant because they had reasonable suspicion of DUII. Police stopped defendant for driving unsafely because one of her tires was flat and she was driving well under the speed limit. Police also suspected that defendant’s vehicle had left the scene of an earlier accident. Police detained defendant so another officer, who was investigating the accident, could look at the car. That officer determined that the car was not involved in the accident. However, the officers had noted the odor of alcohol coming from defendant’s car, and that her eyes were glassy. Although the original purpose of the stop dissipated when the officer concluded that her car was not involved in the accident, the other facts – the odor of alcohol, defendant’s glassy eyes, and her unsafe driving – gave rise to reasonable suspicion of DUII. It is worth examining if an expanded search or questioning after the unavoidable lull of a traffic stop occurs.

DUII – right to counsel during a breath test.

State v. Green, 279 Or. App. 716 (2016)

In a DUII case, officers denied defendant the right to counsel before he consented to a breath test by remaining in the room while defendant spoke to the receptionist for an attorney. The receptionist, although not an attorney, was the means through which defendant could confidentially communicate with, and obtain legal advice from, an attorney. The officers’ presence is presumed to have a chilling effect on the defendant’s exercise of his right to counsel and the state did not overcome that presumption in this case. Therefore, the breath test that followed the conversation must be suppressed.

DUII blood draws require a warrant or exigency.

Birchfield v. North Dakota, 136 S.Ct. 2016

Under the Fourth Amendment, obtaining a blood draw in a DUII investigation requires a search warrant or an exception to the warrant requirement beyond the search incident to arrest exception to the warrant requirement. Although states may punish motorists for a refusal to submit to a breath test based on implied consent laws, states may not criminally punish motorists for reviewing to submit to a blood test.

ORS 813.131(2013) – Certification required to request a urine test.

State v. Hedgepeth 290 Or App 399 (February 22, 2018) (Shorr, J.) (En banc) (Powers, J., dissenting)

An officer was properly certified to request a urine test after suspecting that the defendant was driving under the influence even though ORS 813.131 (2013), the governing statute at the time, required that the officer be certified by the Board of Public Safety Standards and Training and the officer was only certified by the Department of Public Safety Standards and Training. This was because the legislature had impliedly amended the statute in 1997 to require that officers requesting such tests be only certified by the DPSST. Hanson v. DMV, 278 Or. App. 599 (2016)

XI. Final Thoughts

Don’t just file a motion because you have a spiffy form and you want to see what happens. File it for a reason. Good reasons include: winning the motion and thereby increase your chance of winning at trial, getting a good plea offer, setting up an issue for appeal, or making the judge tell you No so many times that he or she feels a little guilty and wants to say Yes to something. A motion to suppress may give you what amounts to an opportunity to depose the arresting officer.

If you are doing your job, judges will say No to you over and over and over. Get over it, and ask again. You are not helping your client if you really need another two weeks to get a report back from an expert and you don’t ask because the judge is cranky and never grants continuances. Be polite and persistent and make your record.
