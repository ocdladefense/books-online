I. Introduction

There are Field Sobriety Tests (FSTs) and there are NHTSA Standardized Field Sobriety Test (SFSTs). They are similar but serve a different purpose. Field sobriety tests are found in Division 25 of the Department of State Police Oregon Administrative Rules1. With a field sobriety test the officer takes note of his observation while the subject performs a test. There are no standardized clues for field sobriety tests. There is standardized criteria for assessing the subject’s performance of the test. The observations of the officer are used to explain the officer’s opinion that the person appeared adversely effected by the intoxicating effects of liquor and/or controlled substances, inhalants and/or marijuana.

In comparison, Standardized Field Sobriety Tests have standardized instruction, clues, and scoring criteria. The scoring criteria is used to gather a rough approximation of the subject’s blood alcohol concentration either being above or below a blood alcohol level of 0.08 percent. The standardized field sobriety tests are designed to establish probable cause that the subject’s BAC at the time of the test is unlawful. It is used to assist the officer in making an arrest/no arrest decision. But, correlating the performance of the Standardized Field Sobriety Test to blood alcohol concentration, by the state, is prohibited by law.

February 2022

State v. Shevyakov, 311 Or App 82, 489 P3d 580 (2021)

Asking for consent to search, including requesting that a suspect perform FSTs, constitutes impermissible interrogation unless the law precludes the use of the person’s refusal against that person at trial.

Defendant appealed convictions for driving under the influence of intoxicants (DUII), unlawful possession of heroin, and unlawful possession of a controlled substance. Defendant assigned error to the lower court’s denial of his motion to suppress evidence of his performance on the administered field sobriety tests (FSTs). On appeal, Defendant argued that after invoking his Miranda rights, the police officer unconstitutionally interrogated him by asking him to perform the FSTs. In response, the State argued that because FST’s are not testimonial, the Defendant’s Miranda rights had not been violated. The Court disagreed and concluded that Defendant was impermissibly interrogated when the police officer requested that he perform the FSTs after he invoked his Miranda rights. Therefore, asking for consent to search, including requesting that a suspect perform FSTs, constitutes impermissible interrogation unless the law precludes the use of the person’s refusal against that person at trial. Reversed and remanded.

Summarized by: Corbin Moeller, Willamette Law Online.

Practice Tip

Carefully review each case for whether your client at anytime invoked their Miranda rights and move to suppress the results of any interrogation, including results of SFSTs or a breath test, that occurred after invocation of those rights. Declining to perform SFSTs could arguably be an invocation of the right to silence since some of the tests require speaking. After Shevyakov police may need to move their investigation directly to service of a blood draw warrant to gather further evidence of impairment from your client after they invoke Miranda rights.

II. Field Sobriety Tests

A. Statutory Definition

ORS 801.272 “Field sobriety test” means a physical or mental test, approved by the Department of State Police by rule after consultation with the Department of Public Safety Standards and Training, that enables a police officer or trier of fact to screen for or detect probable impairment from intoxicating liquor, cannabis, a controlled substance, or an inhalant or any combination of intoxicating liquor, cannabis, a controlled substance and an inhalant.

B. Field Tests Approved by Rule

Approved field sobriety tests are enumerated and described by OAR 257-025-0012:

a. Horizontal Gaze Nystagmus (HGN).

b. Walk and Turn.

c. One Leg Stand.

d. Modified Finger to Nose.

e. Finger Count.

f. Alphabet.

g. Counting.

h. Internal Clock.

i. Romberg Balance.

Note: The Oregon Administrative Rules are silent as to the clues to be noted or the criteria for performance evaluation.

The following additional approved tests may be performed by police officers who have completed the eight-hour “Drugs That Impair Driving” curriculum:

  1. Lack of Convergence.

  2. Pupil Size Estimation.

  3. Pulse Rate Examination.

A police officer may administer any, all, or none of the field sobriety tests described in this rule as deemed appropriate in the sole discretion of the police officer.

C. Acts, Signs and Symptoms of Intoxicant Impairment

OAR 257-025-0010 also lays out acts, signs and symptoms of impairment that police are trained to look for. These are often included in police reports, and if they are not in the police report it is worth mentioning that the officer is trained to look for these but must not have seen it since it was not documented in the report:

1. Difficulty in walking or unusual walking.

2. Difficulty standing.

3. Difficulty following directions.

4. Odor of alcoholic beverage on the breath.

5. Flushed or pale appearance.

6. Speech difficulties or unusual speech patterns.

7. Disorderly or unusual conduct or demeanor.

8. Lack of muscular coordination or muscular tremors.

9. Evidence of mental disturbance.

10. Visual disorders or difficulties.

11. Sleepiness or drowsiness.

12. Dizziness.

13. Nausea or vomiting.

14. Mood swings.

D. Implied Consent

ORS 813.125 – Implied consent to field sobriety tests. Any person who operates a vehicle upon premises open to the public or the highways of the state shall be deemed to have given consent to submit to field sobriety tests upon the request of a police officer for the purpose of determining if the person is under the influence of intoxicants if the police officer reasonably suspects that the person has committed the offense of driving while under the influence of intoxicants in violation of ORS 813.010 or a municipal ordinance. Before the tests are administered, the person requested to take the tests shall be informed of the consequences of refusing to take or failing to submit to the tests under ORS 813.136.

ORS 813.136 – Consequence of refusal or failure to submit to field sobriety tests. If a person refuses or fails to submit to field sobriety tests as required by ORS 813.135, evidence of the person’s refusal or failure to submit is admissible in any criminal or civil action or proceeding arising out of allegations that the person was driving while under the influence of intoxicants, if the police officer adequately explains to the defendant that the field sobriety tests will be “physical tests” only, not testimonial in nature, and warns of the consequences of not taking the tests. State v. Rohrs, 157 Or App 494 (1998)(citing State v. Fish, 321 Or 48 (1995).

E. Search and Seizure

Field sobriety tests are a search State v. Nagel, 320 Or 24 (1994), and must be supported by:

a. A warrant

b. Probable cause and exigent circumstances, or

i. Subjective and objective criteria required for probable cause for field sobriety tests. State v. Nagel, 320 Or 24 (1994).

ii. Physical manifestations of intoxication are not required for an officer to have probable cause to order a driver to submit to FSTs were a succession of driving violations combined with the driver’s behavior indicate intoxication. State v. Miller, 265 Or App 442 (2014).

iii. Slight odor, bloodshot eyes, and admission of drinking not enough for objective probable cause. State v. Stroup, 147 Or App 118 (1997), but see State v. Liebrecht, 120 Or App 617 (1993).

iv. If there is probable cause for a DUII arrest, exigent circumstances exist for administration of FSTs to collect additional evidence for the DUII offense. State v. Mazzola, 36 Or 804, 820 (2015).

c. Consent

i. Probable cause not required before officer can ask for field sobriety tests. State v. Ramos, 149 Or App 269 (1997). But the officer must have reasonable suspicion of DUII to expand the scope of the investigation by inquiry or search. State v. Arreola-Botello, 365 Or 695 (2019).

ii. State must show by preponderance of the evidence that defendant’s consent to take field sobriety tests was voluntary. State v. Maddux, 144 Or App 34 (1996); State v. Warner, 284 Or 147 (1978).

iii. Mere acquiescence to officer’s order is not consent. State v. Freund, 102 Or App 647 (1990); State v. Lowe, 144 Or App 313 (1996).

iv. Voluntariness of consent is determined by looking at the totality of the circumstances. State v. Parker, 317 Or App 225 (1993); State v. Flores, 280 Or 273 (1977).

2024 Update

Please be advised of this recent Court of Appeals decision hot off the press:

State v. Shayna Lynn Hamilton, 330 Or App 258 (2024)(A172776).

In this DUII case, the court determined that, under State v. Banks, 364 Or 332 (2019), refusals to perform FSTs—like refusals to perform breath tests—may be admitted as evidence of guilt if the state proves that law enforcement’s requests to perform the tests could be “understood only as a request to submit to the physical act, and not as a request that defendant provide constitutionally significant consent to the tests.” (Hamilton at 260). The court reiterates that FSTs are a search that implicate Article I, sec. 9, citing State v. Nagel, 320 Or 24 (1994)(FSTs “involve a series of specialized and unusual acts designed to elicit information that the defendant would not have exposed to the public with the officer’s direction.” (Hamilton at 268).

The court did, however, allow the urine sample to be admitted into evidence by reasoning that the defendant’s consent to the urine test was sufficiently attenuated from the original unlawful police conduct regarding the FSTs.

B. Testing Battery

The Standardized Field Sobriety Test battery consist of the Horizontal Gaze Nystagmus (HGN), Walk and Turn (WAT) and One Leg Stand (OLS). These tests are not new. They have been used in modified forms by the medical community to assess neurological dysfunction. Alcohol intoxication is a neurological dysfunction. Some symptoms of neurological dysfunction can be measured by similar physical tests by qualified physician and neurologists. It does not need emphasis that police officers are not qualified physicians and are ill-equipped to render medical opinions. Yet, in a sense, that is what they do when assessing a suspect at roadside.

The SFSTs are divided attention tasks. The subject is required to perform a mental task, such as following instructions, as well as the physical task of balancing. The HGN and Vertical Gaze Nystagmus (VGN) tests are the exception. It is important to remember that SFSTs do not gauge a person’s ability to operate a motor vehicle. They are used to offer a rough approximation of whether the testing subject is at or above a specified blood alcohol concentration (BAC).

Validation of the Standardized Field Sobriety Test Battery at BACs Below 0.10 Percent – Final Report – August 1998. (Jack Stuster and Marcelline Burns)

Many individuals, including some judges, believe that the purpose of a field sobriety test is to measure driving impairment. For this reason, they tend to expect tests to possess “face validity,” that is, tests that appear to be related to actual driving tasks. Tests of physical and cognitive abilities, such as balance, reaction time, and information processing, have face validity, to varying degrees, based on the involvement of these abilities in driving tasks: that is, the tests seem to be relevant “on the face of it.” Horizontal gaze nystagmus lacks face validity because it does not appear to be linked to the requirements of driving a motor vehicle. The reasoning is correct, but it is based on the incorrect assumption that field sobriety tests are designed to measure driving impairment.

Driving a motor vehicle is a very complex activity that involves a wide variety of tasks and operator capabilities. It is unlikely that complex human performance, such as that required to safely drive an automobile, can be measured at roadside. The constraints imposed by roadside testing conditions were recognized by the developers of NHTSA’s SFST battery. As a consequence, they pursued the development of tests that would provide statistically valid and reliable indications of a driver’s BAC, rather than indications of driving impairment. The link between BAC and driving impairment is a separate issue, involving entirely different research methods. Those methods have found driving to be impaired at BAC’s as low as 0.02 percent, with a sharp increase in impairment at about 0.07 percent (Moskowitz and Robison, 1988; Stuster, 1997). Thus, SFST result help officers to make accurate DWI arrest decisions even though SFTS’s do not directly measure driving impairment.

Horizontal gaze nystagmus is the most accurate diagnostics of BAC available to officers in the field. HGN’s lack of face validity to driving tasks is irrelevant because the objective of the test is to discriminate between drivers above and below the statutory BAC limit, not to measure driving impairment. Throughout the United States, DWI laws permit arrest decisions to be made on the basis of the statutory BAC limit, irrespective of a specific motorist’s degree of impairment. Motorists also can be arrested at BACs below the statutory limit if their driving performance is demonstrably impaired by alcohol or other drugs.

CONCLUSIONS

The results of the study provide clear evidence of the validity of the Standardized Field Sobriety Test Battery to discriminate above or below 0.08 percent BAC. Further, study results strongly suggest that the SFSTs also accurately discriminate above or below 0.04 percent BAC.

Page 27-8. See State v. Morgan, 251 Or App 99 (2012), where attorney used OEC 706 and the above paragraph to challenge the value of NHTSA SFSTs in a DUII/CS trial.

C. Three-test battery for NHTSA Standardized Field Sobriety Tests

1. Horizontal Gaze Nystagmus – HGN

Foundation for admissibility – see State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995), for admissibility of HGN test as scientific evidence. (Note: O’Key does not discuss VGN and thus VGN is not admissible as it has not been judicially authorized without a proper scientific foundation being laid, and O’Key does not discuss HGN in controlled substance cases).

a. Pre-test requirements, glasses or contacts, blindness, obvious eye disorders, head injuries, sick, medications, etc. (OSP form 41 questions)

b. Stimulus – can be an ink pen, pen light, finger, etc. Small enough object so the testing subject can focus on it. The stimulus should be held approximately 12 – 15 inches away from the person’s face, slightly above eye level. The person tested must hold their head still. (OAR 257-025-0020)

c. The first pass/passes are made to assure the person tested can track the stimulus with both eyes. The officer also needs to assure both pupils are relatively the same size and resting nystagmus is not present. During these passes the officer is focusing on both eyes together. During the remainder of the test the officer is focusing on each eye independently.

d. The next check is for smooth pursuit. The officer should move the stimulus smoothly to the side, at a speed of approximately two seconds from the center to the officer’s right until reaching the far extreme. Then, the stimulus is moved to the officer’s left to the extreme, taking approximately four seconds. Each eye should be checked twice.

e. The next check is for nystagmus at maximum deviation. The officer should move the stimulus to the right until the subject’s left eye reaches maximum deviation. Verify that no white is showing in the corner of the eye. Hold the stimulus steady for a minimum of four seconds. The officer is looking for a distinct and sustained jerking of the eye. Check each eye twice for each clue.

f. The next check is to determine if the angle of onset of the nystagmus is before or after 45 degrees. Starting in the center and moving to the 45-degree angle should take four seconds. If jerking is noticed the officer should stop the stimulus and be assured the jerking continues, then determine if the angle is before or after 45 degrees. Verify some white is still showing in the corner of the eye. Generally speaking, the higher the BAC, the sooner the jerking will start as the eye moves toward the side. If the jerking begins prior to 45 degree, that person’s BAC could be 0.08 or above. 50 minus the angle of onset is the equation used to predict the BAC. (“Tharp Equation” 2008 NHTSA DRE Participant Manual, Session 4, pg. 12 of 46.) But under ORS 813.010(1)(a) the state is precluded from using the HGN test result as evidence that the defendant had a .08 percent or more. State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285, 322 (1995); State v. Fisken, 138 Or App 396 (1996); State v. Ross, 147 Or App 634 (1997).

HGN Test Clues Summary: Six specific clues. Test criterion is four or more clues.

g. Finally, the officer is trained to check for vertical gaze nystagmus. Currently a sufficient scientific foundation has not been established for vertical gaze nystagmus. State v. Bevan, 235 Or App 533 (2010).

h. If the officer does not administer the test as trained the test is not admissible as scientific evidence. State v. Ingram, 238 Or App 720 (2010), State v O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995).

i. Administering officer is required to have training. The only currently approved training in Oregon is the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) 24-hour course entitled “DWI Detection and Standardized Field Sobriety Testing.”

Practice Tip

When dealing with HGN, keep it simple. The jury does not understand HGN. A good cross-examination will highlight that the HGN test is a complex examination. If it is not done correctly, it is worthless as a scientific test. Do not use the term “stimulus.” If the officer is using a pen, call it a pen, not a stimulus. If the officer is using a finger, call it a finger, not a stimulus. Point out that the test is used by doctors in the controlled environment of a physician’s office. Obviously, the roadside is not a controlled environment, and moving lights and reflections can corrupt the test, and that a physician would never assess a person using that test in the conditions that the officer gave the test to your client. Go to the scene and see for yourself if there are distractions. Point out that the subject perceived a still image of the finger or pen and that the person is totally unaware that the eye is jerking. The subject cannot control whether the eye jerks. It is totally involuntary and unknown to the subject. If you are lucky, some officers will say that a person with HGN has poorer vision than he or she otherwise would have and it could be a driving hazard. Jump on that and have fun.

Practice Tip: “Distinct and Sustained”

All officers in Oregon are trained using the NHTSA method for performing the HGN test. The officers are trained to look for distinct and sustained nystagmus at maximum deviation. Nowhere in the NHSTA manual does it define “distinct.” A potential line of questioning may be, “How far does the eye have to move (jerk) before it can be called nystagmus?” “How fast does it have to jerk per second before it can be called nystagmus?”

2. Walk and Turn

Psychophysical test. This test contains both a mental and a physical component. The test has two phases: the instruction phase and the walking phase.

Instructions:

  • Place your left foot on the line (real or imaginary).

  • Place your right foot on the line ahead of the left foot, with the heal of your right foot against the toe of the left foot.

  • Place your arms down at your side.

  • Maintain this position until I have completed the instructions. Do not start to walk until told to do so.

  • Do you understand the instructions so far? (Make sure subject indicated understanding.)

  • When I tell you to start, take nine heel-to-toe steps on the line, turn, and take nine heel-to-toe steps down the line.

  • While you are walking, keep your arms at your sides, watch your feet at all times, and count your steps out loud.

  • Once you start walking, don’t stop until you have completed the test.

  • Do you understand the instructions? (Make sure subject understands.)

  • Instruct person to begin the test.

Clues:

  • Cannot keep balance while listening to the instructions. Record this clue if subject does not maintain heel-to-toe position throughout the instructions. (Feet must actually break apart or step off the line.) Do not record this clue if the subject sways or uses the arms to balance but maintains the heel-to-toe position.

  • Starts too soon. Record this clue if subject starts walking before you tell them to begin.

  • Stops while walking. Do not record this clue if the subject is merely walking slowly.

  • Does not touch heel to toe. The subject leaves a space of one-half inch or more between the heel and toe on any step.

  • Steps off the line. The subject steps so that one foot is entirely off the line.

  • Uses arms for balance. The subject raises one or both arms six or more inches from the sides in order to maintain balance.

  • Improper turn. The subject removes the front foot from the line while turning. Also record this clue if the subject has not followed directions as instructed, i.e., spins or pivots around or loses balance while turning. (Turning the wrong direction is not a clue.)

  • Incorrect number of steps. Record this clue if the subject takes more or fewer than nine steps in either direction.

WAT Test Clues Summary: Eight specific clues. Test criterion is two or more clues.

Practice Tip

Usually, there is not an actual line for the test. If you want to risk having fun at the expense of the officer (maybe you should save this for someone who the jury may already be getting annoyed at) you can explore the imaginary line. For example, “Officer, what color was your imaginary line? How wide was your imaginary line? How far off of your imaginary line did my client step? Did my client step off his imaginary line?” You get the point.

3. One Leg Stand

Psychophysical test. This test contains both a mental and a physical component. The test has two phases: the instruction phase and the balance and count phase. During the instruction phase the subject is instructed to stand in a specific position while they receive a series of instructions.

Instructions:

  • Please stand with your feet together and your arms down at the sides, like this.

  • Do not start to perform the test until I tell you to do so.

  • Do you understand the instructions so far?

  • When I tell you to start, raise either leg with the foot approximately six inches off the ground, keeping the foot parallel to the ground.

  • Keep both legs straight and your arms at your sides.

  • While holding that position, count out loud in the following manner: “one thousand one, one thousand two, one thousand three,” and so on until told to stop.

  • Keep your arms to your sides at all times and keep watching your raised foot.

  • Do you understand?

  • Go ahead and perform the test. (Officer should always time the 30 seconds. Test should be discontinued after 30 seconds.)

Clues:

  • The subject sways while balancing. This refers to side-to-side or back-and-forth motion of the body or a swaying motion of the foot, while the subject maintains the position.

  • Uses arms for balance. Subject moves arms six or more inches from the side of the body in order to keep balance.

  • Hopping. Subject is able to keep one foot off the ground, but resorts to hopping in order to maintain balance.

  • Puts foot down. The subject is not able to maintain the position, putting the foot down one or more times during the 30 second count.

During the balance and count phase the subject is told to stand on one leg, raising the other leg straight out in front, about six inches off the ground, and count for 30 seconds, with their arms at their sides. The subject is also told to watch their raised foot.

  • The officer is looking for four validated clues; (1) raising arms, (2) swaying, (3) hopping, (4) putting foot down.

  • The test needs to be timed by the officer.

OLS Test Clues Summary: Four specific clues. Test criterion is two or more.

4. Grading

In order for testimony regarding whether defendant “passed” or “failed” the SFSTs to be admissible, the state needs to lay a proper foundation. State v. Beltran-Chavez, 286 Or App 590 (2017). It is error to allow a police officer to testify that the SFSTs are “the product of scientific research.” State v. Eatinger, 298 Or App 630 (2019).

Practice Tip

Some deputy DAs will be able to confuse a judge into letting the officer opine whether the client passed or failed the tests. Sometimes it is to your advantage to let the state go down this path because your theory may be that the officer made a mistake in thinking that the tests have a “pass” or “fail” component and you want to “vamp” on that theme. You can impeach with the NHTSA SFST manual if you brought one to trial. Go to the 2018 student manual, Session 8, page 69 “SFST’s are a tool to assist you in seeing visible signs of impairment and are not a pass/fail test.”

More Practice Tips

Always order the training records of the police officers involved. They can be ordered by sending an email with the officer’s name and DPSST number to dpsst.records@state.or.us You will want to know when the officer was last trained in SFSTs.

Get the NHTSA SFST participant and instructor manuals for review and for impeachment (also contained in the DUII Notebook Resource Guide). State v. Morgan, 251 Or App 99 (2012). A productive subject of impeachment may arise if the officer testifies that the standardized field sobriety tests are helpful in determining if the defendant had the mental and physical faculties to operate a motor vehicle safely. The better you know the manuals’ content and organization; the more avenues will be available for your creativity to manifest. The better that you know all the training manuals, the more relaxed you will be in trial.

Sometimes the best defense is the simplest. For example, a nice theme is that the officer had already made up his mind to arrest because he presumed that the driver, who he never met before, was negatively affected, from the moment he spoke to the client driver-side. The officer presumed guilt. He judged like a biased judge would and he was not open to the reasonable possibility that he may be wrong. You can press that the officer is trained to be biased, and you can show that if the officer had been trained to think in a certain way, all officer/driver encounters are potentially lethal. Sometimes you can get the jury to stand in the shoes of your client and they may not think it fair to be judged by someone who was not open to the reasonable possibility of innocence. And, if you can get the jury to think that they could not do the SFSTs better than your client without some practice, all the better.

Drive the route your client drove if your client was followed for a distance prior to the officer initiating the stop. When you drive, record the route, beginning with where the officer first takes note of your client. Observe the scene of where the field sobriety tests were performed. If there is an appreciable slope, you can demonstrate the slope by placing a ball on the surface and record the movement. If there are reflective surfaces in the direction that you client was facing while doing the HGN test, flashing lights reflecting off that surface may be a cause of the HGN observed.

If the police contact and field sobriety tests were recorded, have people who know the defendant watch the recording and tell you whether the defendant is acting like she normally does. While the witness may not know how the defendant would look normally while doing something abnormal like a field sobriety test, the witness may give an opinion whether the defendant is walking, talking and otherwise acting normally for her.

D. Top Ten NHTSA 2018 DWI Manual Quotes (according to the questionable opinion of Hugh Duvall)***

It was difficult to pick just ten quotes in this edition (even though I cheated and doubled up on a couple of them). Some are oldies but goodies, and some are brand-spanking new…

1. “Effective DWI enforcers do not leap to the arrest/no arrest decision.” (Session 4, p 4/36). While this can be nuanced to mean that the officer is not to leap to a final conclusion, it rubs against the common officer testimony that she believed she had PC to arrest upon contact.

 

2. “Your second task is to observe the driver’s exit and walk from the vehicle.” (Session 4, p 7/36). This is useful where an officer testifies in response to a defense question about whether the client had any difficulty exiting his car with, “He might have. I don’t recall.” The officer is either ignoring her training, or is ignorant of NHTSA protocol.

 

3. “Once the stop command has been communicated to the suspect driver, the officer must closely observe the driver’s actions and vehicle maneuvers during the stopping sequence.” (Session 5, p 5/66). See comment in #2 above.

 

4. “As soon as an officer gives the stop command, the subject’s driving task becomes more complex. If subject is under the influence, the subject may not be able to handle this more complex driving very well.” (Session 5, p 25/66). This is a useful governmental admission, where the client shows no difficulty driving after the officer initiates the stop.

 

5. “There are a number of techniques you can use to assess impairment while the driver is still behind the wheel. Most of these techniques apply the concept of divided attention.” (Session 6, p 15/28). This is useful where the client has physical issues which prevent him from performing the Walk and Turn or One Leg Stand tests. Bonus content: Both the ABCs and Counting are considered divided attention tasks. (See Session 6, pp 20-21/28.)

 

6. “If any one of the SFST elements is changed, the validity may be compromised.” (Session 8, p 17/95.)

 

7. Under the Walk and Turn instructions: “This test may lose its sensitivity if it is repeated several times.” (Session 8, p 69/95). This is useful to explain to the jury why they should not compare the officer’s performance (on the video or in court) with the client’s at 2:30 a.m.

 

8. “No matter how much evidence you collect, if it is not presented clearly, completely, and convincingly in court, the case may be lost.” (Session 12, p 7/42). While an officer may testify that she is simply an unbiased witness in the case, her training encourages her to advocate for a conviction, otherwise, in the words of the NHTSA, “…the case may be lost.”

 

9. This is a “two-fer” – “Record the names and condition of passengers in the vehicle and where they were located.” (Session 12, p 19/42). And, “Make sure passengers are identified, interviewed, and safely released.” (Session 12, p 21/42). At least in my experience, investigating officers frequently ignore passengers and fail to interview them.

 

10. Last, but certainly not least: “The person on trial is never a ‘lady’ or ‘gentleman,’ but is always ‘the defendant.’” (Session 12, p 39/42).

E. Advanced Roadside Impaired Driving Enforcement (ARIDE)

The Advanced Roadside Impaired Driving Enforcement (ARIDE) program was developed by NHTSA with input from the International Association of Chiefs of Police (IACP) to address the gap in training between the Standardized Field Sobriety Testing and the Drug Evaluation and Classification (DEC) Program. ARIDE is intended to bridge the gap between these two programs by providing officers with general knowledge related to drug impairment and by promoting the use of DREs in states that have the DEC Program.2 An officer must be trained in the SFSTs before enrolling in ARIDE training. The purpose of ARIDE training is to “train law enforcement officers to observe, identify, and articulate signs of impairment related to drugs, alcohol or a combination of both.” The training includes many of the topics covered in greater detail in the DRE training: drugs in the human body, observations of the eyes, the seven drug categories, and effects of drug combinations. The ARIDE training adds additional field sobriety tests to the roadside lineup, including the Lack of Convergence test, the Modified Romberg test, Finger to Nose test, and pupil examinations. An officer trained in ARIDE will not be permitted to testify as an expert, but will be allowed to testify as to observations and performance on these additional tests. Refer to Chapter 10, “DREs and Cannabis,” to familiarize yourself with these tests. A copy of the 2018 ARIDE manual and matrix are included in the Resource Guide. Be prepared to cross-examine officers that have been trained using ARIDE.
