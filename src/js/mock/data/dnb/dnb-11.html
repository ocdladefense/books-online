I. Introduction: Breath and Blood Alcohol

Path of alcohol (ethanol) in the body: Alcohol is taken into the body through the mouth and travels to the stomach via the esophagus. A small portion of alcohol is absorbed through the gastrointestinal walls while the majority of alcohol is absorbed and metabolized in the small intestine. The rate of absorption of alcohol through the stomach lining and passage to the remainder of the gastrointestinal tract can vary due to several factors. These factors can include the type of alcoholic beverage consumed, the concentration of ethanol in the beverage, the functioning of the pyloric sphincter, quantity of food in the stomach, and drinking pattern. Once in the bloodstream, the alcohol travels to all parts of the body. In the liver, alcohol undergoes oxidation where it is eventually changed to carbon dioxide and water. The kidneys filter out the end products of the oxidation process which are then excreted from the body. Approximately 95–98 percent of the alcohol undergoes this oxidative process; however, the remaining two to five percent escapes unchanged via sweat, breath, and urine. Alcohol (ethyl alcohol) metabolizes to acetaldehyde and acetic acid. Ethyl glucuronide (EtG) and ethyl sulfate (EtS) are also excreted as minor metabolites (less than one-tenth of a percent) which are found in blood and urine. Alcohol and its minor metabolites can be measured in the urine by gas chromatography-headspace analysis or gas chromatography-mass spectrometry under specific conditions. (See the section regarding urine toxicology, below.)

The exchange of alcohol from the blood to the breath occurs in the small air sacs of the lungs called alveoli. Based on Henry’s Law of gases, the concentration of a volatile substance in the air (lungs) above a fluid (blood) is proportional to the concentration of the volatile substance in the fluid. As the concentration of alcohol in the blood does not equal the concentration of alcohol in the breath, a mathematical model must be incorporated to convert a breath alcohol value to a blood alcohol value. The breath testing device assumes that the majority of the population represents a 2100:1 partition ratio (2.1 L of expired alveolar air containing approximately the same quantity of alcohol as 1 mL of blood).

II. Breath Alcohol Analysis

The majority of drunk driving prosecutions include breath test results from the suspect. From the perspective of law enforcement, breath tests are the preferred method of analyzing blood alcohol content in drunk driving cases. Although breath testing is an indirect method of measuring blood alcohol levels, it is less expensive, provides immediate results, and requires minimal police training.

A thorough understanding of the scientific foundations of breath testing is important so a zealous defense can be mounted against the breath testing device. Otherwise, a jury will be predisposed to accept the breath test results as valid. Many legitimate defenses are available and must be used to challenge a breath testing case. The attorney should carefully review the breath testing machine certification and calibration records, as well as the client’s medical history, to determine which defense is properly tailored to his or her case. Consulting with an expert witness is highly recommended in all breath testing cases.

A. Introduction to Intoxilyzer 8000

The Intoxilyzer 8000, the evidential breath testing device currently in use in Oregon, attempts to measure a breath alcohol concentration using infrared spectroscopy technology. The underlying principle behind infrared spectroscopy is that different molecules, including ethyl alcohol, absorb light energy at different frequencies. The absorption of light by ethyl alcohol is detected by the Intoxilyzer 8000 at both 3.4 and 9.3 micron frequencies. The Intoxilyzer inserts a beam of infrared light through the suspect’s breath sample and measures the amount of light absorbed at those frequencies. The more light that has been absorbed, the higher the breath alcohol concentration. For an excellent resource on the science of infrared spectroscopy, see A Primer for Infrared Spectroscopy: What the Trial Attorney Must Know, Thomas E. Workman Jr. (2010) contained in the Appendix to this section.

As a result of the many scientific studies related to infrared spectroscopy and breath testing, states authorizing the use of the Intoxilyzer 8000 require the operator of the machine to strictly adhere to certain administrative safeguards when testing a suspect arrested for drunk driving. The Oregon State Police Intoxilyzer 8000 Operator’s Guide is contained in the Resource Guide included with the DUII notebook. While failure to adhere to the administrative safeguards may not always render the breath test inadmissible, the reliability of the breath test result can be attacked during trial.

For an illuminating perspective on the accuracy failures of the Intoxilyzer 8000 and other breath test machines, see Stacey Cowley and Jessica Silver-Greensburg, “These Machines Can Put You in Jail. Don’t Trust Them,” The New York Times, Nov. 3, 2019. According to Cowley and Silver-Greensburg, Massachusetts and New Jersey have thrown out 30,000 breath tests in the past year. The article cites a court ruling in Orange County, Florida, related to the Intoxilyzer 8000:

The Intoxilyzer 8000 is a magic black box assisting the prosecution in convicting citizens of DUI. A defendant is required to blow into the box. The defense has shown significant and continued anomalies in the operation of the Intoxilyzer 8000’s operation. The prosecution argues most of the tests do not show anomalies. In fact, a high percentage of the tests may show no anomalous operation. That the Intoxilyzer 8000 mostly works is an insufficient response when a citizen’s liberty is at risk.

See State v. Conley, Case no. 48-2012-CT000017-A/A, Orange County Court, Order on Defendant’s Request to Produce, Sept. 22, 2014.

1. Administrative Regulations/Safeguards of the Intoxilyzer 8000

a. Observation Period

The operator of the machine must observe the client for a minimum of 15 minutes before administering the breath test. The observation requirement is required in all jurisdictions to ensure accuracy of the breath result. The observation period ensures the client has not burped, vomited, or regurgitated, or taken anything by mouth that may contaminate the breath result. The observation period and other breath testing procedural safeguards were recommended by top experts in alcohol forensic research. Dubowski, K.M., “Quality Assurance in Breath Alcohol Analysis,” J Anal Toxicology, 1994, Oct; 18(6) 306-311, p. 306.

The observation period is codified in OAR 257-030-0130(2)(a-b):

The operator is certain that the subject has not taken anything by mouth (drinking, smoking, eating, taking medication, etc.), vomited, or regurgitated liquid from the stomach into mouth, for at least fifteen minutes before taking the test;

There is no requirement that the operator be the person who makes observation of the subject. The person performing the Pre-Test Requirement (observation period) need not possess a permit to test the alcoholic content of blood.

In some cases, the client will claim he or she burped or regurgitated shortly before the breath test. The machine operator (typically the arresting officer) will testify that is not possible because he or she strictly adhered to the required 15-minute observation period. It is common police practice, however, to read the implied consent advice of rights while simultaneously observing the client. The reality is that the officer almost never constantly observes the client for the complete duration of the observation period. An officer’s failure to adhere to the required observation period is a defense frequently raised in both implied consent hearings and trials. Counsel should cross-examine the officer on exactly what he was doing during his surveillance of the defendant. The officer is typically performing a multitude of tasks during the observation period, including reading the implied consent form with his head down, taking notes, filling out the operator checklist, or preparing the breath machine to administer the breath results. How, then, can the officer be absolutely sure the client did not burp or regurgitate? Jurors expect police to follow proper procedures. A jury’s lack of confidence in the police to follow proper protocol may result in an acquittal. In the following case, the police failed to comply with the requirements of OAR 257-030-0070(2):

State v. Barletta, 188 Or App 113 (2003). Conviction reversed because officer allowed defendant to use the bathroom during the observation period, and could not see or hear whether she regurgitated or vomited. Although the officer subjectively believed the defendant did not regurgitate or vomit, the belief was not objectively reasonable.

b. Operator Checklist

The operator must use and complete a checklist of approved procedures when conducting a breath test. The checklist requirement is governed by OAR 257-030-0130(1):

(1) Test Identification: A checklist containing an outline of the approved procedures shall be used and completed by all operators of this instrument. Failure to record information specified in this section does not invalidate the test result obtained if the testing procedures were otherwise followed.

Challenges regarding the operator checklist are difficult. Although the operator checklist is frequently incomplete with boxes left unchecked, the officer will invariably testify his failure to complete the checklist was mere inadvertence. See State v. Olson, 88 Or App 271 (1987) (concluding that scrivener’s error on the operator checklist went to weight, not admissibility of the breath test). Counsel should at a minimum, however, carefully review the starting and ending times of the observation period to ensure a full fifteen minutes has elapsed from the starting time to the first breath sample.

c. Training of Operator

The police officer operating the Intoxilyzer 8000 must have required training to administer breath testing on a suspect. Training of the operator is governed by OAR 257-030-0090 (Training and Operators of Breath Test Equipment)

(1) The Oregon State Police, or instructors approved by the Oregon State Police will provide a course of instruction as provided in ORS 813.160.

(2) Upon completion of the course of instruction, a written examination will be given and a passing grade of 80 percent or above will be required. Each officer or technician obtaining a passing grade will be issued a permit by the Oregon State Police stating the method and equipment the officer or technician is qualified to operate.

Counsel should always ensure that the officer that conducted the breath test was properly trained and qualified to administer the breath test.

d. Agreement Between Samples

Two breath samples must be obtained from the suspect using the Intoxilyzer 8000. Agreement between two valid breath samples within a testing sequence is established when the subject sample measurements agree within plus or minus ten percent of their mean, inclusive of the upper and lower bounds of the range. If the instrument establishes agreement, the lower breath sample measurement shall be truncated to two decimal places and reported as the chemical test result. If the subject sample measurements do not agree, the instrument will abort the testing sequence and display “Sample Correlation Failure”. OAR 257-030-0140 [Determining Agreement of Breath Sample Within a Testing Sequence]. For example, if the subject’s first sample results in .124% but the second sample results in .103%, the mean breath test result is .113%. Since .103% is within ten percent of the mean (i.e., + 10% = .123%, -10% = .101%) of the mean (i.e., .113%) of the results, the machine would accept the results and display a result of .10%.

In the above example, the machine would render an acceptable breath test result despite the .02 percent difference in breath samples in a two-minute period. State experts typically assume an individual has an average alcohol elimination rate of .020 percent per hour. Thus if a suspect is .12 on the first breath test, then it would be expected that suspect would be .10 one hour later. However, the test shows a decline of .02 percent in merely two minutes, which equals an elimination rate of .6 percent per hour! Use this example to expose the flaws in the machine.

2. Distinguishing Between Calibration and Certification of the Intoxilyzer 8000

The Intoxilyzer 8000 must be accurately calibrated to properly measure alcohol on a suspect’s breath. Calibration of a breath testing device is the process of inserting a known sample to the machine and instructing the machine to reset itself so that if the identical sample is observed in the future, the machine will report the measurement that is communicated as part of the calibration. Certification, or a calibration check, on the other hand, is merely a check to see if the device is properly measuring samples. A certification, unlike a calibration, does not involve any adjustments to the machine.

Calibration of the Intoxilyzer 8000 is only done at the factory upon introduction of the machine into the field or upon repair. Certifications, or calibration checks, are done at periodic intervals as prescribed by administrative rule. The certification process of the Intoxilyzer 8000 in Oregon is codified in Oregon Administrative Rule 257-030-0170:

Pursuant to ORS 813.160(b)(C), a trained technician of the Oregon State Police shall conduct an accuracy test of approved breath testing equipment and certify the accuracy of the equipment if accuracy test performance is within a range of 0.010 high to 0.020 low of the expected value. The testing can be performed by either an onsite test, or by remote testing via telephone, modem, or Internet connection utilizing a computer. The computerized testing will utilize a security system to ensure the integrity of the scientific testing of the breath test equipment.

The certifications must occur at intervals of not more than 90 days. ORS 813.160(b)(c). The defense attorney should carefully review relevant records regarding the Intoxilyzer 8000 in each case. Intoxilyzer 8000 calibration and certification records can be obtained through the Oregon State Police Forensic Services Division. The records can be obtained online through the Laboratory Online Information System of the Oregon State Police Forensic Services Division. Click on Access IAP Certification and enter the appropriate serial number, location, or other identifying information for the Intoxilyzer 8000 in the particular case. Example Intoxilyzer 8000 certification and calibration records are contained in the Appendix.

Practice Tip: Calibration Check Cross-Examination

The cases are rare the machine certifications are untimely, thus rendering the breath test inadmissible. In most cases, the records reviewed will suggest the breath device was working correctly at the time of the client’s test. The certification records simply reveal that the breath machine was functioning properly at the time of the certification, not at the time of the client’s breath test. The accuracy of the breath machine must be accomplished every 90 days. In many cases, a significant period has elapsed from the time of the certification to the time of the client’s breath tests. This can be exploited in cross-examination of the arresting officer or state’s expert.

3. Specific Challenges to Breath Testing

a. Margin of Error

The Intoxilyzer 8000, as with any measuring device, has a margin of error. Even if the machine is properly calibrated and otherwise in perfect working condition, there is still a built-in inherent error that must be considered in evaluating a breath test result. The acceptable margin of error varies among jurisdictions. Although the acceptable margin of error of the machine is not contained in Oregon’s Intoxilyzer 8000 Operator’s Manual, the state’s expert will testify the machine has a normal +/- 0.01% variance. This admission is critical, especially in cases where the client’s BAC hovers right around the per se legal limit of .08%. How can the jury conclude your client is guilty beyond a reasonable doubt when it is entirely plausible the client was .07% rather than .08% at the time of the chemical test?

The Intoxilyzer 8000 is also calibrated to calculate a person’s breath alcohol based on a “calibration curve” that has been done at the factory, before the machine is placed into service, and/or when there is a repair to the machine. The calibration involves running a series of standard ethanol solutions through a device that mimics the breath test. This device is a sealed bottle containing an alcohol solution maintained at a specified temperature. This alcohol solution is calculated to give a breath alcohol value. The lab technician runs several standard solutions to obtain a calibration curve that should be a straight line.

The American Society of Crime Lab Directors/Laboratory Accreditation Board (ASCLD/LAB) has established rules regarding quantitative testing. These rules specifically require a researched and reported error rate. However, the OSP breath testing is not ASCLD/LAB certified. This means that when the breath instruments are calibrated, it is assumed that they are one hundred percent accurate. (no machine is ever one hundred percent accurate). Also, since the breath machines are not ASCLD certified there is no external oversight or annual reviews regarding calibration or maintenance of the machines.

b. Partition Ratio Error

Since breath testing devices do not measure blood, the breath test result must be converted to a blood alcohol level. Breath measurements are converted to blood alcohol results using principles of Henry’s Law. Henry’s Law maintains that at a constant temperature, the amount of a given gas that dissolves in a given type and volume of liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas in equilibrium with that liquid. In relation to breath testing, this means there is an assumed constant ratio between the concentration of alcohol in the blood and the concentration of alcohol in the alveolar in the lungs. Breath machines use a ratio that assumes the concentration of alcohol in the blood is 2100 times greater than the concentration of alcohol in the breath. This assumed 2100:1 ratio is called the partition ratio.

It should be strongly emphasized that the partition ratio used by evidential breath testing machines is only an average. Ranges have been reported from ratios of about 1:900 to 1:3400, yet the breath test device assumes the concentration of alcohol in a person’s breath is 2100 times greater than it is in the blood. A defendant’s actual partition ratio may deviate substantially from the norm, resulting in inaccurate breath test results. Partition ratios can also vary significantly during drinking episodes. See Alobaidi, T.A.A., & Payne, J.P., “Significance of Variations in Blood-Breath Partition Coefficient of Alcohol,” British Medical Journal, 1976) (documenting partition ratio as low as 834:1) (contained in Appendix). Documentation of partition ratio variance from the Oregon State Police is also contained in the Appendix.

Oregon, unlike some jurisdictions, has generally allowed broad latitude in challenges based on partition ratio. See State v. Allen, 104 Or App 622, 628, 802 2pd 690 (1990) (“If a defendant wishes to offer evidence that his partition coefficient is other than 2100:1, he is free to do so.”). The breath machine assumes an “average” person, but your client may indeed not be average. Practice Tip: Partition Ratio Calculation

Assume your client’s BAC result is .10%. Further assume your client has a partition ratio of 1500:1, not 2100:1 as used by the breath testing device. To determine the subject’s BAC with the partition ratio of 1500:1, first divide the breath test result by the partition ratio used by the machine: .10 / 2100 = .000047619. Then multiply the result by the new partition ratio value: .000047619 x 1500 = .071%. Thus, if a subject registers a .10% on an Intoxilyzer 8000 but the person’s actual partition ratio is 1500:1, the person’s true BAC is. 07%, which is not guilty.

c. Breath and Body Temperature Error

Differences in body and breath temperature can have profound effects on the breath test result. The variation of a person’s breath temperature on the breath result can be explained by Henry’s Law. As previously stated, Henry’s Law says there is a proportional relationship of a compound in the liquid to the concentration of the same compound in the gaseous phase. When the temperature of the liquid phase increases (core body temperature), the concentration of the ethanol in the gaseous phase (breath) will also increase. Studies have shown the average breath temperature hovers around 34 degrees Celsius. Thus, the Intoxilyzer 8000 incorporates a standard breath temperature of 34 degrees Celsius in all breath test calculations.

Normal breath temperature, however, begins at 31 degrees Celsius and rises to 35 degrees Celsius. See International Association for Chemical Testing, volume 9, no. 2 (July 1998) (attached in the Appendix). A higher breath temperature will increase the breath alcohol content, a lower breath temperature will lower the breath alcohol content. Since the breath device does not correct for potential differences in breath temperature of the defendant, the resulting breath test result may grossly overestimate or underestimate true breath alcohol content. For every one-degree difference in breath temperature, studies have indicated a six percent increase in breath alcohol results. Studies have also revealed that breath alcohol content will overestimate blood alcohol concentration by 8.6% for each degree Celsius in deep-core body temperature. Thus, if your client has a fever at the time of the breath test, the breath test will overestimate the client’s actual blood alcohol concentration.

Practice Tip: Failure To Correct For Breath Temperature Variations

The Drager evidential breath machine used in Alabama has the capability of measuring the breath temperature of a suspect and correcting the breath alcohol concentration to the reference of 34 degrees Celsius. The Intoxilyzer 8000 has no such capability. Although the state’s expert may deny knowledge of Alabama’s device and its breath temperature correction capabilities, the defense expert can be questioned regarding the technology and the fact that Oregon has refused to adopt this technology in its breath testing process.

d. Hematocrit

Differences in blood composition provide another source of error in breath-alcohol analysis. Whole blood consists of a mixture of solid particles (red and white blood cells) suspended in a liquid. The liquid portion is called plasma. Hematocrit is the ratio of the solid components of blood to the liquid components of blood. A hematocrit of 55 percent means that a person’s blood contains 55 percent solid particles and 45 percent plasma. The average hematocrit for males is 46.2 percent with a range of 43–49 percent. The average hematocrit for females if 40.6 percent with a range of 35–45.4 percent. See Taylor and Oberman, Drunk Driving Defense 6th ed., 439-440 (2006).

How does hematocrit affect breath alcohol content? An individual with a higher hematocrit has blood consisting of more solids (red blood cells) and less plasma (water). Since alcohol is soluble in water, plasma will contain a higher concentration of alcohol than will solid particles of the blood. And the less plasma in blood (i.e., the higher hematocrit), the more concentrated the alcohol will be in the existing plasma. Thus an individual with a higher hematocrit will have a higher concentration of alcohol in plasma than an individual with a lower hematocrit. The breath test administered to an individual with higher hematocrit will reflect a higher breath alcohol content than the same test administered to an individual with a lower hematocrit. This is because the person with the higher hematocrit has a higher concentration of alcohol in the air above the alveolar sacs. Studies have shown that with breath tests, normal variation in the hematocrits of subjects can produce errors in the 10–14 percent range. Labianca, Dominick, “The Chemical Basis of the Breathalyzer,” Journal of Chemistry Education, vol. 76, no. 3, p. 261, (March 1990).

Practice Tip: Sample Hematocrit Calculation

The effect of hematocrit on a breath alcohol result can be computed with relative simplicity. That is because the partition ratio of 2100:1 used in breath testing presumes an average hematocrit of 45 percent. If the client’s hematocrit is 53 percent, the breath test result is computed by multiplying it by 45/53. Assuming a breath test result of .09, the true BAC is determined as follows: .09 x 45/53 = .07 percent. Not Guilty.

Practice Tip: Client’s Actual Hematocrit

If resources allow, the client should consider getting a routine blood test, which will contain the
client’ hematocrit value.

e. Breathing Pattern Defenses

A suspect’s breathing technique prior to taking a breath test can cause fluctuations in breath testing results. Studies have shown that hyperventilation “lowers the breath alcohol concentration by as much as 20 percent compared with a single moderate inhalation and forced exhalation used as control tests.” A.W. Jones, “Physiological Aspects of Breath-Alcohol Measurement,” Alcohol, Drugs, and Driving, vol. 6, no. 2 (1982). Similarly, holding breath for a short period of time (20 seconds) before exhalation increases the alcohol concentration in exhaled air by 15 percent. If counsel has a client with diminished lung capacity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, or other breathing related problems, an expert should be retained for consultation.

f. Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)

Medical conditions may affect the reliability of reported breath results. It is commonly understood that breath test results are converted to blood alcohol levels based on assumptions of an average person. Your client may suffer from various medical conditions that disrupt the average assumptions of the breath test. One such disease is gastroesophageal reflux disorder (GERD). GERD is a condition in which the stomach contents (food or liquid) leak backwards from the stomach into the esophagus (the tube from the mouth to the stomach). This action can irritate the esophagus, causing heartburn, acid reflux, chest pain, difficulty swallowing and other symptoms. GERD is typically a chronic condition that can be partially treated through medication. As part of a thorough review of the client’s medical condition, counsel should review whether his client has been diagnosed with GERD or suffers from symptoms consistent with GERD. A client suffering from GERD may result in an elevated breath test result, as the mouth may be contaminated with stomach contents or gases that are introduced into the breath machine. If alcohol is in the stomach at the time of the breath test and your client had acid reflux shortly before the test, the test may be falsely elevated. This results from the breath machine detecting alcohol from your client’s stomach rather than lungs. Consider calling your client’s physician regarding your client’s condition.

Practice Tip

The prosecution will argue that either the breath testing unit’s slope sensor detector will recognize a sharp increase/rise between the first breath test and the second breath test, as well as the difference between the two breath tests being outside of the +/-10% range to justify that this medical issue would have been detected. The slope sensor unit is not infallible and may not recognize this rise in the breath test. No experimental tests have been performed by the Oregon State Police Forensic Division to address this issue.

g. Diabetes

Diabetes is a metabolic disease that reduces or eliminates the body’s natural production of insulin in the pancreas. The resulting high blood sugar produces symptoms of polyuria (frequent urination), polydipsia (increased thirst), and polyphagia (increased hunger). The effect of a diabetic client on breath testing can result from the client being in a state of ketoacidosis, which may affect the outcome of the breath test.

h. Dentures

Dentures, denture adhesives, and bridgework may trap alcohol in the mouth that could interfere with breath testing. This defense may be difficult to mount, however, since studies have shown a 20-minute observation period does not significantly interfere with a breath test. Harding, P., McMurray, M., Laessig, R., Simley, D., Correll, P., Tsunehiro, J., “The Effect of Dentures and Denture Adhesives on Mouth Alcohol Retention.” It should be noted that Oregon requires only a 15-minute observation, so the results of the study have may limited applicability to a denture defense. The removal of dentures is not required before administration of the breath test.

i. Radio Frequency Interference

Breath machines can be affected by the presence of foreign radio waves. Examples of radio frequency interference include police radios, cellular phones, and police radar units. The Intoxilyzer 8000 is equipped with an “RFI” detector. The Operator’s Guide states, “This exception occurs when a radio or cellular phone transmission is detected near the instrument. The instruments will display “RFI Detected” and print “*Invalid Test – RFI Detected. The test is not complete and can be restarted.” In most circumstances, the machine operator will take corrective action, restart the test, and ultimately obtain a valid test from the suspect. If “RFI” is an issue in your case, however, a breath testing expert can provide testimony regarding the lack of reliability of the “RFI” detector, including problems detecting certain types of RFI as well as problems with calibration of the detector.

j. Mouth Alcohol

The presence of mouth alcohol is a major cause of errors in breath testing results. The potential presence of mouth alcohol is precisely why the breath test operator must strictly adhere to the pre-test observation period required under OAR 257-030-0070 (2)(a-b). The Intoxilyzer machine presumes the breath sample is derived from deep alveolar from the lungs, not mouth alcohol. The presence of mouth alcohol may be present on a suspect as a result of recent alcohol consumption, vomiting, regurgitation, or belching. While the police officer will always testify the breath machine will detect the presence of mouth alcohol, the slope detector in the breath device is inherently unreliable in detecting the presence of mouth alcohol.

k. Mouthwash and Breath Spray

Mouthwashes such as Listerine and Scope contain a high percentage of alcohol. Consumption of mouthwash or breath sprays immediately before the breath test may cause an elevated reading on the breath machine. The mouth alcohol dissipates quickly, however, and waiting at least fifteen minutes after use before the breath test will generally eliminate the effect of mouth alcohol or breath spray on the breath test result.

B. Alcohol Absorption and Elimination

Once ingested, alcohol is absorbed, distributed, and eliminated simultaneously throughout the body. Blood alcohol concentration will initially increase after consumption, peak at a certain level, and gradually decrease once the peak level is reached.

Alcohol is absorbed throughout the body via the skin, lungs, or the gastrointestinal tract. Most absorption occurs in the small intestine. The rate of absorption of alcohol depends on many variables, some of which include food consumption, environmental factors such as gender and ethnicity, the type of alcohol consumed, the pattern of drinking, and the physiological state of the drinker. Experiments have shown the elapsed time from the end of alcohol intake to peak blood alcohol concentration can range from 14 to 138 minutes. Dubowski, “Absorption, Distribution and Elimination of Alcohol,” Journal of Studies on Alcohol, Supplement, No. 10, p. 99 (July 1985).

Alcohol is eliminated from the body primarily through metabolism. Alcohol dehydrogenase in the liver is the enzyme that is primarily responsible for 90 percent of alcohol metabolism. The remaining alcohol is excreted from the lungs, urine and sweat. Just as with absorption rates, elimination rates of alcohol can vary highly among individuals, and depend on variables such as food consumption, environmental factors, pattern of drinking, and the alcohol tolerance of the drinker.

State experts typically assume the subject has an average rate of elimination (usually between .015 to .020 g/dL/hr)(0.01 to 0.025 g/dL or %). Elimination rates of alcohol, however, can vary widely among individuals. Widmark’s studies reported a mean elimination rate of .016 g/dL/hr. Studies have, however, documented elimination rates outside this range. For an alcoholic drinker, the elimination rate of alcohol can be upwards of .030g/dL/hr. A.W. Jones, “Disappearance Rate of Ethanol from the Blood of Human Subjects: Implications in Forensic Toxicology,” Journal of Forensic Sciences, Vol. 38, No. 1, p. 104-118 (January 1993).

C. Widmark Calculations and Retrograde Extrapolation

Prosecutors and defense attorneys frequently employ forensic toxicologists to testify regarding a suspect’s blood alcohol level at a particular time. Erik M.P. Widmark (1889-1945), a Swedish physician and expert in alcohol kinetics, developed a mathematical formula for predicting alcohol levels after alcohol consumption. This section is designed to provide the defense attorney with an arsenal of tools to challenge the state breath alcohol expert’s conclusions regarding a defendant’s drinking history. Knowledge of the Widmark formula and its variables will also assist the defense attorney in preparation and examination of his or her own expert witness.

The state toxicologist will often declare that the amount of alcohol the defendant claims he consumed cannot be true based on the breath test result. The state’s expert, using a simplified version of the Widmark formula, will attempt to calculate the number of drinks the defendant must have consumed based on the evidential breath test result. It should be no surprise that the opinion of the state’s expert is generally drastically different than the reported alcohol consumption by the defendant.

In addition to calculating the number of drinks the defendant has consumed, the Widmark formula is useful in conducting retrograde extrapolation calculations. Retrograde extrapolation is the mathematical process by which someone’s blood alcohol concentration at the time of driving is estimated by projecting backwards from a later chemical test. In Oregon, it is unlawful to drive a motor vehicle if BAC is .08 percent or greater at the time of driving, not the time of the chemical test. In cases where the driver is below .08 percent at the time of the chemical test, the state toxicologist will rely on retrograde extrapolation to put the driver at .08 percent or higher at the time of driving.

The use of retrograde extrapolation to determine BAC is fraught with reliance on assumptions and statistical averages and is scientifically unsound. However, the defense attorney must be prepared to defend against the state’s expert by strenuously challenging the assumptions underlying retrograde extrapolation and Widmark calculations.

1. Understanding the Widmark Formula and Its Variables

Estimating blood alcohol concentrations at a previous point of time based on a later chemical test (retrograde extrapolation) is a crude exercise in science. Prosecutorial attempts to conduct retrograde extrapolation calculations require analysis of many factors, assumptions and variables, many of which are simply not available in a given case. At a minimum, the following facts are necessary to conduct retrograde extrapolation with even a remote degree of accuracy and precision:

  • Time drinking began

  • Time drinking ended

  • Pattern of drinking

  • Time of driving

  • Time of chemical test

  • Weight

  • Gender

  • Elimination rate of alcohol (burnoff rate)

  • Widmark factor (“r” factor)

  • Assumption that one hundred percent of the alcohol consumed has been absorbed.

A person’s blood alcohol concentration is proportional to the total body water content of the person. The Widmark “r” factor is the ratio of total body water and blood water content. Although water content differs between individuals, Widmark calculations use an average for men and women. The Widmark average “r” factor in men is .68 and for women is .55.

For purposes of the Widmark equation, the number of fluid ounces is 100-proof liquor or a drink equivalent. Scientists must determine the approximate ethanol content of alcoholic beverages. A 12-ounce beer, 4-ounce glass of wine, and one ounce of 100-proof liquor each contain .05 ounces of ethanol. A shot is approximately 1.25 ounces, a pint is 16 ounces; a fifth is 25.6 shots, a quart, 32 shots. See Bartell and Imobersteg, Attacking and Defending Drunk Driving Tests (2008).

Alcohol and body weight must be converted to grams to utilize the Widmark equation. The number of ounces consumed is converted to milliliters (mL) by multiplying ounces by 29.6. The proof of alcohol is expressed as a percentage, and the entire amount is then multiplied by the specific gravity of alcohol:

[(ounces)(29.6mL/ounce)] % Alcohol x .079 g/mL = dose in grams

The Widmark formula can be stated as follows:

BAC = (# of fluid ounces)(% alcohol)(29.5 m/L/fluid ounce)(.79 gm/mL) X 100

(Weight in lb.)(“r” factor)(16 oz/lb)(28.3 gm/oz)

D. Challenging Widmark Calculations and Retrograde Extrapolation in Trial

In a divided opinion, the Oregon Supreme Court upheld the admissibility of retrograde extrapolation testimony in State v. Eumana–Moranchel, 352 Or 1, 277 P3d 549 (2012). However, when the state offers an expert on blood alcohol dissipation and retrograde extrapolation, it must first make a foundational showing that the expert’s methods were scientifically valid pursuant to the standards set forth in Brown/O’Key. State v. Whitmore (2013).

The Whitmore ruling opened the door for counsel to challenge the scientific validity of retrograde extrapolation in pretrial evidentiary hearings. In State v. Ohotto, 261 Or App 70 (2014), the court elucidated that testimony regarding the likely amount of drinks a person consumed to get a certain blood alcohol content is scientific evidence. It requires “a formulaic calculation derived from scientific understandings of physiological processes that cannot be achieved through reading a training manual, conducting routine DUII investigations in the course of law enforcement, and attending an NHTSA course.” Here, the arresting officer in a DUII case testified that defendant would have had to have imbibed way more than the amount of drinks to which he testified at trial in order to get to a .17. Such testimony was scientific evidence on which the officer was not qualified to opine. The error was prejudicial because the case essentially amounted to a credibility contest. The officer arrested defendant at a campsite, to which defendant had driven an hour earlier. Defendant testified that he had a few drinks since arriving at the campsite. The officer was essentially testifying that the defendant had to be lying about how many drinks he’d had since he drove.

In State v. Trujillo, 271 Or App 785 (2015), the court affirmed that “Retrograde extrapolation” using the Widmark formula is scientifically valid evidence under Brown/O’Key for the purposes of OEC 702:

• It is generally accepted in the field

• It is admitted through an officer that has been trained to administer and record the test

• It’s used in other alcohol-related litigation and other disciplines, like pharmacology

• It’s methodology has been widely tested outside of the courtroom

• Using a range for elimination rates serves as a check against a high rate of error

• There are many studies on elimination, absorption and the Widmark formula

• It’s not novel—the first studies were published in 1932

• There’s a relatively low degree of subjectivity and a high degree of transparency in the formula

State v. Lusareta, 270 Or App 102 (2015), held that where a qualified forensic scientist from the state police testified to the following in a DUII case, it was admissible as scientific evidence for him to testify that a breath test within two hours of driving is very unlikely to overestimate BAC:

1. Based on a study out of Washington, a breath test within two hours of a stop, generally speaking, will not overestimate the BAC at the time of the stop.

2. While there can be variation, alcohol is absorbed quickly and, based on another study, people reach 80% of their peak within the first 15 minutes after drinking.

3. Thus, estimating conservatively and ascribing all unknown factors to the defendant’s benefit, a BAC of .15 two hours after the stop would mean that defendant had at least a BAC of .12 at the time of driving.

The court found that while the testimony clearly was scientific evidence (given by a forensic expert based on studies), it was not an application of a scientific technique like retrograde extrapolation. It was really nothing more than the straightforward application of the two simple conclusions from the studies. Since the methodology of the studies was undisputed and the articles were peer-reviewed in a well-studied field, they were scientifically valid and the testimony was properly admitted.

State v. Hedgpeth, 365 Or 724 (2019), held that common knowledge that blood alcohol concentrations fall over time is insufficient to make any reasonable inference about a defendant’s blood alcohol at the time of driving. Id. at 739.

In Hedgpeth, the only evidence in the record was that the defendant registered .09 BAC two hours after he drove and consumed no alcohol in the interim. The state did not put on any evidence when or how much the defendant drank before the stop; no evidence as to why the officer arrested defendant for DUII after stopping him for not wearing a motorcycle helmet; no evidence that defendant had any signs of intoxication before or after the stop; and no evidence of blood alcohol dissipation rates.

The court affirmed the Court of Appeals’ decision reversing defendant’s conviction. Although the court did not specify the exact evidence necessary to withstand a motion for judgment of acquittal, the court concluded that something more than common knowledge is necessary to connect BAC results to the BAC at the time of driving:

“Here, the only evidence was that defendant’s blood alcohol level was over the legal limit when measured almost two hours after he drove, and that he had consumed no alcohol in the interim. In the absence of any other evidence, the jury’s common knowledge of the generic (and incomplete) proposition that alcohol dissipates from the blood over time is not sufficient to constitute the ‘[s]omething more’ that—as we said in Eumana-Moranchel—is necessary for a non-speculative connection between the BAC test results and defendant’s blood alcohol concentration when he was driving approximately two hours earlier. See 352 Or at 9.” Id. at 744.

The facts of Hedgpeth are unusual in that the state did not present any evidence of defendant’s impairment. Although the court did not suggest that evidence of alcohol and dissipation rates is necessary, Hedgpeth provides good ammunition to challenge cases where the state fails to connect the dots between the BAC result to the BAC at time of driving.

If results of the Widmark formula and retrograde extrapolation calculations are damaging to the defense case and deemed admissible by the trial court, the defense attorney must vigorously challenge the foundations for the calculations. Just as the breath testing device relies on a number of variables and assumptions in calculating blood alcohol content, so do Widmark and retrograde extrapolation calculations. Dispelling the aura of science attached to these calculations by prosecutors and state toxicologists is critical in convincing the jury the defendant is not guilty. Unless the jury is educated by the defense attorney and defense expert, a conviction is inevitable.

Challenges to the Widmark calculations and retrograde extrapolation routinely focus on (1) the lack of qualifications and knowledge of the state toxicologist regarding Widmark and use of retrograde extrapolation, (2) cross-examination regarding the variables inherent in the Widmark formula, (3) cross-examination using learned treatises written by leading experts in the study of alcohol, and (4) previous court rulings on the state’s expert against retrograde extrapolation.

1. Challenge the Qualifications of the State Expert

The experts employed by the state to testify in court regarding blood alcohol analysis typically hold no advanced degrees and have limited knowledge of the Widmark formula. The version of the formula utilized by state’s experts is a simplification of the actual formula. Additionally, the state’s expert frequently does not have the necessary information about your client’s situation to conduct retrograde extrapolation with any precision.

2. Cross-Examination on the Variables Underlying the Widmark Formula

The state’s expert conducting retrograde extrapolation should be vigorously cross-examined regarding many variables and assumptions utilized in Widmark and retrograde extrapolation questions. Often the facts necessary to conduct calculations are not available or inaccurate, including body weight, food consumption, drinking history and alcohol concentration, etc. In addition, the state’s expert assumes the driver has an “average” rate of elimination (usually .015 g/dL/hr) and absorption is complete at the time of driving. The state’s expert will use a range of 0.01 to 0.025%/hr in their calculation. The client may still be absorbing alcohol at the time of the chemical test, which means the blood alcohol may have been lower at the time of driving than at the time of the chemical test (Rising Blood Alcohol Defense). In addition, absorption and elimination rates can vary widely among individuals and rely on factors such as food consumption and tolerance to alcohol.

3. Cross-Examine the State Expert with Learned Treatises

Leading scientists in the field of alcohol have strongly criticized the forensic reliability of retrograde extrapolation. Several peer-reviewed journal articles written by these leading authorities can provide excellent material for cross-examination of the state’s expert. These materials demonstrate that retrograde extrapolation is not generally accepted in the scientific community as a means of determining blood alcohol content.

Oregon rules of evidence authorize the impeachment of an expert witness with learned treatises. OEC Rule 706 provides:

Upon cross-examination, an expert witness may be questioned concerning statements contained in a published treatise, periodical or pamphlet on a subject of history, medicine or other science or art if the treatise, periodical or pamphlet is established as a reliable authority. A treatise, periodical or pamphlet may be established as a reliable authority by the testimony or admission of the witness, by other expert testimony or by judicial notice. Statements contained in a treatise, periodical or pamphlet established as a reliable authority may be used for purposes of impeachment but may not be introduced as substantive evidence. [1999 c.85 §2]

The forensic scientists called by the state to testify regarding retrograde extrapolation are merely technicians. They rarely conduct research or draft peer-reviewed articles. They will testify that based on their training and experience, retrograde extrapolation is a reliable and acceptable method of estimating blood alcohol concentrations. While the state expert may deny knowledge of the various studies and peer-reviewed articles regarding breath testing and retrograde extrapolation, most will be familiar with Kurt Dubowski and A.W. Jones and consider them reliable sources in the field of forensic alcohol analysis. If the results of retrograde extrapolation are unfavorable to the facts of your case, consider impeaching the state’s expert with statements from these pioneers in forensic alcohol analysis. However, it is always preferable to have your own expert witness discuss the scientific unreliability of retrograde extrapolation (but, of course, an expert may not always be available for your case).

Regarding the other factors, there was only one test of Mata’s BAC, and it occurred over two hours after the alleged offense. Add to that the fact that McDougall did not know one single personal characteristic of Mata — he did not know whether Mata had eaten anything that night and if so, how much; how much Mata had had to drink; what Mata had been drinking; when Mata’s last drink was; the length of Mata’s drinking spree; or even Mata’s weight. III. Blood Alcohol Analysis

A. Suppression of Warrantless Blood Draw

Can you suppress the results of a blood test taken in violation of the implied consent laws? If your client’s blood was drawn without consent, without a warrant, and outside of a hospital, the answer is yes—though it’s still an uphill battle. ORS 813.140 establishes that police may administer a chemical test in two circumstances: (1) if the individual expressly consents, or (2) without the person’s consent if the officer has probable cause for DUII and the person is unconscious or otherwise incapable of consenting. If a blood draw is taken in violation of that statutory provision, under ORS 813.320(2) it is inadmissible unless it was drawn while the person was hospitalized and receiving medical treatment, or pursuant to a warrant. But what if the blood draw was performed without a warrant and outside of a hospital?

In the past, the Oregon courts have treated ORS 813.320(1) as a suppression statute—that is, it renders the blood draw evidence inadmissible. E.g., State v. Moylett, 313 Or 540, 836 P2d 1329 (1992). There is substantial legislative history from the enactment of ORS 813.320 that supports that interpretation. The question is whether ORS 813.320’s statutory suppression survived the enactment of ORS 136.432, which states that evidence obtained in violation of a statute will not be excluded unless (1) the evidence is irrelevant or otherwise inadmissible, or (2) exclusion is required by the state or federal constitution or the evidence code.

In State v. Thompson-Seed, 162 Or App 483, 489-491, 986 P2d 732 (1999), the Court of Appeals held that that the legislature did not intend ORS 136.432 to implicitly repeal other statutes that require suppression of evidence; rather, it intended to prevent courts from creating new exclusionary rules. After that decision, the Oregon legislature added subsection (2) of ORS 813.320. Essentially, the bill was framed as allowing a small subset of test results to be admitted in DUII cases, while all other results would continue to be excluded. The legislative history and later interpretations of the bill support reading ORS 813.320(2) as a valid suppression statute. See State v. Snyder, 337 Or 410, 425-26, 97 P3d 1181 (2004); State v. Cabanilla, 351 Or 622, 638, 273 P3d 125 (2012).

So, although the Oregon courts have not yet suppressed blood draw evidence that violated only ORS 813.320(2) but did not violate the Constitution, the statute itself, the legislative history, and later case law support suppression of blood draw evidence taken in violation of the implied consent laws. It is an issue worth preserving in the trial court.

B. Hospital Analysis (Indirect Method)

Due to time constraints and cost issues, the most common method for the analysis of ethanol in clinical laboratories is the enzymatic method. Blood (serum) is placed onto an auto analyzer and systematically tested for a variety of clinically-related compounds including alcohol. The results are typically reported as milligrams per deciliter (mg/dl) or grams per deciliter (g/dl).

Note: Forensic results are reported as % or g/100 ml. Example: 0.10% is the same as 100 mg/dl or 0.10 g/dl.

In the reaction, alcohol is oxidized to acetaldehyde by ADH (alcohol dehydrogenase) in the presence of a coenzyme—nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD). The alcohol reduces the NAD to NADH. The NADH is measured spectrophotometrically at 340 nm. The concentration of alcohol is directly proportional to the rate of change of NADH.

ADH catalyst

Advantages of this indirect technique:

• short analytical time

• cost effective

• multiple clinical analytes tested at the same time

• standards and controls utilized during the testing are certified blood-alcohol mixtures.

Disadvantages of this indirect technique:

• Blood (serum or plasma) is 10–20 percent higher in alcohol concentration as compared to whole blood.

• Sample is tested only one time (no duplicate analysis).

• In the absence of alcohol, common medications will cause the chemical reaction to produce NADH, which can create erroneous results (e.g., aspirin, ibuprofen).

• In the absence of alcohol, trauma will cause the chemical reaction to produce NADH

• Introduction of IV solutions containing lactate (i.e., Ringer’s Lactate) will cause the chemical reaction to produce NADH.

• No internal standard utilized during the analysis.

• False positives have been reported in the literature.

• Blood comparison to forensic analysis reveals an 84% disagreement.

• Blood samples are generally destroyed after several days.

• Unable to discriminate between other volatiles and alcohol metabolites (e.g., acetaldehyde).

Trial issues:

• Most hospitals will attempt to have either the physician or the laboratory manager testify to the laboratory results. Many physicians are not aware of the analytical process for alcohol testing. For this type of testing, many hospitals require only a high school diploma and additional hospital training for the specific analyzer. The laboratory technician that performed the testing should be subpoenaed for testimony.

• Chain of custody in a clinical setting should be addressed.

• Many hospitals will add a disclaimer stating that the blood alcohol analysis should not be used for medico-legal purposes.

C. Forensic Analysis (Direct Method)

The forensic analysis for alcohol involves the use of a gas chromatograph-headspace (GC-HS) analyzer. A blood sample (typically whole blood) is placed into a vial along with an internal standard and sealed. The sample is then heated until the components of interest (ethanol and internal standard) establish an equilibrium within the vial. At this point, the concentration determined in the headspace (area above the liquid portion) is proportional to the concentration of the ethanol in the blood (Henry’s Law). An aliquot of the gaseous phase above the blood sample is withdrawn and injected into the gas chromatograph. After injection of the sample into the gas chromatograph, the instrument separates the volatile compounds potentially present in the sample (ethanol, acetone, methanol, and acetaldehyde). At the gas chromatograph’s detector, each volatile compound produces an electrical current that is measured and displayed as a peak on a chart. The area under the peak is measured and calculated for alcohol concentration.

Advantages of this direct technique:

• No interferences with common medications.

• Samples are run in duplicate to ensure accuracy.

• Internal standard utilized with each blood sample to minimize any variations in sampling.

• Samples are generally whole blood samples, which minimizes any variations commonly seen with serum blood-alcohol issues.

• Samples are considered as evidence and not destroyed.

Disadvantages of this direct technique:

• Samples are manually placed on an automated analyzer, samples can be mixed up during this manual application (see trial issues).

IV. Introduction: Urine and Blood Toxicology

Many biological specimens may be tested for drugs of abuse. Blood, saliva, semen, fingernails, hair, meconium, sweat, breast milk, as well as fly larvae all have potential as drug-testing matrices. Blood is the useful matrix if the intent of the testing is to relate drug concentrations to pharmacological effects or potential recent use. Fingernails and hair yield the detection of long-term or chronic drug use.

Oregon has chosen to use urine as the specimen of choice for impaired driving situations. The advantages of a urine specimen include the collection of evidence vs. the time of collection, noninvasive collection process, automated testing, the high concentrations of the parent drug or its metabolite for detection purposes, and the relatively low cost of testing. The limitation of using urine as a testing matrix is that drug concentrations cannot be related to impairment. Also, drugs and metabolites may be present in urine for many days after use, consequently not imparting any adverse effects. Unlike blood collection, urine can be subjected to adulteration or tampering. Such adulteration may include substitution of specimen, dilution with water, or addition of substances to alter the testing. Compounds or products that have been used to adulterate samples include alcohol, ammonia, vitamin C, Visine, lemon juice, peroxide, detergents, goldenseal root, Stealth, the “Wizzinator,” and bleach. These products are designed to adversely affect the chemical analysis of the urine sample. In the forensic setting, precautions have been taken in the collection and analytical process to avoid these issues.

The analytical process consists of a presumptive screening test and a nonquantitated confirmation test. If the identification of the drug and/or its metabolites meets the forensic division’s criteria, the drug is reported in the formal report.

A. Collection

Officers are provided with a standardized urine collection kit. This kit includes a sterilized urine cup, thermal strip, evidence seals, and an outer box for transport. In that a direct observation is not required, the defendant hands the filled specimen cup to the officer. The officer should make a notation of the thermal strip temperature (normal range is considered to be 900 – 1000F) to do an initial evaluation of potential adulteration. The officer then labels the urine cup and outer packaging prior to transport of the evidence to the forensic laboratory.

Trial issue: In most cases, the report indicates the urine was labeled as having been drawn from…. However, in some cases the report indicates that the urine was “reportedly drawn from….” The term “reportedly drawn from” is an indication that there was an omission in the labeling process. For example:

• The urine sample vial was unlabeled/unmarked.

• The container with the urine sample was only marked with the agency’s case number.

• No markings were noted on either the urine samples or the outer packaging. The associated name and agency case number was only on the Oregon State Police Form 49 Evidence Transfer Form.

B. Analytical Process

Preliminary screening of the urine sample is performed by a two step process. The first step is the enzyme immunoassay analysis (EMIT). EMIT assays use an enzyme-linked antigen that relies on the drug or its metabolite converting nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) to NADH. NADH is measured spectrophotometrically at 340 nm. The chemical analysis involves the presumptive identification of various drug categories. In that this testing is considered presumptive, the forensic laboratory must utilize a more specific analysis for confirmation. Each EMIT drug category has an assigned cutoff value. If the chemical reaction rate exceeds the cutoff calibrator reaction rate, the test is reported as positive. An example of the analysis is provided below:

Creatinine: Creatinine is a normal body excretion. When urine becomes dilute, creatinine levels (as well as drug levels) are reduced.

Specific gravity: Specific gravity of a substance is the ratio of the density of a substance compared to the density of water. Dilute urine has a lower specific gravity.

pH: Ingestion or addition of materials to alter the normal body pH depresses the enzymatic reaction rate. Significant alterations may also affect the stability of certain drugs.

Oxidant: Oxidizing agents such as hydrogen peroxide, chromates, bleach, nitrites and commercially available adulterants are not found in normal urine.

The limitations of this process are as follows:

The analysis is only for drug categories at levels that exceed the calibrator’s cutoff value.

• Many drugs and/or medications and their metabolites cannot be detected by this method.

• Use of contaminated or expired calibration reagents can produce false positive tests.

• False positive tests can be the result of common medications.

• A negative response means that the drug or metabolite is either absent or its presence fell below the calibrator value.

The second screening test is called liquid chromatography time-of-flight mass spectrometry, or LC/Q-ToF/MS. The LC/Q-ToF/MS was implemented to expedite the screening process and to eventually eliminate the EMIT test. However, at this time, both types of screening tests are being performed. The advantage of the LC/Q-ToF/MS is that it has the ability to detect more compounds than the EMIT analysis.

The confirmation process is by a technique called gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC/MS). The gas chromatograph portion of the instrument is utilized to separate organic compounds. The mass spectrometer is used for molecular identification. The fragmentation pattern produced by the mass spectrometer section allows for the isolation of a single mass to charge ratio (m/z).

In that a urine sample cannot be directly injected into this instrument, a specific extraction methodology must be utilized. In most cases, extractions that incorporate both acidic and basic drugs will suffice. Complicating the analytical process is the fact that many drugs or their metabolites must be subjected to additional chemical treatment so that the instrument can “visualize” the drugs or their metabolites. For example:

• The “inactive” metabolite of marijuana (carboxy-THC) must be hydrolyzed and derivatized before the instrument is able to “visualize” the material. In addition, the instrument must be set in a different analytical mode, called SIM (Selected Ion Monitoring), to identify the extremely low level of this inactive metabolite in the urine.

• The date rape drug called GHB (gamma-hydroxybutyrate) must undergo a specific extraction and derivatization along with the SIM mode for identification. Additionally, GHB is an endogenous compound in human urine that occurs at levels approaching 10 mcg/mL.

• Vicks inhalers contain methamphetamine. A specific derivatization/treatment of the urine extract must be performed to separate out a Vicks inhaler product versus a street product of methamphetamine.

• Opiates and their analogues may be present in urine as either the free compound, and/or their corresponding conjugated metabolites. In order to visualize these “conjugated metabolites”, the analyst must hydrolyze the extracted sample prior to injection into the instrument.

After extractions and any chemical treatment to the urine extracts, the samples are manually loaded onto an auto sampling device on the instrument. Since the samples are not quantitated for drug levels, the forensic division implements a protocol to prevent samples from contaminating each other (due to the instrument’s syringe transferring materials from each sample vial, called carry over). The protocol suggests that the analyst place a blank sample between each specimen to ensure sample integrity

Reporting: a minimum of two independent tests for controlled substances and/or medications is required before a specimen can be reported as positive/confirmed. If the initial immunoassay test or LC/Q-ToF/MS is negative, the analyst can use two separate extractions on the gas chromatograph mass spectrometer to facilitate a positive report. The report may also indicate; “Unable to detect a controlled substance or common pharmaceuticals. The urine toxicology procedures of this laboratory are designed to identify and confirm most controlled substances and many common pharmaceuticals. Trial Issues Continued:

The question of blood vs. urine analysis and the significance of each specimen is commonly addressed at trial. The forensic division’s comment is that urine is the only biological specimen currently authorized. It should be noted that previous OSP protocols allowed the testing of blood. Even though the medical examiner’s OSP testing allows for the testing of blood, OSP has deleted the term “blood” in recent protocols.

The determination of alcohol in urine is possible with the use of the gas chromatograph-head space instrument as described above. However, special precautions must be recognized in the interpretation of the results.

Urine concentrations of alcohol are often used to estimate blood alcohol concentrations. It has been determined that the urine/blood ratio of 1.3 applies and provides a valid estimate in most cases. However, when a urine sample is taken, the alcohol results can range from the same as the blood alcohol concentration to as high as two times the blood alcohol concentration. To properly apply the urine/blood ratio concept, the individual must void a specimen, wait approximately 15–20 minutes, then void a second sample. The second sample’s result is mathematically adjusted for the urine/blood ratio.

After several hours, the metabolism of alcohol in the blood or urine will sufficiently deplete the detectable alcohol levels so that analysis for ethyl alcohol is not useful. Analysis of alcohol’s metabolites (EtG and EtS) can determine if alcohol was consumed in the last twelve hours. Since this analysis is performed by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry, the sensitivity of the instrument will also detect consumption of common products such as mouthwash. In addition, under certain circumstances, the analysis can also produce positive results due to dermal exposure to other alcohol-related chemicals.
