I. Sentencing and Related Suspensions

Since 1983, Oregon law has provided that those convicted of DUII are subject to certain mandatory sentencing provisions, including a minimum of two days in jail or 80 hours of community service, a requirement of alcohol evaluation and treatment, minimum fines based on the number of prior convictions and the BAC, and license suspensions based on number of prior suspensions. However, this does not reduce the importance of advocacy at sentencing, where the terms of the sentence can dictate where your client can live and whether she can work for years to come.

Link to 2019 edition of DUII statutes, chapter 813, Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicants.

A. Plea Bargaining Limited

1. ORS 813.170 Limits Plea-bargaining in DUII Cases

The statute reads as follows:

(1) Notwithstanding ORS 135.405 to 135.445, a person charged with the offense of driving under the influence of intoxicants shall not be allowed to plead “guilty” or “no contest” to any other offense in exchange for a dismissal of the offense charged. No district attorney or city attorney shall make any motion and no judge shall enter any order in derogation of this section. This section does not prohibit diversion as provided under ORS 813.200.

(2) Notwithstanding ORS 135.881 to 135.901, a person charged with the offense of driving under the influence of intoxicants shall not be allowed to enter into any program of supervised performance or diversion except as provided under ORS 813.200.

2. Oregon Attorney General Opinion No. 7493 (77–78 AG 1148, 1152–1153)

The Oregon Attorney General answered the question as to whether a prosecutor may later amend a citation charging a driver with driving under the influence of intoxicants to reckless or careless driving. The opinion reads in pertinent part:

A prosecutor is not required to proceed on a charge just because an arresting officer based his arrest on that charge. There may have been probable cause for the arrest, but insufficient evidence or other factors may persuade the prosecutor not to proceed with that charge. Under the prosecutorial discretion discussed above, a district attorney may elect to proceed on another charge, such as reckless or careless driving, if he has probable cause for such a prosecution. There is, however, one significant restriction on prosecutorial discretion with regard to the charge of driving under the influence of intoxicants. 813.170(1) provides: A person charged with the offense of driving under the influence of intoxicants shall not be allowed to plead guilty or no contest to any other offense charged. No district attorney shall make any motion and no judge shall enter any order in derogation of this section.

ORS 813.170(2) deals with plea agreement situations, where a dismissal of a DUII charge is offered in return for forfeiture of bail or a guilty or no contest plea to another offense. The statute does not involve non-plea bargaining situations where a prosecutor decides to drop or change a charge solely based on analysis of available evidence and the strength of the particular case, without any agreement by defendant to plead guilty on another charge. This statute was not intended to force a district attorney to prosecute a DUII charge merely because an arrest is made on that charge.

Practice Tip

As a matter of good practice some district attorneys, when confronted with a DUII charge that is not supported by sufficient evidence, may choose to unilaterally dismiss the charge and file a charge of reckless or careless driving. In those situations, the aforementioned Attorney General’s Opinion is seen by district attorneys and judges as permitting subsequent plea bargaining on a new charge of reckless or careless driving. For example, the prosecutor might unilaterally file a new charge of reckless driving and then offer to reduce the charge of reckless driving to careless driving in exchange for a plea or bail forfeiture to careless driving.

3. Reckless Driving

Reckless driving is defined as follows: A person commits the offense of reckless driving if the person recklessly drives a vehicle upon a highway or other premises described in this section in a manner that endangers the safety of persons or property. ORS 811.140. “Recklessly” in this section is defined in ORS 161.085 as “the condition of being aware of and consciously disregarding a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the result will occur or that the circumstance exists.” Reckless driving is a class A misdemeanor. which carries up to one year of imprisonment and/or a fine of $6500. ORS 809.410(6) provides for driver license suspension in the event of conviction for reckless driving. A first conviction carries a 90 day suspension. In the event of a second conviction within five years, the period of suspension is one year. And finally, in the event of a third or subsequent conviction within five years, the period of suspension is three years. ORS 809.420(1).

4. Careless Driving

Careless driving is defined at ORS 811.135 as follows: A person commits the offense of careless driving if the person drives any vehicle upon a highway or other premises described in this section in a manner that endangers or would be likely to endanger any person or property. Careless driving is a class B traffic violation punishable by a basic base fine of $237 plus fees and assessments, or a class A violation if it contributes to an accident, subject to a basic base fine of $421 plus fees and assessments. If careless driving results in injury or death, ORS 811.135 provides for additional penalties including traffic safety class, community services, fines up to $12,500, and license suspension.

5. ORS 813.170(2) restricts diversion agreements in DUII cases to the structured program outlined in ORS 813.200 only.

B. No Merger of DUII and Reckless Driving

DUII and Reckless Driving do not merge. Each has an element the other does not. A driver must be intoxicated for DUII but you don’t have to drive recklessly to be guilty of DUII. ORS 161.067. However, DUII and Reckless Driving convictions arising from the same criminal episode should be subject to concurrent sentences unless the requirements of ORS 137.123(5) are met.

Practice Tip

At trial, ask for an instruction that says merely driving intoxicated is not, by itself, enough to support a conviction for reckless driving. If the state opposes, because—they say—anytime you’re DUII, you’re reckless, then arguably the two counts would merge under State v. Blake, 348 Or 95, 99, 228 P3d 560 (2010) (crimes with different elements merge if committing one necessarily requires committing the other).

C. Sentencing Procedures

1. Time

Unless waived by the defendant, the time appointed for sentencing shall be at least two days after the plea or verdict is received unless the court does not stay in session. If the court does not intend to stay in session, the sentence must be as remote as possible before adjournment, but cannot be less than six hours after the verdict, except with the consent of the defendant. ORS 137.020(2). Such time limitations are not applicable to municipal and justice courts as they do not maintain regular sessions. Mayhew v. City of Eugene, 56 Or 102 (1910).

2. Substance

The taking of evidence in aggravation or mitigation at sentencing requires notice to the court and the adverse party. ORS 137.080, State v. Brown, 44 Or App 597 (1980), State v. Collins, 43 Or App 265, 268 (1979).

3. Personal Appearance of Accused

While the defendant may waive appearance at a misdemeanor trial (ORS 135.030), personal appearance of the defendant at a misdemeanor sentencing may be ordered by the court (ORS 137.030). Waiver of appearance at trial is discretionary and may be denied by the court, especially when identity is an issue. State v. Ferguson, 84 Or App 565 (1987). Appearance at trial or sentencing by electronic means (video or telephone conference) is permitted at the discretion of the court. A defendant who is in custody in another jurisdiction may “appear” for sentencing on a felony or misdemeanour charge by means of “simultaneous electronic transmission” (ORS 137.040, which is described in ORS 131.045 and UTCR 4.080.)

4. What to Cover at a Sentencing Hearing

Counsel may want to cover the following at a sentencing hearing:

a. Plea bargain sentencing recommendation.

b. Defendant’s time served in jail.

c. The amount of bail or security posted.

d. Restitution.

e. Court costs and a report of attorney time in appointed cases.

f. Explanation of detainers or parole or probation currently in effect.

g. Explanation and challenges to defendant’s prior record.

Consideration of a prior conviction at sentencing that is constitutionally invalid may result in the case being remanded for reconsideration of sentence. United States v. Tucker, 404 US 443, 92 S Ct 589, 30 LEd2d 592 (1972).

The constitutional validity of a prior conviction used to enhance a subsequent offense may be challenged in the subsequent prosecution. Baldasar v. Illinois, 446 US 222, 100 S Ct 1585, 64 LEd2d 169 (1980). The use of a collateral attack is appropriate to challenge the constitutional validity of the prior conviction. The defendant need not rely on the Post-Conviction Relief Act. State v. Grenvik, 291 Or 99, 101, 628 P2d 1195 (1981). In Grenvik, the court stated:

We hold...a defendant is able to challenge the use of constitutionally invalid prior conviction in a subsequent criminal proceeding whenever the court relies on the prior conviction to enhance punishment. When a constitutionally invalid prior conviction leads to punishment more onerous than that to which a defendant might be subjected without a prior offense, the sixth amendment prohibits its use. City of Pendleton v. Standerfer, 297 Or 725, 688 P2d 68 (1984).

A valid waiver will not be presumed from a silent record. Burgett v. Texas, 389 US 109, 88 S Ct 258, 19 LEd2d 319 (1967); State v. Grenvik, 291 Or 99, 101, 628 P2d 1195 (1981).

A conviction for DUII obtained when the defendant was without the assistance of counsel and had not waived counsel is invalid. Brown v. Multnomah County District Court, 280 Or 95, 570 P2d 52 (1977).

Under ORS 813.470, on defendant’s presentation of a certified copy of judgment of acquittal in a DUII case clearly showing date of arrest and of finding, DMV must note the acquittal on the driving record where driving privileges were suspended from the same incident due to a .08 or higher breath test resulting in an implied consent suspension.

h. Factual matters in mitigation concerning the offense and personal matters in mitigation concerning the defendant (age, marital status, employment, if defendant is a veteran, education and other matters such as drug or alcohol programs in which the defendant may have enrolled).

Entering your client into a certified treatment program prior to a plea or a verdict of guilty is always a good idea if your client has prior DUII convictions. You can receive a free directory to certified treatment programs by requesting the Treatment Program Directory from:

5. Under Oregon law defendants in criminal cases have the right of allocution (the right to be heard by the court prior to sentencing). DeAngelo v. Schiedler 306 Or 91 (1988).

6. The court may not, by its sentence, punish a defendant for exercising the right to stand trial as opposed to pleading guilty. State v. Smith, 52 Or App 681 (1981).

7. In a DUII case, judges will sometimes permit a short delay following sentencing before a defendant must begin serving any confinement imposed so that the defendant may make necessary personal arrangements. Check with local defense counsel to find out what a particular jurisdiction commonly does.

8. At the time the court pronounces sentence, the defendant, if present, must be advised of the right to appeal and other procedures for protecting appellate rights. If the defendant is not present, the court must advise the defendant in writing of the right to appeal and of the procedure for protecting appellate rights. ORS 137.020(3). As a practical matter, many courts request counsel to advise the defendant of the right to appeal and the procedure for protecting appellate rights.

D. Terms of Sentence

1. Alcohol Screening Interview or Evaluation

Upon any conviction for DUII, the court must require the convicted person to complete a screening interview, also referred to as an evaluation, with the agency or organization designated by the court. ORS 813.020. The cost of the screening interview is set by statute ($150). The purpose of the screening interview is to determine the level of mandatory programming the person shall undertake. ADES (Alcohol and Drug Evaluation Services) screeners determine whether the person should be placed in an “Information Program” or a “Rehabilitation Program.” Screeners also make a referral to a different agency for treatment, unless the jurisdiction is small enough that an exception is made and screening and treatment are allowed to be done by the same agency. ORS 813.025.

2. Programming or Treatment

By Heidi Brockman-Astrue, MA LPC NBCC MAC

Treatment is a mandatory component of a DUII sentence; the court must order treatment as part of a sentence. ORS 813.020. The type of programming (Information or Rehabilitation) is recommended by the screener who makes a referral, but the treatment provider must only be certified by the state and follow some minimum requirements in terms of length and curriculum: at least four session over a four-week period, with 12–20 hours of education, at least one urinalysis test within the first two weeks. There are also topics which must be covered in the program. OAR Division 54

In general, clients are required to demonstrate at least 90 days of documented abstinence, typically accomplished by random urinalysis (UA) testing. There is no other established standard for length of treatment, so programs vary greatly in both length of stay and cost. These variables are determined by individual programs and (hopefully) the needs of each particular client.

In both the case of diversion and conviction, clients are required to attend an appointment with a county sanctioned Alcohol and Drug Evaluation Specialist (ADES). These individuals often recommend a treatment provider, but it is important to note that clients have the right to attend treatment at any state certified DUII provider. It is acceptable for clients to begin treatment prior to a formal court referral, but whether or not the time counts toward court requirements is at the discretion of the court and/or ADES. Search the Oregon Health Authority website for a list of ADES and state-certified providers. You can also view a PDF copy of the directory (accessed February 1, 2020) in the Resource Guide.

The ADES is responsible for the communication between treatment and the courts. They are required to notify the court monthly regarding each defendant’s treatment, and in most counties, tend to be the individuals who monitor reports from ignition interlock companies. Treatment providers report on each client’s progress, UA results, and ultimately completion status to the ADES who then informs the court. It can be helpful to obtain a release of information from the treatment program so that a client’s legal counsel can also receive progress and completion data.

The treatment process must include a formal evaluation and a treatment plan that includes objectives and completion criteria. During the course of treatment clients must maintain regular appointments, participate in UA testing as requested, and remain abstinent. If objectives are completed, treatment fees are collected, and the client demonstrates abstinence, the treatment provider can notify the ADES of successful completion. For individuals with a conviction, providers will also issue a DMV treatment completion certificate.

The most common reasons for unsuccessful completion of treatment include UAs positive for substance use, non-attendance and unpaid treatment fees. For clients who have insurance, it is now mandatory in Oregon for insurance companies to cover treatment services for DUII (some providers do not take insurance, so provider selection is important when considering this option).

3. Fines and Fees

Misdemeanor DUII is subject to a $6250 statutory maximum limit on the fine unless there was a minor in the motor vehicle at the time of the offense over three years younger than the driver, in which case the court may impose a fine of up to $10,000.

Felony DUII is subject to the statutory maximum fine of $125,000.

ORS 813.010(6) sets out mandatory minimum fines for DUII convictions based on number of prior convictions and BAC. For a third or subsequent conviction, the $2,000 fine can be waived if the defendant is sentenced to a term of imprisonment.

I

The legislature did not delineate how proof of BAC is to be established so it must be a jury question, to be dealt with after a guilty finding. ORS 813.010(6)(c).

A DUII conviction fee of $255 is added to the fine and may be waived by the court. ORS 813.020(1)(a); 813.030. Statutory fees and assessments generally run another $150. ORS 813.020(3) adds a $5–$50 fee for the Victim Impact Program, which may be imposed if the court requires attendance at a Victim Impact Program. The offender is also responsible for the $150 cost of an alcohol screening pursuant to ORS 813.020 and 813.021. Costs may include court-appointed attorney fees. ORS 161.665(1). The court may waive some of the DUII conviction fee or allow for payment on an installment basis. However, the court may add a finance charge of $50 to $200 for amounts not paid within 30 days of entry of judgment. ORS 1.202.

See Section IV, Estimated Costs to a Defendant Associated with a Conviction for a First DUII Offense, and Section V., 2018 Case Law Supplement, for more.

4. Jail and/or Community Service and Probation

In addition to these costs, ORS 813.020(2) requires the court to impose a sentence of at least 48 hours imprisonment in the county correctional facility or the performance of community service for a period of time as outlined in ORS 137.129(d), which is not less than 80 hours, nor more than 250 hours. A court may provide for the imprisonment to be served in jail, minimum security facilities or inpatient rehabilitation or treatment centers. 813.020(2)(a). The court may not suspend execution of this minimum sentence although the sentence may be served at in-patient treatment under ORS 813.020(2)(a).

Probationary periods for misdemeanor DUII are at the discretion of the court but cannot exceed the five-year limit set out in ORS 137.010(3). Probation (and post-prison supervision) periods for felony DUII are set pursuant to the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission sentencing guidelines.

Community service: Many courts will approve community service programs formulated either by the Department of Community Corrections, by the district attorney’s office, or some other court-approved agency, or by the convicted person himself. The only requirement is that the benefit of the community service work must run to a nonprofit organization.

5. License Suspension/Revocation and Hardship Waiting Periods

The court is also required to suspend a convicted person’s driving privileges or right to apply for a license. The suspension periods and hardship waiting periods are discussed below.

a. First DUII conviction: ODL suspension = 1 year. ORS 809.420(2)(a). There is a 30-day waiting period for the defendant to apply for a hardship permit. ORS 813.520(2).

b. Second DUII conviction within five years: ODL suspension = 3 years. A hardship permit is not allowed in this situation.

c. Second conviction beyond five years =1 year.

d. The court is required to revoke a person’s driving privileges permanently upon a third or subsequent conviction for “any combination” of the following offenses: DUII under ORS 813.010, a “statutory counterpart” of DUII from another jurisdiction, “an offense in another jurisdiction that involved the impaired driving of a vehicle due to the use of intoxicating liquor, a controlled substance, an inhalant or any combination thereof,” or a driving offense that involves operating a vehicle with a prohibited blood alcohol content. ORS 809.235(1)(b). This does not include offenses in other jurisdictions that proscribe a lower blood alcohol content for persons under 21 years of age. ORS 809.235(1)(c).

If a driver is subject to a conviction suspension after already being subject to an implied consent suspension, DMV will credit that time toward the 30- or 90-day wait time. See DMV form 6826.

ORS 809.235(2), provides for a method to petition a court for the restoration of driving privileges. A petitioner must wait at least ten years from the date that the petitioner is either released on parole or post-prison supervision or sentenced to probation if the probation is not revoked and is discharged without a sentence of imprisonment. A petitioner is be required to undergo a court-ordered “psychological evaluation…to determine whether the person is presently a threat to the safety of the public.” ORS 809.235(3)(d). The burden is on the petitioner to establish by “clear and convincing evidence” that the petitioner is “rehabilitated” and “does not pose a threat to the safety of the public.” ORS 809.235(4). There is a requirement of SR 22 Insurance and an Ignition Interlock Device for five years to get driving privileges after a court has granted restoration.

e. If the DMV occupational license waiting period has expired at the time of conviction, then the convicted defendant will be eligible for an occupational license immediately, unless within the previous 12-month period he or she has been convicted of a major traffic offense as defined in ORS 153.500 or has failed or refused an Intoxilyzer test. If so, he or she must wait 30 days from the date the court orders the suspension in order to be eligible to apply for an occupational license. If the waiting period has not expired, the 30-day waiting period after conviction, nonetheless, begins to run from the date the court orders the suspension and the convicted defendant is eligible for an occupational license at the end of the 30-day period or upon the expiration of the occupational license waiting period, whichever time is longer.

f. Hardship Permit applications are available in fillable format online, and a printable copy in Chapter 3, The File.

g. The Department of Transportation may not issue a hardship permit authorizing a person to drive a commercial motor vehicle. ORS 807.240(2).

6. Restitution

The restitution statute, ORS 137.103 et seq., applies only if the defendant is convicted of, or admits to, criminal activity resulting in injury to persons or damage to property. Restitution may only be recovered from the defendant if the defendant could be made to pay it in damages in a civil case. State v. Young, 119 Or App 470 (1993). If a person is convicted of DUII and the conviction involves injury to a person or damage to property, the court may order restitution as a condition of sentencing or probation. In 2003, the legislature modified the restitution statute to remove from the court’s consideration the defendant’s ability to pay. However, this statutory change is arguably unconstitutional and should be challenged. See State v. Hval, 174 Or App 164, 180 (2001) (hit-and-run restitution statute, which does not contain a requirement that the court consider a defendant’s ability to pay, nevertheless requires a trial court to consider it); State v NRL, 249 Or App 321 (2012) (finding that the juvenile restitution statute does not violate Article I, section 17, of the Oregon Constitution, despite that statute’s failure to consider defendant’s ability to pay).

7. Vehicle Impoundment/Registration Suspension

Vehicles registered to persons convicted or vehicles used in second or subsequent DUII offenses face mandatory registration suspensions pursuant to ORS 809.010 for not more than 120 days. Clear and convincing findings of knowledge must be made where the vehicle owner is not the defendant. A convicted defendant may also have his vehicle impounded for a second conviction. ORS 809.700.

8. Ignition Interlock Device (IID)

Pursuant to ORS 813.602, once a person is convicted of DUII or admitted into a diversion program, the defendant will be required to have an ignition interlock device installed in any vehicle operated by defendant. The defendant will need to have the interlock installed before a hardship permit is granted. ORS 813.602(1)(a). The interlock must remain on the vehicle for the first one year after the ending date of the suspension caused by the conviction (two years for the second or subsequent conviction). ORS 813.602(1)(b). ORS 813.603(2). A person who fails to have the IID installed, fails to submit proof of installation, or tampers with the IID may have his license suspended. ORS 813.602(6). Additionally, the convicted defendant cannot avoid the IID requirement by simply waiting out the six months. DMV will not fully reinstate a convicted defendant until the defendant has the interlock installed for one year (first conviction) or two years (second or subsequent conviction). ORS 813.602.

9. Ignition Interlock Standards and Procedures Revised by 2017 Legislature

Under HB 2638 in 2017, the legislature set new standards and procedures regarding ignition interlock devices by creating new provisions and amending ORS 813.200, 813.600, 813.602, 813.603, 813.630, 813.635 and 813.645. Read the statutes beginning at 813.599 here.

The bill established standards for those who provide ignition interlock device services.

As of July 1, 2019, certification of those who provide ignition interlock device services is under the obligation of the Oregon State Police. The bill required employees of service centers receive criminal background checks. It requires service centers provider fees, sufficient to cover the costs of administering the program. It establishes a complaint process regarding service providers. Additionally, it establishes an ignition interlock fund, separate from the general fund, that shall consist of fees obtained from issuance and renewal of service center certificates.

Procedures: The bill requires the service center document by GPS the location of the ignition interlock device at the time of a negative report. It requires service centers to notify the court, the district attorney, and the Oregon State Police, when a customer produces a negative report. It authorizes courts to order additional participation in drug and alcohol treatment if a person produces at least two negative reports during the diversion agreement.

OAR 257-100-0005 through 257-100-0080 now cover Ignition Interlock Devices with the Department of State Police.

10. Juvenile Suspension Issues

A juvenile (between age of 13 and 18 years) convicted or adjudicated in juvenile court of any crime, violation, infraction, or any offense involving the possession, use or abuse of alcohol or controlled substances, faces an order of denial of driving privileges of not less than 90 days on the first order and not less than one year on a second or subsequent order. ORS 809.260.

Practice Tip

The particulars of sentencing terms vary greatly from county to county due to a disparity of resources available to misdemeanor corrections programs. If you are unfamiliar with the sentencing practices in a county you have to appear in, you should call a local attorney to find out what is customary.

E. Seizure of Motor Vehicle for Civil Forfeiture

A motor vehicle may be seized and forfeited if the person operating the vehicle is arrested or issued a citation for driving while under the influence of intoxicants in violation of ORS 813.010 and the person, within three years prior to the arrest or issuance of the citation, has been convicted of DUII or its statutory counterpart in another jurisdiction, or certain other alcohol-related traffic offenses. ORS 809.730. Note that the forfeiture is authorized under this section upon arrest or citation, not conviction, if the driver has a prior recent conviction.

Practice Tip

Use of vehicle forfeiture is usually a matter of local practice and can also be authorized by a local government ordinance.

F. Suspension of Commercial Drivers License After Conviction

If the defendant is convicted of driving a commercial motor vehicle under the influence, there are different BACs and suspension lengths. The suspension is in addition to any suspension the person would receive if he or she were driving a regular motor vehicle. ORS 813.403(2).

The legal blood alcohol limit for the operation of a commercial motor vehicle is .04. ORS 813.404(1)(d).

The suspension lengths for the Implied Consent Law:

If the defendant was not driving a vehicle containing hazardous materials, the suspension for refusing a breath test is three years. ORS 813.404(1)(a). If the defendant was driving a vehicle containing hazardous materials, the suspension length is five years. ORS 813.404(1)(c).

If the defendant blows above a .04 and is not driving a vehicle containing hazardous materials, the suspension length is for one year. ORS 813.404 (1)(b). If the defendant is driving a vehicle containing hazardous materials and blows over a .04, the length of the suspension is for three years. ORS 813.404 (1)(d).

If the person has had their commercial drivers license suspended for failure to perform duties of a driver of a commercial motor vehicle, operating a commercial motor vehicle under the influence, or has been suspended in another state for an offense that would lead to suspension for something other than a serious traffic infraction in Oregon, the suspension length is for life. ORS 813.404 (2)

Note: No hardship permit may be obtained authorizing operation of a commercial motor vehicle. ORS 807.270(4).

II. Felony DUII

A. ORS 813.010(5), ORS 813.011

The 1999 Oregon Legislative Assembly created the crime of felony DUII. It is a class C felony. The maximum statutory fine is $125,000 (ORS 161.625) with a maximum term of imprisonment of five years (ORS 161.605). However, like all felonies, the actual time of incarceration is dictated by the Oregon Sentencing Guidelines.

In 2010, Oregon voters passed Ballot Measure 73, which made a third DUII in ten years a felony, and any DUII committed subsequent to conviction of a felony DUII is a felony. It is also imposed a required minimum sentence of 90 days. The felony ranking is triggered by two prior convictions within ten years of the date of the current offense. Convictions for boating under the influence of intoxicants (BUII) count towards the ten-year total. Felony DUII has a minimum Sentencing Guidelines ranking of 4D.

B. ORS 813.010(5)

(5) Driving while under the influence of intoxicants is a Class C felony if the defendant has been convicted of driving while under the influence of intoxicants in violation of this section or its statutory counterpart in another jurisdiction at least three times in the 10 years prior to the date of the current offense and the current offense was committed in a motor vehicle. For purposes of this subsection, a prior conviction for boating while under the influence of intoxicants in violation of ORS 830.325 or its statutory counterpart in another jurisdiction, or for prohibited operation of an aircraft in violation of ORS 837.080(1)(a) or its statutory counterpart in another jurisdiction, shall be considered a prior conviction of driving while under the influence of intoxicants.

C. Ballot Measure 73 DUIIs

ORS 813.011 states:

(1) Driving under the influence of intoxicants under ORS 813.010 shall be a class C felony if the defendant has been convicted of driving under the influence of intoxicants in violation of ORS 813.010, or its statutory counterpart in another jurisdiction, at least two times in the 10 years prior to the date of the current offense.

(2) Once a person has been sentenced for a class C felony under this section, the 10-year time limitation is eliminated and any subsequent episode of driving under the influence of intoxicants shall be a class C felony regardless of the amount of time which intervenes.

(3) Upon conviction for a class C felony under this section, the person shall be sentenced to a mandatory minimum term of incarceration of 90 days, without reduction for any reason.

The Oregon Court of Appeals has asserted no reduction or suspension is allowed of the mandatory 90-day sentence under ORS 813.011. In State v. Urie, 268 Or App 362 (2014), the court held that a trial court is not permitted to suspend execution of, or reduce in any way, the 90-day mandatory minimum sentence for felony DUII under ORS 813.011. In reference to the 90 days, ORS 813.011(3) uses the language “shall be sentenced,” “mandatory minimum term” and “without reduction for any reason.” The legislative history of 2010 Measure 73 confirms the plain language of the statute by indicating that the measure would cause “at least 90 days” to be imposed for a felony DUII.

In State v. Epps, 271, Or App 232 (2015), the court reaffirmed its ruling in Urie, that no reduction was allowed under the statute. The court went further to address the defendant’s argument that Measure 73, which created the 90-day minimum sentence, did not violate the single-subject rule in Article IV, section 1(2)(d), of the Oregon Constitution. The Epps court cited to State v. Mercer, 269 Or App 135 (2015), in holding Measure 73 did not violate Article IV, section 1(2)(d).

D. Predicates and Proof

When a driver is charged with felony DUII or in certain cases where a driver may be subject to enhanced license suspensions because of alleged prior convictions, counsel needs to evaluate whether she should file a challenge to the alleged previous conviction pursuant to ORS 813.328. This section requires that the notice of challenge be filed no less than seven days prior to the first trial setting in the case and provides for a pretrial hearing on the validity of the prior convictions. See also ORS 135.433 regarding the means of proving priors, and ORS 135.434 which sets out the form of the notice and the burdens of proof when statutory enhancements are sought by the state, as well as certain types of priors (uncounseled or vacated convictions) which cannot be counted.

E. Sentencing Guidelines’ Crime Seriousness Ranking and Criminal History Score

Minimum criminal history score is D. (Note: Pursuant to OAR 213-004-0008, “every two prior adult convictions of person class A misdemeanors in the offender’s criminal history shall be counted as one adult conviction of a person felony for criminal history purposes.”) Thus, two prior DUII or BUII convictions are equal to one person felony.

If the person convicted has additional DUIIs or person crimes, her criminal history score can be a B (presumptive sentence = 19 to 24 months prison) or an A (25 to 30 months prison).

1. Crime Seriousness

The crime seriousness ranking for a felony DUII varies depending upon which felony DUII statute is implicated. The Oregon Criminal Justice Commission adopted OAR 213-018-0037 which establishes (1) if it is an ORS 813.011 (two prior DUII convictions in the last 10 years) felony conviction, then it is a level 4 person felony on the Crime Seriousness Scale and (2) if it is an ORS 813.010 (5) (three prior DUII convictions in the last 10 years) it is a level 6 person felony on the Crime Seriousness Scale.

2. Criminal History

Criminal history for sentencing on a felony DUII is established by OAR 213-004-0009 which in relevant part states: (1) two prior misdemeanor DUIIs shall be counted as a person felony; (2) each prior felony DUII shall be counted as a person felony; (3) any additional misdemeanor DUII shall be a person misdemeanor.

F. Departure

If the state gives notice pursuant to ORS 136.765, and proves an enhancement fact beyond a reasonable doubt (ORS 136.760 et. seq.), the court can impose an upward departure sentence up to double the greater number in the presumptive range. The court has discretion to impose a downward durational departure to fewer months in prison, or a downward dispositional departure to probation. Enhancement and departure factors are listed at OAR 213-008-0002.

In the case of felony DUII, it is crucial that the defense begin preparing early to protect the client from a possible prison sentence. A few suggestions:

a. Initiate a private evaluation and participation in a certified treatment program immediately. OAR 213-008-002(I) allows the court to depart downward to a probation sentence (or a shorter prison sentence) if the court is satisfied that treatment is available and more likely than prison to reduce the risk of recidivism.

b. Consider requesting a pre-sentence investigation to determine if the investigator identifies downward departure factors that justify a shorter or non-prison disposition.

Practice Tip

Prior to requesting a pre-sentence investigation, defense counsel should satisfy herself, and
educate the client, regarding the risks that a PSI can also identify upward departure factors that would be brought to the court’s attention and used by the prosecutor to request an upward departure sentence.

c. One useful tool in any felony sentencing is a Sentencing Memorandum analyzing each departure factor that may apply. Refer to OCDLA’s Felony Sentencing in Oregon for case law on how each departure factor has been interpreted.

ORS 813.011 creates a mandatory term of incarceration of 90 days ‘without reduction for any reason’ whereas ORS 813.010 (5) does not establish any mandatory minimum incarceration. Additionally, ORS 813.011 makes any subsequent conviction for DUII another Class C Felony regardless of the length of time between convictions, unlike ORS 813.010 (5).

G. Double and Triple Counting

Time and time again, defendants have unsuccessfully challenged the concept that their prior misdemeanor DUII convictions are used to increase their new crime to a felony, then used again to increase their criminal history to at least one person felony, then used again to depart their sentence for “persistent involvement.” The courts have repeatedly affirmed the position that under OAR 213-008-0002 and 213-004-0009, prior convictions are used for both criminal elements and crime seriousness. McCoin, 190 Or App 532 (2003); Eades, 208 Or App 173 (2006); Forrest, 213 Or App 151 (2007).

The major point of contention which should still be argued by counsel (and is not settled) is the “persistent involvement” departure that is alleged for someone convicted of a third or subsequent DUII. Under OAR 213-008-0002(2), because the prior DUII convictions are a predicate element of the felony DUII, there is no evidence that these priors are “significantly different from the usual criminal conduct captured by the aspect of the crime.” Therefore, there is no statutory basis for an upward departure. Note: This argument becomes far less persuasive when there are additional prior convictions for DUII or other alcohol-related crimes.

H. Alternative Incarceration Programs (AIPs)

Many counties will not allow individuals on any form of AIP for ORS 813.011 felony DUII (two prior) based on the language of the statute. ORS 813.011 states, “Upon conviction for a class C felony under this section, the person shall be sentenced to a mandatory minimum term of incarceration of 90 days, without reduction for any reason.” This language has led several counties to believe no alternatives to physical custody are available. Even if the judge orders the individual eligible for all AIPs the county jail may not allow these programs.

Counsel should advise the defendant to actively engage in inpatient or outpatient treatment as soon as possible. Sometimes inpatient treatment can be substituted for time in custody through “treatment dorms,” but options vary by county.

I. Misdemeanor Treatment

Whether earned misdemeanor treatment by the defendant at the time of sentencing, or petitioned for many years down the road, a felony DUII may be reduced to a class A misdemeanor pursuant to ORS 161.705. This is an important factor to always keep in mind. This will not retroactively affect the license revocation but would certainly aide in some of the other collateral consequences.

J. Collateral Consequences

1. License Suspension

Typically the most onerous burden on a defendant is the lifetime license revocation. Pursuant to ORS 809.235, 809.280, and 809.409, the DMV shall revoke the license of anyone convicted of a felony DUII under ORS 813.010, or any individual convicted of a third or subsequent DUII. Additionally, no hardship permit or application will be permitted during this revocation per ORS 807.250(4). Although the term “Life Time” Revocation is used throughout the statutes and in ordinary language, a defendant may apply for relief from the Revocation after ten years. See ORS 809.409 for relief from revocation.

2. Felony Status

Felony status can affect defendants in a number of ways, including: employment, housing, impeachment in future court proceedings, possession of firearms (see ORS 166.274) and inability to sit on a jury. The defendant should be fully informed of all these potential consequences as soon as possible before proceeding with the case.

K. Other Post-Conviction Pre-Sentencing Strategies

1. Negotiated Sentence

In some jurisdictions prosecutors may agree to a joint sentencing recommendation even after a conviction at trial due to a client’s impressive progress in self-rehabilitation, or to avoid a laborious and time-consuming sentencing hearing, or to avoid an appeal. Consider setting sentencing over and initiating new negotiations with your DA to recommend a long work-release sentence or inpatient treatment program or a downward departure to probation with an upward departure in the length of the probation (up to 60 months total).

2. Timing

Many felony DUII clients will be eligible for boot camp and other programs in the Department of Corrections. Sometimes sentencing can be scheduled at a time that minimizes the amount of time a client spends in the regular DOC facility before shipping out on a platoon of boot camp-bound inmates. DOC staff is usually very helpful in answering questions about the various programs. See details of available programs and other facilities at the DOC online.

L. Fines

As a class C felony, the maximum fine for a felony DUII is $125,000. However, ORS 813.010 (6) establishes the minimum fine amounts for a DUII conviction. “In addition to any other sentence that may be imposed, the court shall impose one or more of the following fines on a person convicted of driving while under the influence of intoxicants as follows:

(a) For a person’s first conviction, a minimum of $1,000.

(b) For a person’s second conviction, a minimum of $1,500.

(c) For a person’s third or subsequent conviction, a minimum of $2,000 if the person is not sentenced to a term of imprisonment.”

While ORS 813.010 (6) seems to indicate a mandatory minimum $2,000 fine for a 3rd DUII, the Court of Appeals has ruled the $2,000 fine is not mandatory. In State v. Frier, 264 Or App 541 (2014), the court held, a sentence of incarceration, even if it is less than a year and in a local jail, is “imprisonment” and thus the $2,000 minimum fine is not required. The sentencing court may impose such a fine but is not required to do so.

$2,000 Fine for Third DUII, $255 State Obligation, $60 Mandatory State Amount–Plain Error

Trial court plainly erred in imposing $2,000 fine for defendant’s third DUII conviction because court erroneously believed that the fine was mandatory rather than discretionary when the sentence includes jail. Trial court failed to exercise discretion when imposing a $255 fine. Trial court had no authority to impose $60 mandatory state amount.

State v. Loudermilk, 288 Or App 88 (2017) (Per Curiam)

Sentencing—Fines and Fees: DUII conviction fee not included within statutory maximum fine for Class A misdemeanor

Trial court’s imposition of a $6,200 fine and $255 DUII conviction fee did not violate statutory maximums. Defendant was convicted of DUII, a Class A misdemeanor, the maximum fine for which is $6,250. The trial court imposed a $6,200 fine and a $255 DUII conviction fee, for a total financial obligation of $6,455. Defendant argues that the conviction fee must be included within the statutorily proscribed maximum fine of $6,250. Based on the text and context of the relevant statutory provisions, the legislature intended fees and fines to be separate things. Because the fee and fine were both within statutory limitations, the trial court did not err.

State v. Coates, 288 Or App 586 (2017) (Ortega, P.J.)

Multnomah County judgments have a box for “DUII conviction fee $255” —Error to impose pre-marked $255 DUII conviction fee on form judgment.

Judgments – DUII Conviction Fee: The court reverses a $255 DUII conviction fee imposed in defendant’s fourth-degree assault and second-degree criminal trespass case. The court writes specifically to note that Multnomah County used judgment forms that have the DUII conviction fee already typed into the form under the “Money Award” section. Accordingly, the DUII conviction fee is imposed unless it is affirmatively deleted, even in non-DUII cases. A judgment that carries a money award can be enforced by the state, and therefore even an erroneous form may carry consequences for a defendant.
