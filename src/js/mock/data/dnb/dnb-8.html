Investigators and Experts

By Robert Crow and Eidar Gonzales

This chapter covers the special focus of investigators in DUII cases. The end of the chapter covers expert witnesses and provides lists of some possible experts available to the DUII practitioner via the OCDLA Expert Witness Directory.1

I. DUII Investigation

The following subjects will be addressed in this section:

• What information do I need?

  • Scene

  • Witnesses

A. What Information Do I Need?

1. Scene

Visiting the crime scene can be very valuable in DUII cases. Knowing the roadway will help you understand why the “bad driving” may have been due to environmental conditions rather than your client’s intoxication. Often times the “delayed response for pulling over” can be explained when you see the roadway was narrow or the client chose a much safer spot to pull over. You always learn something by visiting the scene. For example, familiarizing yourself with the terrain will be helpful in attacking the officer’s conclusions about your client’s “poor performance” on the standardized field sobriety tests.

There is no substitute for familiarizing yourself with the roads, stop area and visiting the location personally, so employing the assistance of an investigator can be a huge help on the case. Beyond the obvious point that sometimes two sets of eyes are better than one, an investigator can also aid in trial preparation and developing exhibits. Since the attorney cannot be a witness at trial, having an investigator gather evidence and photographs creates a valuable defense witness. An investigator can help by recording measurements, obtaining photographs, maps, aerial maps, and other tools to assist the jury in understanding the scene.

Beneficial skills include comprehensive H&P; medical charting; having a medical/pathology background or autopsy background; being multilinguistic; having a fearless devotion to go where most people won’t; to ask the most crucial questions at the most isolated or cold places, such as under a bridge in the company of a guy holding a Bowie knife itching to use it.

2. Witnesses

Let’s face it: in DUII trials, the witness lists often are limited to the arresting officer, the officer administering the breath test, a lab tech explaining the breath test sample and extrapolating a BAC at the time of driving, and possibly your client. Regardless of what a judge instructs, human nature tells us the jury is going to want to hear your client’s side of the story. Obtaining witnesses (or video from the nearby area) in your favor who can explain the scene, your client’s drinking pattern, and your client’s driving can help you formulate a plan of attack and construct a convincing story for the jury. Of course, obtaining such witnesses is not always easy.

An investigator has many tools to track down witnesses and contact information. Your investigator can interview witnesses, take notes on their statements, and serve them subpoenas for trial. Not to mention, in those rare instances when a witness’s story suddenly changes at trial, your investigator can testify to impeach the witness.

A licensed investigator can also serve as the middleman in assisting cases, especially if the investigator is also a notary, such as in securing civil compromises when possible.

B. Can I Do It On My Own Or Do I Need An Investigator?

Witness Interviews: People do not like lawyers; they do not want to talk to us. However, sometimes people will be more willing to talk to investigators. Investigators can get information from witnesses when witnesses would otherwise not speak to us. Additionally, tracking down witnesses is not always easy and can be quite time consuming. An investigator likely has resources the attorney does not to track down witnesses and can find them more efficiently than the attorney. Investigators can establish contact/initial reviews but the attorney should speak with the witnesses because the attorney will be trying the case.

Trial Testimony: Having an investigator who has interviewed trial witnesses can be valuable if the need to impeach a witness arises. Since the attorney cannot be both advocate and witness, it is important to have someone available who can testify.

Consumption of Time/Resources: Tracking down witnesses, running background checks, photographing scenes, and preparing trial exhibits can be very time consuming. The attorney’s time can be spent more wisely preparing for trial and preparing legal arguments. Hiring an investigator saves the attorney time and ultimately can save the client’s money by freeing up more of the attorney’s time.

Practice Tip

Make sure your investigator understands the possible defenses so you can utilize all of their skills in accordance with how you might present the case.

C. Funding for Investigators

1. Court-Appointed Cases

The Office of Public Defense Services provides resources and funding for non-routine expenses (such as money for investigators and experts). In order to obtain funding from OPDS,2 a non-routine expense request must be submitted, along with a justification statement outlining why it is necessary to employ the assistance of an investigator. The standard fee allowed for investigators, paid by PDSC, is $34/hour for English speaking cases and $39/hour for bilingual cases (although this may change in the future). 2. Retained Cases

In most cases when the attorney is retained, it is the responsibility of the attorney, or client, to hire an investigator. It is imperative that the attorney hire the investigator in order to create confidentiality not only between the investigator and client, but also investigator, attorney and client. This does not mean, however, that the attorney must pay for the investigator. Having the client pay the investigator, through the attorney, will create the necessary conditions for confidentiality between the investigator, attorney and client. In cases in which the client is paying for the investigator, having the client pay the fee for the investigator to the attorney, and the attorney placing the funds in the client trust account and then disbursing it according to the fee agreement between the client and investigator will create the requisite conditions to establish confidentiality between investigator, attorney and client.

In some cases, it is possible to obtain OPDS funding for retained cases. If a client is indigent, an attorney may seek funds from OPDS for an investigator. If a client has previously been declared indigent, was appointed a public defender and then later hires an attorney, the fact that the client previously was declared indigent can serve as a justification to OPDS to obtain state funding for an investigator. If a client has not previously been declared indigent, the client may ask the court verifier or a judge to review their financial status in order to establish their indigence so they can receive state funding for an investigator. If the client is declared indigent, the attorney would need to complete the same process as would be required for a court-appointed case. Also, see ORS 135.055, Compensation and Expenses of Appointed Counsel, which grants that an indigent client may receive state funding for preparation, negotiation, and conduct of trial even if he or she has retained counsel.

There are judges who do not understand the important concept that just because someone has retained counsel, that person is not necessarily precluded from getting PDSC funding for “nonroutine expenses.” Do not take no from a verifier or court without fighting for your client’s right to put on a defense and to have financial assistance from the state when they are unable to pay for “nonroutine” but necessary expenses.

See the appendices for a sample motion, affidavit/declaration and order. These examples are taken from a case in which the client retained counsel, through the assistance of family and friends, but was not financially able to pay for other “nonroutine” but necessary expenses. When the trial judge saw the client had retained counsel, despite the verifier’s office finding the client financially eligible for PDSC funding, he denied the request for extraordinary expenses. The sample documents were filed, and the judge reversed his ruling without a hearing.

II. For Investigators

Investigators, like attorneys, have a code of ethics they must follow in conducting their investigations. The Code of Ethics for investigators can be found at OAR 259-061-0190 and at

OCDLA’s Library of Defense (secure login required).3

Additional information for private investigators can be found under DPSST as well.

A. Investigation Confidentiality

Confidentiality relating to investigators is important and can be a liability if not protected. It is important not to divulge any information relating to a case to anyone other than the attorney on the case or the client. If information must be shared, it should only be done with a release of information signed by the client. But even with permission from the client, it may altogether be limited in the type of case information disclosed, and the best interests to the client as determined by the attorney on the case is to avoid inadvertently revealing factual information to potential adverse parties.

B. Avoiding the Practice of Law

The investigator and attorney team is a very important element in any case. Each member complements the other(s) to develop the best possible case. However, it is important that investigators know they are not allowed to practice law. There is a fine line between conducting the function of an investigator and an attorney. That line is generally drawn at the following two points:

  • Giving legal advice.

  • Aiding in filling out legal paperwork.

If a client asks the investigator for legal advice, it is important for the investigator to tell the client that only the attorney can provide legal advice or answer legal questions. The client should be instructed to contact the attorney about any such questions.

C. Police Witnesses

As you read through a report, take note of all of the witnesses with whom the investigators spoke as well as the details of their statements. You may want to contact the witnesses while conducting the defense investigation to see if any witness changes his or her story or provides different details than those given to the police.

In addition to witnesses listed in the report, take care to read through and look for other potential witnesses who were not identified and spoken to by the police. These witnesses may be valuable and/or key in a defense case. They may even dispute what other state’s witnesses are saying.

D. Neighborhood Canvassing

When looking for witnesses not already identified, check the areas in proximity to an incident. Witnesses may have some valuable information for you even though they were not spoken to at the time of the initial investigation. It is a good rule of thumb to check residences on each side of a location, i.e., north, south, east and west. Your goal is to identify witnesses to the sights, sounds or knowledge of a case. Even if someone doesn’t have direct evidence, they may have some circumstantial evidence to provide.

E. Referrals to Other Witnesses

When contacting witnesses in a case, remember that person may know of others who may have beneficial information. It is often a good idea to ask witnesses you interview if they know of any other people who may have seen or heard the incident or have knowledge about the case. The witness may be able to give you names, addresses or phone numbers of other potential witnesses. If followed, those leads may be able to provide a clearer and more complete picture of the details of the case.

Another thing to keep in mind when speaking to witnesses is whether they have ongoing cases where they are represented by counsel; establish that fact first—due professional courtesy may be needed to obtain permission to speak to any potential witnesses(s), provided the attorney concedes.

F. Resources for Dealing with Witnesses

Many valuable resources on conducting investigations and interviewing witnesses can be found online. The Library of Defense has a thorough discussion on investigations.4 Other OCDLA resources include The Investigators’ Manual5 (with a revised edition in the works for 2021), the Oregon Legal Investigators’ Committee6 and the investigators’ listseve.7 Additionally, there are some good websites to site and utilize in conducting witness interviews.

G. Complainants/Victims

The State of Oregon grants crime victims many rights, including the right to refuse an interview, deposition, or other discovery request by the criminal defendant or other person acting on behalf of the criminal defendant. Oregon Constitution Art. 1, §42.

Before interviewing any victim/complainant, check with the attorney to make sure there is no “victim letter” in the file, in which the victim has invoked his or her right to refuse to speak to the defense team. If no victim letter exists or the victim has not invoked the right not to speak with us, you must still notify the victim of his or her rights before speaking with himor her. You must inform the victim of the following:

1) You work with the defense attorney for defendant (give defendant’s name).

2) As a victim, she (or he) has certain rights, including the right not to speak with you at all or that she may request the presence of the district attorney while speaking with you.

3) If the victim has not already told you she does not want to speak with you, ask for confirmation that she does want to speak to you now (over the phone or in person) and obtain confirmation before continuing the interview.

Do this process each time you speak to the victim.

J. What to Look for in a Search Warrant

A search warrant provides a tool to law enforcement that allows them to search areas and things that they would not normally have access to due to the expectation of privacy that the United States Constitution and Oregon Constitution provide. As part of that exception, the law enforcement investigator must show that he or she has the training, experience and case information to show they know the items they are looking for will be in the area or item they describe.

As you read a search warrant, you will find that they have different parts to them. Depending on the person applying for the warrant, or affiant, the parts may be in a different order, but the information should be the same. A completed search warrant consists of the warrant itself and a supporting affidavit. In the affidavit, the officer must include specific information to justify to the court why the warrant should be issued. This information includes:

  • Introduction of the affiant.

  • Training and experience of the affiant.

  • Probable cause information that will lead the judge to believe the warrant is justified.

  • Request for the warrant to be authorized by the judge.

As you read the warrant affidavit, make sure the affiant describes in the training and experience section that he or she has the training and experience necessary to support the probable cause, e.g., knows that drug dealers have been known to carry guns for protection, therefore is asking to search for and seize any and all firearms.

A very important part of the search warrant is the description, which must state with particularity the place or items to be seized and searched. Make sure that description, including the description of the address numbers and the color of the front door, is accurate. There have been times when the warrant describes one place but it is served on a location that does not exactly match that description. If the description is not correct, that may be a reason for your employing attorney to file motions to controvert or suppress.

You should note that the affiant does not have to be the person who actually serves the warrant. If you read the warrant, you will notice that in essence it states, “To any police officer of the State of Oregon....” That allows any officer to serve it, not just the affiant.

III. Experts

Experts can be valuable people in a DUII case. Many of our DUII cases involve breath test samples that were gathered thirty minutes or perhaps even hours after our client was driving. The state likely will call a witness from the state crime lab to provide an estimation of what the client’s blood alcohol content would have been at the time of driving. The crime lab technician will discuss retrograde extrapolation to suggest that the client’s BAC had been dropping since the time of driving and he or she likely was higher at the time of driving. In such cases, it may be helpful to contact an expert who can help you with the calculation to determine what would be an appropriate estimation. The expert can also help you identify issues and concerns in the sample, the pattern of drinking, and the absorption/dissipation rates that will affect the rate at which alcohol is eliminated and why the lab’s estimation might be too high. Also see Chapter 11, “Breath, Blood and Urine,” for a thorough analysis of retrograde extrapolation and theories to refute.

A. Controlled Substances Cases

Experts can also be very helpful in controlled substance DUII cases. An expert can help you identify what drugs in your client’s system actually are controlled substances. Sometimes the crime lab will list a drug as a controlled prescription. Just because a drug is a controlled prescription does not mean it is a controlled substance; it is important to be able to point this out if the prosecutor or a state’s witness suggests otherwise. An expert can help you identify what symptoms, or vitals, should be expected for your client based upon the substances found in your client’s system. Not all controlled substances are the same, and it is important to be able to point out when an officer’s observations are inconsistent with what should be noted for the substances in your client’s system. Getting medical records or your client’s doctor can be extremely persuasive to a jury.

B. Other Charges – Accident Reconstruction

Many DUII cases also involve other charges. For example, we often find clients charged with reckless driving, reckless endangering, failure to perform duties of a driver, or criminal mischief when arrested for a DUII. A DUII case in which an accident occurs with injury to another person may involve charges of assault, or even criminally negligent homicide or manslaughter. When a death occurs, the difference between criminally negligent homicide, manslaughter in the second degree, and manslaughter in the first degree very well could be the conduct of the driver and the manner of his driving. In such cases it could be valuable to have an accident reconstructionist demonstrate how the accident occurred and how your client’s driving might not have been so bad after all. The difference in driving in such a way as to demonstrate negligent driving or in a manner that “manifests extreme indifference to the value of human life” could be many months of prison. An accident reconstructionist can help you develop a theory and show a jury how the accident, and injury, were not caused by your client’s extreme indifference to the value of human life, but rather by the tragic confluence of many independent factors.

C. Medical Experts

Injury cases also involve the issue of how bad the injury is. The difference in the varying level of offenses can be the extent of injury. Do not assume the state’s doctor, or expert, will provide accurate information. Having an expert conduct an independent review and possibly testify as a witness at trial can help in convincing jurors that the victim was not hurt as badly as the state suggests and thus your client cannot be guilty of the more serious charge.

D. Jury Consultants

Beyond providing insight as to the evidence at trial, experts can also be valuable tools in preparing for trial. Picking a jury is an art and requires your own individual style. However, it is also important to remember you must be able to read your voir dire in picking your jury. Jury consultants can help you refine your style and better relate to, and read, jurors. Experts can help you in preparing your witnesses and client for testifying at trial. There is so much more to testifying than simply what is said, and it is important to make sure your witnesses are prepared and up to the task.

It is important to obtain experts early on in preparing a case. It is vitally important you, as the lawyer, understand the issues and complexities of scientific evidence and other evidence requiring specialized knowledge. It also is important to remember that you understanding it is not enough. You must make a jury understand the concepts and evidence. As the lawyer, it is hard to do this without the assistance of an expert because, as the lawyer, you cannot provide testimony or evidence to a jury. I once witnessed one of the most knowledgeable and experienced DUII attorneys I know struggle to educate his jury because he could not get any of the government’s witnesses to provide the answers he knew to be true, but he, himself, could not testify or provide that evidence to the jury. Without the assistance of an expert witness, he could not properly educate his jury because he did not have an admissible vehicle to do so.
