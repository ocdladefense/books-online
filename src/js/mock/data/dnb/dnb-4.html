Chapter 4 Implied Consent Hearings

Hugh Duvall

I. Introduction

Implied consent suspensions are imposed administratively, using a process which affords drivers far fewer protections against unjust results than the related criminal proceeding. Additionally, because these suspensions either impose a partial or complete period of denial of any hardship privileges, they often have significantly greater impact on one’s client than an actual conviction for Driving Under the Influence. (Much appreciation is due to Ben Eder for his assistance in a previous version of this chapter.)

Included in the Resource Guide is a copy of the Office of Administrative Hearings Guide to Oregon Implied Consent Hearings. This is the publication used by administrative law judges to determine how to rule on implied consent hearings. This document is full of valuable case law to prepare for and conduct an implied consent hearing. The guide is also available at the top of the page.

There are many reasons to request a DMV hearing, such as the fact that you might win. There is no penalty if you lose and you get an opportunity to cross-examine the police officer close in time to the arrest and develop a transcript for future use.

Note: In State v. McRae, 271 Or App 558 (2015), the defendant sought to admit the opinion of the ALJ, from the Implied Consent Hearing, to impeach an officer at a pretrial suppression motion. The trial judge ruled the opinion was inadmissible. The Court of Appeals disagreed, but affirmed based upon harmless error. In the ruling, the court held, “A hearing on a motion to suppress evidence in a criminal case is a hearing involving a preliminary question concerning evidence within the meaning of OEC 101(4)(a) and OEC 104(1) and is not governed by the rules of evidence. State v. Wright, 315 Or. 124, 129, 843 P.2d 436 (1992). In excluding the DMV order, the trial court described the ‘opinion’ of the ALJ as not relevant to the ‘fact finding and decision-making’ task of the trial court at the suppression hearing. To the extent the trial court meant that the ALJ’s ‘opinion’ regarding credibility and probable cause had no bearing on how the trial court decided those issues, defendant does not disagree. But defendant sought to admit the order because it describes prior statements that Ginnow made regarding pertinent facts. Although the trial court would have been free to give that evidence little or even no weight in assessing Ginnow’s credibility, the court should have admitted and considered the offered evidence at the suppression hearing and then determined what weight to afford it.” McRae at 560-61.

The best hearings are done with short questions using eye contact and pacing to create a good transcript that can be used at the criminal trial.

Warning: “If the prosecuting attorney or the attorney for the defendant in a prosecution for driving while under the influence of intoxicants obtains a tape or a transcript of a hearing held for the defendant under ORS 813.410 (suspension upon receipt of police report on implied consent test), the attorney must provide a copy of the tape or transcript to the attorney for the other party at least seven days prior to the first date set for trial. If the attorney fails to supply the material in the time required, testimony from the hearing may not be admitted in evidence in the trial for any purpose, unless the attorney shows good cause for the failure to make the material available.” ORS 813.324(1) (Emphasis added).

II. Relevant Statutes and Oregon Administrative Rules

Chapter 813 of Oregon Revised Statutes – Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicants

Division 90 of Oregon Administrative Rules – Implied Consent Hearing Procedures

A fundamental familiarity and understanding of the following statutes and administrative rules is essential to adequately challenging an implied consent suspension:

  • ORS 813.410, statute specifically explaining challenging an implied consent suspension, and ORS 813.132, urine test refusal statute.

  • ORS 183.310–470, statutes explaining contested hearings.

  • OAR 735-090-0000 to 735-090-0130.

A familiarity with how the courts enforce the rules an agency promulgates upon itself is helpful, and even critical. A good place to start is reading Marshall’s Towing v. Department of State Police, 339 Or 54 (2005).

Remote Hearings

The hearing will be, “by telephone or other two-way electronic communication device.” However, petitioner or officer may request hearing be in person. ORS 813.410(4-5).

III. Requesting the Hearing

A. Timely Requests

ORS 813.410(4) and OAR 735-090-0020 set forth how to request a hearing.

There is a ten-day deadline from the date of the arrest, not counting the date of the arrest, to deliver a hearing request to DMV. See ORS 813.410(4) and OAR 735-090-0020(4). ORCP 10A applies. OAR 735-090-0020(4). If the tenth day falls on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday, the deadline for DMV to receive the hearing request is the next business day. Id.

DMV must actually receive the hearing request within the above deadline; the mailbox rule does not apply. Id.

Instructions to request a hearing are set forth on the Implied Consent Combined Report.

The hearing will be, “by telephone or other two-way communication device.” See ORS 813.410(5)(b) (Except as noted in ORS 813.410(6)).

B. Late Hearing Requests

Reasons a petitioner may request a hearing after the ten-day deadline are set forth in ORS 813.440. The last basis listed in this statute is for, “Other just cause as defined by the department by administrative rule.”

“Just cause” is defined in OAR 735-090-0000(5).

Note: If the petitioner was in custody without access to his/her implied consent documents, or in the hospital, a late hearing will likely be granted if the request is received by the department within ten days plus the number of days when the petitioner was not in position to submit the request.

Practice Tip

Instruct whomever answers your phone to always ask anyone inquiring about representation on a DUII case what was the day of the arrest, and to warn everyone that a ten-day deadline to request a suspension hearing likely applies to their case. Make sure your assistant knows that the day of the arrest does not count toward the ten days, and that if the tenth day falls on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday, the next business day is the deadline.

IV. Subpoenas

The petitioner may request a subpoena from the Office of Administrative Hearings (OAH). See ORS 813.410(5)(f). DMV and OAH will only enforce subpoenas served on the witness at least 72 hours prior to the hearing. See OAR 735-090-0051.

An attorney of record for a party may also issue a subpoena upon the attorney’s signature. See ORS 183.440(1).

Witness fees and mileage are as follows:

“Witnesses appearing pursuant to subpoena, other than the parties or officers or employees of the agency, shall receive fees and mileage as prescribed by law for witnesses in ORS 44.415 (2).” See ORS 183.440(1).

ORS 44.415(2), says that witness fees are $5.00/day, and witnesses get eight cents a mile; however, “Total mileage reimbursement shall not exceed the necessary cost of transportation on reasonably available common carriers.” (So, bus fare is “fair” game.)

Note: It appears that ORS 136.595(2) allows one to subpoena a law enforcement officer by “desk service” with at least ten days’ notice.

Practice Tip

There are two camps within the defense attorney community. One approach is to always subpoena duces tecum the reporting officer to bring all records to the hearing. The other is to subpoena the officer only when information from the client or early production of a police report indicate it may be useful. The dilemma is caused by the fact the greatest percentage, by far, of implied consent suspension “wins” are a result of an officer default. Will your subpoena duces tecum result in being one more reminder to the officer to show up for the hearing on time?

Telephone hearings may create challenges regarding how to view and use subpoenaed material at the hearing. This may be a reason to request an in-person hearing under ORS 813.410(6).

V. Postponements

A petitioner may request a postponement of the hearing due to the “inability of the person’s attorney to appear due to the attorney’s illness, vacation or scheduling conflict arising from other court or administrative hearing appearances.” ORS 813.440(1)(f). [Note: When you see John Henry Hingson, you owe him a drink. He singlehandedly got us this provision.]

While a postponement by the department stays a suspension, a postponement by the petitioner does not.

A reset request may be made on behalf of a police officer under ORS 813.440(1)(d) and OAR 735-090-0120(4). Under OAR 735-090-0120, “if a subpoenaed police officer is unable to appear at a hearing under ORS 813.410 due to an officer’s illness, vacation or official duty conflicts, a hearing will be re-scheduled as soon as practicable.” OAR 735-090-0120(4) states, “an official duty conflict exists if the subpoenaed police officer is unable to attend the hearing due to any of the following conditions: (a) Community caretaking pursuant to ORS 133.033; (b) Court appearance on behalf of the police officer’s agency; (c) Participating in employer approved training; or (d) Duty-related physical incapacity.”

In Johnson v. DMV, 261 Or App 641 (2014), the Court of Appeals held an officer who had jury duty did not have an “official duty conflict” under the law. Because jury duty was not an obligation of the officer’s position as a police officer, it does not justify extending a DMV hearing.

Practice Tip

Warn your client prior to requesting a postponement that his/her license suspension will go into effect on the currently scheduled date. However, you can also tell your client that you will likely have discovery by the new hearing date that will allow you to be more effective during the hearing.

Also, “A hearing must be rescheduled no later than 45 days after the date of the original hearing and may not be rescheduled more than once for reasons described in this paragraph.” ORS 813.440(1)(f).

A blood test for DMV purposes may only be given after an accident. ORS 813.100.

(3) The DMV or OAH may receive notification of an officer’s illness, vacation or official duty conflict before or after a scheduled hearing. (4) An official duty conflict exists if the subpoenaed police officer is unable to attend the hearing due to any of the following conditions: (a) Community caretaking pursuant to ORS 133.033; (b) Court; (c) Hazardous or impeding travel conditions; (d) Participating in employer approved training; (e) Physical incapacity; or (f) Service in the U.S. Armed Forces, military reserves, National Guard or the organized militia. B. State’s Case-in-Chief

  • Validity of officer’s initial contact with the client.If justification for stop is based on hearsay, consider asking ALJ to consider the nonexclusive, five factors from Cole/Dinsmore prior to determining that such hearsay supports “substantial evidence in the record” to find such stop was legal. If there are two layers hearsay, your argument to exclude is stronger. Although it is highly unlikely you will prevent an officer’s report from being offered as evidence, you may argue under Cole/Dinsmore:

1. There are alternatives to relying on hearsay (weighs against DMV). 2. The importance of facts sought (without valid stop/arrest, petitioner prevails). 3. The state of the supporting or opposing evidence. Check on presence of missing officer’s report. If no report, then this weighs in favor of petitioner (neutral if report present). 4. The potential efficacy of cross-examination. Question officer re 2nd factor (i.e., has he reviewed written report of missing officer; does he have the missing officer’s report with him; does he recall exactly what the missing officer told him?). Ask the officer step-by-step questions which the missing officer would know and which would confirm either an infraction or reasonable suspicion. 5. The significance of consequences. See Cole/Dinsmore v. DMV, 336 Or 565 (2004). Note: OAR 735-090-0130: “In accordance with the definition of “Error of the Department” specified in OAR 735-090-0000(2), in a hearing that determines the validity of a suspension of driving privileges under ORS 813.410, if hearsay evidence is used to establish the required elements under ORS 813.410(8) and a petitioning party presents substantial evidence that contradicts the hearsay evidence, DMV will rescind the suspension and OAH will continue the hearing pursuant to ORS 813.440(1)(c) and subpoena the hearsay witness to the continued hearing.”

  • Validity of officer’s continued contact with the client.Did officer have reasonable suspicion of a crime justifying a continuation of the stop? See Rodgers/Kirkeby, 347 Or 610 (2010): “Here, both defendants have shown the required nexus. In both cases the officers had completed the traffic violation investigation and should have either issued a citation or informed each defendant that they could leave. As already explained, the officers in each case did neither and instead unlawfully detained each defendant. It was during the period of unlawful detention that the officers requested that each defendant consent to a search.... [W]e conclude that each defendant’s consent, even if voluntary, was the product of police conduct that violated Article I, section 9. Because the consent to search in each case was a product of the unlawful seizure, the evidence obtained during the search, in both cases, must be suppressed.”

The police officer must have probable cause of a traffic infraction or reasonable suspicion of a crime to effectuate a stop of your client. If the officer believes that a traffic violation occurred but they are mistaken about the law then it is not a lawful stop. State v. Tiffin, 202 Or App 199 (2005). Also look to see if the reason for the stop dissipated such as an expired tag was not actually expired. Was the stop based on an anonymous phone tip? See State v. Black, 80 Or App 12 (1986). The officer must be in a uniform displaying a badge under ORS 810.400 to enforce the traffic code. If the officer came into your client’s home then Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution may protect your client. The police are not allowed to enter a home because a door is open and they think a drunk driver is inside their home. See State v. Apodaca, 85 Or App 128 (1987). Did the police walk into the garage to speak to your client? See State v. Somfleth, 168 Or App 414 (2000), declaring the garage curtilage. The burden is on the state to show that they could not have gotten a warrant to enter a home. State v. Roberts, 75 Or App 292 (1985). Did the officer violate Article I, section 9, when he stuck his head in the car to smell an odor of alcohol? This is a search under State v. Hendricks, 151 Or App 271 (1997). Officer may not ask questions unrelated to the basis for the traffic stop, without independent reasonable suspicion for such questions. See State v. Arreola-Botello, 365 Or 695 (2019).

  • Validity of officer’s request for client to perform FSTs:

  • Client consented?3

  • Officer had probable cause (and exigent circumstances)?4

  • Validity of officer’s arrest:

  • Client drove.5

  • A motor vehicle.

  • While under the influence of intoxicants.

  • On a public highway or premises open to the public.6

  • Within the appropriate venue.

  • Client was arrested for DUII under state statute or municipal ordinance.7
  • Officer honored client’s request to communicate with counsel or others.See Brown v. DMV, 219 Or App 607 (2008): “[I]f an arrested driver asks to speak with someone (including but not limited to counsel), the driver “must be afforded reasonable opportunity to do so unless or until it would interfere with effective administration of the [breath] test,” including by interrupting the observation period. Moore, 293 Or at 715, 723, 652 P2d 794 (1982). Always ask if the officer was worried that your client would be under the legal limit if they allowed more time to speak to an attorney.

The request for the need or desire for legal representation is an invocation of the right to counsel and should be argued that it triggers an obligation to have an opportunity to speak with an attorney about taking a test. See State v. Trenary, 114 Or App 608, 614 (1992), for any glancing reference for the desire of legal representation, and State v. Ohm, 224 Or App 390 (2008), about equivocal statements about calling an attorney. Did the client ask for legal advice from the officer? The police are not authorized to give legal advice. Oregon State Bar v. Taub, 190 Or App 280 (2003). Therefore, asking for legal advice from the officer may trigger the obligation for the police to give an opportunity to communicate with a lawyer. “If the police failed to afford the driver a reasonable opportunity to communicate, the driver’s refusal to take the test would have resulted from a legally unauthorized procedure, and an administrative penalty imposed based on that procedure would be invalid. See Moore v. MVD. “[I]n a license suspension proceeding, the driver bears the burden of establishing that his or her opportunity to communicate with counsel or others was unreasonably restricted. Walls v. DMV, 154 Or App 101, 107, 960 P2d 888 (1998).” Brown at 613-14. Also see State v. Freytag, 230 Or App 694, 698 (2009): “Opinions from the Supreme Court and this court appear to presume that the Fourteenth Amendment right to communicate includes the right to communicate, not only with an attorney, but with others as well. In State v. Newton, 291 Or 788, 807, 636 P2d 393 (1981), overruled by Spencer, 305 Or 59, 750 P2d 147 (1988), a plurality of the Supreme Court held that the Fourteenth Amendment protects an arrested driver’s liberty interest in communication not only with counsel, but also with the outside world. The opinion referred to the ‘[f]reedom of an arrested person to communicate’ and ‘liberty to communicate as he chose.’ Newton, 291 Or at 807, 636 P2d 393. The plurality opinion on that issue was restated in Moore v. Motor Vehicles Division, 293 Or 715, 719, 652 P2d 794 (1982), in which the court described Newton as ‘holding that an arrested person is entitled to communicate with counsel or others.’ Newton, however, was overruled in Spencer, albeit on grounds not implicating the Fourteenth Amendment. And in Brown, 219 Or App at 612-13, 185 P3d 459, we wrote, ‘[I]f an arrested driver asks to speak with someone (including but not limited to counsel),’ the driver must be afforded the opportunity to do so.” “In a civil proceeding for revocation of a driver’s license, the revocation is invalid if the breath test is not properly administered, regardless of causation. Moore, 293 Or at 723-24, 652 P2d 794; Hays v. DMV, 230 Or App 559, 562, 216 P3d 902 (2009).” Freytag at 699.

  • Officer informed client of “rights and consequences.”State v. Lyons, 118 Or App 660 (1993): “[A]n officer must always inform a driver of the ‘rights and consequences’ before administering a breath test. [citations omitted]. The officer has that obligation, even if the driver has voluntarily consented to take the test without initially refusing. See State v. Trenary, supra; State v. Weishar, 78 Or App 468, 478, 717 P2d 231, rev den, 301 Or 338, 722 P2d 737 (1986). Defendant had the right to be properly informed before taking the test, and he was not.” At 663.

The officer is not allowed to mislead your client to taking or refusing a test. State v. Freymuller, 26 Or App 411 (1980). ORS 813.410(8) states in part: “A suspension under this section is valid if all of the following requirements have been met:… (e) The person had been informed under ORS 813.100 of rights and consequences as described under ORS 813.130.” ORS 813.100(1) states in part: “Before the test is administered the person requested to take the test shall be informed of consequences and rights as described under ORS 813.130.”

  • At the time of the sample request, police still had reasonable grounds to believe client had driven under the influence.8

D. If Refusal Alleged

  • Client actually refused and was physically able.ORS 813.410(8) states in relevant part: “A suspension under this section is valid if all of the following requirements have been met:… (c) The person refused a test under ORS 813.100[.]”

Davis v. DMV, 209 Or App 39 (2006): “On appeal, DMV argues that the trial court erred in ordering reinstatement of petitioner’s driving privileges. According to DMV, the ALJ correctly concluded that petitioner had refused to take the breath test. DMV argues that the ALJ did not err in concluding that petitioner had refused the breath test because, under the applicable case law, anything substantially short of “unqualified, unequivocal consent” amounts to refusal. Petitioner responds that her insistence that Hoesly reread the rights and consequences notice should not be taken as a refusal without proof that rereading the notice would have substantially delayed the test process. We agree with DMV.” Note: Can silence be a “refusal”? In response to silence, must operator tell client to “blow into the mouthpiece”? See OAR 257-030-0130(5)(b), which states in relevant part: “If during either of the breath sample collection periods, the subject refuses, through some willful act, to follow the instructions to provide an adequate breath sample, the operator may depress the “Start Test” button or the “R” key on the instrument keyboard to terminate the breath testing sequence.” (Emphasis added.) And subsection (6)(b) states in relevant part: “Nothing in this subsection precludes an operator from terminating the breath testing sequence as “Refused” if the subject refuses, through a willful act, to follow the instructions of the operator.” (Emphasis added.) Cobine v. MVD, 102 Or App 17 (1990), states that physical incapacity is a defense. If your client was unable to blow due to lung capacity, passing out or getting sick, then you may have a physical incapacity defense. Also note Shakerin v. MVD, 101 Or App 357, 359-60 (1990): “MVD concedes that a painful need to urinate might make a refusal involuntary if the officer indicates that the arrested person must refuse in order to use the bathroom immediately.” Note further that if a language barrier or hearing impairment prevents the client from understanding the “rights and consequences” of which the officer has “informed” him, this may violate due process and could be argued that it is not a willful act. Although in State v. Nguyen, 107 Or App 716 (1991), rev den 312 Or 528 (1991), the Court of Appeals found that while an officer must read the rights and consequences to a non-English speaking driver, the driver need not understand the information. And in State v. Weishar, 78 Or App 468 (1986), where a hearing impaired driver also submitted and failed a breath test, the issue is similar. But note that in both Nguyen and Weishar, the drivers subjected themselves to the test, and thus did not suffer the longer suspension lengths of a refusal. A due process violation may be more pronounced if the driver’s status prejudices the result of the procedure.

K. Appealing the Final Order

The petitioner may appeal a final order to circuit court. ORS 813.410(10). Instructions for appealing a final order are found in ORS 813.410(10)), and UTCR Chapter 10.

VII. Banks and Implied Consent Suspension Hearings

On February 7, 2019, the Oregon Supreme Court handed down the decision State v. Banks, 364 Or 332 (2019). The defendant in Banks refused to consent to a blood test, and the state introduced evidence of that refusal over defense objection. The Oregon Supreme Court reversed, initially recognizing that a person arrested for DUII has a right under ORS 813.100(2) to decide whether to submit or refuse a breath test. Banks, 364 Or at 339. See also State v. Swan, 363 Or 121, 145 (2018) (same).

The Banks court then addressed the state’s primary contention that the defendant’s refusal to submit was merely a refusal to perform a physical act, not an invocation of a constitutional right to insist on a warrant. Id. at 340. The court acknowledged that a driver’s refusal to consent may be either an invocation or a refusal to cooperate without constitutional significance. Id. at 342. But the majority noted that, as proponent of the evidence, the state bears the burden of establishing admissibility:

The state must demonstrate that the officer’s question could reasonably be understood only as a request to provide physical cooperation and not as a request for constitutionally-significant consent to search. If the state fails to establish that fact, then a driver’s refusal cannot be admitted in evidence against the driver.

Id. at 343. The court found that the officer’s question – “Will you take a breath test?” – was ambiguous: the officer could have been asking for physical submission to the test or could have been asking for consent to search, thereby establishing a warrant exception. “Consequently, the state did not establish that defendant’s refusal to take the test was admissible as evidence of guilt.” Id. The court concluded by rejecting the state’s suggested exception to the general rule of exclusion when the officer has a constitutional basis to search other than consent. Id. at 349 (admission of such evidence would “place an impermissible burden” on assertion of the right to refuse consent).

In the administrative context of an implied consent hearing, an open question remains whether this decision impacts the validity of a suspension imposed after this language (or alternative language currently being employed by law enforcement) is read to the petitioner. The focus should be on whether the procedure used to suspend the petitioner is a “legally unauthorized procedure.” See Hays v DMV, 230 Or App 559 (2009).
