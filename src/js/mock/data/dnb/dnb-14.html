The Defense Case/The State’s Case

By Bob Thuemmel & David Eder

The Defense Case

I. Should There Be a Defense Case?

Once the state rests its case, it is the lawyer’s job to try and determine whether or not a defense case should be put on. Obviously, if the defense strategy has never included the possibility of presenting evidence, this decision has already been made and is easy.

However, if the “game plan” has always included calling the defendant and a few of his or her friends to establish innocence, it is always a good idea to revisit that idea before beginning the case. Try and get a feel for these things:

• How strong has the state’s case been? How well did the state’s evidence come in?

• How effective was your cross-examination of the arresting officer? Does the jury have trouble believing whatever that person said now?

• How strong is your case really? How good are your witnesses? Do they have any problems of their own? Are they all present and ready to go?

• How much has the jury heard? Are they still listening?

• Can you just as well deal with any issues in the evidence in your argument, instead of calling
witnesses?

Any experienced trial judge will tell you this privately: over the years, judges see case after case grabbed from the jaws of acquittal and turned into guilty jury verdicts because of a poorly organized or thoughtlessly presented defense. In your career as a criminal defense lawyer, you hope this never happens to you. For that reason, don’t ever just go forward with your case—no matter how well prepared, no matter how important the client thinks it is, no matter how many exhibits you have ready to go—unless you have re-decided at the close of the state’s case that this is the best course of action available to you.

II. Witnesses

A. Witnesses in Contact with Defendant Before or After Defendant’s Arrest

In every case, defense counsel should seek out witnesses capable of testifying about the defendant’s actions either before or after defendant’s encounter with the police.

• Wife or husband.

• Friends and companions.

• Bartender or cocktail waitress (maybe).

• Bystanders to stop.

• Person car released to.

• Jail booking personnel.

• Person who picked defendant up from jail.

• Taxi driver who brought defendant home from jail.

• Lawyer defendant called (as long as not trial counsel or in same firm). In re Lathen, 294 Or 157, 654 P2d 1110 (1982). However, ORS 40.340, Rule 606.1 (competency of attorney as witness) has been repealed.

• Towtruck driver.

• Witnesses acquainted with defendant’s physical or speech problem.

• Cellmates.

• In the case of an accident, check with the fire or ambulance people who responded. Ambulance personnel always have records of the patient’s condition, as do emergency rooms if your client went to one of them. However, interview these people carefully before they take the stand. Many of them will tell you, “drivers in these kinds of accidents are always drunk, in my opinion.”

• It may be helpful to the defense to call witnesses who would have noticed impairment with the defendant and didn’t. This can work even with a witness who does not remember the defendant (i.e., 7-11 clerk who would have noticed a problem and would not have sold him the beer he bought there).

• You should drive the area where your client was arrested.

• Ask the client questions so you know where to look for evidence/witnesses.

B. Preparing Testifying Witnesses

Don’t be afraid to ask the witnesses tough questions to prepare for cross. You need to know if the witness will hurt you on cross. It’s important to examine the demeanor and tone and how the witness will answer questions to know if she or he will be a good witness or not. This requires you to think about and do a mock cross-examination of the witness so you and the witness know what to expect.

The prosecution will want to pin witnesses down to show they remember one thing (how many drinks defendant consumed), but not how defendant was dressed, etc. Likewise, the prosecution will seek to establish bias, intoxication of the witness and finally trick the witness into using descriptions of the defendant (such as relaxed, loose, having a good time) which can then be used in closing argument. Each witness should be briefed about the following specific areas of inquiry and their answers reviewed.

Listed below are the general areas and types of questions witnesses should be prepared to answer:

1. Period of time spent with defendant and a fairly good idea of times involved—such as, “we left the restaurant at about 10:15.” It always hurts the defense case when witnesses are too inconsistent on the times involved.

2. Amount of alcohol consumed by defendant or rate at which he was drinking (“I am not sure of the number involved but everyone was drinking slowly or moderately”).

3. Physical and mental condition of defendant when contact ended.

4. Witness must understand the definition of “under the influence.”

5. Amount of alcohol consumed by witness.

6. Opinion whether defendant was “under the influence.”

7. The witness would not lie or distort the truth to help a friend.

8. “Did you have any concerns about letting John get in his car and drive it away that night? How did you react when you first heard that he received this citation?”

9. The “reluctant friend witness” should be left at home. From time to time, the lawyer may contact a friend and potential witness who is (or seems) uncomfortable about coming to court on the client’s behalf. Trust your instincts and judgment on this person, and maybe just discuss your feeling about him or her openly. If for any reason the person is less than genuine in the desire to testify truthfully and thereby help the defense, it is always the best choice to remove the person from your case. Sometimes that reluctance will also show on the stand and may lead to cross-examination that will make you wish you’d kept the witness out of the case.

C. Joint Meeting with Witnesses

If more than one independent witness observed defendant, it may be wise to have a group meeting so that all the facts can be sorted out and memories refreshed if necessary. If you conduct a group meeting with witnesses, or when meeting with them individually, consider doing it without your client present. Witnesses will tell you things this way that they may not say if the defendant is in the room.

Time. So often, and for no very good reason, witnesses will get confused about “times.” While one witness may say the group stopped drinking and went home at about 9:30, another will say it was 11:45. Neither is lying, but juries pay attention to these kinds of discrepancies. It may be worthwhile to have the witnesses put together a rough timeline of events for you, and that way they are able to independently refresh their own memories of the times involved.

D. Expert Witnesses

In many cases the defense may want to consider the use of various expert witnesses depending on the facts regarding defendant’s handicap, physical or mental condition or ability to perform the tests.

1. Physicians. A personal physician may be able to corroborate defendant’s alleged handicap or physical disability which caused defendant’s physical or mental condition or prohibited defendant from adequately performing the field sobriety tests. For those unable to afford the testimony of a physician, consider introducing the defendant’s medical records; State v. Robinson, 104 Or App 613 (1990), allows the use of those records to establish or rebut a fact in issue.

Be careful in using physicians at trial. On cross, the prosecution will ask if the symptoms observed by the police (odor of alcohol, slurred speech, etc.) are symptoms of defendant’s medical condition. Unless the doctor is “bullet proof” on cross, use defendant’s medical records to establish his condition.

2. Pharmacists or Pharmacologists. Often the defendant will have taken prescription or nonprescription drugs, and there is an issue concerning their effect when combined with alcohol. Depending on the substance involved and the facts, an expert in this area may be helpful. If it helps you, you can ask the court to take judicial notice of the Physicians Desk Reference (PDR) and the information contained in it concerning the properties of any particular substance. It is best to photocopy the page you are directing the court’s attention to, so the judge has it in front of them.

3. Chemists. A chemist or other expert trained in the operation of the Intoxilyzer or the determination of blood alcohol content may be utilized in attacking the Intoxilyzer results.

4. Ophthalmologists or Optometrists. Pupil reaction, bloodshot eyes and the effects of flashing lights or passing cars on balance and coordination may also be considered.

5. Crime Lab. In some breath test cases, you may want to consider calling a witness from the crime lab on the machine itself, if you want to establish some point that even a state expert will provide for you.

E. Character Witnesses

Rarely used, but don’t overlook the possibility. Consider the character traits of:

• Good driving.

• Moderation with alcohol.

• Honesty.

F. The Defendant as a Witness

Whether or not your client should testify in his or her own defense is a decision that changes in every case. It depends on a number of different factors, including:

1. What does your client have to say?

2. Does your client have a truthful and helpful opinion of his or her own sobriety that evening?

3. Does your client understand what “under the influence” means?

4. Can your client do well under cross-examination? Have another lawyer cross examine if possible to create a less familiar environment.

5. What does your client really have to add, other than the statement “I was not under the influence”? (Always ask yourself: why am I putting my client on in this case?)

6. How is the case going without the testimony?

7. What does the jury expect as far as defense testimony in this case?

8. The “exhibit sticker” client. From time to time you will come across the client who has a physical trait or a personality component that matches exactly what the arresting officer cited as one of the reasons for his opinion that the person was under the influence. Characteristics such s an argumentative personality, a slur to the speech, and general overall confusion are some examples. You want to put these clients on the witness stand if at all possible essentially only to “put an exhibit sticker” on their forehead to let the jury see firsthand that this is “just the way he is.” This can always be a powerful part of any defense case where it applies and it should not be overlooked.

9. Speak with your client about keeping composure, answering the questions asked and how the jury will evaluate his or her testimony.

Every lawyer has their own way of making this decision. One suggested approach is to have the client in a few weeks before the trial to go over trial mechanics and what to expect in the courtroom. In this discussion, explain that you are trying to determine whether or not the client will be testifying in their own defense. Sometimes it is helpful to get out a copy of the jury instruction defining “under the influence of intoxicants” and read it to your client, following up with some general questions about how they would answer certain questions about themselves on the date in question.

It is important to make your client understand that the case can be prepared either way, and testifying is not a critical part. Free your client up to be truthful with you, instead of having to feel like they need to tailor their testimony in any way.

Sometimes your client will quickly tell you, “I cannot testify in this case because I will not be helpful and I don’t want to lie,” or words to that effect. Assure them that you don’t want them to lie, you would never ask them to lie, and that you appreciate their honesty with you now. Explain to them that you will handle the case without their testimony, and that the judge will instruct the jury that no negative inference can be drawn from the fact that they did not take the stand.

On some occasions, you are best off to prepare your client as if they will be taking the witness stand, then leave the decision itself until mid-trial when you are able to take the temperature of the proceedings and see whether or not the testimony is needed or well advised.

Remember, the burden of taking a witness stand and being subjected to direct and cross-examination is a very big one. For those lawyers who have testified from time to time on collateral matters, it is easy to understand that testifying is an uncomfortable job. It is much more uncomfortable for a person accused of a crime. The decision to have your client undertake it should not be taken lightly.

In other cases, it is obvious from the first time the client comes through your door that they will be taking the witness stand. In those cases, thoroughly prepare the client for what to expect from your direct examination and the cross-examination of the prosecutor. Here are some questions that prosecutors will often want to ask a defendant in a DUII case:

1. If 1 is stone cold sober and 10 is falling down drunk, how would you rate yourself on the night you were arrested?

2. Are you telling the jury that this whole episode would have happened just the same way if you had had nothing to drink?

3. You drink alcohol for its effects, don’t you? And yet, are we to understand that after your six beers on this evening, you felt no effects?

4. How do you think you would have done on that test if you had had nothing to drink that evening?

5. Why did you say you had only consumed one beer when you were arrested and now you are telling us you had four? Why did you lie to the police?

6. Why didn’t you take the breath test?

7. Why didn’t you ask for an independent blood test, if you thought the breath test was wrong?

8. What does “under the influence of intoxicants” mean?

9. Are you telling us you felt no effects from the drinks you had?

The list can go on and on, but these questions come up regularly. You may wish to make a copy of this list and go over it with your clients in trial preparation.

Every testifying defendant should know these things:

1. The legal definition of under the influence. In Oregon, the words “drunk” and “intoxicated” have no legal meaning. The word to know is “affected.” Drill your client on this definition so by the time they take the witness stand they will have it committed to memory.

2. How much the defendant had to drink that evening. Every client should have an idea of a number of drinks that represents the outside limits of what they had that evening.

On cross-examination, some prosecutors like to ask the client, “Well, if you’re not sure if it was two or three, it could have been four, couldn’t it? You’re not sure? Maybe four, maybe five—you weren’t counting drinks, were you?” After a little bit of this line of questioning, the client may sound as if s/he had twelve drinks. So, it is best to have the client be able to draw a line and say, “What I am sure of is that I did not have more than x number of beers.” Period. That will cut off this line of cross-examination. (Note: Today’s jurors will lose interest in your defense if that number is over four or five at the outside, so it may be best to revisit the decision on whether or not to call your client to the witness stand if this is what you hear in trial preparation).

3. The legal definition of under the influence: “adversely affected.” Your client may have felt “some effect” of the alcohol consumed, but did not feel his mental or physical faculties were “adversely affected.” The legislature has defined the crime to require more than defendant just being affected. The state must show beyond a reasonable doubt that these affects are adverse. If affected and adversely affected were the same standard, the legislature would never have defined affected with adversely.

4. For clients with a prior DUII arrest or conviction, those people need to be educated and trained as witnesses to make no reference to their prior arrest. If they don’t open the door to this information, the prosecution cannot bring it out. Some people unwittingly may make a mistake in this area in their direct examination, so they need to be told how to avoid “referring to the past.”

Once the decision is made to have your client to testify, then, depending on your style, you may wish to prepare the client in a variety of ways. Have a lawyer friend drop by to cross-examine the client; take your client to the courthouse and find a vacant courtroom the afternoon before your trial so s/he can sit in the witness box and answer questions from there; videotape the client in your office, using office personnel or friends for mock jurors. The only limits to methods of preparing your client for the rigors of testifying are those in your imagination, depending on the client and the case. In any case though, create your own “witness school” or a method of preparing your client that is unique to you. Your goal is to make your client feel as comfortable or more comfortable with the entire courtroom process than any of the other participants involved in it. If you have done that, you’ve done your job of preparing the defendant for his or her trip to the witness stand.

In terms of the substance of their testimony, some of these issues routinely pop up in cross-examination styles of prosecutors around the state:

1. Driving Pattern. The prosecution will generally require the defendant to try and explain any driving irregularities, if he admits to any of them. Since everyone makes a driving mistake now and then, there shouldn’t be any hesitation in doing this. Go over defendant’s driving explanation. If defendant offers no excuse and insists the officer is wrong, look for independent evidence to corroborate defendant.

2. Activities Before Arrest. Defendant will be asked in depth about everything he did and the people he saw prior to his arrest. Defendant should be prepared to go through this on direct and to answer why any people encountered were not called as witnesses.

3. Other Testimony. The prosecutor can no longer ask any witness whether he or she agrees or disagrees with another witness’s testimony. See State v. Isom, 306 Or 587 (1988), and State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621 (1988). Questions to your client like, “Was Officer Smith lying when he told the jury that your eyes were watery and bloodshot?” are impermissible. You may also want to move for a mistrial when and if they are asked. See State v. Watts, 259 Or App 560 (2013).

4. Performance. Defendant should come to a conclusion about his performance on field sobriety tests and be prepared to offer any excuses for poor performance. For example, defendant should be prepared to show how difficult it is for him to get his license out of his wallet. Likewise, defendant should be prepared to exploit any physical handicaps, abnormalities or disabilities. Defendant should never respond that he could not do something sober.

5. Defendant’s Statements. If defendant admitted he was affected by the alcohol, defendant will be questioned about this statement. Defendant should be prepared for the “Weren’t you just a little affected?” question. Maybe the defendant was scared and nervous and didn’t know what he was to say. Defendant certainly wasn’t telling the officer he was guilty of the offense. Remind the jury that it is their opinion, not the defendant’s, that controls.

6. Instruct the defendant not to talk to the police officer prior to or during trial. Restrict defendant’s movements in the presence of the officer. In other words, get there early, seat the defendant and leave him there.

III. Introduction of Defendant’s Statements When Defendant Is Not Present for Trial

On some occasions the defendant will elect to be tried in absentia (although this is legally permitted in Oregon, as a practical matter courts today will reward the defendant’s non-appearance only with a bench warrant). In other cases he or she will elect not to take the witness stand. If the defendant has made favorable statements to the officer, the prosecution will argue that self-serving hearsay declarations are not admissible. The easy way around this objection is to argue that the statements are admissible not for their truth but as evidence of defendant’s clear intellect and coherence. In support of the argument for admissibility, counsel should request a limiting instruction pursuant to OEC, Rule 105. Clearly, such an instruction is advantageous to the defense since the evidence (statements) appears to have the judge’s imprimatur.

IV. Physical and Demonstrative Aids

In addition to independent testimony, the defense case very often should include physical or demonstrative aids. Some examples are listed below.

• Photographs or videotape of the scene. Counsel should always visit the scene with his or her client to get the “lay of the land” and determine whether the area is as described by the officer. Pictures of the scene in conflict with the officer’s description are devastating.

• Was the defendant booked upon his arrest? Often the booking procedures include a photograph of the defendant and the preparation of a medical form. Both these items may refute the officer’s description of defendant’s physical description.

• A cup or glass illustrating the size of the “small” drinks defendant consumed before being stopped.

• The windshield wiper which wasn’t working properly.

• Any items that will explain some problem the client was having that night. For example, the boots that were being worn by the client who is a lineman weigh about 10 pounds. Mark them and offer them. Let the jury feel what he had on his feet when he had slight balance problems.

• Google Maps! Don’t ever forget the value of a document to show any of the geographic features of your case that you want the jury to understand.

• Many areas in the state have enlargement services. In Portland, you can have any document blown up to 2’ x 3’ size in one day for about $30. This helps with jury instructions, certain pages of police reports (if they happen to disclose something that is favorable to your defense), or portions of DMV transcripts that you may have used to impeach the arresting officer.

• Do you have other police reports from the same cop saying the same sorts of things about a different person? Is it similar enough language that it gives the impression that he says it in every case? If so, this may be worth bringing to court for impeachment.

The Prosecution’s Case

By David Eder

I. The Goal of the Prosecution

Driving under the influence cases are fairly straightforward to prepare and present. The state’s case must be based on logic and reason. If the case theory is not logical, it clearly will not rise to beyond a reasonable doubt.

The goal for the prosecution is to build a trusting relationship with the jury during jury selection and not overpromise facts that cannot be delivered during opening statement. The same is true for defense attorneys. However, for prosecutors, during the trial the focus should be on the witnesses. In my opinion, the best prosecutor directs the case but is unnoticed by the jury during his or her case-in-chief. The reasoning is simple: the jurors are going to figure out the prosecutor wasn’t there when the defendant was driving, pulled over, performing tests, back at the police station or hospital or for any part of the interviewing/investigating stage. Any attention placed on the prosecutor is a lost opportunity for focus on the witnesses. At the end of the case, the jury should be basing the verdict on the evidence. And the witnesses and exhibits provide the evidence.

II. The Five Sections of the Prosecution’s Case

A prepared prosecutor breaks down a DUII case into five easy sections.

A. Initial Tactical Decisions

1. Is there reckless driving that could bring in prior DUIIs?

2. Is there a way to get in the Miles instruction?

B. Themes – Driving and Impaired

1. The defendant was driving—no bad driving is required.

2. Drunk driving is not the standard. Check out the uniform jury instructions regarding the definition of impairment.

3. Deciding to convict is hard for a jury. Therefore, the more emphasis on examining the actions rather than the person will make it easier for the jury to convict.

C. Direct – Organizing the Evidence to Prove the Elements of DUII

1. The experience of the officer—build credibility that is either corroborated by blood/breath test or put up against defendant’s refusal and willingness to take all the penalties with a refusal.

2. The driving observed, including how/where the defendant pulled over.

3. The “informal field sobriety tests” or the personal contact between officer and defendant through initial screening before field sobriety tests.

4. The standardized field sobriety tests.

5. Inventory/transport/misc.

6. DUII interview.

7. Observation/breath/blood test.

8. Prior treatment/diversion/conviction.

State v. Berliner, 232 Or App 539 (2009). Admission of specifics from defendant’s prior treatment allowed to prove that defendant was aware that driving under the influence is dangerous behavior for the purpose of any companion charge that includes a reckless state of mind.

State v. Coen, 231 Or App 280 (2009). Defendant’s prior DUII comes in to prove recklessness (if defendant is charged with a crime that has a reckless mens rea) without a balancing test under OEC 403(4). See Chapter 18, Appeals.

D. Redirect

After defense brings up the list of what is missing, get the case back to how those indicators aren’t needed as not everyone shows every sign.

E. Cross-examination – Don’t Ruin Closing Argument Material

1. Field tests—would they have been performed the same without alcohol in system?

2. Breath test—never asked for a breath test. Never said disagreed with the breath test. Never asked to take another test.

3. Refusal—apparently license isn’t important. Took longer suspension period. Hiding something?

4. State v. Tyon, 226 Or App 428 (2009). Bias is never collateral. Defendant had been arrested for DUII before by the same officer and had been acquitted. The officer testified that he thought the client was guilty and had gotten off. He might have a bias based on that and want to be sure that defendant didn’t get off this time.
