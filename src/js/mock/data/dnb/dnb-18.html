2020 edition, with 2024 Update

Appeals

By Bear Wilner-Nugent

I. Basis for Appellate Jurisdiction

Appellate jurisdiction is statutory and therefore limited. A criminal defendant has no constitutional right to appeal. “An appeal is not a matter of absolute right, but a statutory privilege.” State v. Carmickle, 307 Or 1, 6, 762 P2d 290 (1988), citing State v. Endsley, 214 Or 537, 539, 331 P2d 338 (1958). In criminal cases, appellate jurisdiction and procedure are controlled by ORS 138.010 to 138.310. These statutes were comprehensively revised by the 2017 Legislative Assembly. It is important for all appellate practitioners to be familiar with both the new provisions of chapter 138 and with the ORAP 2024 Oregon Rules of Appellate Procedure] (ORAPs), which were most recently revised at the beginning of 2024.

II. Appeals Are Taken from Final Judgments

A defendant’s appeal in a criminal case is taken from a written judgment of conviction, State v. Bonner, 307 Or 598, 600, 771 P2d 272 (1989) (per curiam), which “represents the combined factual and legal determinations that the defendant committed acts constituting a crime and that there is no legal impediment to so declaring; it is the string that ties up the package.” State v. McDonnell, 306 Or 579, 582, 761 P2d 921 (1988) (per curiam).

A judgment is a writing clearly stating the court’s disposition of each charge, including all legal consequences the court establishes or imposes. ORS 137.071(2)(g). A judgment may be appealed if the disposition: (1) imposes a sentence on conviction, (2) awards restitution, or (3) extends or modifies probation or sentence suspension or imposes or executes a sentence after revoking probation or sentence suspension. ORS 138.035.

III. Appeals by the DUII Defendant

On a defendant’s appeal from a trial loss, the appellate court may review any decision of the circuit court in an intermediate order or proceeding, the denial of a motion for new trial based on juror misconduct or newly discovered evidence, the denial of a motion in arrest of judgment, and most sentencing issues. ORS 138.105(3), (4), (7). The appellate court may not, however, review the correctness of a felony sentence within the applicable presumptive range, nor a court’s decision whether or not to impose probation in an optional probation gridblock, nor a sentence resulting from a sentencing agreement to which the parties stipulated. ORS 138.105(8), (9).

Appeals from guilty or no contest pleas are generally limited to sentencing issues. ORS 138.105(5), (7). A defendant may also enter a conditional plea of guilty or no contest pursuant to ORS 135.335(3), taking care to reserve clearly in writing the right to appeal an adverse decision on a specified pretrial motion. ORS 138.105(5)(a). But the appellate court lacks authority to review the validity of a guilty or no contest plea itself. Therefore, clients should be advised that the denial of a motion to withdraw a plea of guilty or no contest is not appealable. ORS 138.105(5).

IV. Appeals by the State

The state may appeal from only the following types of trial court decisions: (1) an order made prior to trial dismissing or setting aside the accusatory instrument, in whole or in part, (2) an order allowing a demurrer, (3) an order arresting the judgment, (4) an order made prior to trial suppressing evidence, (5) an order made prior to trial for the return or restoration of things seized, (6) a sentence, (7) a decision not to award restitution or to award less restitution than the state requested (8) an order in a probation revocation hearing finding that a defendant who was sentenced to probation as a departure from Ballot Measure 11 under ORS 137.712 has not violated a condition of probation by committing a new crime, (9) an order made after a guilty finding dismissing or setting aside all or part of the accusatory instrument, or (10) an order granting a new trial. When the state appeals an order made prior to trial suppressing evidence under ORS 138.045(1)(d), the defendant may then cross-appeal by filing a notice of cross-appeal within 10 days of the expiration of the 30-day period allowed for the filing of the notice of appeal. ORS 138.035(5); ORS 138.071(3).

The state cannot invite suppression or dismissal in order to obtain the right to appeal an underlying non-appealable order. State v. Langlois, 287 Or 503, 505, 600 P2d 872 (1979) (per curiam). An appeal by the state may take so long as to violate the state’s obligation under Article I, section 10, of the Oregon Constitution to bring a defendant to trial “without delay.” State v. Harberts, 331 Or 72, 11 P3d 641 (2000).

V. Notice of Appeal; Record on Appeal; Effect of Appeal

A notice of appeal must generally be served and filed no later than 30 days after the judgment or order from which the appeal is taken was entered in the register. ORS 138.071(1). Unlike the practice in federal court and some other states, in Oregon an original notice of appeal must be filed in the Court of Appeals, not in the trial court. ORS 138.081(3).

Certain kinds of post-trial motion practice can extend this timeline, based on what sort of further proceedings they require in the trial court. ORCP 64 F, made applicable to criminal cases by ORS 136.535(2), requires motions for new trials to be heard and determined by the court within 55 days from the entry of the judgment, and they are deemed conclusively denied if not heard and determined within that time. A motion in arrest of judgment must also be made within the time allowed to file a motion for a new trial. ORS 136.500. There is no similar period after which a motion in arrest of judgment is deemed denied, however. Moreover, it is important to note that a notice of appeal filed before the circuit court rules on the motion in arrest of judgment deprives the circuit court of jurisdiction to rule on the motion. State v. Starr, 210 Or App 409, 150 P3d 1072 (2007).

When such post-trial motions are filed, the 30 days for serving and filing the notice of appeal begins to run from the earlier of (1) the date of entry of the order disposing of the motion or (2) the date on which the motion is deemed denied. ORS 138.071(2). But see State v. Sullens, 314 Or 436, 839 P2d 708 (1992), for a discussion on the reviewability of motions for new trials and State ex rel Schrunk v. Bonebrake, 318 Or 312, 865 P2d 1289 (1994), for a discussion on post-trial motions to set aside accusatory instruments.

The Court of Appeals must allow the defendant leave to file a late notice of appeal if the defendant: (1) files a motion for leave to do so and a notice of appeal no more than 90 days after entry of the order or judgment being appealed, (2) shows by clear and convincing evidence that the failure to file a timely notice is not attributable to the defendant personally, and (3) shows a colorable claim of error in the proceeding from which the appeal was taken. The state must have notice and an opportunity to respond to the defendant’s motion, although in practice it rarely does. Importantly, the denial of a motion for leave to file a late notice of appeal is a bar to post-conviction relief on the defendant’s proffered colorable claim of error unless the court otherwise specifies. ORS 138.071(5).

The required contents of a notice of appeal are found at ORS 19.250 and ORAP 2.05. See also ORS 138.081(1). The appendix to the Oregon Rules of Appellate Procedure contains a form notice of appeal. The original notice of appeal and proof of service should be filed electronically. See generally ORAP chapter 16 and the Oregon Judicial Department’s eFiling page.1 It is imperative when filing a notice of appeal to designate the record, including any recordings that were played during trial or hearings, correctly in order to obtain a transcript of all necessary hearings. ORAP 2.05(6).

A defendant filing a notice of appeal must serve hard copies on (1) the Attorney General via the office of the Solicitor General, 400 Justice Building, 1162 Court Street NE, Salem, Oregon 97301; (2) the district attorney of the county in which the judgment was entered; (3) the trial court administrator; and (4) the trial court transcript coordinator. ORS 138.081(1); ORAP 2.05(10). Service of a notice of appeal on a party, trial court administrator, or transcript coordinator may be accomplished by first class, registered or certified mail, with the date of mailing being the date of service. Proof of the mailing must be certified by the party filing the notice and filed with the court to which the appeal is taken. ORS 19.260(2) (for a complete list of the provisions of ORS chapter 19—which governs civil appeals—that also apply to criminal cases, see ORS 138.015). It is customary to incorporate the proof of service into the body of the notice of appeal, rather than appending it as a separate document. If the appellate attorney is not the trial attorney, it is a good practice to serve a copy of the notice of appeal on the trial attorney as well.

When a criminal defendant appeals from a judgment following a guilty plea, no contest plea, resentencing pursuant to a remand from an appellate court, resentencing from a judgment from a court granting post-conviction relief, or from an order or judgment revoking probation or a sentence suspension, the caption of the notice shall identify the notice as a “Notice of Appeal Pursuant to ORS 138.085,” and the body of the notice shall identify the type of proceeding from which the appeal arises and identify at least one colorable claim of error from the proceeding. ORS 138.085; ORAP 2.40(1). The defendant need not comply with this rule when filing a motion for leave to file a late notice of appeal under ORS 138.071(5), because the late notice procedure already requires that the defendant not only show that the failure to file was not attributable to the defendant personally but also that there is a colorable claim of error in the proceeding from which the appeal is being taken. ORAP 2.40(2).

In any appeal from a trial court, the record on appeal consists of the trial court file, the exhibits, and as much of the record of oral proceedings as has been designated in the notice of appeal. ORS 19.365(2); ORAP 3.05(1). The record of oral proceedings must be a transcript unless the oral proceedings were recorded by audio or video recording equipment and the appellate court has waived preparation of a transcript and ordered that the appeal proceed on the audio or video record alone, which is unheard of in practice. ORS 19.365(2); ORS 19.385; ORAP 3.05(2). The parties may also file an agreed narrative statement in lieu of or in addition to the transcript, but such an agreed narrative statement must be signed by the parties or their attorneys and filed with the trial court administrator within 30 days after the filing of the notice of appeal. ORS 19.380; ORAP 3.05(2). If an appeal cannot be pursued because the record is lost or destroyed through no fault of the appellant, the judgment appealed from may be reversed and a new trial ordered “as justice may require,” ORS 19.420(3), but the appellant must make a prima facie showing of error or unfairness in the proceedings as well as demonstrate that every reasonable effort has been made to secure a substitute for the missing record. State v. Dam, 116 Or App 210, 840 P2d 1317 (1992).

The trial court retains authority even after the notice of appeal is filed to modify its judgment and sentence to correct any arithmetic or clerical errors or to delete or modify any erroneous term in the judgment. A sentencing court entering an amended judgment must immediately forward a copy of the amended judgment to the appellate court. ORS 137.172(1). A judgment that orders payment of restitution but does not specify the amount of restitution imposed is final for the purposes of appealing from the judgment. ORS 138.035(2)(a). The sentencing court retains the authority to file a supplemental judgment specifying the amount of restitution notwithstanding the filing of a notice of appeal. ORS 137.105(1). ORS 137.105(2) and ORAP 3.10(3) require the trial court administrator to immediately send a copy of any judgment entered after the notice of appeal is filed to the defendant (or defendant’s attorney), the state court administrator, the district attorney, and the solicitor general. The defendant must file and serve an amended notice of appeal from any corrected or supplemental judgment if the defendant wishes to assign error to any amendment made by the corrected or supplemental judgment. ORS 138.071(4); ORAP 8.28.

An appeal by the state stays the effect of the judgment or order in favor of the defendant, holding the release agreement and any security for the appearance of the defendant upon final determination of the appeal and subsequent proceedings. Release may be ordered by the trial court pending appeal. ORS 138.295; see also ORS 135.285.

All or part of a sentence may be stayed pending appeal on a defendant’s motion. ORS 138.285. There is no statutory or Oregon constitutional right to bail on appeal, however. Priest v. Pierce, 314 Or 411, 840 P2d 65 (1992).

Suspensions and revocations of non-commercial driver’s licenses based on convictions, including DUII convictions, can be rescinded during the pendency of an appeal based on a defendant’s motion and a trial court’s order. ORS 809.460(1), (2). Affirmance, dismissal, or failure to perfect the appeal results in reinstatement of the suspension when DMV receives notice of such action from the court. ORS 809.460(3). Suspensions of commercial driver’s privileges may not be rescinded until the conviction is reversed on appeal, though. ORS 809.460(4).

VI. Scope of Review and Harmless Error

ORS 138.105(2) provides that on appeal the judgment or order appealed from can be reviewed only as to questions of law appearing on the record. Article VII (Amended), section 3, of the Oregon Constitution requires the appellate courts to affirm the judgments of lower courts regardless of error if, in the opinion of the appellate court, the judgment achieved the correct result. Affirmance is required “if there is little likelihood that any error affected the outcome.” ORS 138.257(2) (codifying the rule in State v. Davis, 336 Or 19, 77 P3d 1111 (2003)). A federal constitutional violation must be shown to be harmless beyond a reasonable doubt in order for the conviction to be affirmed. State v. Walton, 311 Or 223, 231, 809 P2d 81 (1991).

VII. Standard of Review

The appellate courts review trial courts’ legal conclusions regarding suppression issues for legal error, but they are bound by the trial courts’ factual findings so long as they are supported by constitutionally sufficient evidence. State v. Delfino, 281 Or App 725, 727-728, 386 P3d 133 (2016), rev den, 361 Or 525 (2017) (citing State v. Krause, 281 Or App 143, 145, 383 P3d 307 (2016)). If findings are not made on all pertinent historical facts and there is evidence from which those facts could be decided more than one way, an appellate court will presume that the trial court found the facts consistently with its ultimate conclusion. Ball v. Gladden, 250 Or 485, 487-88, 443 P2d 621 (1968).

A similar standard applies to other dispositive motions, such as speedy trial motions. State v. Stinnett, 291 Or App 638, 639, 422 P3d 372 (2018) (citing State v. Johnson, 342 Or 596, 608, 157 P3d 198 (2007), cert den, 552 US 1113, 128 S Ct 906, 169 L Ed 2d 753 (2008)).

The appellate courts review the exclusion of evidence under the Evidence Code for legal error. State v. Scott, 265 Or App 542, 543, 335 P3d 1283 (2014) (citing State v. Beisser, 258 Or App 326, 334, 308 P3d 1121 (2013)). They review relevancy determinations under OEC 401 for legal error, but review the application of OEC 403 for abuse of discretion. State v. Naudain, 300 Or App 222, 227, __ P3d __ (2019) (citing State v. Titus, 328 Or 475, 481, 982 P2d 1133 (1999) and State v. Minchue, 173 Or App 520, 523, 24 P3d 386 (2001)). Whether testimony constitutes scientific evidence is reviewed for legal error. State v. Beltran-Chavez, 286 Or App 590, 610, 400 P3d 927 (2017) (citing State v. Marrington, 335 Or 555, 561-63, 73 P3d 911 (2003)).

The standard for review of a trial court’s denial of a motion for judgment of acquittal is whether, viewing the evidence and the reasonable inferences therefrom in the light most favorable to the state, any rational trier of fact could have found the elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt. State v. Walker, 296 Or App 1, 2, 437 P3d 275 (2019) (citing State v. Rader, 348 Or 81, 91, 228 P3d 552 (2010)); see also Jackson v. Virginia, 443 US 307, 99 S Ct 2781, 61 L Ed 2d 560 (1979) (putting the standard of review in a Fourteenth Amendment due process context).

Review of questions of statutory construction is for legal error. State v. Spainhower, 251 Or App 25, 27, 283 P3d 361 (2012).

The denial of a motion for a mistrial is reviewed for an abuse of discretion. The Court of Appeals will reverse only upon a showing that the defendant was denied a fair trial. State v. Hunt, 297 Or App 597, 600, 442 P3d 232 (2019) (citing State v. Veatch, 223 Or App 444, 454-55, 196 P3d 45 (2008))..

The appellate courts “review a trial court’s refusal to give a requested jury instruction for legal error and view[s] the record in the light most favorable to the requesting party.” State v. Paul, 289 Or App 408, 409, 410 P3d 378 (2017) (citing State v. Oneill, 256 Or App 537, 538, 303 P3d 944, rev den, 354 Or 342, 313 P3d 1126 (2013)). The courts, however, “review a trial court’s choice among legally correct requested jury instructions that provide the necessary information for abuse of discretion.” State v. Moore, 324 Or 396, 427, 927 P2d 1073 (1996).

Appellate courts review trial court decisions regarding whether to merge convictions for errors of law. The appellate court is bound by the trial court’s findings of fact if there is constitutionally sufficient evidence in the record to support them. ORS 138.105(2); State v. Watkins, 236 Or App 339, 345, 236 P3d 770 (2010).

The appellate courts review sentences for errors of law. ORS 138.105(7); State v. Young, 249 Or App 597, 598, 277 P3d 645 (2012).

VIII. Preservation

The Oregon Supreme Court has stated that a matter is sufficiently preserved for appeal if a party provides the trial court with an explanation of the objection that is specific enough to ensure that the court can identify its alleged error and take immediate action to correct it. State v. Wyatt, 331 Or 335, 15 P3d 22 (2000). In determining whether an issue has been presented to the trial court with sufficient specificity, the Court of Appeals asks, “If the trial judge were sitting here today, would he or she be surprised by your argument?” and, “If we were to reverse based on your argument, would the trial judge feel ‘blind-sided’ by our ruling?” State v. Rumler, 199 Or App 32, 41, 110 P3d 115 (2005). In State v. Hitz, 307 Or 183, 766 P2d 373 (1988), the Oregon Supreme Court distinguished between raising an issue at trial, identifying a source for the position, and making a particular argument, saying that the first was essential, the second less so, and the third the least. In State v. Marroquin, 215 Or App 330, 168 P3d 1246 (2007), however, the Court of Appeals reversed a conviction based on a lab report that would have been improperly admitted under the confrontation clause of the Oregon Constitution although the defendant objected only under the federal confrontation clause.

An issue not preserved in the trial court will usually not be considered on appeal, but the appellate court possesses the discretion to consider errors of law apparent on the face of the record. Ailes v. Portland Meadows, Inc., 312 Or 376, 823 P2d 956 (1991); ORAP 5.45(1) & n 1. The error must be one of law that is “obvious, not reasonably in dispute,” and the court must not have to “go outside the record or choose between competing inferences.” State v. Brown, 310 Or 347, 355, 800 P2d 259 (1990). The court must expressly state the basis on which it decided to exercise its discretion to review the unpreserved claim. If the Court of Appeals fails to expressly apply this methodology, the decision to review may itself be reversed by the Supreme Court. State v. Castrejon, 317 Or 202, 856 P2d 616 (1993); State v. Kelsey, 124 Or App 446, 863 P2d 475 (1993) (Court of Appeals declined to exercise its discretion to consider defendant’s unpreserved claims despite a state concession of error); but see State v. Barber, 343 Or 525, 173 P3d 827 (2007) (Supreme Court reversed burglary and theft convictions obtained through a stipulated facts trial when no written jury waiver was obtained and the Court of Appeals had decided that it would not exercise its discretion to review the lack of a jury waiver even though the error was apparent on the face of the record).

Obtaining a ruling is not necessarily a guarantee of a sufficiently preserved record to ensure appellate review. An offer of proof on the record may be required, State v. Affeld, 307 Or 125, 764 P2d 220 (1988), although the nature of the claimed error and the ruling may make it unnecessary. State v. Olmstead, 310 Or 455, 461, 800 P2d 277 (1990). The appellate court may decide not to address an argument that it feels was not adequately presented to the trial court, State v. Sanger, 89 Or App 493, 749 P2d 1202 (1988), or it may decide that a theory raised and preserved in the trial court was waived by failure to adequately raise it at the appellate level. State v. Rivas, 100 Or App 620, 622, 788 P2d 464, rev den, 310 Or 122 (1990). Further, because appellate courts are not confined to choosing only among the arguments for or against a properly identified claim, Cooper v. Eugene School District No. 4J, 301 Or 358, 370 n 12, 723 P2d 298 (1986), a party cannot compel an appellate court to decide a constitutional issue by failing to raise a dispositive statutory issue. State v. Thomas, 311 Or 182, 186 n 1, 806 P2d 689 (1991); State v. Kennedy, 295 Or 260, 266-67, 666 P2d 1316 (1983) (an Oregon court should not let parties by their choice of issues force the court into a position to decide that the state’s government has fallen below a nationwide constitutional standard, when in fact the state’s law when properly invoked meets or exceeds that standard); State v. Spada, 286 Or 305, 309, 594 P2d 815 (1979). Finally, any ambiguities or inconsistencies in the trial court’s rulings will be construed against the party appealing the ruling, so it is incumbent upon that party to require the ruling judge to make a consistent and unambiguous record. City of Portland v. Levi, 98 Or App 341, 779 P2d 192 (1980), on recons, 100 Or App 349, 352–53, 786 P2d 196, rev den, 309 Or 645 (1990).

IX. Sequence of Arguments

The appellate courts analyze statutory issues before reaching constitutional questions, State v. Hitz, 307 Or 183, 188, n 1, 766 P2d 373 (1988), and will analyze state constitutional arguments before federal constitutional arguments. State v. Campbell, 306 Or 157, 162, 759 P2d 1040 (1988). The United States Supreme Court’s interpretations of federal constitutional provisions set the minimum standard with which the states must comply, but such interpretations are not binding upon the Oregon Supreme Court in interpreting the Oregon Constitution. State v. Moore, 334 Or 328, 49 P3d 785 (2002). State statutory and constitutional claims should be raised separately and explicitly to guarantee appellate consideration of state grounds rather than federal grounds. State v. Mendez, 308 Or 9, 19, 774 P2d 1082 (1989). The Oregon Supreme Court may reconsider one of its prior interpretations of Oregon law when a principled argument demonstrates that the ruling was wrongly decided. State v. Birchfield, 342 Or 624, 157 P3d 216 (2007); Stranahan v. Fred Meyer, Inc., 331 Or 38, 54, 11 P3d 228 (2000).

X. Appellant’s Opening Brief

Upon the filing of the notice of appeal, the state court administrator notifies counsel of the case name, case number, and opening brief due date by email. Such information may also be obtained by calling the court at 503-986-5555 in Salem. The appellant’s opening brief must be served and filed within 49 days after (1) the entry of the trial court order settling the transcript, (2) the filing of an agreed narrative statement with the trial court, (3) the settling of the transcript 15 days after its filing by the absence of any motion to correct it, (4) the entry of an appellate court order waiving the transcript, or (5) the filing of the notice of appeal when no transcript, audio record, or narrative statement is designated. ORAP 5.80(1). It is possible to obtain an extension of time by filing a motion for extension of time (MOET) with the court which contains statements about (1) the date the notice was filed, (2) the date the brief is due, (3) the date to which the extension is requested, (4) whether it is a first or other request, (5) the specific circumstances which caused the failure to complete the brief on time, and (6) whether the defendant is incarcerated. ORAP 7.25(2). The motion must also include a statement of opposing counsel’s position on the motion when the motion is one other than a first motion for an extension of 28 days or less. ORAP 7.25(3).

The specifications for briefs are found at ORAP 5.05, including a provision that no opening or answering brief may exceed 10,000 words unless permission for a longer brief is obtained from the court. ORAP 5.05(1). Briefs should be filed and served electronically. See generally ORAP chapter 16 and the Oregon Judicial Department’s eFiling page.

The appellant’s brief should contain: (1) an index of the contents of the brief, including a statement of each assignment of error and the page number at which the assignment may be found, (2) an index of appendices, if any, and (3) an index of all authorities referred to, classified by alphabetically arranged and completely cited cases, constitutional and statutory provisions, texts, treatises, and other authorities, indicating the page numbers where they are cited. ORAP 5.35.

ORAP 5.40 sets out the requirements for the statement of the case which is required at the beginning of each appellant’s brief. The statement of the case is followed by assignments of error and arguments, which are governed by ORAP 5.45.

ORAP 5.45(4) requires that each assignment of error demonstrate that the question or issue presented by the assignment of error was timely and properly preserved in the lower court. Each assignment of error must be followed by the subheading “Preservation of Error,” which must specify and quote the place in the record where the issue presented by the assignment of error was raised and resolved by the lower court. ORAP 5.45(4)(a). Each “Preservation of Error” subheading must be followed by a subheading entitled “Standard of Review,” which must contain a citation of the applicable standard of review for the assignment of error and a citation of legal authority for that standard. ORAP 5.45(5). The “Standard of Review” subheading is followed by the subheading “Argument.” ORAP 5.45(6). The appellate courts may decline to consider any assignment of error that requires the court to search the record to find the error or to determine if the error was properly preserved. ORAP 5.45(4)(a). Relevant portions of ordinances, charters, statutes, and other similar provisions must be set forth verbatim and with proper citation in either the body of the brief, a footnote, or an appendix. ORAP 5.30. At the end of the brief the appellant must include an excerpt of record, which must contain a copy of the final charging instrument, the judgment or order from which the appeal is being taken, the register of actions, and copies of any other documents from the record that are essential or significantly helpful for understanding the argument, set forth in chronological order and consecutively paginated with the first page being “ER-1”. ORAP 5.50(2), (5)(b). The excerpt of record must be preceded by an index which is organized chronologically, describes each item, and identifies both where it may be found in the record and the page where it may be found in the excerpt. ORAP 5.50(5)(b). Pertinent motions must be included in the excerpt of record, but not memoranda of law filed in the trial court, unless the fact that a particular argument was or was not made in the memorandum has independent significance. ORAP 5.50(2)(c), (3). In criminal cases in which the defendant appealed after entering a conditional plea of guilty or no contest under ORS 135.335(3), the defendant must include in the excerpt of record the writing in which the defendant reserved for review on appeal the trial court’s adverse determination of a pretrial motion. ORAP 5.50(2)(f).

XI. Respondent’s Brief

The respondent’s brief follows the form prescribed for the appellant’s opening brief, and contains a concise answer to each of the appellant’s assignments of error preceding its own argument to each. ORAP 5.55(1). Under its statement of the case, the respondent must either specifically accept the appellant’s statement of the case or identify any omissions or inaccuracies, and respondent may cite to additional matters in the record as required. ORAP 5.55(2). If the respondent does not file a brief, the case is submitted on the appellant’s brief and the appellant’s oral argument. ORAP 5.60. The respondent’s brief is due within 49 days after the filing of the appellant’s brief. ORAP 5.80(2). Briefs replying to the respondent’s brief may not be filed in criminal cases without the permission of the court, ORAP 5.70(3)(a), though in practice this permission is granted liberally.

XII. Citations

Rules governing citation form are found at ORAP 5.20. All pertinent questions are addressed in depth in the Oregon Appellate Courts Style Manual (2021 ed.).

XIII. Oral Arguments

A party may present oral argument only if the party has filed a brief. ORAP 6.10(1). The default practice of the Court of Appeals is to consider cases without hearing oral argument, but a party may obtain oral argument upon request by filing a simple form. ORAP 6.05(2). Each party is ordinarily permitted 15 minutes for argument in the Court of Appeals. ORAP 6.15(2)(a). The appellant is allowed to reserve up to five minutes of the time for reply. ORAP 6.15(2)(b). A point raised in the brief is not waived by failure to address it in oral argument, ORAP 6.15(4), so it is better to focus oral argument on a few strong points rather than to spend equal time on a larger number of weaker points.

Oral argument should begin with the words, “May it please the court.” No time should be wasted, however, before moving directly to the strongest points of the argument. The Court of Appeals panel members will be far more familiar with the contents of the briefs than the details of the trial court record, so it is important to be prepared to answer questions concerning what is and what is not in the record.

Do not expect to be able to read a prepared statement. Prepare an outline to cover the major points, but also be prepared to disregard the outline if the judges decide to ask questions. Take the time to understand the questions, then answer them directly. If you do not know the answer, say so.

It is not necessary to use all of the allotted time. The court may stop an advocate who attempts to use reply time to address an argument which was not addressed in the opening oral argument or opposing counsel’s response, so keep an eye on the time in your opening if it is absolutely necessary to address a particular point. Above all, remember that Oregon appellate courts address questions of law rather than questions of fact. If the oral argument depends on the appellate court having to find facts contrary to those found in the trial court in a criminal case, it will not succeed.

The appellate courts discourage reference at oral argument to authorities not cited in a brief or a pre-argument memorandum of additional authorities (ORAP 5.85). Counsel should inform opposing counsel at the earliest practicable time if counsel wishes to refer in oral argument to an authority not previously cited, and the court may give leave to the opposing party to file a post-argument responsive memorandum if it allows such reference. ORAP 6.15(6).

XIV. Decisions

The Court of Appeals is not required to write full opinions in every case. ORS 19.435. The court may reverse, affirm, or modify the judgment appealed from, or it may order a new trial. ORS 138.240. The court may also remand the case for findings of historical fact. State v. Wise, 305 Or 78, 81, 749 P2d 1179 (1988). The state court administrator enters the original appellate judgment and sends copies to the parties not less than 35 days after the date of the decision by the Court of Appeals in order to allow the filing of a petition for review by the Oregon Supreme Court. ORAP 14.05(3)(a). A party may move for an immediate issuance of the appellate judgment, however, upon a showing that no party intends to file a petition for review, petition for attorney fees, or any other thing requiring a judicial ruling. ORAP 14.05(3)(d).

2024 Update

Recently, the Court of Appeals adopted new procedures whereby many criminal appeals will be disposed of through nonprecedential opinions. Only opinions that materially advance the development of the law are now citable as precedent. Nonprecedential opinions are clearly labeled as such. The court can still affirm without opinion, but does so much less often now. The Ninth Circuit has long used similar procedures.

Beginning as early as 2022, the Court of Appeals may adopt new procedures whereby most criminal cases will be disposed of through nonprecedential opinions. If the court adopts these procedures, only opinions that materially advance the development of the law will be published and citable as precedent. The point of these changes would be to eliminate the practice of affirming without opinion. The Ninth Circuit already uses similar procedures.

[End 20222 Update]

XV. Petitions for Reconsideration

A party may file a petition for reconsideration in the Court of Appeals pursuant to ORAP 6.25, but the grounds for such reconsideration are limited to: (1) a claim of factual error in the order or opinion, (2) a claim of error in the procedural disposition of the appeal requiring correction or clarification to make the disposition consistent with the holding or rationale of the opinion or the posture of the case below, (3) a claim of error in the designation of the prevailing party or award of costs, (4) a claim that there has been a change in the statutes or case law since the Court of Appeals decision, or (5) a claim that the Court of Appeals erred in construing or applying the law. Claims that were already argued in the briefs and addressed by the Court of Appeals are disfavored in petitions for reconsideratio. ORAP 6.25(1)(e) A petition for reconsideration must be filed within 14 days of the Court of Appeals decision. ORAP 6.25(2). The filing of a petition for reconsideration is not a prerequisite for filing a petition for review with the Oregon Supreme Court, ORAP 6.25(3), but the time for filing a petition for review with the Oregon Supreme Court does not begin to run until the petition for reconsideration is decided. ORAP 9.05(2)(b).

XVI. Petitions for Review

A party seeking to have the Oregon Supreme Court review a Court of Appeals decision must file a petition for review within 35 days of the date of the Court of Appeals decision. ORAP 9.05(2)(a). The petition may not exceed 5,000 words in length and is in the form of a brief prepared in conformity with ORAP 5.05 and ORAP 5.35. There is a strict list of requirements for the contents of the cover of a petition for review. ORAP 9.05(3)(a). The petition must contain: (1) a short statement of the historical and procedural facts relevant to the review (but not restating facts correctly stated in the Court of Appeals decision), (2) concise statements of the legal questions presented and the rule of law that petitioner proposes to be established if review is allowed, (3) a statement of specific reasons why the legal questions presented have importance beyond the particular case and require decision by the Oregon Supreme Court, (4) if desired, a brief argument concerning the legal questions presented on review, and (5) a copy of the decision of the Court of Appeals. ORAP 9.05(4).

Criteria for granting discretionary review are listed in ORAP 9.07. It is more important to use a petition for review to argue why the case deserves the Supreme Court’s attention than to argue why you should win the case. That is what merits briefing is for.

A petition for review should be filed electronically but two copies must be physically served on opposing counsel. ORAP 9.05(3)(b). A separate brief on the merits of the review may be filed if the petitioner gave notice of intent to file such a brief in the petition for review, and the petitioner is allowed 28 days from the date review is allowed to file the brief. ORAP 9.17(2)(a). The requirements for such a brief are listed at ORAP 9.17(2)(b). Merits briefs should be filed electronically.

A party may file a response to a petition for review within 14 days of the petition’s filing. ORAP 9.10(2). If no response is filed, the party’s brief in the Court of Appeals will be considered as the response. ORAP 9.10(1).

A petition for review will be allowed if one less than a majority of the judges eligible to vote on the petition vote to allow it. ORAP 9.20(1). If the Oregon Supreme Court allows review, it may request the parties to address specific questions either in writing before oral argument or at oral argument, ORAP 9.20(3), and it may limit the questions on review, ORAP 9.20(2). The parties’ briefs in the Court of Appeals will be considered as the main briefs in the Oregon Supreme Court, supplemented by the petition for review and any response, brief, or memorandum that may be filed. ORAP 9.20(4).

If the petition for review is denied, the appellate judgment will not be issued by the state court administrator for at least 21 days after the petition is denied so that a petition for reconsideration may be filed. ORAP 14.05(3)(b).

XVII. Key Appellate Cases Regarding Diversion

Objections Based on Prior Convictions:

A defendant with a prior conviction may still be able to get into diversion if there is a significant constitutional defect in the prior conviction. State v. Koellermeier, 75 Or App 126, 704 P2d 1169 (1985). Case law since Kollermeier has not made doing so any easier, however. In State v. Probst, 339 Or 612, 124 P3d 1237 (2005), the Oregon Supreme Court abrogated City of Pendleton v. Standerfer, 297 Or 725, 688 P2d 68 (1984), and State v. Grenvik, 291 Or 99, 628 P2d 1195 (1981), on which Kollermeier was based. Under Probst, the standard for making such a “collateral attack” on a prior conviction is essentially the same as in a post-conviction proceeding. A defendant has to show a defect, such as lack of counsel, and then show how that resulted in an involuntary plea. Probst at 629.

State v. Boyer, 87 Or App 643, 743 P2d 1116 (1987). You cannot collaterally attack a prior conviction at a contested diversion entry hearing if the defendant was represented in the case resulting in the prior conviction. You may be able to petition for post-conviction relief in the prior case and thus establish eligibility, however. (Note, though, that all the prerequisites for post-conviction relief must still be met, including the statute of limitations in ORS 138.510(3). For more information, see Post-Judgment and Extraordinary Remedies (OCDLA 2012).)

A prior conviction may not be considered “uncounseled” if the defendant obtained the advice of an attorney, even if that attorney did not appear in court with the defendant. See State v. Conklin, 87 Or App 669, 743 P2d 779 (1987).

State v. Maynard, 85 Or App 631, 738 P2d 210 (1987). The assistance of counsel on a prior diversion is moot, as the prior diversion itself is a disqualifier.

State v. Olson, 91 Or App 312, 754 P2d 630 (1988). Uncounseled “out-of-state” conviction(s) may be collaterally attacked and do not bar eligibility.

State v. Barnes, 92 Or App 355, 758 P2d 419 (1988). Diversion may not be denied based on anything defendant was required to do as a result of an uncounseled conviction. This is when the DAs object because defendant went through the old rehabilitation program on a DUII infraction.

State v. Manfredonia, 105 Or App 537, 805 P2d 738 (1991). It may be, and some judges have held, that it is the defendant’s burden to establish that the prior conviction was uncounseled. Thus, when the defendant testified about eligibility, the DA should have cross-examined about the prior. An open question is whether the DA can call the defendant as a state’s witness at the contested diversion entry hearing. If the DA has not given you discovery of the prior conviction, you can call the DA as your witness first to avoid a possible perjury charge on your client.

State v. Meyrick, 313 Or 125, 831 P2d 666 (1992). Is there a record that your client’s prior waiver of counsel and subsequent conviction passed constitutional muster? In Meyrick the court held that a colloquy on the record between the trial court and defendant wherein the court, in some fashion, explains the risks of self-representation is the preferred means of assuring that a defendant understands the risks of self-representation. So if your client waived counsel and was convicted in a municipal court or some other court where there is no record, you may have a shot at getting your client into diversion.

A written waiver of counsel form from the prior plea, however, may well suffice to establish the validity of the prior conviction, making close reading of a plea transcript or other inquiry unnecessary. State v. Forrest, 213 Or App 151, 159 P3d 1286 (2007).

The Legislature’s 2009 amendment of ORS 813.215, which increased the diversion ineligibility look-back period from 10 to 15 years, could be applied retroactively without violating the state or federal constitutions’ ex post facto clauses. State v. Carroll, 253 Or App 265, 290 P3d 864 (2012).

A trial court’s decision to set aside a judgment of dismissal and enter a judgment of conviction for violating a diversion agreement when it later came to the court’s attention that the defendant was accused of a new DUII during an extension of the diversion period was held to be unreviewable on appeal. State v. Landahl, 254 Or App 46, 292 P3d 646 (2012).

For more on this subject, see Chapter 6, Diversion.
