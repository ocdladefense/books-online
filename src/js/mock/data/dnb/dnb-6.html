Read the complete DUII Diversion statutes.

Diversion is a statutory program whereby certain offenders can earn a dismissal of the DUII charge. Instead of heading to trial and possibly negotiating a guilty plea, Oregon law allows certain first-time offenders (see below for eligibility) the option of diversion.

In spite of many recent legislative changes, the basics of diversion have remained the same: defendants enter a plea of guilty, pay a filing fee, and have a year to do an evaluation, complete treatment, and attend a victim’s impact panel. A few additional requirements such as only drive a vehicle with a validly installed ignition interlock device and refrain from using intoxicants during the diversion agreement now apply. At the end of the diversion if they have successfully met the requirements, the charge is dismissed. Although the charge is dismissed the arrest stays on the record because it is non-expungeable if dismissed as a result of the DUII diversion program.

If unsuccessful, the conviction is entered and the defendant is sentenced. Thus, it is no surprise that so many defendants opt for diversion instead of a trial. Diversion agreements account for 43 percent of DUII offenses every year. See DUII Data Book for Oregon Counties, 2009–2013 (Oregon Dept. of Transportation, still the most current edition).

Because a DUII cannot be dismissed by plea negotiations (see ORS 813.170), the best result for a DUII case may be an entry into diversion. While other states may allow a “DUI/DWI” charge to be reduced to a “wet reckless,” Oregon does not. Also, a DUII conviction in Oregon requires a judge to impose the same requirements found in diversion of the evaluation, treatment, and victim’s impact panel. ORS 813.020. This means that a person who chooses diversion completes the same basic requirements they would have completed had they been convicted and put on probation – without the jail, without the bigger fine, without the mandatory license suspension, and without the permanent conviction on their record.

Thus, a diversion entry is often the best result for a DUII charge in that it results in a dismissal of the charge. Whether or not a particular defendant should enter diversion depends on the facts of the case and ultimately on the defendant’s choice.

Practice Tip

If your diversion-eligible client decides to take their case to trial, consider having them sign an acknowledgement that you advised them of their option to enter diversion and they declined it and chose to try the case. An example of a diversion eligible case that you may want to take to trial is a cannabis DUII where your client had not recently used cannabis.

1. The current diversion statute was enacted in 1981 and has been amended many times since then. It seems that every legislature makes changes to the DUII and diversion statutes. See Appendix A for the complete statute(s).

2. Diversion agreements by law are available at all courts in the state (ORS 813.200(2)). The Oregon Judicial Department also posts the forms on the website for download. Some jurisdictions have their own diversion forms so check with the court on which form is preferred.

3. If your client is in the military and facing the possibility of being called to active duty the court cannot deny entry into diversion on the grounds that the potential deployment may affect your client’s ability to complete the diversion requirements.

813.220(12) has been added to the “matters to be considered by the court in determining to allow a diversion agreement.” This subsection states that the court may not deny a diversion petition solely on the basis that Petitioner is a member of the Armed Forces of the United States, the reserve components of the United States or the National Guard and has been called or demonstrates that Petitioner will be called to active duty, and the military service will impair Petitioner’s ability to complete the Diversion Program. [2011 c. 197 sec. 1].

4. In keeping with the legislature’s intent to accommodate men and women who are on active military status, 813.233 was added to the diversion statute allowing for participation in a comparable treatment program authorized “by a government entity in another jurisdiction.” While the DUII evaluator’s offices in many counties have routinely allowed for civilian diversion petitioners to participate in out-of-state treatment programs so long as those programs comport with Oregon’s treatment requirements, 813.233 seems to allow for a little more flexibility in terms of the type of treatment program that a service member participates in while deployed in another jurisdiction.

5. ORS 813.240 The current diversion filing fee is $490. See ORS 813.240.[2012 c. 81 sec. 4&5].

6. The ignition interlock device. The ignition interlock device requirement is not actually found within the diversion statutes (e.g., 813.200 et seq.). Rather, that code provision is found at ORS 813.602(3), which specifically requires:

(3)(a) Except as provided in paragraph (b) of this subsection, the court shall require as a condition of a driving while under the influence of intoxicants diversion agreement that an approved ignition interlock device be installed and used in any vehicle operated by the person during the period of the agreement when the person has driving privileges.

Nothing in this statute actually requires a person in diversion to install such a device. Rather, it is written as a limitation on the vehicles a person in diversion may drive when they have driving privileges. The exceptions to this requirement include a medical exception, ORS 813.602(7), and an employment exception, ORS 813.606. These forms are available in Chapter 3, The File, as Appendix F and G.

2015 HB 2660/SB 397 made several changes to the diversion law regarding the ignition interlock device requirement aspect of the diversion agreement.

HB 2660, Sec 1 Section 1 (as amended by Section 11 of SB 397): Gives the court in a DUII diversion case discretion whether to order IID if the defendant submitted to a chemical test of his or her breath, blood or urine with result less than .08 percent by weight and the chemical test detected presence of alcohol only.

SB 397 Section 2: Imposes duty on provider who installed IID to notify court or court’s designee, and DA or city prosecutor of the occurrence of a “negative report.” Defines “negative report.” Authorizes ODOT to further define by rule “test violation.” Requires provider to download all reports each time provider has access to IID; provider shall submit “negative reports” within seven days of download.

SB 397 Section 3: Person with IID may not have it removed until person submits to ODOT a certificate issued by provider of IID that device did not record a negative report for the last 90 consecutive days; suspension period shall continue until person submits said certificate.

SB 397 Section 4: Person in DUII diversion program may move to vacate IID requirement if person can establish absence of negative report for at least six consecutive months and compliance with treatment program. Requires evidentiary hearing, findings by court by preponderance of evidence. Typically you can file a motion and order with the 6 month certificate and completion of outpatient treatment as exhibits and ask the court to remove the IID requirement. Often the DA will not object so send the proof to the DA and ask for their position before filing.

SB 397 Section 7: Streamlines fee waiver provisions for costs of leasing, installing and maintaining IID.

The employer exemption may also apply when the client is driving employer owned vehicles.

II. Plea/Waiver of Rights

1. DUII diversion agreements must be accompanied by a plea of guilty or no-contest. ORS 813.200(4)(a) (effective January 1, 2004). The plea may only be accepted as part of the diversion agreement, so clients do not have to worry that the judge will deny the petition and then find them guilty. Additionally, entry of conviction is withheld until such time as the diversion agreement is terminated. See the diversion agreement listed in Appendix C. Thus, a client who has entered Diversion may reply that they have not been convicted of the charge.

2. Defendants waive former jeopardy rights as to other charges based on the same criminal episode. ORS 813.200(4)(g). This means that while the DUII charge can be resolved in diversion, any other remaining charges will need to either be negotiated with the state or will need to go to trial. Some counties have enacted regular procedures whereby some additional charges such as Reckless Driving or Failure to Perform the Duties of a Driver can similarly be dismissed.

3. Lawyers need to be aware that speedy trial waivers mean that when old cases come back from warrant status, the waiver may prevent the case from being dismissed for speedy trial reasons. See, e.g., State v. McQueen, 153 Or App 277 (1998) (46-month delay was caused by defendant and speedy trial did not mandate dismissal).

III. Costs of DUII Diversion Program

Although the specific costs to an individual will vary, each one will invariably include: the filing fee ($490), the diagnostic assessment ($150 plus a monitoring fee if applicable), the victim’s impact panel (no more than $65), treatment (whatever that costs, is sometimes covered by insurance), and lawyer’s fees.

Defendants agree to pay court-appointed attorney fees as part of the diversion agreement. ORS 813.200(4)(i). However, the court may still waive all or part of the court-appointed attorney fees.

The fee for the diagnostic assessment is $150 and shall be made to the agency performing it. ORS 813.240(2). Practitioners should be aware and inquire locally of procedures and standards to waive some or all of this fee with their local diagnostic assessment agency.

The addition of the interlock ignition device requirement has caused some administrative problems. Some counties have used their probation departments to supervise this requirement, while other counties’ court administrators have authorized the court’s designated evaluating agency to charge extra fees to those who opt to install an ignition interlock device.

If the defendant has posted bail or security, counsel may move the court to apply it against the diversion fee. If it is not so applied, the bail or security is continued on deposit with the court until the conclusion of the case and may be applied in the event the defendant’s diversion agreement is terminated and the defendant is returned to the court for prosecution. Some counties automatically apply bail/security to any outstanding monies owed, such as diversion fees.

Note: Be careful. Such a request may need to be done in advance of a “show-cause” hearing. See, e.g., State v. Vargas-Garcia, 217 Or App 70 (2007), where defendant admitted he had not paid and asked for security to be applied to the fees, the court concluded that termination was lawful result.

ORS 813.200(4)(c) provides that in addition to the filing fee, the defendant must pay (according to the defendant’s ability) the expenses of the program of treatment indicated necessary by the diagnostic assessment. The cost of this program may vary from county to county and may depend upon the defendant’s ability to pay. Counsel should be prepared to inform the defendant as to the range of availability within the local area, and the potential to apply at the program for partial state funding based on indigence. See ORS 813.270(1) relative to the Intoxicated Driver Program Fund. If your client has the benefit of health insurance it is possible that client’s insurance may pay for some of the costs of the treatment program. The Affordable Care Act will likely assist many defendants with their treatment costs as they go through diversion.

The diversion agreement also authorizes the court to order attendance at a victims’ panel if the county has a victim impact program at a cost of no less than $5 and no more than $50. ORS 813.235. Note that the operative word in ORS 813.235 is “may.” Some treatment providers have indicated that the victim impact panel causes sufficient emotional discomfort to cause some with serious addiction problems or certain psychological conditions to relapse. Thus, it may be necessary to ask the court to waive the normal victim impact panel requirement.

Each defendant should be aware that he or she may be subject to the fines and fees on a conviction for the DUII if he or she is not successful. Such fees include mandatory minimum of $1,000 for a first offense and go up $500 for each subsequent offense. There is a minimum fine of $2,000 for a person convicted of DUII who had a BAC of .15% or greater. Additionally, the maximum fine for a DUII is $10,000 if the offense was committed in a motor vehicle and there was a passenger in the motor vehicle who was under the age of 18 and at least three years younger than the driver. See ORS 813.010(6)&(7).

Practice Tip

If your client has a problem with gambling, he may be able to complete gambling treatment at the same time as the drug/alcohol treatment, for which certain state funding may be available. See ORS 413.520 & 413.522.

One of the issues involving DUII diversion that should be remembered is the costs associated with completing the program. In the diversion agreement, the client is required to agree to pay the costs and fees associated with participating in diversion. A judge is allowed to consider the client’s ability to pay and may reduce or eliminate some of the fees. The judge is not, however, required to consider the client’s ability to pay when setting the fees. This is in contrast to the requirement that the court make a determination of a defendant’s ability to pay when fixing attorney fees in other criminal cases.

The Court of Appeals dealt with arguments involving judges’ misunderstanding of the law that they could consider ability to pay when setting fee amounts. In both State v. Soto, 268 Or App 822 (2015), and State v. Johnson, 269 Or App 497 (2015), the Court of Appeals held the trial courts misinterpreted the law in deciding they did not have discretion to waive DUII diversion fines and fees. Despite this misinterpretation, the Court of Appeals also stated failure to consider ability to pay is not appealable because imposition of the fines and fees did not expose the defendants to a greater sentence (fine/fee) than is allowed by law.

Thus, it is important to remember that although a judge’s refusal to reduce fees and fines may not be appealable, the judge does have the authority to consider it and do so. We, therefore, should be educating our judges that they do have the power to hear argument for reduction in fees and fines and may reduce or eliminate these costs.

IV. Procedures for Filing the Petition

1. ORS 813.210(1) provides that a petition for the DUII diversion agreement must be filed within 30 days after the date of the defendant’s first appearance on the summons, unless a later filing date is allowed by the court upon a showing of good cause. Good cause may be found to exist: 1) where a defendant appears without counsel in a county justice court and requests that the case be removed to the county district court, 2) where the defendant is later appointed counsel for the first time based on indigency, or 3) when discovery has not been received. Legislative history reveals that the 30-day period was designed to give the defendant an opportunity to review discovery. Another example of good cause to extend the 30-day deadline might be for the purpose of filing a petition for post-conviction relief relative to a prior DUII conviction which would otherwise disqualify the defendant from the diversion program. Another example is where a defendant had two pending charges for DUII and the other charge was dismissed or acquitted. In such cases, the state’s argument has often been that the petition was not filed within 30 days. The creative practitioner will file within the first 30 days to get around this future argument. Additionally, lawyers should be careful not to trust the court dates and should actually file the petition within the thirty days, as the diversion entry dates set by the court are not always within the 30-day period.

The filing of a demurrer, motion to suppress or motion for an omnibus hearing does not constitute good cause. ORS 813.210(1)(a).

ORS 813.200(c) allows the filing of the petition up to 14 days from the date the prosecuting attorney sends the laboratory test results of blood/urine test.

Note: Always file the petition and serve a copy on the prosecutor within 30 days of arraignment or eligibility. This triggers the prosecutor’s 15-day timeline to file an objection and to request a hearing. ORS 813.210(5). If no hearing is requested within the timeline, the court shall determine whether to allow or deny the petition. ORS 813.220(1).

2. The trend has been for the court to inform the defendant of the diversion program and provide petition forms at an initial appearance, setting an arraignment and possible filing of the petition a short time later. Such a procedure often excludes court-appointed counsel for the indigent if the defendant petitions for diversion.

3. A copy of the petition must be served upon the district attorney, who may, within 15 days after the date of service, file with the court a written objection to the petition and request a hearing. ORS 813.210(4). The rules of evidence do not apply at such a hearing. ORS 40.015(4)(I). However, one can argue that due process considerations place certain limits on the prosecution’s use of hearsay. See, e.g., State v. Johnson, 221 Or App 394 (2008), using the due-process balancing test of [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=2547302101631979186&q=U.S.+v.+Comito,+177+F3d+1166+(9th+Cir.,+1999)&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38 U.S. v. Comito, 177 F3d 1166 (9th Cir., 1999)].

a. ORS 813.250(4) provides that no statement made by the defendant about the offense with which the defendant is charged shall be offered or received in evidence in any criminal or civil action or proceeding arising out of the same conduct which is the basis of the charge of driving while under the influence of intoxicants if this statement was made during the course of the rehabilitation program and to a person employed by the program.

b. Statements made by the defendant in court at the district attorney’s objection hearing to the petition might later be used against the defendant, however.

Note: The district attorney may object on specific grounds. You may be able to force the district attorney to identify the basis for objection under a due process notice argument. If they are forced to elect or do elect, then object to their changing the basis after the time has expired.

V. Eligibility for Diversion

Eligibility is governed by statute and is provided for in ORS 813.215.

The contents of the diversion petition include a sworn statement that the defendant meets the criteria set out in ORS 813.215. See ORS 813.200(4)(i). Note that although a judge “shall deny the petition if defendant failed to appear at arraignment,” ORS 813.220(6), a defendant need not swear about appearing at the arraignment.

Practice Tip

When in doubt, openly litigate. If you have doubts as to a legal decision regarding a client’s potential eligibility concerning, for example, prior “treatment” in conjunction with a legal matter, such matters should be addressed prior to or at the time of entry into diversion so that a prosecutor is barred by the doctrine of res judicata from moving at a later date to revoke the diversion based on a previously undisclosed ineligibility.

A. Prior Treatment

State v. Warrington, 219 Or App 566, 184 P3d 1160 (2008). Defendant’s own voluntary course of alcohol rehabilitation treatment eight years prior did not disqualify her from being eligible for diversion. A disqualifying “similar alcohol or drug rehabilitation program” is one in which a defendant participates to avoid a substantial, judicially imposed adverse consequence.

State v. Wright, 204 Or App 724, 131 P3d 838 (2006). Defendant’s participation in treatment as ordered by a judge in juvenile court in regard to her custodial rights was sufficiently similar so as to preclude her eligibility for diversion.

State v. Young, 196 Or App 708, 103 P3d 1180 (2004). Defendant’s participation in a county “STOP” drug rehabilitation program was sufficiently similar to preclude eligibility for diversion.

State v. Dunbrasky, 122 Or App 90, 856 P2d 1054 (1993). Court-ordered participation in a drug and alcohol treatment program as part of a conviction for possession of methamphetamine was sufficiently similar to preclude eligibility for diversion because it was accepted by defendant in exchange for a milder sanction.

State v. Schatz, 225 Or App 688, 202 P3d912 (2009). Where defendant is convicted of a prior PCS and received a presumptive sentence, there is no proof that he participated in a similar program to “avoid a substantial, judicially imposed adverse consequence.”

State v. Parker, 235 Or App 40, 230 P3d 55 (2010). Where defendant previously “agreed” to undergo a drug evaluation and treatment for a prior drug conviction but then failed to complete the evaluation and failed to engage in treatment, defendant had not previously “participated” in a similar treatment program. Note: the trial court did not mention anything about exercising its discretion under 813.220 to deny diversion on the grounds that defendant was unlikely to comply with treatment. If your client is in a similar fact situation as Parker, be prepared to argue why your client is now ready and willing to participate in treatment. It is advisable for the client to enter treatment or attend AA voluntarily to demonstrate their commitment to participate.

State v. Bentley, 239 Or App 18, 243 P3d 859 (2010). Diversion denied where defendant had been ordered to complete treatment as a condition of a prior DUII conviction within the previous ten-year period.

State v. LaGrassa, 235 Or App 150, 230 P3d 96 (2010). Diversion denied where defendant had been previously ordered to undergo drug and alcohol treatment as a condition of post-prison supervision. The court rejects the distinction between treatment imposed administratively by the Department of Corrections or the court. Treatment is “similar” if it is imposed by a government actor to avoid a substantial adverse consequence. As a practice tip, it is a good idea to examine if the treatment was in fact similar as to what Oregon defines as treatment under OAR 415-054-0020. So compare what your client actually did because you might be able to aruge it is not similar to treatment in Oregon.

B. Commercial Driver’s License (CDL)

The 2005 Legislative Assembly added a requirement that a defendant not “have” a commercial driver license at the time of the offense. The 2007 legislature amended this provision again, striking “have” and replacing it with “holds”. This appears to be in light of State v. Orueta, 343 Or 118, 164 P3d 267 (2007), which affirmed that defendant was not eligible for diversion as he “had” a commercial driver license (CDL), and that the statutory provision did not violate constitutional prohibitions.

ORS 813.215(1)(h) states “the defendant did not hold a commercial driver license on the date of the commission of the offense.” In State v. Crisafi, 271 Or App 267 (2015), the Court of Appeals clarified its reading of the statute and the term “hold.” The court ruled that a person who has an active commercial drivers license (CDL) is ineligible to enter a DUII diversion program, even if the CDL is invalid and subject to cancellation. In Crisafi, the defendant argued he was eligible for diversion because ODOT improperly renewed his CDL without the requisite medical certificate. The court held that the validity of the CDL is not the correct question. The correct question when a CDL has been issued is whether the CDL has “expired or been canceled or revoked.” A CDL that is “subject to cancellation” has not been canceled for the purposes of ORS 813.215(1)(h).

C. Physical Injury

The state often objects to a DUII entering into diversion based on either “physical injury” and/or an additional charge of Assault IV. This is an ongoing procedural problem that has yet to be conclusively addressed by the appellate courts. Options to consider include: a) moving to enter diversion initially, based on lack of proof of physical injury, or b) severing the DUII charge in order to reserve the right to enter diversion once acquitted on the Assault IV. Notably, in State v. Romanov, 210 Or App 198, 149 P3d 1224 (2006), the defendant won the eligibility hearing by distinguishing the DUII from the facts of the Assault IV, which were alleged to have happened in his garage, a premise not open to the public. Thus, having won the eligibility by differentiating the elements of the charges, a prosecution of the Assault IV was not barred by the doctrine of issue preclusion, as defendant had already proved it was a separate issue. This means that issue preclusion applies when the state argues causation and fails to prove the injury at a hearing on the merits.

For definition of physical injury see State ex rel Juv. Department v. Salmon, 83 Or App 238, 730 P2d 1285 (1986).

VI. Objections Based on Prior Convictions

A. A defendant with prior convictions may still be able to get into diversion if there is a defect with the prior conviction.

State v. Koellermeier, 75 Or App 126, 704 P2d 1169 (1985). The issue is whether the prior conviction was constitutional, similar to the standard for post-conviction relief. However, case law since Kollermeier has developed some distinctions and specific standards. Note that in State v. Probst, 339 Or 612, 124 P3d 1237 (2005), the Oregon Supreme Court abrogated State v. Grenvik, 291 Or 99, 628 P2d 1195 (1981), and City of Pendleton v. Standerfer, 297 Or 725, 688 P2d 68 (1984), upon which Kollermeier was based. Under Probst, the standard of making such a “collateral attack” on a prior conviction is essentially the same as in a post-conviction proceeding. A defendant has to show a defect, such as lack of counsel, and then show how that resulted in an involuntary plea. Probst at 629.

1. State v. Boyer, 87 Or App 643, 743 P2d 1116 (1987). You cannot collaterally attack prior conviction at a contested entry hearing if the defendant was represented in the prior conviction. However, you may be able to PCR it and thus establish eligibility. A prior conviction may not be considered “uncounseled” if the defendant obtained the advice of an attorney even if that attorney did not appear in court with the defendant. See State v. Conklin, 87 Or App 669, 743 P2d 779 (1987).

2. State v. Maynard, 85 Or App 631, 738 P2d 210 (1987). The assistance of counsel on a prior diversion is moot, as the prior diversion itself is a disqualifier.

3. State v. Olson, 91 Or App 312, 754 P2d 630 (1988). Uncounseled “out-of-state” conviction(s) may be collaterally attacked and do not bar eligibility.

4. State v. Manfredonia, 105 Or App 537, 805 P2d 738 (1991). It may be, and some judges have held, that it is the defendant’s burden to establish that the prior conviction was uncounseled. This case said that when the defendant testified about eligibility the DA should have cross-examined about the prior. An open question is whether the DA can call the defendant as their own witness. If the DA has not given you discovery of the prior conviction, you can call the DA as your witness first to avoid a possible perjury charge on your client.

5. State v. Barnes, 92 Or App 355, 758 P2d 419 (1988). Diversion may not be denied based on anything defendant was required to do as a result of an uncounseled conviction. This is when the DAs object because defendant went through the old rehabilitation program on a DUII infraction.

6. State v. Meyrick, 313 Or 125, 831 P2d 666 (1992). Is there a record that your client’s prior waiver of counsel and subsequent conviction passed constitutional muster? In Meyrick the court held that a colloquy on the record between the trial court and defendant wherein the court, in some fashion, explains the risks of self-representation as the preferred means of assuring that a defendant understands the risks of self-representation. If your client waived counsel and was convicted in a municipal court or some other court where there is no record, you may have a good shot of getting your client into diversion in that situation.

B. Is the prior conviction sufficiently similar?

State v. Guzman, 366 Or 18 (2019), restricts the prior court rulings on what counts as a counterpart. Examine the out-of-state prior conviction to see how the elements match the Oregon legal standard. The standard in Colorado of driving while impaired to the slightest degree was sufficiently dissimilar to Oregon’s noticeable/perceptible degree standard and therefore did not count as a prior conviction. Likewise, a Kansas statute that said driving or attempting to drive while impaired was not the statutory counterpart to Oregon because attempted DUII is not the same as DUII in Oregon.

VII. Factors Determining Petitioner’s Suitability for Diversion—813.220

Matters to be considered by court in determining to allow diversion agreement; reasons for denial. The court will look at a number of factors in determining not only petitioner’s eligibility but also their suitability for diversion. Subsections 2 through 5 enumerate the discretionary factors that a court may consider in either allowing or denying a diversion petition. If you have a good argument why your client should get into diversion over the state’s objection on one issue, be prepared to argue why the court should not exercise its discretion to deny diversion based on any one of the discretionary factors. Getting into treatment early satisfies several of the following in your client’s favor.

VIII. Right of Victim to be Present at Hearing—ORS 813.222

If a DUII results in damage to property to one other than the defendant, the victim of the property damage shall be notified by the district attorney or city attorney that the defendant may be eligible for diversion and that if there is a hearing on a petition for diversion, the victim has a right to be present and heard at the hearing.

IX. Duration of Agreement—ORS 813.230–813.250

1. The agreement is for a period of one year. ORS 813.230(3).

2. When the court denies a petition for diversion, it shall continue the offense proceeding against the defendant. ORS 813.230(4). The plea petition cannot be used against the defendant if a court denied it.

3. At the conclusion of the period of diversion, the defendant may move for an order dismissing the charge with prejudice. ORS 813.250(1).

4. If the defendant does not move for dismissal with prejudice, six months after the conclusion of the diversion period if the court finds the defendant fully complied with and performed the conditions of the diversion agreement and with notice to the district attorney or city attorney, the court may on its own motion enter an order dismissing the charge of DUII with prejudice. ORS 813.250(3). See the form at Appendix D.

X. Petition for Extension of Diversion Period—ORS 813.225

1. Within 30 days prior to the end of the period for the DUII diversion agreement, a defendant may petition the court for an extension to complete the DUII diversion program. Each defendant is allowed only one extension, and the maximum extension period is 180 days.

Practice Tip

Always request 180 days when requesting an extension. Make sure to file the request for an extension prior to the end of the year. You only get one request, so ask for the maximum. Also note: some courts will set a review hearing close to or prior to the end of the 180 days. Make sure to count the actual days, in that such a review hearing may be before the expiration of the 180 days.

State v. Maul, 341 Or 80, 136 P3d 1123 (2006). The maximum length of extension is 180 days and a court does not have authority to extend or “toll” beyond that time.

2. ORS 813.225 has been amended to accommodate diversion participants that are serving in the military on active duty. A petitioner on active duty may ask the court for an extension at any time prior to the end of the diversion period. ORS 813.225(1)(b). An extension granted to service personnel under this section may be extended by the court as necessary to allow defendant sufficient time to complete diversion. 813.225(7). It would seem as if the court is not bound by the absolute 180-day rule of 813.225(5) when granting an extension under this subsection.

However, in order for the court to grant an extension under subsection (7) petitioner must be able to show:

• They are a member of the Armed Forces of the United States, Reserves or National Guard;

• They are on active duty or will be called to active duty; and

• That military service will impair petitioner’s ability to complete the conditions of the diversion agreement and no comparable treatment program under 813.233 is available.

The crux of subsection (7)(c), supra, is that if a petitioner is called to active duty but is perhaps stationed at a base or facility that offers a treatment program comparable to a diversion program, then petitioner must take advantage of that program and proceed along the statutory diversion timeline. But, if petitioner is perhaps deployed to an area where no comparable treatment is available, such as a combat zone or forward support base, then the indefinite extension of (7)(c) would apply.

XI. Termination of Diversion—ORS 813.255

1. If the court terminates the diversion agreement, the court must enter the judgment of conviction and sentence the defendant.

At any time before the court dismisses with prejudice the charge of DUII, the court on its own motion or on the motion of the district attorney or city attorney may order the defendant to appear and show cause why the court should not terminate the diversion agreement. ORS 813.255(1).

The standard of proof at a show-cause hearing is a preponderance of the evidence. ORS 813.255(3)(a). If the defendant fails to appear at the show-cause hearing or if at the show-cause hearing the court finds by a preponderance of the evidence the defendant failed to fulfill the terms of the diversion agreement, or the defendant did not qualify for the diversion agreement under the criteria in ORS 813.215, the court shall terminate the diversion agreement and continue the offense. ORS 813.255(3). Check to see if the notice was sent to both the defendant and attorney. Also did it state the reason for the revocation as required under ORS 813.255?

In the event of proposed termination of the DUII diversion agreement, if termination is disputed the court may follow such procedures as will enable it to fairly determine the existence of the requisite facts.

Defendant has a right to an attorney at the diversion termination hearing. State v. Vest, 88 Or App 101, 744 p2d 288 (1987). And see State v. Gaino, 210 Or App 107, 149 P3d 1229 (2006).

Note that if the court terminates, at sentencing, the court may take into account at time of sentencing any partial fulfillment by the defendant of the terms of the diversion agreement. ORS 813.255(4). In the sad situation where a defendant is being sentenced for failure to pay fees, but has otherwise completed the diversion requirements, a judge may be persuaded with this statute to avoid the result of requiring another course of attendance at an evaluation, treatment, and the victim impact panel.

2. The court shall allow a diversion participant serving on active military duty to appear at a show cause hearing by telephone or other communication device so long as petitioner’s military service permits such an appearance.

If defendant is unable to appear telephonically, the court shall stay the show cause proceedings. 813.255(4).

February 2022 Update

At the end of the section add the following Practice Tip:

State v. Zook, 307 Or App 49 (2020), now allows a court some discretion about whether to terminate someone from diversion. The operative language is whether the person has made a “good faith effort” to complete the requirements and terms of the diversion agreement. Prior to Zook, most courts interpreted the diversion statutes to be inflexible, which left no discretion to the judge to continue a person on diversion if there was a violation.

Use Zook to convince your judge to extend that ruling in your case, showing how your client has made a good faith effort.

XII. Termination Based on Non-Payment

1. In State v. Hunt, 83 Or App 684, 732 P2d 956 (1987), the defendant had entered the DUII diversion program and the agreement required him to pay certain fees within one year. At the end of the diversion period, the court terminated the diversion agreement and reinstated the charge because defendant had not paid the required fees. Counsel was then appointed and the court was asked to reconsider on the grounds of defendant’s indigency, which it concluded was not relevant. The Court of Appeals reversed a subsequent conviction for DUII and remanded for reconsideration of termination of diversion with instructions.

2. In State v. Cardew, 168 Or App 610, 7 P3d 631 (2000), the court terminated the diversion agreement of the defendant who did not make his monthly payments as scheduled towards the diversion filing fee, nor did he appear in court immediately after missing such payments as required by the diversion agreement. ORS 813.255.

3. In State v. Vargas-Garcia, 217 Or App 70 (2007), defendant admitted to not paying fees, and the court concluded that the statute is inflexible and that the trial court must terminate when fees are not paid.

4. State v. Reed, 241 Or App 47, 249 P3d 557 (2011), yields another harsh result regarding non-payment. After nearly eight months had lapsed after the end of the one-year diversionary period, the court on its own motion sent notice to the state that it intended to dismiss the DUII charge pursuant to 813.255(3) because the evaluation, treatment and victim’s panel had been completed. The state objected because the diversion fees were still outstanding. During a several month period that the court continued the matter, defendant paid $355 of the $358 diversion fee. The court waived the remaining $3 still owed and dismissed the charge. The Court of Appeals reversed. The court agreed with the state’s argument that implicit in the statutory scheme is an agreement that all fees not reduced or waived by the court must be paid by the end of the diversionary period, whether that be the original one-year or the 180-day extension.

It is worth noting that defendant in this case also managed to pick up a second DUII after the end of the one-year period that the court found did not violate the diversion agreement because defendant was not bound to the agreement beyond the one-year period.

5. State v. Rowland, 234 Or App 494, 228 P3d 670 (2010). Another case following State v. Maul that all requirements of diversion must be complete within the statutory time frame. Here the trial court dismissed the DUII charge because defendant had paid his fees three months after the expiration of his SB 35 extension. The Court of Appeals reversed.

XIII. Driving Record and Insurance

1. The court shall notify the Department of Transportation within 48 hours after allowing the petition in a form agreed to by the State Court Administrator. The Motor Vehicles Division shall make the diversion agreement a part of the defendant’s operating record. ORS 813.230(2).

The Oregon Motor Vehicles Division explained how they would enter a diversion agreement into their records in a “Traffic Court Newsletter,” Volume 12, No. 2, July 1982:

New File Added to Diversion Segments—A new file on driver records will now show when a diversion agreement has been terminated. Here’s an example of a terminated diversion and an explanation of the fields:

(a) (b) (c) (d) (e) (f)

DIVR 12/04/81 D-LINC 819999 T 12/21/82

a. Diversion.

b. Effective date of diversion agreement.

c. Type and location of court authorizing diversion agreement.

d. Court docket number, if six digits or less.

e. Terminated (This field will be blank if the agreement has not been terminated

or DMV has not been notified that it was terminated.)

f. Original ending date of diversion agreement.

Traffic courts should notify DMV when a diversion agreement has been terminated so it can be put on the driver’s record. This may be the only way for another court to know that the driver previously entered a diversion program but did not complete that program.

DMV does not supply a form for this purpose. Any letter or form submitted by the court and signed by the judge stating that an agreement has been terminated will be accepted by the Division.

2. The Oregon Motor Vehicles Division explained the probable effect of diversions under this law in the “Traffic Court Newsletter” Volume 11, No. 4, December 1981:

DUII Diversions and Insurance—There have been quite a few questions and concerns expressed about how the insurance industry will treat the new DUII Diversion program. The new law provides that these diversion must be reported to DMV and that they must appear on the three-year abstract of driving record available to insurance companies and others under ORS 486.056.

(Note: Now under ORS 802.220–802.230.)

3. Insurance

DMV personnel talked with the state insurance commissioner’s office and several insurance industry representatives and can pass along this information. Insurance companies normally request driving records only when writing original (new) business. This occurs when a person first obtains insurance or changes companies. Records are usually not ordered for policy renewals unless a high-cost, at-fault claim or several at-fault claims have occurred. Records also are rarely obtained during the policy term. This may occur if the company learns of a major change in auto use or a major driving incident. Thus, a person’s insurance carrier will not necessarily become aware of a DUII diversion even though it’s on the DMV record.

There is no SR-22 filing required in connection with a DUII diversion entry on the driver’s record. When a driver requests an insurance company to make an SR-22 filing, the company is immediately aware of the person’s DUII arrest. Thus by participating in the diversion program, the person saves the extra cost of the SR-22 filing, does not “self-report” the incident to the insurance company, and may avoid increased premiums and/or nonrenewal. However, an SR-22 filing is required if the person seeks a hardship/occupational license. Therefore, it might be in the person’s interests to “tough it out” and not drive if the suspension length is for only 90 days. (First DUII convictions do carry a mandatory suspension and an SR-22 is needed for a hardship/occupational license, and for three years following the suspension period.) The other option is to purchase temporary supplemental insurance from another insurance company solely for the SR-22 requirement.

DMV does not automatically notify or send records to insurance companies when an accident, conviction, suspension or other entry is made. Records are sent only upon individual request.

Rating procedures currently filed with the insurance commissioner provide that an insurance policy can be canceled mid-term for only a few specific reasons, such as license suspension. The DUII diversion entry on a driver’s record cannot be used for mid-term cancellation. It could, however, be considered by a company in deciding whether or not to renew the policy or by the insurance agent to write the policy through a substandard carrier.

The effect of a DUII diversion on insurance premiums, in cases where the company at some point does find out about it, will vary from company to company.

Preferred insurance companies request random checks of motor vehicle records at the time of policy renewals in about twenty percent of all cases.

XIV. The Mental Health Division Evaluation

Oregon Administrative Rules 415-054-0461 through 415-054-570, explain the criteria used to place DUII diversion clients into a Level I, Level II or Level III program and the qualifications for evaluators. The level of program is a subject of increasing interest as expense to the client greatly increases in the most involved Level II program, and insurance companies may begin adjusting rates based on the classification.
