I. Voir Dire Is an Art, Not a Science

How to learn to voir dire: Watch other lawyers. As an observer you independently see what works and what doesn’t. The panel will react to questions by both sides when the lawyers aren’t watching. You can read the panel’s facial expressions and body language. Laughter is a universal barrier breaker.

Remember that voir dire is not an appendage to a trial; it is a key element of the trial, where the seeds of your defense or narrative are first sown.

II. The Rules of Jury Selection

A. Statutes

Link to 2019 edition of Oregon Revised Statutes, Chapter 136, Criminal Trials.

1. ORS 136.005 Challenge to jury panel.

(1) The district attorney or the defendant in a criminal action may challenge the jury panel on the ground that there has been a material departure from the requirements of the law governing the selection of jurors. (2) A challenge to the panel shall be made before the voir dire examination of the jury.

2. ORS 136.210 Jury number; examination.

(1) Except as provided in subsection (2) of this section, in criminal cases the trial jury shall consist of 12 persons unless the parties consent to a lessern number. It shall be formed, except as otherwise provided in ORS 136.220 to ORS 136.250, in the same manner provided by ORCP 57 B, D (1)(a), D(1)(b), D(1)(g) and E. When the full number of jurors has been called, they shall thereupon be examined as to their qualifications, first by the court, then by the defendant, then by the state. After they have been passed for cause, peremptory challenges, if any, shall be exercised as provided in ORS 136.230. (2) In criminal cases in the circuit courts in which the only charges to be tried are misdemeanors, the trial jury shall consist of six persons.

3. ORS 136.220 Challenge for implied bias.

A challenge for implied bias shall be allowed for any of the following causes and no other:

a. Consanguinity or affinity within the fourth degree to the person alleged to be injured by the offense charged in the accusatory instrument, to the complainant or to the defendant.

b. Standing in the relation of guardian and ward, attorney and client, physician and patient, master and servant, debtor and creditor, principal and agent or landlord and tenant with the:

  • Defendant

  • Person alleged to be injured by the offense charged in the accusatory instrument or

  • Complainant.

c. Being a member of the family, a partner in business with or in the employment of any person referred to in subsection (2) (a), (b) or (c) of this section or a surety in the action or otherwise for the defendant.

d. Having served on the grand jury which found the indictment or on a jury of inquest which inquired into the death of a person whose death is the subject of the indictment or information.

e. Having been one of a jury formerly sworn in the same action, and whose verdict was set aside or which was discharged without a verdict after the cause was submitted to it.

f. Having served as a juror in a civil action, suit or proceeding brought against the defendant for substantially the same act charged as an offense.

g. Having served as a juror in a criminal action upon substantially the same facts, transaction or criminal episode.

4. ORS 136.230 Peremptory challenges.

(1) If the trial is upon an accusatory instrument in which one or more of the crimes charged is punishable with imprisonment in a Department of Corrections institution for life or is a capital offense, both the defendant and the state are entitled to 12 peremptory challenges, and no more. In any trial before more than six jurors, both are entitled to six. In any trial before six jurors, both are entitled to three. (2) Peremptory challenges shall be taken in writing by secret ballot as follows:

The defendant may challenge two jurors and the state may challenge two, and so alternating, the defendant exercising two challenges and the state two until the peremptory challenges are exhausted. After each challenge the panel shall be filled and the additional juror passed for cause before another peremptory challenge is exercised. Neither party shall be required to exercise a peremptory challenge unless the full number of jurors is in the box at the time. The refusal to challenge by either party in order of alternation does not prevent the adverse party from exercising that adverse party’s full number of challenges, and such refusal on the part of a party to exercise a challenge in proper turn concludes that party as to the jurors once accepted by that party. If that party’s right of peremptory challenge is not exhausted, that party’s further challenges shall be confined, in that party’s proper turn, to such additional jurors as may be called. (3) Notwithstanding subsection (2) of this section, the defendant and the state may stipulate to taking peremptory challenges orally. (4) Peremptory challenges are subject to ORCP 57 D(4).

5. ORS 136.240 Challenge of accepted juror.

If the peremptory challenges of the moving party are not already exhausted, the court may for good cause shown permit a challenge to be taken to any juror before the jury is completed and sworn, notwithstanding the juror challenged may have been theretofore accepted.

6. ORS 136.250 Taking of challenges; number of challenges if two or more defendants.

All peremptory challenges may be taken by the state or the defendant, but when several defendants are tried together, the defendants are entitled to the number of challenges they would have had if each defendant had been tried separately. When two or more defendants are tried together, the state is entitled to the same total number of peremptory challenges as the sum of peremptory challenges the defendants could have exercised.

7. ORS 136.260 Selection of alternate jurors; peremptory challenges.

(1) In the trial of a person charged with a felony, the court may in its discretion, after the jury is impaneled and sworn, direct the calling of one to six additional jurors to be known, as “alternate jurors.” Such jurors shall be drawn from the same qualifications as other jurors in the case. They shall be subject to the same examination and be challenged in the same manner as other jurors. In the drawing of alternate jurors, the names of the jurors excused for cause or on peremptory challenges in the selection of the jury to which such jurors shall serve as alternates shall be excluded from the names from which such drawing is made. (2) Each side is entitled to the following peremptory challenges in addition to those otherwise allowed by statute: If one or two alternate jurors are to be impaneled, each side is entitled to one peremptory challenge. If three or four alternate jurors are to be impaneled, each side is entitled to one peremptory challenge. If five or six alternate jurors are to be impaneled, each side is entitled to three peremptory challenges. (3) The additional peremptory challenges may be used against an alternate juror only, and the other peremptory challenges allowed by statute shall not be used against an alternate juror.

8. ORS 136.270 Oath, rules governing conduct and attendance of alternate jurors at trial.

Alternate jurors shall take the same oath and shall be subject to the same laws, orders and rules, including any order preventing the separation of the jury during the trial, shall be seated near the other jurors in the case, with equal opportunity and facilities for seeing and hearing the proceedings and shall attend at all times upon the trial of the case in company with the other jurors.

9. ORS 136.280 Substitution of alternate for juror dying or becoming disabled; dismissal.

If, before the final submission of the case, any juror dies or is unable to perform the duty because of illness or other cause which the court deems sufficient, the juror shall be dismissed from the case. Except as provided by ORS 163.150, the court shall cause to be drawn the name of an alternate juror, who shall then become a member of the jury as though the alternate juror had been selected as one of the original jurors. Any alternate juror not selected to become a member of the jury shall be dismissed from the case upon its final submission to the jury.

B. Case Law

1. Purpose of Voir Dire

Voir dire is a statutorily authorized and important part of jury trial. We need not elaborate on the fact that inquiry into a prospective juror’s previous experience in litigation as a party, witness or juror is generally considered an important part of voir dire, relevant both to challenges for cause and peremptory challenges. It is ordinarily the rule that once a juror has been sworn, counsel can make no subsequent inquiry into whether extraneous events in a juror’s life during the course of trial have affected his objectivity. State v. Miller, 10 Or App 636, 638 639, 501 P2d 326 (1972) (emphasis added); State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 860 P2d 860 (1993) (the purpose of voir dire is to ascertain whether to challenge for cause and whether to challenge peremptorily).

2. Challenge for Implied Bias

State is not complainant referred to in ORS 136.220 because state is not a person. State v. Rogers, 313 Or 356, 836 P2d 1308 (1992). “It is the general rule that a police officer is not disqualified from jury service on that ground alone, but her employment may well serve in a particular case as a proper basis for challenge on the ground of implied bias.” Parks v. Cupp, 5 Or App 51, 53, 481 P2d 372 (1971). But see Hernandez v. State, 563 SW2d 947 (Tex Crim App 1978) (where the court held a prospective juror’s predisposition to believe police testimony amounts to a cause for challenge).

3. Challenge for actual bias—can the juror try the case impartially without prejudice to the rights of the challenging party?

Whether juror is actually biased is a question of fact that the court determines within its discretionary powers. State v. Tucker, 315 Or 321, 845 P2d 904 (1993) (trial court’s decision will not be interfered with unless it was an abuse of discretion); initial responses by jurors should be given more weight when determining the juror’s true frame of mind as opposed to later statements that the juror would be fair. Lambert v. Sisters of St. Joseph, 277 Or 223, 560 P2d 262 (1977) (abuse of discretion found when trial judge refused to excuse juror for actual bias).

4. Challenges for Cause

See ORS 136.210(1)

ORCP 57 D(1)(a), D(1)(b), and D(1)(g)

ORS 10.030

Senate Bill 936, section 9b(3)(a).

The qualifications for a juror are that the juror must be a citizen of the United States, an inhabitant of the county for the year next proceeding the time he/she is drawn or called, that he/she is over eighteen years of age, and of a sound mind. A juror is improperly summoned if called more than once in any one year. A juror may not be convicted of, or sentenced for, a felony within the last fifteen years and must be a registered voter.

a. Proper challenge of a juror for cause. “We submit the matter for the court’s consideration.” State v. Nagel, 185 Or 486, 495, 202 P2d 640 (1949).

b. Waiver occurs if cause challenge is not properly asserted and preserved. Peremptory challenge of a juror improperly allowed to remain (because of implied bias) waives the right to object on appeal. Twitchell v. Thompson, 78 Or 285, 289, 153 P 45 (1915); State v. Humphrey, 63 Or 540, 546, 128 P 824 (1912); “A party whose peremptory challenges have not been exhausted is not in a position to complain of the overruling of his challenge for cause to a juror who afterwards serves on the panel.” Lambert v. Sisters of St. Joseph, 277 Or 223, 229, 560 P2d 262 (1977); “The overruling of a good challenge for cause, thereby compelling the use of a peremptory challenge, is not prejudicial error where it does not appear that the challenger was compelled to accept an objectionable juror.” State v. Megorden, 49 Or 259, 263–264, 88 P 306 (1907). A challenge to a juror for implied bias is waived unless submitted before the jury is sworn. Holmgren v. Westport Tow Boat Company, 260 Or 445, 447, 490 P2d 739 (1971). The failure to object to a juror’s incompetence waives the right to object later. State v. McDonald, 8 Or 113, 119 (1879). If the court refuses to excuse a juror as incompetent on the defendant’s challenge for cause during voir dire, the defendant must explain to the court that she was ultimately forced to accept an objectionable juror because of the use of a peremptory challenge to remove the juror in question. This must be done in order to preserve the error. State v. Griffin, 19 Or App 822, 825, 529 P2d 399 (1979).

5. Peremptory Challenges

a. Improper to challenge a juror based on race. Baston v. Kentucky, 476 US 79, 106 S Ct 1712, 90 LEd2d 69 (1986) (Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment forbids race-based challenges); State v. Ross, 154 Or App 121, 961 P2d 241 (1998) (DUII case reversed and remanded for a new trial after prosecutor used peremptory challenges of African-American prospective jurors and trial court relied on its personal knowledge about prosecutor in making assessment of whether or not purposeful discrimination occurred); State v. Millage, 125 Or App 92, 864 P2d 868 (1993) (whether prosecutor intentionally discriminated is a question of fact, trial court properly found that prosecutor’s peremptory challenge of minority prospective juror did not violate defendant’s constitutional rights).

b. Establishing purposeful discrimination. 1) Prima facie case of purposeful discrimination is shown when it is shown that juror is a member of a cognizable racial group and that the prosecutor has exercised a peremptory challenge to remove from the jury panel a member of the defendant’s race and relevant circumstances of the jury selection process that raise an inference that the prosecution exercised the peremptory challenge for the purpose of excluding a member of the defendant’s race (pattern of strikes and nature of questions asked). Then, the burden shifts and requires a 2) race-neutral explanation by the prosecutor for the peremptory challenge. Finally, the burden shifts back to the defendant to establish that the reason given was 3) a pretext for discrimination.

c. Improper challenge to a juror based on gender. [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=J.E.B.+v.+Alabama+ex+rel.+T.B.,+511+U.S.+127,+&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38,44&case=7113990036820068030&scilh=0 J.E.B. v. Alabama ex rel. T.B., 511 US 127, 114 S Ct 1419, 128 LEd89 (1994)] (Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment forbids gender-based challenges).

d. Timely peremptory challenge. Counsel must exercise a peremptory challenge in a timely manner because there is no abuse of discretion in denying the exercise of a peremptory challenge not made in a timely manner. State v. Mulvihill, 35 Or App 627, 629, 582 P2d 43 (1978).

6. Questions to the Jury

a. Improper to ask questions regarding juror’s religion, unless relevant to the facts of the case. State v. Barnett, 251 Or 234, 445 P2d 124 (1968) (citing Article I, section 6, of the Oregon Constitution).

b. Improper for court to prohibit defense counsel’s questions of jury about defendant’s prior conviction(s). “[A] defendant should be allowed latitude in voir dire questioning about matters relevant to trial in order to intelligently exercise his peremptory challenges. State v. Ziebert, 34 Or App 497, 501, 579 P2d 275 (1978) (reversible error for the trial court to prohibit defense counsel from inquiring whether jurors might be prejudiced by the defendant’s prior conviction).

c. Rehabilitative questioning. The exercise of the court’s discretion must be guided by legal principles when a juror has a strongly held opinion adverse to a party. “The purpose of rehabilitative questioning is investigation, not persuasion.” Lane County v. Walker, 30 Or App 715, 722, 568 P2d 676 (1977), and State v. Nefstad, 309 Or 523, 533, 789 P2d 1326 (1990). If a juror says that he has formed an opinion from reading the newspaper, but when questioned by the judge says that he will form his opinion based upon the evidence, the overruling of a challenge to that juror is not reversible error. State v. Ingram, 23 Or 434, 435, 31 P 1049 (1893).

d. Answers Given By Jurors

  • The trial judge is not bound by the answers given by jurors in determining the qualifications of a juror. State v. Brumfield, 104 Or 506, 529, 209 P 120 (1922).

  • It is presumed that the answers of jurors on voir dire are honestly given. State v. McDonald, 231 Or 24, 37, 361 P2d 1001, cert den 370 US 903 (1962). A juror who lies on voir dire may prejudice the defendant sufficiently to require a new trial. State v. Holcomb, 131 Or App 453, 886 P2d 14 (1994).

  • It is essential to designate voir dire examination on appeal if a point relied upon for reversal pertains to qualifications of jurors. “Where an appellant fails to provide us with a record essential to review matters claimed to be error, we do not speculate as to what the record might have shown had it been properly designated.” State v. Krohn, 15 Or App 63, 67, 514 P2d 1359 (1973).

  • In order for error in accepting an arguably biased juror to be preserved, the entire record must be before the appellate court. State v. Tom (a Chinaman), 8 Or 177, 179 (1879); accord, State v. Stigers, 122 Or 113, 121, 256 P2d 649 (1927); exception to rule, State v. Miller. 46 Or 485, 489, 81 P 363 (1905).

  • It may be necessary to make an offer of proof to preserve error. State v. Nefstad, supra.

  • Merely because a juror has an opinion that the defendant is guilty does not automatically disqualify that juror, State v. Olberman, 33 Or 556, 557, 55 P 866 (1899), even if it would take evidence to change that opinion, State v. McDaniel, 39 Or 161, 168, 65 P 520 (1901).

  • If a juror separates due to illness, and sits on another case in the eight-day meantime, defense counsel could not re-voir dire about this, so a mistrial should have been allowed. State v. Miller, 10 Or App 636, 638–639, 501 P2d 326 (1972).

e. Form and Scope of Voir Dire Questions

  • It is error to prohibit defense counsel from asking, “From what you have heard or read have you formed or expressed an opinion as to the guilt of Joseph Kelly, indicted with Steeves?” State v. Steeves, 29 Or 85, 94, 98, 43 P 947 (1896).

  • It is improper to ask a prospective juror, “If you were acting as a district attorney in this case, would you be willing to have a man who was in your present state of mind sit on a jury in a case that you were conducting?” The examination should be conducted as to disclose bias in favor of or against either side. State v. Wakefield, 111 Or 615, 619–20, 628 P 115 (1924). The examination should also be so conducted as to disclose bias in favor of or against either side. See State v. Hoffman, 85 Or 276, 280, 166 P 765 (1917).

  • It is improper to single out a class of witnesses (informers, Ethiopians, descendants of the African race, ministers) and ask the juror if more credence would be attributed to that class. “These questions are so apparently improper that we do not feel called upon to enter into discussion of them.” State v. Hoffman, 85 Or 276, 280, 166 P 765 (1917). But compare with Ham v. South Carolina, 409 US 524 (1973); cf., Ristaino v. Ross, 424 US 589 (1976).

  • It is appropriate to ask juror what her attitudes are regarding organizations such as the Black Panther Party. State v. Dixon, 5 Or App 113, 123, 481 P2d 629 (1971).

  • It is appropriate to question prospective jurors about racial bias. State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 860 P2d 860 (1993).

  • It is permissible to forbid voir dire inquiry about jurors’ view of penalty (death). State v. Shumway, 44 Or App 657, 666, 607 P2d 191 (1980), modified on other grounds, 291 Or 153, 630 P2d 796 (1981).

  • Objection on the basis that a hypothetical voir dire question based upon what the facts may or may not show was inappropriate. State v. Elam, 37 Or App 365, 372, 587 P2d 491 (1978) (fn 5).

  • Each party in a criminal case must be afforded a reasonable opportunity on voir dire to conduct “pertinent inquiries” to ascertain whether the prospective jurors possess conscientious scruples or other moral obstacles which are likely to interfere with their proper application of the law. The court reversed the conviction where the trial judge refused to permit inquiry as follows: “Do you believe in self-defense?” Everly v. State, 395 NE2d 254 (Ind 1979).

  • A rape defendant has the right to ask, “Have you or any members of your family been involved previously with a rape or proceeding involving a sexual crime?” A defendant has a right to probe for prejudice and to inquire into the past victimization among the jurors of crimes similar to those with which the defendant stands accused. Commonwealth v. Fulton, 413 A2d 742 (Pa Superior Ct 1979).

  • It is reversible error for the trial court to restrict the defense examination of jurors as to their understanding of a defense (in this case insanity). State v. Hawkins, 376 So2d 943 (La 1979).

  • Time limits on voir dire can be imposed. However, if the purpose of voir dire (to challenge for cause or peremptorily) is thereby thwarted, it will result in reversible error. State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 860 P2d 860 (1993). See also State v. Loke, 131 Or App 751, 886 P2d 507 (1994) (reversible error to limit defendant to 32 minutes for voir dire.)

f. Improper Voir Dire by Prosecution or Judge

Move for mistrial. Improper questioning of a jury regarding insurance required a mistrial. “You cannot unring a bell nor unscramble an egg.” Leishman v. Taylor, 199 Or 546, 558, 263 P2d 605 (1954).

Examples of Improper Prosecution Voir Dire

  • Asked of a juror who previously sat in State v. Anchoberry, a co-defendant separately tried from the defendant Morris, “Who was the juror who wanted to convict both defendants on the Anchoberry case?” State v. Morris, 83 Or 429, 435, 163 P 567 (1917).

  • District Attorney: “And you think you have reasonable grounds for supposing that a crime has been committed?”

  • Juror: “Yes, I think I have.”

  • District Attorney: “I think any reasonable man would have that impression.” State v. Morris, 83 Or 429, supra.

  • The prosecutor asked a juror if he was aware that probably 9of the persons indicted actually plead guilty and that of the other 10% that go to trial, probably 70, 80 or 90% are convicted. This was highly prejudicial and required a mistrial. The judge’s comment, in front of the jury, “If you are going to move for a mistrial now, it would be denied,” in all probability was seen by the jury as condoning the improper remarks of the prosecutor and lending support to the inferences the comments suggest. State v. Flores, 31 Or App 187, 570 P2d 94 (1977).

  • “Now, the statute may require the judge to give a life sentence for murder, but it doesn’t mean that she stays for that length of time. The parole board can let him out the next day or at any other time.” Such a comment by a prosecutor on voir dire is inappropriate, but was invited by numerous equally inappropriate statements by defense counsel, so the error was invited and would not occasion a reversal. State v. Wampler, 30 Or App 931, 935, 569 P2d 46 (1978).

III. The Purpose of Jury Selection Is to Find People Who Will Invest in Your Theory

A. Jurors Come from Every Socio-Economic, Cultural and Political Background

Every trial is an opportunity for you to communicate your theory of the case to this group of different people. Depending on your theory of the case, some jurors will not even listen to it, others will consider it, and some will invest in it. For this reason, you must watch members of the venire. Find the leaders who will invest in your theory in voir dire. Watch how different people on the panel react to the various statements. Does the man in seat 11 roll his eyes when the woman in seat 3 speaks? Does seat 4 nod when seat 3 speaks? Do seat 14 and 15 seem to be very friendly with one another?

B. Have an Honest Conversation

Have an honest conversation with panel members and the panel as a whole with the goal of selecting a fair and impartial group to decide the facts of your case. In so doing, you want the jury to like you, agree with your facts, and to believe that what you say is credible.

C. Reduce Information to a Single Theory

Although jury selection is not the time or place to try your case, apart from finding jurors who are biased for or against your client, a general key to solid trial preparation is reducing voluminous amounts of information to a single theory that is easily communicated and reinforced through every stage of the trial, including voir dire. Some simple examples are:

  • “.08 is a line in the sand, below a .08 means that you are not impaired.”

  • “If there is no evidence of bad driving, therefore performance on FSTs is irrelevant because FSTs are supposed to measure one’s ability to drive.”

  • “The result of the Intoxilyzer inflated the defendant’s BAC level because it was taken two hours after driving.”

Your theory may be communicated in voir dire by asking questions using examples for panel members to respond to about how they feel about a standard (.08), or an opinion (FST), or the accuracy of machines (Intoxilyzer), or the science of alcohol absorption and dissipation (below a .08 at time of driving).

D. Plant Seeds/Highlight Favorable Language in Instructions

During jury selection, you may plant seeds that will later be developed and grow during the trial. One method to plant seeds is to review the jury instructions for favorable language. You may ask opinions of panel members and whether or not they will follow such instruction, all the while highlighting favorable language with subtle repetitiveness. In addition, you may gather commitment from jurors to adhere to the legal instructions that you believe will help determine your case. Some examples are:

1. UCrJI 1009: “Reasonable doubt exists when, after careful and impartial consideration of all the evidence in the case you are not convinced to a moral certainty that the defendant is guilty.”

2. UCrJI 2701: “Oregon law provides that it is not unlawful for a person to drive a vehicle after having consumed intoxicating liquor. It is unlawful, however, for that person to drive a vehicle if the person is under the influence of intoxicating liquor.

“In this case, you do not have to find that [defendant’s name] was drunk or intoxicated, as those terms are commonly understood. ‘Under the influence of intoxicating liquor’ means that [defendant]’s physical or mental faculties were adversely affected by the use of intoxicating liquor to a noticeable or perceptible degree.

“ ‘Under the influence of intoxicating liquor’ includes not only the well-known and easily recognized conditions and degrees of intoxication, but also any abnormal mental or physical condition that results from consuming intoxicating liquor and that deprives the person of that clearness of intellect or control that the person would otherwise possess.”

Note: Prosecutors often omit the word “adverse” when discussing “under the influence” in voir dire. Merely “affected” is a lower standard than “adversely affected.” If this occurs, object and have the judge instruct the jury with the appropriate language. Adversely affected vs. affected also ties in with many other aspects of the DUII trial (example: alcohol influence report: “What effects do you feel from the alcohol? You may be affected without being adversely affected”).

3. UCrJI 2709: “Although a witness may be allowed to state an opinion whether another person was under the influence of intoxicants, you are not required to accept that opinion. To determine the value you will give to a witness’s opinion, you should consider such things as the witness’s ability to form the opinion, the witness’s believability, how the witness reached that opinion, the experience of the witness, and the witness’s opportunity to observe the defendant.”

4. UCrJI 2711: “It is up to you to determine what weight you will give to breath test evidence. You are not required to accept such evidence. You should give the breath test evidence such weight as you feel is appropriate in reaching your verdict.”

IV. Jury Selection in the Courtroom

Practice Tip

Jury selection begins before you and your client enter the courthouse. Potential jurors are entering the same building, parking in the same vicinity, getting coffee in the same places around the courthouse. You and you client must be aware of this. Prior to the day of trial, you must prepare your client for “good trial day behavior.” You client must be instructed to avoid making any comments about being a defendant in a trial when near the courthouse; to not wear the “Budweiser” t-shirt; to not get a ride from a friend if they have pot leaf and/or alcohol-themed bumper stickers. Remember, potential jurors are all around the courthouse.

This caution applies to the lawyer as well. It is embarrassing at best when you tell a fellow lawyer in line at Starbucks that you are trying another drunk driving case for the same drunk client again today, only to have a potential juror during voir dire say she overheard you talking about your case in line getting coffee. Likewise refrain from comments about your hangover, party plans for the weekend, or anything you would not want a juror to know. Potential jurors are everywhere. It is also a good practice to request the judge instruct the jurors to wear their juror identification when they are within three to four blocks of the courthouse. Most judges welcome this request, and the jurors gain an understanding of the importance of their role; more importantly, it may save you or your client profound embarrassment.

A. Pre-selection Work

Most counties in Oregon (except Multnomah) have jury books—the names and backgrounds of panel members. This is a helpful tool to prepare your list or chart of the panel ahead of time. Multnomah County now has panel lists without background information, so at least know the name of the jurors and the order in which they will be seated ahead of time. Prepare your worksheet before the jury is called. Also, try to beat the prosecutor to the table closest to the jury box. If your client has a great demeanor have them sit closest to the jury; if not, you sit close to the jury.

Practice Tip

Finding jurors who will invest in your theory is not always about conservative vs. liberal—I have often had conservative jury foremen and women who invested in my case. It is important to be flexible, if you are practicing in a conservative county or have a conservative jury pool and your facts permit it, a government intrusion theory may work better than reasonable doubt theory.

B. Eye Contact

When the jury arrives, you and your client should make eye contact in a friendly and sincere manner. Give your client a notepad to write on while jurors are asked questions from the court. Show interest in their background and highlight personal information on your worksheet (law enforcement, MADD members, served on a criminal or civil jury, etc.).

After the court asks questions, it’s your turn! As soon as you stand up the butterflies will go away. Be yourself! Be confident! Welcome laughter!

C. Take Immediate Command of the Courtroom

  • Introduce everyone:

  • Your client—by name.

  • The prosecutor—by name. Never again state the prosecutor’s name in trial. Henceforth, refer to “the prosecutor.”

  • Yourself.

  • The bailiff—by name. Bailiffs are people too. The bailiff has more intimate contact with the jury than anyone in the courtroom. If the bailiff likes you, and hopefully your client, it will show and may rub off on the jury.

D. Ask Open-ended Questions – and Listen

As a general rule, keep it short and ask open-ended questions designed to get the jurors to talk—then listen, and follow up.

1. Exception to the Rule: When “educating” jurors about a defense or laying a foundation for a question about the defense.

2. This is the opposite of cross-examination, so ask “why” and “explain more fully” types of questions.

a. But, just as in cross-examination, know when to stop. A juror’s response that he does not know if he can be fair to the defendant’s DUII trial is enough to trigger a challenge for cause, or at least use of one of your peremptory challenges. Asking “why” may well result in an answer that effectively poisons the other five jurors, such as, “Because a drunk driver killed my four-year-old granddaughter last year.”

b. Don’t ask questions just to hear yourself talk. If the court has already asked the jurors certain questions or they have been answered on a juror questionnaire, never repeat that question. You do not want the juror to think you have ignored her previous identical answer. Let the new DA make that mistake; surprisingly, they often will.

E. Utilize the Juror Questionnaire

Don’t merely repeat what is on it—use it as a springboard for other questions.

F. Utilize the Judge’s Questioning

Judges range from making cursory statements to extensive questioning of jurors. Where the questioning is extensive, what more can you learn? Are you going to be able to “impeach” jurors who have already told the judge they will be fair?

Ask yourself if you really need to ask any question. With a good juror questionnaire, and extensive questioning by the judge, you may know everything you can learn already. Have you considered asking no questions on voir dire, passing the panel for cause and sitting back politely as the flustered DA begins repeating the identical questions six times over without respite? Remember, you still have your peremptory challenges.

Practice Tip

Talk to your judge and the DA before trial; if there are questions you would like for the judge to ask the panel, most judges are happy to include information-gathering questions in the preliminary “judge questions.”

G. Address the Entire Panel

1. Ask permission to address the panel as a whole to “speed things up” (no judge has ever denied this request).

2. Introduce yourself, your client, the prosecutor, and all witnesses for both parties. Ask if the juror knows any of them.

3. Explain the purpose of voir dire so as not to embarrass or put anyone on the spot; explain that it is not cross-examination; encourage jurors to talk back to you.

4. Sample on En Masse

  • Are any of you non-drinkers?

  • Has anyone here been a witness in a criminal matter?

  • Are you acquainted with, related to, or have you applied to be a law enforcement officer (including police, sheriff, prison guard, district attorney or employee of any of the above).

V. Sample Voir Dire Questions Stolen from the “Great Ones” (you know who they are)

One way to start off —just touch on these things, don’t read the entire question sequence:

“Good morning, my name is _______, I am Mr. (first and last name)’s attorney.

“This is a time for both sides to select a jury, it’s called Voir Dire: It’s a time for the attorneys to have a conversation with the panel about legal concepts and individual biases—the goal is to impanel six to twelve people who will be fair and impartial—listen to both sides and make up their own mind about the evidence.

“I will ask questions to each of you individually and to the panel as a whole. Please feel free to participate at any time.

“Let’s start with some questions about basic protections all people charged with a crime have and some of the legal principles that you will apply in this case.”

A. The Presumption of Innocence

“Ms./Mr._______________, what can you tell me about the presumption of innocence?”

“Our system asks you as a juror to do a very difficult task—and that is to presume __________ is innocent of any criminal allegations.” Ms./Mr._______________, how could you possibly do this? He is charged with Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicating Liquor. He must have done something wrong?”

“Does knowing that he is charged with a particular crime mean he has done something illegal? Why or why not?”

“Has anyone ever contested any government action—like a traffic ticket?”

“Ms./Mr. ________________, why did you contest it?”

“Can we all agree to follow this instruction and presume _________________ innocent? Can we all agree to wait until the entire case has been presented before deciding this case?”

B. State’s Burden

“The next legal concept that we will discuss involves the burdens and obligations placed on the state, the prosecutor, Mr. (or Ms.) ________________________.”

“I will begin this topic with a trick question, for the entire panel: What does ________________ need to do to prove to you that he is not guilty of these charges? Nothing, right? Why?”

“The state shoulders the entire burden of proving the charges against ______________. But, government must meet a specific standard of proof before an individual can be criminally punished. Is this fair to require just one side, the government’s side, to have the entire burden of gathering, producing and proving the evidence for your decision of guilty or not guilty? Why?”

“In the criminal trial forum, you are not selecting whose case you like better—the prosecution or the defense—you are measuring only one side. Did the prosecution rise to the highest legal standard of proof—proof beyond a reasonable doubt.”

“Ms./Mr.____________________, are you comfortable measuring just one side—the state. Let me put it to you this way: _________________ does not need to testify, call one witness, raise one objection or make any argument whatsoever, and you could still find him not guilty—how, if the evidence in this case did not rise to the level for conviction?”

C. Proof Beyond a Reasonable Doubt

“Mr./Ms._______________, what does ‘proof beyond a reasonable doubt’ mean to you? Not beyond all doubt, but you must be convinced to a moral certainty.”

“Ms./Mr.______________________________, you said that you served on a jury before—was that a criminal or a civil case?”

If criminal: “So, a criminal case, like this one, requires the prosecutor to meet the highest burden of proof—beyond a reasonable doubt—because a criminal defendant’s liberty is at stake if convicted of a crime.”

“Unlike civil cases where the standard is a preponderance of the evidence—meaning in a civil case if you’re convinced fifty-plus percent that one side is right—then you can decide in their favor. Criminal cases are far different. It requires one side, the prosecutor, to prove its case. If after you consider all the evidence you are not convinced to a moral certainty that the defendant is guilty—you have reasonable doubt.”

“Will everyone agree that if you’re not convinced to a moral certainty that ________________ is guilty, you should find him not guilty?”

“Will everyone agree that it is the prosecution and only the prosecution’s burden to establish by proof beyond a reasonable doubt that _________________ is guilty?”

D. Innocence vs. Not Guilty

“In some countries criminal cases have three potential verdicts—like in Scotland, where the verdict possibilities are Innocence, Not Proven, and Guilty.”

“In our system there are only two possible verdicts that a jury may return guilty or not guilty.”

“Ms./Mr._____________________, is there a difference between not guilty and innocence? Why?”

“Will you be issuing a verdict of innocence—no. Why?”

“Would you be comfortable issuing a not guilty verdict if the state did not prove its case, but you were not certain about absolute innocence? Why?”

“In some countries there is no jury and only a judge decides the case. Our system is that of the citizenry—power held by six to twelve people. Why is it important to have a jury of citizens vs. just a judge to decide this case?”

VI. Trial Tips

  • If you are thoroughly prepared in all aspects of your case, voir dire will not be so nerve-wracking and embarrassing to you. It will, however, be an opportunity for you to seek out and find the leader of your jury.

  • Watch the people, not the papers in front of you like most lawyers.

  • Pitch your theory to other people to see its responsiveness in general. If your client can afford it, try a focus group with temporary labor employees, then do your opening statement (all confidential rights protected), create questionnaires and fix any weaknesses and exploit strengths.

Bill Uhle (ed., 2004): As a practical matter, most courts allow approximately thirty minutes for defense voir dire. Develop an objective for your voir dire that is obtainable given the number of jurors involved and the time available. The most important objective of voir dire is to select jurors who will actually apply constitutional provisions to the facts they are going to hear. The constitutional provisions that are always applicable in a DUII trial and are difficult to get jurors to apply are: 1) the state’s burden of gathering and producing the evidence, 2) defendant’s presumption of innocence, and 3) proof beyond a reasonable doubt. These constitutional provisions sound great to all of us (as well as to jurors), but in a criminal trial involving drinking, driving and credibility of police, these provisions become difficult for jurors to actually apply. Jurors will often pay “lip service” to these constitutional provisions. For example, for jurors to acquit somebody they are clearly convinced was under the influence may be very difficult for them, even though being clearly convinced is insufficient under the reasonable doubt standard.

If you can develop your own style of convincing jurors to truly apply these constitutional provisions and convince them that applying these constitutional provisions is the right thing to do, you will be well on your way to a not guilty verdict.

Opening Statement

Edward Kroll

Your opening statement in a DUII case is the first time the jurors will hear directly why your client is not guilty. This is really your only chance to establish the narrative of the case. Once evidence starts, you will be cross-examining hostile witnesses (police officers), with very little chance to dictate how the story is told. Thus, your opening must be a persuasive story which will set the tone for the remainder of trial.

I. Preparation Before Opening

A. Know Your Jury

If you have done a thorough and effective voir dire, you should have a good idea of the makeup of your jury. Some juries are very intelligent. Some juries are very emotional. Some juries are just plain dumb. You should do your best to tailor your opening to the particular traits of your jury. If it’s clear that there are several “leaders” and several “followers,” you want to convince the leaders. If the leaders happen to be scientists or analytic types, a more fact-based style can be appealing. If the leaders appear to be more emotion-driven, then stay away from long recitations of what the evidence will show.

B. Know the Prosecutor

Your knowledge of prosecutors will increase with time and experience. In large counties, such as Multnomah, you may have never seen (or even heard of) the prosecutor before today. In smaller counties, you may get to know each prosecutor and his or her individual traits. Regardless of whether you have prior familiarity, you should carefully observe your opponent during voir dire and his or her opening. It is to our detriment that the prosecution gets to go first with openings. They get to set the tone and frame the narrative. You will want to ensure that your presentation and tone provide a suitable counterpoint to theirs. This doesn’t mean be their polar opposite.

For example, your opponent may be very high-energy and over the top with their statements of guilt. In this situation, perhaps you are cool, calm, and collected, stating that the evidence clearly won’t show everything that the prosecutor promised.

Or your opponent may be direct and blunt, without much fanfare. Depending on your jury, you may choose to counter this with warmth, compassion and carefully measured emotion. I find that it’s helpful to set yourself apart from the prosecutor and give the jury something of yours to latch on to and follow.

C. Know Yourself

Despite the above discussion, you must take care not to force things in opening. If you are not naturally ebullient or emotional, trying to force it just looks awkward. The more trials you do, the more you will find your own style. The more natural and genuine you can be, the better. That said, here are some things you should always be aware of in your presentation.

1. Always make eye contact with the jurors. I’ve seen many attorneys lose jurors’ trust or focus because the attorney looks everywhere but at the jurors. These are the people who are deciding your client’s fate. You need them on your side. How can they trust someone who won’t look at them?

2. Do your best to move away from notes and podiums. I see this frequently with young lawyers. They hold their notes or the podium like a life preserver, looking down frequently to check their place and make sure they’re not forgetting things. Stop it. It makes you look nervous and stilted. I love it when I see a prosecutor use their notes or a podium in opening, because I know that I can trample them (in personality, if not facts…). The more you can move away from notes and podiums, the more natural you will become. The more natural your presentation, the more the jury will like it. Think of a conversation you have with friends. You don’t use notes. The opening statement is a conversation with 6 or 12 friends who need to be convinced of the right thing to do.

3. Put yourself in the jurors’ shoes. If you have to constantly look at your notes to remember the facts of the case, what impression does that send to the jury? In any case, you should be the person who knows all the facts and details of the case, without having to look them up. A quick and ready knowledge of the case shows that you are competent, prepared, and intelligent.

4. This goes hand-in-hand with the above discussion, but move around a bit! Now that you’re not tied to a podium, there’s no need to stand stock-still. Move around the well. For people who aren’t comfortable moving about, try this: pick three or four fixed geographic locations in the well, perhaps by counsel table, the witness box, the back of the well, and a couple feet from the jury bench. Make a key statement at a location, then move to the next one, while transitioning to your next key statement. This keeps you moving around and when you stop, the jury subconsciously understands that you’re making an important point. Then you walk on to the next location while transitioning, and when you stop – boom, another important point.

5. This is more a personal preference, but take your hands out of your pockets. Hands are for gesturing to emphasize a point. College professors lecture with their hands in their pockets. We’re not professors. We are advocates. We are courtroom lawyers. Putting your hands in your pockets can hunch your shoulders and make you slouch. Nobody likes that. The jurors should see you as strong and upright. If you really must put your hands in your pockets, then make sure you don’t mess up your suit pocket flaps when you take your hands out.

6. Don’t be too academic or “lawyerly” with the jury. You can appear confident, capable, and intelligent without sounding high-falutin’. The jurors aren’t lawyers. They don’t speak legalese. Less is more. For example, your client did not “bring his vehicle to a stop,” he parked his car. He didn’t “exit his vehicle,” he got out of his truck. The state won’t “fail to prove its case beyond a reasonable doubt,” your client is not guilty.

7. Avoid long discussions of “beyond a reasonable doubt” and other legal concepts. It’s the judge’s job to instruct the jury. You can hammer on the burden of proof in closing, but it fits awkwardly into opening, and can lead you into impermissible argument.

8. Lastly, whatever your personal style, be confident in your case. You are telling these jurors why the state’s case is baloney. You need to believe it. As far as that jury is concerned, you must appear confident in your narrative and your case. That’s really the most important thing of all. If you look nervous, timid, or unsure of your statements, why should the jury go along with you?

II. Narrative

Opening is not the time to go into voluminous detail about the case. It’s the time to tell your side of the story, succinctly and persuasively.

A. Theory

You must have a cohesive theory as to why your client is not guilty. I’ve found that making the theory broad and universally acceptable helps win over jurors. No juror is going to agree that DUII is an acceptable thing to do. Very few jurors (and none that make it on to your actual jury panel) will agree that police lie, fabricate, or stretch the truth. But almost all jurors will agree that police need to be thorough – in fact, their job requires an extra degree of thoroughness and care. They’ll agree that police can make mistakes. Because of this, they’ll probably agree that if that extra degree of care isn’t taken, society can suffer. They’ll probably agree that if the police make mistakes, innocent people can get convicted. It’s not because of police malice, but human error. They’ll agree that happens, and that citizens shouldn’t be punished for it. And now that’s your broad theory. Jurors can get behind that. And if you show them the facts of this case fit that broad, universally acceptable theory, they will feel comfortable voting not guilty, because they no longer have to think the police officer is lying or malicious – just mistaken or careless.

B. Facts

Sticking with the above theory – police carelessness / lack of thorough investigation– let’s discuss some common facts that can play into this. Perhaps your client was stopped only for speeding. Perhaps he did not do the Walk-and-Turn or One Leg Stand due to injury. Perhaps he declined the breath test. How can these facts play into your theory?

All officers have some training on “non-standard” FSTs. There are other, simple tests they could have done, such as a finger-count or alphabet test, but they did not – because they jumped to conclusions, because they weren’t thorough. They could have gotten a warrant for a blood draw so we would know exactly what the BAC was, but they did not – because they weren’t thorough.

With these facts, you’re arguing your broad, universally acceptable narrative. Had the police gone the extra mile – the mile that society trusts them to go – they would have taken two minutes and done these extra tests. They would have taken the extra half hour and gotten the blood draw warrant. But they didn’t. Not because they’re liars, not because they hate Mr. Client, but because they just weren’t thorough and careful. And now the state is making Mr. Client pay the price for their lack of care.

Naturally, your facts must fit your theory. If your client ran her car into a tree, puked on herself, and blew a .15, you will probably need to choose a different theory than the ones we just discussed.

III. Nuts and Bolts

So far, we’ve discussed broad topics of preparation and narrative. Now we need to work on putting everything together.

A. Delivery

We touched on this above. Don’t use notes, don’t be rigid, and be an effective counterpoint to whatever the prosecutor did.

B. Damaging Evidence

Unless you are exceptionally lucky, there are going to be damaging facts against your client. You must have a rational, succinct explanation for them. Did client swerve while driving? A squirrel ran into the road. Did client smell of alcohol and admit drinking? It’s only a crime to drive while intoxicated. Did client do badly on SFSTS? She was nervous, it was late, and it was cold out.

If you don’t address damaging facts, the jury will wonder why. This isn’t to say that you must explain away every single thing. Often, the prosecution just throws a ton of stuff out there to see what sticks. If you delve into each statement or detail, your opening will be boring and you appear defensive. Put yourself in the prosecutor’s or jurors’ shoes. Which facts are most damaging? How do we explain those?

In your narrative, be careful not to admit the evidence is damaging. Your narrative is a story, from your client’s point of view. So, don’t say something like, “Officer Jones saw Mr. Client swerve, but what really happened was that a squirrel ran into the road.” Instead say, “Mr. Client was driving along perfectly fine until a squirrel ran in the road.” When it’s from your client’s point of view, it can be seen as reasonable, what anyone would do under the circumstances. Plus, it humanizes the client. If you just stand there trying to refute what the prosecutor said, you’re not setting up a proper narrative.

C. Helpful Evidence

Unless you’re exceptionally unlucky, there will always be some useful evidence for you. I’m not sure what they’re teaching prosecutors in DUII school lately, but it’s the rare prosecutor who will ever acknowledge your client did things right. Lots of things. And it’s the rare prosecutor who will acknowledge that the police did something wrong.

In a typical DUII case, when you stand up to speak, the jury has probably heard a parade of horrible things your client said and did. Time to tell them some of the stuff that was done right, and time to tell them where the police went wrong. Again, don’t be over inclusive here. Pick out a few key details and make those the linchpins of your theory.

If the prosecutor has indeed put on his or her “parade of horribles,” the jury may be surprised to hear that your client parked his car correctly, that he produced the requested documents appropriately, and that the SFSTS were conducted incorrectly. If tests (e.g., blood draw in a refusal case) weren’t done, the prosecutor probably hasn’t mentioned this. The jury may be surprised to hear that if the police had been thorough, we would have definite proof of BAC here today. But, they weren’t, and so we don’t.

D. Technical Evidence

In general, openings should not be overly technical. You run the risk or boring of confusing the jury. Nevertheless, some cases hinge on such evidence. To avoid getting bogged down, try to keep technical discussions short and simple. Even if you’re not planning to call an expert of your own to testify, chat with one beforehand. Ask him or her how best to communicate a complicated concept (e.g., retrograde extrapolation) to an unscientific jury.

E. Visual Aids

There are many studies that show people learn better by hearing and seeing. This doesn’t mean that you need a visual aid in every opening. But I like to have something, even a little thing for the jury to look at. In an extremely complex case, I’ll often have a powerpoint that highlights parts of my statements. In a simpler case, that often seems like overkill. I’ve had good results simply using an easel with butcher paper and some markers to write out a few key points. Most courtrooms have these. Or you can have a few Google Earth pictures of the scene and the road to show.

Another fun thing can be if the prosecutor brings charts or writes stuff on the board or easel. If they leave those things accessible, have some fun with them. If they’ve written down all the bad stuff your client did, write the good stuff, in a different color, right next to (or over!) the bad stuff. Write in the stuff the police did wrong. Enjoy it. As long as you’re respectful and not obnoxious about it, the jury tends to like it.

If the prosecutor has brought things from his or her office (e.g., blown-up jury instructions on posterboard, etc.), don’t try to write on those. The judge and the prosecutor will yell at you. That’s no fun. One thing I’ve seen a very excellent DUII attorney (Bob Thuemmel) do is bring in e-z-peel stickers/sticky notes so he can plaster the prosecution’s stuff with them, without altering or damaging the posterboards at all. No one can get mad about that.

F. Lies or Mistakes

As discussed above, it’s the rare jury that will buy into a theory that the police are lying. Do your best to avoid setting up a “Who will you believe” question in your opening. Given that option, almost every jury will believe the uniformed police officer over you or your client. Instead, attempt to harmonize as much of the defense case and the prosecution case as you can. Then it’s much simpler to bring out testimony, and later, closing argument, showing that no one is accusing the police of lying. To find Mr. Client not guilty, no one has to think the police lied. All that happened here is that the police made an honest, human mistake. But that mistake is why Mr. Client is here. And that mistake is why he’s not guilty.

IV. Tactical and Legal Considerations

One of the best things about being the defense is that we can’t really mistrial ourselves. The prosecution has a lot of holes they can step into, leading to a do-over. That can’t really happen to us. So, it may sometimes be worthwhile to push the envelope a bit. Note: I’m not advocating for anyone to go overboard here. Edging up to the line is acceptable (and I encourage it – effective advocacy is tenacious advocacy), but running across it and flaunting it just brings trouble. There are few things more damaging to your credibility than to have the judge admonish you in front of the jury. Having the prosecution’s objections sustained is one thing, but not all prosecutors wear suits and sit at counsel table. Some of them sit behind the bench in black robes, waiting for a chance to punish you. Be aware.

A. Argument

As the jury instruction says, opening statement is not to argue the facts but to present them as you expect them to come out. Here’s an area where we can edge up to the line. In closing argument, you can go full-out and say, “Officer Jones was careless and his investigation was clearly incomplete.” You probably can’t say that in opening. But you can deliver the exact same message just by changing a few words. Opening is for telling the jury what you think the evidence will show, right? So why not tell them, “Folks, you’re going to hear about what steps Officer Jones took. And, frankly, I think you’re going to hear that he didn’t take many. In fact, he didn’t even conduct a test that’s so simple – a finger-count test – that it would have taken just one more minute in the field.” Is that statement argumentative? Possibly. But it also shows exactly what I think the evidence will be. Another example: “The field sobriety tests were designed to make people fail.” That’s argument. Save it for closing. Why not say, “You’re going to see and hear just how easy it is to fail these tests – you’re going to hear that just a single step off an imaginary line gives a police officer enough ‘clues’ to say that someone failed. And because of that, you’re going to see why these tests shouldn’t be relied upon.” Those are all true statements. The facts will very likely bear them out. I don’t see a problem here. Play with the wording, and you’ll be surprised just how much you can say to the jury without it being labeled as argument.

B. Dealing with Objections

Even if you (correctly!) think you’re on the proper side of the line, you must still be aware of the prosecutor. Their job is to rain on your parade. This is where knowing their tendencies can be important. Some prosecutors will simply sit back and object only if you clearly go over the line. Some are hyper vigilant and will object to things as much as they can. In any case, when you receive an objection stop talking. Don’t start arguing or explaining your side (even if you’re right). If the judge wants to hear from you, they will ask you. If the objection is sustained shake it off, and don’t let it bother you. If the jury sees you getting rattled, they remember. I usually just nod and say, “No problem, Judge, I’ll rephrase” and carry right on.

Don’t engage with the prosecutor. Don’t even look at them. If they make a silly objection, don’t react. Sometimes, the prosecutor may make silly speaking objections – “Objection, judge. Defense counsel is arguing and that’s obviously improper in opening statement He’s supposed to stick to what the evidence will show!” I love it when a prosecutor does this. Whether you’re arguing or not, tell the court you’ll rephrase and say the exact same thing you just did – simply preface it with a humble smile to the jury and the phrase, “The evidence will show [exactly what you just said].” Most jurors are perceptive, and they can pick up on the humor. Just be warned – be humble, and be polite. If you overdo it or venture into smirks, eye-rolls, or other such smarmy territory, the jury will dislike you, and rightly so.

C. Making Objections

Most prosecutors are pretty good about avoiding objectionable material in opening. After all, they’re the ones that can get a mistrial and have to go tell all their prosecutor buddies about how they screwed up opening. So they tend to be pretty careful. Despite that, you should always be vigilant.

Everyone’s style is different, however. If you object too often (even rightly so), the jury may not appreciate it. They definitely won’t appreciate it if you keep getting overruled. Some defense lawyers like to keep the prosecution on their toes with objections, others are more laissez-faire. It’s really up to you, and how you want to play it. What we discussed above cuts both ways. If the prosecutor is veering into argument feel free to object, but be prepared for them to simply rephrase and continue.

However, you should always be on the lookout for certain things. Never let the prosecutor comment on your client wanting to make a phone call or speak to a lawyer. Never let them comment on questions your client chose not to answer. Never let them comment on what your client might say if she testifies. If the prosecution goes into this territory you must object, and be prepared to ask for a mistrial or a curative instruction. Generally I prefer asking for a mistrial first, since all a curative instruction really does is highlight the inadmissible evidence. See generally Fiswick v. United States, 329 US 211, 218 (1946). If you don’t get your mistrial, however, you need to ask for a curative instruction, as failure to do so may waive error by the court in not granting a mistrial. See generally State v. Flores, 31 Or App 187, 190, (1977).

D. Judicial Admissions

Statements made during opening, although not evidence, may constitute a “judicial admission,” which the state may use in meeting its burden of proof. State v. Adams, 29 Or App 827, (1997); ORE 801(4)(b); Cf. State v. Howell, 56 Or App 6, (1982). So don’t agree to any of the elements of DUII if you intend to challenge that element. This includes things like date, venue, and public highways/premises open to the public.

Be aware of what you say about your client, especially if he is not testifying or you haven’t decided yet. If you say something like, “Mr. Client will tell you [facts]” during your opening, you may be perilously close to forfeiting his right to remain silent. The difference between a statement like that and something like “Mr. Client didn’t feel Officer Jones treated him fairly, which is why he declined the breath test,” is that the latter statement doesn’t refer to anything Mr. Client might say when he’s on the stand. And, the prosecutor can’t stand up in closing and say, “We don’t know what was in Mr. Client’s head because he didn’t testify.” That would be a mistrial. But if you said that Mr. Client would testify to that and he doesn’t…you’re walking the razor’s edge.

V. Conclusion

I’ll turn to Bill Uhle, another excellent DUII lawyer and the 2004 DUII Trial Notebook editor to conclude:

“Your opening statement should be used to outline your theory of defense. In a DUII trial, your theory of defense is limited only by your imagination in coming up with a logical explanation as to why, using the facts and the law, your client did not commit the crime of DUII beyond a reasonable doubt. Having a viable theory of defense gives your defense direction and something to hang onto [even] when the state’s case is overwhelming.”

Closing Argument

Edward Kroll

I. Introduction

The closing statement is your last word in any trial. It’s here that you sum up all the evidence, argue your theory of defense, and show why all the questions you asked in voir dire and cross-examination were important. It’s here that you show why the state hasn’t come close to proving its case. It’s here where you ask the jury to find your client not guilty.

Unless you’re reading this manual out of order, you’ve already looked at the chapter on opening statements. Several of the concepts discussed there will be mentioned again here.

II. Preparation Before Closing

A. Know Your Case

Unlike opening, closing is sometimes more off-the-cuff. You almost always know what day (and usually right around what time) you will be opening, giving you a better chance to think it through and prepare it. Closing can sneak up on you, especially if a witness is very brief or a judge decides to keep the jury into the lunch hour so you can close. This isn’t to say you should plan to freestyle your closing without preparation. Just be aware that you likely won’t have as much preparation. That’s why knowing your case – both the objective facts and your theory of defense – is important. Being able to flow smoothly in closing can help persuade the jury, far more than a bunch of “um, uh, ah” and fumbling with notes.

B. Know Your Jury

At the end of the case, you’ll have spent at least a solid day with these 6–12 folks, possibly longer. If you’ve paid attention during trial, you may have some small idea how they’re feeling. This can be difficult as even the worst poker player is hard to read when you put him or her on a jury. Hopefully you’ve been able to gauge some bit of their moods or reactions to key evidence, such as how they appeared when the police testified, or during your client’s testimony (if you put him on the stand). Based on what you’ve observed, you can make alterations to what you were going to say, or how you planned to say it.

C. Listen to the Prosecutor

As usual, the state gets to go first. You should pay close attention to what the prosecutor says to see what he or she thinks are the state’s biggest strengths and biggest weaknesses. Perhaps you’ll agree, perhaps you won’t. But you need to understand what angles they will be pushing. We’ll discuss what to say often below, but for now, just realize that you will have to counter some of their points, even if you didn’t necessarily plan to. Otherwise, the jury may be left wondering why you didn’t mention it.

D. Be Natural

As discussed in the openings chapter, I’m a big advocate of moving around the courtroom, ditching the podium, and shedding your notes. Closing is the time to harness your inner orator, and you don’t want to be tied to notes or a podium like crutches. Besides, by this point, you had better know the case facts and your theory of defense by heart. If you don’t know either of these, the jury will clearly see this and they will conclude that you are not confident in your case. And if you’re not confident, how can they be?

III. Narrative – What Do You Actually Say?

A. Theory

Your entire closing should be built around your theory of the case. From voir dire to opening to cross you should be planting this theory with the jury and nurturing it. As mentioned in the openings chapter, I find that broad, easily supportable theories do best and resonate most with jurors. Whatever your theory is, every single piece of your case is tied to it, culminating with this closing. Don’t lose sight of your theory. That’s the idea you want the jury to take with them when they go into that room and start talking. Make sure your theory is concise and easily conveyed. Too many closings fail due to disorganization, rambling, and needless repetition.

B. Demonstrative Aids

If the prosecutor has any experience, he or she likely has some visual aids with them. These can range from self-created charts listing out all the things your client did wrong to blown-up jury instructions on posterboard, or anything in between. Regardless, most competent prosecutors have something for the jury to look at. Whether they do or not, you often should.

What you use/draw/create will depend on the facts of your case. Again as mentioned in Opening Statement, the prosecutor will almost never mention what your client did right. So point this out. Draw your own chart. For example, perhaps your client was pulled over for speeding and weaving:

State Mr. Client

  • Weaved twice • Used turn signals appropriately

  • 15 mph over the speed limit • No sudden acceleration or braking

    • Obeyed traffic signals

    • Pulled over in a timely manner

    • Parked appropriately

    • Used flashers

If the state made a chart, draw over it in a different color. If they brought a pre-made chart, don’t write on it directly – that will just get you yelled at. Instead, break out some sticky notes and plaster their nice posterboard with notes showing everything Mr. Client did right. This also works for physical observations:

State Mr. Client

  • Red, watery eyes • He’s a smoker and it was 2:00 a.m.

  • Odor of alcohol • He admitted drinking

  • Slow production of documents • He took care with his movements, as

  • Poor balance during FSTs. he was in the presence of an armed officer

• No balance problems getting out of the car

• The only balance problems were during these awkward tests

• Appropriately and calmly answered questions

Etcetera. Overwhelm the state’s evidence with your own. The jurors often don’t think about all the things your client did right, and the state’s not going to mention them. But just think about an intoxicated, impaired, or drunk person – would they be able to do all of these things?

C. Specific Arguments

It is difficult to give a blueprint for a “standard” DUII closing as there are so many moving parts, and a great deal depends on the evidence and cross-examination of the police in your particular case. Nevertheless, there are certain areas you should often touch on. We’ve discussed several of these above – lack of “bad driving” and rational and obvious explanations for physical appearance. We’ll touch on a few more below.

1. FSTs

The state loves FSTs. They love to spend hours (well, it feels like hours) talking about how badly your client did on these tests. Assuming you have the facts to make the argument (cross-examination of the officer will be key), turn the FSTs on their head.

a. HGN

It’s an involuntary movement of the eyes. It does not cause any vision problems. It does not cause any reaction-time issues. The person doesn’t see the world hitch and jitter in their vision. It merely shows that alcohol has been consumed. Mr. Client admitted to having a drink! If he’d said he had nothing to drink, then this test would show him to be a liar, but that’s not the case here.

b. Walk-and-Turn

It’s a crazy test. If you step off the line, you will naturally miss heel-to-toe as well, unless you do something crazy. (Demonstrate a crazy step that is somehow off-line but still heel-to-toe – this usually involves putting my feet at right angles to each other. If you’ve gauged the jury’s mood correctly, this can make them laugh.) And so if you step off the line once, you’ve committed two “clues.” And that’s all it takes to fail. The state says this test should be easy. Well, how many of us walk this way on a daily basis? Weekly? Monthly? Yearly? Oh, right, we never do.

c. One-Leg Stand

How many of us can stand on one leg without swaying at all for 30 seconds? I mean, this test presumes that the average person should be able to stand on one foot, with no sway, for 30 seconds. Preposterous. And if you sway, you’ll put your arms out for balance, or put your foot down. And boom, you’ve failed the test.

d. General Performance

Remind the jury that these tests aren’t done in a vacuum. The police officer says they’re easy, but he does them on a daily basis. He’s used to them. He’s not nervous when he does them. He hasn’t been pulled over by an armed person. He’s not in danger of going to jail if his nerves get the better of him. In a controlled, safe environment, we could probably all do these tests fine. But in the dark, on the side of the road, your heart hammering out of your chest, the state expects people to do these tests just fine? Nonsense.

2. Scientific Evidence

Here, you’re probably dealing with a breath test result, or some sort of back-extrapolation rubbish from a state “expert.” Ideally, during cross you would have shown that the state expert’s scientific evidence was just so much noise. Perhaps you had an expert of your own testify during the defense case. If you intend to tackle scientific evidence during closing, do so succinctly. The concepts are nebulous and difficult to grasp. Make sure you can communicate your argument clearly to a jury without getting hung up on things. Whether or not you had an expert during your case, it can be worthwhile to talk to one beforehand. He or she can often give you some pointers on how best to explain certain technical concepts to a lay jury.

If you’re challenging the Inoxilyzer result, you need to be prepared to argue why it was not accurate in this case. Perhaps your client did well on the FSTs and had very little bad driving, but inexplicably produced quite a high blow. Ask the jury – will they trust objective facts that show someone in control of their mental and physical faculties, or will they trust a machine they know nothing about?

3. Refusals

The state will often use some sort of analogy about a young child asking his parents to stay home from school because he’s sick, but when the parents ask to take the kid’s temperature, the kid says “No, just believe me, I have a terrible fever.” The state argues that the kid’s reasoning and Mr. Client’s are the same – they know an actual test will show the truth, that no fever exists, and that Mr. Client was intoxicated.

Countering this argument depends a great deal on how you handled cross. The Closing Argument chapter is not the place to discuss cross-examination techniques, but ideally you’ll have at least gotten the officer to admit that he or she did not get a blood draw after a breath test refusal. Then you can argue that it’s the state’s burden and the state’s burden alone to provide evidence. And we know just how possible it is for the state to get this evidence. But they chose not to. We could be here with concrete evidence – proof, one way or another. But the police refused to do this. Much like the child who refuses the thermometer, why did the state refuse to produce evidence? It’s their burden, isn’t it? There – we’ve (hopefully…) turned the state’s analogy back on them. This argument works even better if your client refused the breath test but asked for a blood test.

4. Lies or Mistakes?

There is almost always going to be a discrepancy with what the police saw / believed, and what you want the jury to do. The jury will be wrestling with this. “Modern” juries want to believe the police are on their side and will always tell the truth. Such juries will react rather negatively to claims that the police are lying or fabricating. Unless you have a specific, provable lie, you want to avoid making the jury decide if they believe the officer or not. If they’re put in this position, it will end badly for your client. Instead, through your case theory and your cross-examination, show that the police simply made mistakes. They weren’t thorough. They didn’t record any part of the encounter. They didn’t go the extra mile to get a blood draw. They didn’t give alternative field tests that don’t involve bizarre balancing acts. They simply jumped to a conclusion and stuck with it. It doesn’t make them bad or malicious people. It makes them human. But humans make mistakes, and that’s why this jury is here – because the police made a mistake and Mr. Client is caught up
in it.

Framing the issue this way sidesteps calling the police liars or even challenging their credibility. It gives the jury an option to vote not guilty without having to decide that the police are lying. For better or worse, the police have – by far – the upper hand in a credibility contest, so don’t go there unless you have something concrete.

5. Meet Damaging Evidence Head-On

Sometimes, you’ll have a case with one or two (or more…) pieces of particularly bad evidence. Your client rates themselves a 9 on the 1–10 scale of sobriety, with 10 being the most drunk he’s ever been, or tells the officer “I’m really drunk,” or almost hits a bus full of nuns and puppies. If you run away from this and don’t address it in some form during closing, the prosecutor will hammer on this in rebuttal: “Did you notice how Mr. Kroll didn’t even mention the nuns and the puppies that his client endangered with his drunk driving?” When you have bad facts, you need to give the jury some sort of framework to work around them. This is where your theory of the case comes back. From the very beginning of your case, you should be aware of the very worst facts for you, and your theory must have some part that addresses this. If you meet challenges like this without shrinking away and let the jury see that it doesn’t bother you, perhaps they’ll realize that this is just one piece of evidence that doesn’t fit in with everything else you’ve brought to light. Then they can work around it to get to not guilty.

IV. Tactical and Legal Considerations

A. Length

There’s no hard-and-fast rule about length with your closing. As with everything, it’ll depend on your particular case. That said, beware of the closing that rambles on or repeats unnecessarily. Jurors get bored, and if you’re making the same points over and over, you come across as dull and uninspiring.

If you have a lot of ground to cover, then it’s fine to take a while, as long as you keep the pace moving and don’t repeat yourself. The main issue that confounds defense attorneys is that in closings, the prosecutor gets the first and the last word. So many attorneys feel they have to cram as much into their closing as possible, taking apart everything the prosecutor said during their first closing and anticipating and rebutting arguments the prosecutor will make in their rebuttal closing. In a perfect world, we would be able to do this in a timely fashion, but it’s often unwieldy. Focus on telling your story and strongly communicating your theory. That’s really the best way to go about it.

Don’t worry so much about anticipating rebuttal arguments. Ask the jury to do it for you: “Folks, I’m about to sit down, and this is the last you’ll hear from me. But Mr. Prosecutor over there gets to have the last word, so that you’ll go to that jury room with his arguments in your ears. He’s going to get up here and tell you why I’m wrong. Well, that’s because it’s his burden. He’s got to prove his case to you. That’s why he gets to talk so much. It’s why he got to go first in opening, first with the evidence, and first in closing. So while he’s up here, talking to you again, I’m asking you all – ask yourselves, what would Mr. Kroll say if he got a chance to respond? What logical, reasonable answers would he give – what ones has he already given?”

Now, I’m not saying do this verbatim. Everyone has to tailor it to themselves. But this can accomplish a few things - you don’t have to anticipate rebuttal arguments, you can play the “underdog” card, and if the prosecutor takes a long rebuttal, it may turn the jury off.

All that being said, sometimes Shakespeare is right – “Brevity is the soul of wit.” One of the most effective closings I’ve seen was when the police really dropped the ball in a case – witness statements were omitted, suspect statements were omitted, tests weren’t done properly – and despite that, there was a substantial amount of damaging evidence against the client. The defense attorney’s closing was very short – just a minute or two – and basically said

“Folks, we deserve better from the police. They were careless in this case. Not malicious, not bad, but incredibly careless. That’s not acceptable. But if you think it is acceptable, then I’m not going to be able to change your mind. If you’re comfortable with this level of effort, go back there and convict in five minutes. But if you think we, as a society, deserve more, if we deserve competence, then send a message. Find [Mr. Client] not guilty.”

The jury did indeed come back not guilty. After three hours of deliberation, but still. That kind of argument and timing isn’t for every case, or really, for many cases at all. Just keep in mind the power that a few well-chosen words can sometimes have under the right circumstances.

B. Making Objections

As in opening, objections to the prosecutor’s closing should be fairly rare. It’s argument, and pretty much anything goes. Your main objections are likely to be:

  • If the prosecutor comments on facts that aren’t in evidence.

  • If the prosecutor comments on your client remaining silent, either to the police or at trial.

  • If the prosecutor tries to scare the jury, e.g. “Next time, it might not be puppies and nuns that Mr. Client endangers – it might be your whole entire family!” or “Don’t let the defendant walk out of the courtroom acquitted because next time it might be one of you at the end of his barrel.” State v. Reynolds, 43 Or App 619 (1979).

  • If the prosecutor says that the state’s evidence is “uncontradicted” or “unimpeached” if your client is the only possible person who could contradict the evidence. State v. Buck, 239 Or 577 (1965).

  • If the prosecutor makes derogatory personal statements about you or your client.

  • If the prosecutor personally vouches for a witness, e.g. “I believe Officer Smith.” Note that say-
    ing “You should believe Officer Smith,” or “Officer Smith has no reason to lie” are probably permissible.

If the prosecutor does any of these things, you must preserve the record. Generally, you need to request both a mistrial and a curative instruction. Just asking for a mistrial (without curative instruction) can waive the appeal on the lack of a curative instruction. See generally State v. Flores, 31 Or App 187, 190, (1977).

If your objection isn’t for something listed here, then it’s likely better not to object, as you’ll simply be overruled and look silly or awkward in front of the jury.

C. Taking Objections

Basically, unless you find venture into the no-no territory discussed above, you shouldn’t draw an objection. It’s argument, after all. But if you do draw an objection for something not taboo, don’t let it upset you. Don’t engage with the prosecutor. Don’t even respond to them. See if the judge wants to hear from you or will just overrule the objection. If the judge wants a response to a silly objection,
you can often simply say “Judge, this is my argument,” which should suffice. If the prosecutor’s objection is sustained, accept it and say, “No problem, judge, I’ll rephrase/move on.” Don’t let anything ruffle you.

V. Conclusion

Closing argument is the last time we talk to the jury. Don’t confuse it with Law & Order – this isn’t the place to go over the top. It’s the place to succinctly, intelligently, and persuasively show the jury why your theory of the case makes sense, and why the state – the police and the prosecution – haven’t met their burden.
