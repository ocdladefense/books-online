Jury Instructions

By L. Todd Wilson

I. Rules for Jury Instructions

Note: All the rules of civil procedure may be found online here: Oregon Rules of Civil Procedure.

A. ORS 136.330

ORS 136.330 lists the applicable rules of civil procedure in criminal cases for jury instructions, ORCP 59 A through F, and ORCP 59 G (1)(3)(4)(5) and (H). Following are certain rules of note.

1. ORCP 59 (B). Jury instructions can be reduced to writing or recorded and given to the jury for deliberations.

a. Must be requested at the commencement of the trial.

b. If you are requesting taped instructions be provided to the jury for deliberations, it is a good idea to bring your own recording device or check with the clerk before instruction to find the best way to make the recording. See State v. Looper, 77 Or App 660, 713 P2d 1099 (1986); Hansen v. Bennett, 162 Or App 380, 986 P2d 633 (1999) (failure of trial court to comply with ORCP 59(B) requires a new trial, but trial court’s failure to comply must be on the record in order for reviewing court to determine compliance).

c. UTCR 6.070 provides that:

(1) A party requesting written or recorded jury instructions as provided by ORCP 59 (B) shall have the responsibility for recording or reducing the court’s instructions to writing in a timely manner; and

(2) No identifying information relating to the parties or any other extraneous material, including authorities, must appear on the typed or copied instructions.

2. ORCP 59 (C) Deliberation.

a. The jury may take four items with them into deliberations:

i. an impartial written statement summarizing the issues to be decided by the jury

ii. their notes

iii. exhibits received (jury cannot take any exhibit not received into evidence into the jury room); and

iv. copies of documents that may be substituted for any public records or private documents that ought not, in the opinion of the court, be taken from the person in possession.

b. The jury must remain together and separate from all others, absent a valid order of the court; if allowed to separate, must be instructed by the court on the reason for separation.

3. ORCP 59 (D) Questions by jurors.

a. Judge may require the jury to be brought back into court and reinstructed.

b. Counsel must be present or notified prior to reinstruction.

c. If jurors have questions, the question is presented to the court through the bailiff. Typically, the judge will confer with counsel and then have the bailiff return with a written answer. Sometimes, the nature of the question merely requires that the jury be reinstructed.

4. ORCP 59 (G). Polling the jury; the hunt for inconsistencies and misconduct.

a. There is a strong preference for finality of jury verdicts. DC Thompson and Co. v. Hauge, 72 Or App 116 (1985) (jurors’ post-verdict affidavits stating that they did not understand jury verdict forms are not sufficient misconduct to cause reversal); State v. Gardner, 230 Or 569, 371 P2d 558 (1962); [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=Leland+Properties+V.,+152+Or+App+557,+954+P2d+851+%281998&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=7283320957668130064&scilh=0 Leland Properties V. Burton Eng’g and Survey, 152 Or App 557, 954 P2d 851 (1998)] (long discussion on long history of refusing to consider jurors’ post-verdict affidavits to impeach a verdict).

b. May be requested by either side.

c. Always poll each individual juror. See State v. DeVault, 78 Or App 307, 715 P2d 1353 (1986). Even if a juror votes for conviction in the jury room, that juror may change his/her vote when polled and defeat the verdict. Defendant is entitled to have deliberations continue until enough jurors are willing to assure individual responsibility for verdict to permit verdict to stand. State v. DeVault, 78 Or App at 309.

d. If jurors answering in the affirmative are less than the number required to reach a verdict, the jury shall be sent out for further deliberation. State v. Imlah, 204 Or 43, 281 P2d 973 (1955). However, once the jurors’ verdict has been received by the court, absent some egregious juror misconduct, the verdict will stand, even if the jurors come back to the court later claiming error. DC Thompson and Co. v. Hauge, 72 Or App 116 (1985).

Practice Tip

Retired Oregon Supreme Court Justice Van Hoomissen has dissented on the issue, suggesting that where all jurors unanimously agree on a verdict but fail to accurately record or communicate that verdict, the trial court should have broad discretion to set the verdict aside. See State v. Barmon, 67 Or App 369, 384, 679 P2d 888 (1984) (Van Hoomissen, J. dissenting).

5. ORCP 59 (H) Exceptions.

a. No instruction shall be subject to review by the Court of Appeals unless an exception is noted by the judge.

b. The exception must be made and recorded immediately after the court instructs the jury.

c. Exceptions are not necessary to an adverse ruling such as refusal to give an instruction or refusal to give a statement of issues.

B. Instructions Requested in Writing

1. Check supplementary local court rules (SLRs) and UTCR 6.070.

2. Instructions must be provided by the party requesting and cannot contain information that identifies the party or points and authorities. It is the responsibility of the requesting party to make the record clear for appeal; the party cannot simply rely on the “usual” practice of the trial court. Leland Properties V. Burton Eng’g and Survey, 152 Or App 557, 954 P2d 851 (1998).

3. All Oregon Uniform Criminal Jury Instructions are available to members of the bar via the BarBooks section of the Oregon State Bar website.

C. Uniform Jury Instructions

UCrJI 2700–2712a set forth all of the current instructions regarding DUII. Although the courts generally do not agree to modify the uniform instructions, the accused can challenge or request modification of the instructions.

D. Limiting Instructions

1. The court shall, upon request, give a limiting instruction. The commentary states that the effectiveness of the limiting instruction must be taken into consideration in deciding whether to exclude evidence for unfair prejudice under OEC 403.

2. Request the instruction immediately after the evidence comes in, unless you think it would draw undue attention to the prejudicial testimony.

3. Request it at the end of the case together with the other instructions.

4. Case Law

Examples of cases where DUII is alleged along with other crimes where limiting instructions are appropriate.

Evidence of a defendant’s past participation in a DUII diversion program has been determined relevant in an Assault III prosecution. State v. Cavaner, 206 Or App 131, 135 P3d 402 (2006).

Evidence of a defendant’s prior conviction for DUII has been allowed in a prosecution for an allegation involving extreme indifference to the value of human life. State v. Hopkins, 173 Or App 1, 21 P3d 134 (2001). See also State v. Wyant 217 Or App 199, 175 P3d 988 (2007) (evidence of prior DUII conviction admissible in Assault III prosecution).

II. Specific Instructions

A. Insanity Defense

Under State v. Olmstead, 310 Or 455, 800 P2d 277 (1990), the affirmative defense of guilty but insane, ORS 161.295, is applicable in DUIIs. See “DWI and the Insanity Defense: A Reasoned Approach,” 20 Vt. L. Rev. 161, 175 (1995).

B. Theory of the Case Instruction

Under State v. McBride, 287 Or 315, 599 P2d 449 (1979), defendant is entitled to have his theory of the case presented to the jury through instruction if there is evidence to support it. But it is not error for a trial court to refuse to give a requested instruction that is incorrect in some respect in the terms in which it is proposed. State v. Francis, 284 Or 621, 626, 588 P2d 611 (1978).

See Sample Jury Instruction No. l on page 16–9.

C. Unanimity — No Boots Instruction — UCrJI 2708

The jury has to be unanimous only as to whether the accused was under the influence as the element of DUII, the jury need not agree on the how the accused is impaired, just whether he or she is impaired. There is not a separate crime for each theory of impairment. The accused can be convicted if the fact finder believes either: 1) being under the influence of intoxicating liquors, 2) having a BAC of .08 or greater, 3) being under the influence of controlled substances, or 4) being under the influence of controlled substances and intoxicating liquors. Therefore, three jurors can find the accused had a BAC of .08 or greater and three could find that the accused was simply under the influence of intoxicants, but it is still one crime. State v. King, 316 Or 437 (1993). Accordingly, there is no instruction allowed requiring unanimity of theories under State v. Boots, cert den, 510 U.S. 1013, 114 S Ct 606, 126 L Ed 2d 571 (1993).

D. Attempt Instruction

An accused is entitled to request a lesser included attempted DUII instruction if such an instruction is supported by the evidence. State v. Baty, 243 Or App 77, 259 P3d 98 (2011). If the facts support it, you may request it. Baty involved a question as to whether a driver in a parking lot, who was attempting to place her vehicle in reverse, actually placed her vehicle in motion or not.

But see State v. Snyder, 288 Or App 58 (2017) (Shorr, J.), where attempted DUII instruction was requested based on a defense that the driver’s blood alcohol content was rising after the stop based on the driver having consumed “5 or 6 beers in a four hour period.” The trial court refused to give an attempted DUII instruction and the Court of Appeals affirmed noting that intoxication is a status that does not require conduct, intent, or a mental state rendering a theory of criminal attempt liability inapplicable.

Note: The Snyder decision does not restrict defense counsel’s ability to present evidence and argument that the .08 legal limit was not met at the time of actual operation of a vehicle.

The legislature did not consider the crime of attempted DUII, so there is no license suspension or mandatory alcohol evaluation and treatment.

See Sample Jury Instruction No. 2 on page 16–9.

E. Operating a Vessel While Under the Influence (BUII)

With the dawn of the Intoxilyzer 1400 and increased traffic on the state’s waterways, it is now a crime to operate a vessel under the influence. BUII jury instructions are found at 2900, 2901 and 2902. 2902 has a cross reference in the Comment section back to 2700–2712 (General DUII instructions).

Oregon law applies a very broad definition of the term “boat” for purposes of application to the BUII statute, ORS 830.005 – “every description of watercraft, including a seaplane on the water and not in flight, used or capable of being used as a means of transportation on the water, but does not include boathouses, floating homes, air mattresses, beach and water toys or single inner tubes.” According to State v. Lambert, 227 Or App 614, 206 P3d 1065 (2009), a person floating in a one- or two-person raft on a navigable river, whether actually propelling the raft or not, is exercising actual physical control of the raft to a degree that brings him or her within the BUII statute, 830.325. Id at 621.

Practice Tip

There are no definitions for “propel or be in actual physical control” in the statute nor is there a definition of these terms in the jury instructions. However, “operate” is defined in ORS 830.005(8) and also in UCrJI 2900(2). The language “or otherwise uses” may be subject to a vagueness challenge. However, the Lambert court indicated that evidence of a person occupying a raft, who was allegedly attempting to go upstream but was found on the shore, did not persuade them to reverse a BUII conviction but also declined to define “operate” or “actual physical control” for purposes of BUII.

Make the government choose which theory because the verdict must be unanimous as to each element of the offense. See State v. King, 316 Or 437 (1993).

F. No Instruction on Being Under the Influence of Controlled Substances Unless Pled

Under State v. McFeron, 166 Or App 110, 999 P2d 470 (2000), no evidence regarding an accused’s use of controlled substances is admissible and no instruction is allowed regarding being under the influence of anything except intoxicating liquors unless the state alleges being under the influence of controlled substances in the complaint.

G. Felony DUII

The felony DUII instructions are now UCrJI No. 2704 (intoxicating liquor) and 2706 (combination of intoxicating liquor/inhalants and/or controlled substances). However, see Chapter 1 on strategy for felony DUII cases. If you are not successful challenging the validity of prior convictions, then you will probably want to stipulate to their validity so the jury does not hear about them. In that case, you would not use 2704 or 2706, which has convicted of DUII previously as an element, and instead use 2703 or other appropriate misdemeanor DUII instruction.

H. When the Court Erroneously Allows a Breath Test Result

When the court erroneously allows in a breath test result, the defense is that the individual was not under the influence and the government relies on UCrJI 2703, then it is not harmless error. State v. Coen, 203 Or App 92, 125 P 3d 761 (2005).

I. Evidence Instruction Less Than Satisfactory

For a discussion on the use of less satisfactory evidence instruction in DUII cases see State v. Schneider, 201 Or App 546, 120 P3d 16 (2005).

J. General Driving Duties Instruction Allowed

State v. Clark, 256 Or App 428, 300 P3d 281 (2013). In a prosecution for DUII and other traffic crimes alleging recklessness, the trial court instructed the jury with the following statement:

The driver of a motor vehicle is required by law to drive upon a highway or premises open to the public at a speed no greater than is reasonable and prudent, having due regard for the traffic, surface and width of the road, hazards at intersections and any other conditions existing. A driver has a continuing duty to keep a reasonable lookout and maintain the automobile under reasonable control, and that is such as would be exercised by a reasonable person.

This instruction, in the author’s opinion, can possibly be used in a DUII case where the accused’s driving is good and impairment is alleged on other grounds other than driving.

K. Voluntary Act (UCrJI 1065)

The state must prove the accused in a DUII case acted consciously. State v. Newman, 353 Or 632, 302 P3d 435 (2013). This is especially relevant in any case where your client has taken a sleep medication or is otherwise arguing that their driving was not done consciously due to other medications.

L. Contrasts Between Confessions and Admissions

A driver was not entitled to the “Confession Instruction” (UCrJI 1050) where a driver’s statement, “Oh, yeah, I drove around but not like that,” was considered to be an admission of driving but not a confession to the crime of DUII. State v. Barbero, 297 Or App 372 (2019) (James, P.J.).

M. Alternative Theories of DUII

The court rejects defendant’s argument that the trial court erred in instructing the jury that it could find defendant guilty of DUII if it found that she had driven under the influence of intoxicants, the influence of controlled substances, or a combination of the two, when the state alleged that defendant committed DUII by driving under the influence of intoxicants and a controlled substance. Defendant contended that ORS 813.010(1)(a),(b), and (c), set forth different ways of committing DUII and each were essential elements of the offense. The court concludes that they are not elements, but are alternative factual theories of proving the element of intoxication. The court further concludes that ORS 813.010(2), which requires the state to allege controlled substances if proceeding under that theory, does not change that rule. State v. Leachman, 285 Or App 756 (2017) (Armstrong, P.J.).

III. Exceptions

A. No Presumptions

Watch for any instruction that allows the jury to presume that defendant’s blood alcohol level at the time he took the test was the same or greater than when he was driving. State v. Conway, 75 Or App 430,707 P2d 618 (1985). State v. Kohlasch, 11 Or App 459, 502 P2d 1158 (1972), gets the state past judgment of acquittal on the issue of whether the defendant’s BAC is over the legal limit at the time he is driving because of a disputable presumption (or inference), but in State v. Rainey, 298 Or 459, 639 P2d 635 (1985), the court states “it is up to the advocate not the judge to comment on inferences.” In Conway, the court states that it is doubtful that the holding and rationale of Kohlasch survives Rainey.

B. DUII By Itself Is Not Reckless Endangering

See State v. Maciel-Cortes, 231 Or App 302, 218 P3d 900 (2009) (The trial court, in a prosecution for Recklessly Endangering Another, instructed the jury that “[d]riving under the influence of intoxicants is, itself, evidence that a person created a substantial risk of physical injury to passengers.” The conviction was reversed on appeal as the instruction was determined to be an improper comment on the evidence).

C. Miles Instruction – Person’s Physical Condition (UCrJI 2708): May Not Be Dead but Wounded Severely

Make sure if the state requests a Miles instruction that there was actual evidence, not an inference, in the case that indicates that particular defendant was more susceptible to the effects of alcohol when driving, otherwise the instruction is not appropriate. State v. Massey, 240 Or App 689, 278 P3d 130 (2012). In Massey, the evidence at trial showed a 71-year-old man with back and leg problems who experienced dizziness from a low blood pressure medication, who testified he’d been cutting brush all day and felt progressively worse as the day went on, yet the evidence was not sufficient to allow a Miles instruction, even in the context of a bench trial, which Massey was.

Also, remember, an allegation that a defendant was operating under the influence of controlled substances must be pled and proven, or no instruction. State v. McFeron, 166 Or App 110 (2000); State v. Stiles, 165 Or App 584 (2000), and State v. Jayne, 173 Or App 533, 24 P3d 920 (2001). Make sure to take an exception if you believe the evidence is insufficient.

But see State v. Moody, 201 Or App 58, 116 P3d 935 (2005).

1. Defendant’s testimony that he was tired or taking medication does not support the instruction. In Miles, defendant testified that his “condition” was a result of his medication.

2. There must be some evidence suggesting the medication increased the effect of the alcohol, if controlled substances are pled, or fatigue if controlled substances not pled. See State v. Huck, 100 Or App 193, 785 P2d 785 (1990) (Miles instruction was improper). But cf., State v. Anderson, 117 Or App 495, 844 P2d 923 (1992) (Miles instruction was proper).

3. The instruction may not be appropriate where defendant is alleged to be under the influence of a controlled substance and not alcohol, as the Uniform Jury Instructions appear to cover those circumstances.

4. See State v. Rich, 259 Or App 655, 314 P3d 979 (2013) (although the defendant told the arresting officer that he had taken “pain medication” because of a bad knee, that evidence, without more, was insufficient to allow a Miles instruction. See also State v. Curtis, 182 Or App 166, 47 P3d 929 (2002) (although there was evidence the defendant was exhausted, there was no evidence the exhaustion made him more susceptible to the effects of alcohol; giving the Miles instruction was improper); State v. Roller, 181 Or App 542, 47 P3d 52 (2002) (although there was evidence the defendant had the flu, there was no evidence that this made him more susceptible to the effects of alcohol; giving the Miles instruction was an error).

Use the Miles counter attack instruction, sample No. 3, on page 16–10.

D. Refusal Instruction UCrJI 2712 Impacted by State v. Banks

State’s failure to demonstrate that officer’s request for driver to take a breath test could reasonably be understood as a request only to provide physical cooperation, and not as a request for constitutionally-significant consent to search, rendered a breath test refusal inadmissible against the driver. State v. Banks, 364 Or 332, 434 P3d 361 (2019) ( Walters, P.J.). Naturally, this ruling will impact the viability of UCrJI 2712 and 2712a in the even of urinalysis. In the event you are faced with a refusal case, be sure to review the Banks opinion to determine whether the refusal is admissible into evidence.

The Banks case involved a one-vehicle accident where the driver was determined to be under the influence of alcohol by the investigating officer. The driver would not open his mouth at the request of the officer. The officer then initiated the implied consent process and read the back of the Implied Consent Combined Report concluding with a request for the driver to take a breath test. The driver refused and the arresting officer lodged a refusal. The state at trial offered the refusal evidence at trial, over defense objection. The Banks court ruled that the request of the officer to open the driver’s mouth and reading of the implied consent rights did not overcome the driver’s rights to refuse a warrantless search. The Court ruled a refusal to submit to a breath test after reading of the implied consent rules, without additional evidence that would satisfy the state’s burden of proof to prove refusal to take a breath test as evidence of consciousness of guilt, is inadmissible.

E. General Instructions that Require Exceptions

1. It is not the function of the court in instructing the jury to tell them what the state should not prove (e.g., bad driving).

2. Instructions referring to guilt or innocence:

The jury does not find anyone “innocent,” only that there is reasonable doubt as to guilt or not.

3. Flight.

State v. Stilling, 285 Or 293, 590 P2d 1223 (1979), and State v. Greene, 30 Or App 1019, 568 P2d 716 (1977), both say instructions on the significance of flight should not be given. The subject of flight, or lack thereof, can be argued by counsel in closing, but a jury instruction on flight is inappropriate.

Bill Uhle (ed., 2004): The trend in the legislature and judiciary is to close as many of the defendant’s defenses to the charge of DUII as possible. A fertile but often forgotten or overlooked area in selling a case to a jury or creating reversible error is in creative use of jury instructions. Requesting and receiving a jury instruction on your theory of defense lets the jury know that your theory of defense is legitimate and recognized by the court. The more you can incorporate the language of jury instructions to support your closing argument, the more legitimate and acceptable a not guilty verdict becomes in the eyes of the jury.

  1. Special Jury Instruction No. 2

Jury May Consider Attempted Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicants

Although the complaint charges the offense of Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicants, it is within your power to consider whether the Defendant may be guilty of a lesser included offense.

If, from the evidence in this case, you are not satisfied that the state has proved to you beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant is guilty of any offense, you must return a verdict of not guilty. If, however, you are not satisfied that the state has proven the offense of Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicant beyond a reasonable doubt, you may consider whether or not the state has proven to you beyond a reasonable doubt that the Defendant is guilty of the lesser included offense of Attempted Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicants.

Oregon law provides: A person commits the crime of Attempted Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicants if the person intentionally engages in conduct which constitutes a substantial step toward the commission of the offense of Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicants.

In order to establish the crime of Attempted Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicants, it is necessary for the state to prove beyond a reasonable doubt each of the elements of the crime as follows:

First, that crime was committed in __________ County, Oregon.

Second, that the crime was committed on or about the day of ____ of ________, 20___, the date alleged in the complaint.

Third, that the person charged drove a vehicle on premises open to the public.

Fourth, that while driving the vehicle [select appropriate language from above].

Fifth, that the person intentionally engaged in conduct which constituted a substantial

step toward the commission of the offense of Driving Under the Influence of Intoxicants.

  1. Special Jury Instruction No. 3

Miles Counter Attack

If you find from the evidence that the Defendant was in such a physical condition that he/she was less susceptible to the influence of intoxicating liquor than he/she otherwise would have been, and because of his/her (physical condition) was not under the influence of intoxicants, regardless of the amount of intoxicating liquor he/she had consumed, he/she is nevertheless not guilty of driving under the influence.
