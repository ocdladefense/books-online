Discovery, Disclosure & Notice

By Rich Oberdorfer

Discovery rules are not “technicalities.” They are rules of fairness. “Society wins not only when the guilty are convicted but when criminal trials are fair; our system of the administration of justice suffers when any accused is treated unfairly.” Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83, 87 (1963).

“[T]he prosecutor’s role transcends that of an adversary: he is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty…whose interest…in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done.” United States v. Bagley, 473 US 667, 675 n 6 (1985) (internal quotations omitted). Always ask whether the prosecution is living up to its obligation “to assist the defense in making its case” – whether it has “transcended” its role as “adversary.”

Defense counsel is not asked to transcend her role as an adversary. She’s an adversary. Her obligations are a matter of statute and ethics.

I. The Two Main Bases for Pretrial Discovery

1. Oregon’s statutory discovery, ORS 135.805-135.873; and

2. Brady. 373 US at 87 (“the suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to an accused…violates due process where the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment, irrespective of the good faith or bad faith of the prosecution.”).

In the 2013 legislature, OCDLA was instrumental in amending Oregon’s discovery statute to include most Brady material. See SB 492 (2013) (“Brady Bill”). Before that time, Brady and Oregon statutory discovery were not synonymous. State v. Koennecke, 274 Or 169, 176, 545 P2d 127 (1976).

A. Ask for Discovery on Both Grounds

Because the discovery rules stem from different sources, demands for discovery should be made under both so that all possible methods of receiving discovery will be covered.

You may ask, “Why would I need to make a formal, written demand for discovery? Aren’t these rules self-effecting?” The answer is: sure. See Strickler v. Greene, 527 U.S. 263, 281-82 (“defense request” not an element of a Brady); Bagley, 473 U.S. at 668, supra (rejecting different standard of review based on whether defendant made specific Brady request).

But it’s wise to make a formal, written request for purposes of preservation; to demonstrate that defendant values particular information (e.g., in DUIIs, photo or video evidence); to make it more likely a judge will be offended by the state’s later discovery violation; and finally, because some judges erroneously believe a formal, written request is required. See, e.g., State v. Sheppard, 32 Or App 345, 347, 573 P2d 1276 (1978) (“While we do not intend to discourage informal communication between the district attorney’s office and the attorneys in pursuing discovery, more is required to make formal demand under the statute than is demonstrated here”), rev den 282 Or 537 (1978). And finally, a written demand underscores the non-delegable nature of the district attorney discovery obligation.

Under ORS 132.260 there likely is a recorded grand jury proceeding (unless the county is exempted due to population less than 150,000 or between 300,001 and 699,999. You need to request this as part of your discovery request to ensure you get a copy.

B. Prosecutors Cannot Condition Plea on Waiving Discovery

The “Brady Bill” also amended ORS 135.405 to bar prosecutors from conditioning a plea agreement on defendant waiving discovery. SB 492 (2013) amended subsection ORS 135.405(5) to read, “The district attorney may not condition a plea offer on a requirement that the defendant waive the disclosure obligation of ORS 135.815 (1)(g).” “Missing discovery” is common in DUII cases, primarily due to the slowness of the Oregon State Crime Lab and missing video evidence. A defendant should not be denied a favorable plea agreement, or entry into diversion, because they are waiting on discovery.

If a party can get desired evidence outside of the discovery process, it should. State v. Spada, 286 Or 305, 594 P2d 815 (1979), held that evidence should not be suppressed if available to the defendant through either a subpoena duces tecum or through Oregon’s Public Records Inspection Act. In short, you may want to seek the documents outside of regular discovery.

C. Document Production Outside Discovery

If your client was stopped for speeding based on radar or LIDAR evidence, you may seek pertinent training manuals either from the prosecutor in discovery, from the device manufacturer, or from the police agency. (Note: For the release of public records, visit the Oregon State Police site here: http://www.oregon.gov/OSP/PRR/pages/prr_policy.aspx

Also see Appendix B, Defendant’s Request for Discovery, Preservation & Inspection of Evidence.”) These documents may be used to impeach in the same way we use the NHTSA manual. See State v. Morgan, 251 Or App 99 (2012) (reversible error to forbid cross exam on NHTSA manual); James v. The State, 260 Ga App 536, 580 SE2d 334 (2003) (same); State v. Hubbard, 297 Or 789 (1984) (reversible error to forbid cross-exam on department manuals; relevant both to officer’s knowledge and bias). Successful impeachment on the radar or LIDAR evidence may lead to suppression of the stop. See State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995) (scientific evidence only admissible when strictly administered in accordance with standardized procedures).

Pursuing these documents through regular discovery channels in the prosecutor’s office is not always wise. Even when you receive the requested documents without a fight (but cf. Spada, supra), you may end up educating your opponent more than yourself.

II. The Oregon Pretrial Discovery Statute

ORS 135.815, as effective January 1, 2014, provides for discovery to a represented defendant as follows:

(1) Except as otherwise provided in ORS 135.855 and 135.873, the district attorney shall disclose to a represented defendant the following material and information within the possession or control of the district attorney:

(a) The names and addresses of persons whom the district attorney intends to call as witnesses at any stage of the trial, together with their relevant written or recorded statements or memoranda of any oral statements of such persons.

(b) Any written or recorded statements or memoranda of any oral statements made by the defendant, or made by a codefendant if the trial is to be a joint one.

(c) Any reports or statements of experts, made in connection with the particular case, including results of physical or mental examinations and of scientific tests, experiments or comparisons which the district attorney intends to offer in evidence at the trial.

(d) Any books, papers, documents, photographs or tangible objects:

(A) Which the district attorney intends to offer in evidence at the trial; or

(B) Which were obtained from or belong to the defendant.

(e) If actually known to the district attorney, any record of prior criminal convictions of persons whom the district attorney intends to call as witnesses at the trial; and the district attorney shall make a good faith effort to determine if such convictions have occurred.

(f) All prior convictions of the defendant known to the state that would affect the determination of the defendant’s criminal history for sentencing under rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.

(g) Any material or information that tends to:

(A) Exculpate the defendant;

(B) Negate or mitigate the defendant’s guilt or punishment; or

(C) Impeach a person the district attorney intends to call as a witness at the trial.

(2)(a) The disclosure required by subsection (1)(g) of this section shall occur without delay after arraignment and prior to the entry of any guilty plea pursuant to an agreement with the state. If the existence of the material or information is not known at that time, the disclosure shall be made upon discovery without regard to whether the represented defendant has entered or agreed to enter a guilty plea.

(b) Nothing in subsection (1)(g) of this section:

(A) Expands any obligation under a statutory provision or the Oregon or United States Constitution to disclose, or right to disclosure of, personnel or internal affairs files of law enforcement officers.

(B) Imposes any obligation on the district attorney to provide material or information beyond the obligation imposed by the Oregon and United States Constitutions.

(3) Except as otherwise provided in ORS 135.855 and 135.873, in prosecutions for violation of ORS 813.010 in which an instrument was used to test a person’s breath, blood or urine to determine the alcoholic content of the person’s blood the district attorney shall disclose to a represented defendant at least the following material and information within the possession or control of the district attorney:

(a) Any report prepared by a police officer relating to field tests, interviews, observations and other information relating to the charged offense;

(b) Any report relating to the test results;

(c) A copy of the form provided to the defendant under ORS 813.100 (3)(b); and

(d) Any checklist prepared by the operator of the instrument for the test.

A. What Must Be Disclosed

1. Generally

For purposes of the discovery statutes, the police are considered part of the prosecution. State v. Johnson, 26 Or App 651, 554 P2d 624 (1976); cf. Kyles v. Whitley, 514 US 419, 437 (1995) (for Brady purposes, “the individual prosecutor has a duty to learn of any favorable [i.e., exculpatory] evidence known to the others acting on the government’s behalf in the case, including the police”). In State ex rel Wilson v. Thomas, 74 Or App 137, 700 P2d 1045, rev den 300 Or 64, 707 P2d 582 (1985), a person arrested by an officer on a prostitution charge filed a complaint against the officer. The officer made a statement to an investigator for the internal affairs division about the facts of the case. This officer was a witness for the prosecution in the prostitution case. The Court of Appeals held that the statement by the officer to the investigator was discoverable under ORS 135.815(1) because it was within the prosecutor’s “control” since the internal affairs division is a division of the police and the police are an arm of the prosecution. The court held that it was not material that the division’s records are “civil” or that the officer was told that the statement was confidential.

Police officers with a history of dishonesty or “not credible” findings are sometimes referred to as “Brady cops.” Prosecutors should be providing defendants in any case involving such an officer with information related to those findings. Beware the prosecutor who tells you that he “doesn’t know” why an officer is unavailable for trial, or that he “doesn’t know” why the officer is on leave from the department.

Statements and witnesses’ memoranda in the possession of the police are subject to discovery under the statute, even though they are not given to the prosecutor. State v. Warren, 304 Or 428, 433, 746 P2d 711 (1987) (“In a prosecution, the prosecutor is responsible for evidence in the possession of the police.… And, of course, information that the prosecutor may obtain directly is within the prosecutor’s ‘control’ as well, even if it was not in the prosecutor’s physical possession”). See also State v. Divito, 152 Or App 672, 675 n. 1, 955 P2d 327 (1998) (Divito I) (“Statements and memoranda of witnesses in the possession of the police are subject to ORS 135.815, even though they are not given to the prosecutor”), aff’d on other grounds, 330 Or 319, 5 P3d 1103 (2000) (Divito II), appeal after remand, 180 Or App 156, 42 P3d 918 (Divito III), rev den 334 Or 288, 49 P3d 797 (2002). See Section II G for a more exhaustive look at the Divito trilogy.

If a judge orders a district attorney to produce a file for in camera inspection and the district attorney refuses to produce the file, the district attorney can use appeal or mandamus to challenge the judge’s order. State v. Crenshaw, 307 Or 160, 168, 764 P2d 1372 (1988). However, if the district attorney gives the file to the judge for an in camera inspection then refuses to comply with the judge’s order to produce information in the file, mandamus is not an appropriate method of challenging a contempt order for refusal to comply. State ex rel City of Portland v. Keys, 96 Or App 669, 773 P2d 1347 (1989); State v. Heisler, 106 Or App 7, 806 P2d 1154 (1991).

Department of Human Services caseworkers may be part of the “prosecution” in certain cases. State v. Johns, 44 Or App 421, 606 P2d 640, rev den 289 Or 1 (1980). If statements of witnesses for the state are contained in a DHS file, they are in the “possession or control” of the district attorney for purposes of ORS 135.815 and the defendant is entitled to disclosure of those statements. To preserve the confidentiality of materials in the file if disclosure is not warranted, the proper method of obtaining files is to go through an in camera review by the trial court of the file. See State v. Graville, 304 Or 424, 746 P2d 715 (1988), and State v. Warren, 304 Or 428, 746 P2d 711 (1988). The judge’s duty to examine the files cannot be delegated to the district attorney who is prosecuting the case. State ex rel Dugan v. Tiktin, 313 Or 607, 837 P2d 959 (1992). The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment requires the state to turn over exculpatory evidence in confidential government files to the defense after an in camera inspection. Id.; State v. Warren, supra; State v. Graville, supra; and Pennsylvania v. Ritchie, 480 US 39 (1987). However, the court cannot order DHS to make children in the custody of DHS available for pretrial interviews by the defendant where the prosecutor has done nothing to impede the defendant’s attempt to interview the children. State ex rel O’Leary v. Lowe, 307 Or 395, 769 P2d 188 (1989).

The rules regarding DHS should apply to material in the possession of other state agencies, in light of the prosecutor’s affirmative duty to seek out exculpatory information. See Kyles v. Whitley, 514 US 419, 437 (1995).

In DUII cases, ORS 813.324(1) imposes additional disclosure requirements on the parties relating to tapes and transcripts of DMV hearings. Specifically, counsel must “provide a copy of the tape or transcript to the attorney for the other party at least seven days prior to the first date set for trial,” or the testimony may be excluded absent a showing of good cause.

You are not required to produce the tape or transcript for free. ORS 813.324(2) specifically provides, “The cost of a copy of a tape or transcript furnished under subsection (1) of this section shall be borne by the party who receives the copy.”

Practice Tip

Arguably, defense counsel can satisfy his duty under ORS 813.324(1) via a Notice like the one in Appendix E which informs the prosecutor that a tape or transcript is available subject to payment of reasonable costs associated with production. Prosecutors commonly ignore these notices and fail to pony up the money. If you get to the day of trial and the prosecutor has not paid for the tape or transcript yet raises a stink on your use of the transcript (usually as you are embarrassing the officer with his prior inconsistent statement), you can argue that you have “made available” the tape and transcript since the date of your Notice. There is no discovery violation where one party makes documents or evidence available and his opponent does not bother to inspect or copy them. State v. Addicks, 34 Or App 557, 579 P2d 289 (1978). If the prosecutor argues that Addicks interpreted the general discovery statute and not ORS 813.324, the response would be that the prosecutor’s failure to respond to the Notice provided “good cause” as contemplated by the statute in subsection (1). In any event, most prosecutors’ offices certainly do not provide discovery absent payment for copying costs, and what’s good for the goose is good for the gander. Note: you are not guaranteed to win the argument, so you may want to play it safe—give the prosecutor the tape or transcript at least seven days before trial, and thereby avoid the issue altogether. Note addendum: since the author wrote the previous note a decade earlier, he has indeed had one judge (erroneously, but that’s cold comfort) bar cross-exam from a DMV transcript in exactly this situation. For that reason, the author now routinely emails to the DA a PDF of the transcript if he intends to cross-examine from it – that is, if the officer’s testimony is more favorable than surprisingly damaging. By “surprisingly damaging,” I mean “bad facts not mentioned in the police report.

2. Witness Names, Addresses and Testimony

The parties must supply the names and addresses of witnesses who will be testifying. If a party does not know information he is required to disclose, for instance a witness address, but knows how to obtain this information, the party must obtain it and provide it to the other side. State v. Clarke, 106 Or App 204, 206–207, 808 P2d 92 (1991).

Experts don’t have to talk to the adversary – but the lawyer calling them must disclose what the experts have said to them in broad strokes. ORS 135.815(1)(c) (requiring disclosure of “reports or statements of experts, made in connection with the particular case, including results of physical or mental examinations and of scientific tests, experiments or comparisons”). In DUII cases, this particularly applies if the state’s expert has engaged in “retrograde extrapolation” calculations. You need to know the results of the expert’s calculations – what is the range of BAC the expert will testify to – and the calculations themselves to check for error, and to tailor your defense.

In State v. Schmid, 80 Or App 545, 723 P2d 334 (1986), it was error to exclude the testimony of a defense psychologist simply because the psychologist would not tell the prosecutor what his testimony would be. Defense counsel had notified the prosecutor that the psychologist would testify, gave the prosecutor the psychologist’s notes, and made the psychologist available for an interview by the prosecutor, who never attempted to interview the psychologist until the day of trial, did not consult another psychologist on the issues the defense witness would discuss and did not ask for a continuance. The Court of Appeals seriously questioned whether any discovery violation had even occurred.

B. Defendant’s Duty to Disclose

ORS 135.835 provides that a defendant must disclose certain information to the district attorney. It is important that a defendant turn over the required materials. Failure to do so can have serious consequences, such as having the court preclude the testimony of a defense witness. See State v. Wolfe, 273 Or 518, 542 P2d 482 (1975). In State v. Young, 94 Or App 683, 767 P2d 90 (1989), the Court of Appeals upheld the suppression of a tape recording in a sodomy case where the defense attorney failed to disclose to the prosecutor the existence of the tape and a transcript of the tape, even though defense counsel intended to use the evidence only to impeach the victim. On the tape, the victim told the defendant’s son that the defendant had not committed the offense and that a CSD (now DHS) worker had promised the victim immunity from prosecution on juvenile charges if the victim would testify against the defendant. As an addendum to the previous “Practice Tip,” this is yet another admonition: if you intend to impeach a witness with a DMV or other transcript, it really is probably best to provide it to the DA ahead of time – ideally more than seven days before trial. ORS 813.324(1).

1. Sanctions

In State v. Wolfe, supra, Justice Dennecke, in a concurring opinion, suggested that a judge who keeps a defense witness from testifying may be violating Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution and Amendment VI of the United States Constitution, which provide that an accused has the right to have compulsory process for obtaining witnesses. That right includes the right to examine the witnesses obtained. Washington v. Texas, 388 US 14 (1967). In State v. Mai, 294 Or 269, 656 P2d 315 (1982), a defendant was charged and convicted of driving under the influence of intoxicants and evading a police officer. The defendant’s attorney refused to comply with the reciprocal discovery statutes, claiming that the defendant had an absolute right to call witnesses. He cited the sections of the United States and Oregon constitutions just referred to. The trial court imposed a sanction under ORS 135.865 and refused to let the defendant’s witnesses testify.

On appeal, the Oregon Supreme Court held that the right to compulsory process protects the right to subpoena witnesses and the right to have a witness testify. However, Oregon’s reciprocal discovery rules were passed to ensure that both sides have access to all the facts prior to trial so that a jury can best determine where the truth lies. The court held that preclusion is a legitimate sanction for the failure to make discovery, on the theory that the sanction does not deny the right to compulsory process but merely poses a reasonable condition to the exercise of that right. Moreover, the court also held that an intrusion on a defendant’s right to call witnesses should be no more than is necessary to achieve the purpose of the discovery statutes. The court further held that the preclusion sanction should be imposed only where no lesser sanction would accomplish the aim of the discovery statute and then only if the state would be prejudiced if the witness is permitted to testify, even though the statute has not been complied with.

In State v. Johanesen, 110 Or App 348, 822 P2d 154 (1991), the court held: “Before a court can exclude defense evidence because of a discovery violation, there are at least two prerequisites: First, it must find actual prejudice to the state; and, second, it must determine that no other sanction short of exclusion would remedy the prejudice to the state.” As noted above, no specific finding is required to support a sanction which is less than exclusion.

In the cases of State v. Girard (I), 106 Or App 463, 808 P2d 1017 (1991), and State v. Girard (II), 113 Or App 238, 832 P2d 463 (1992), the trial court excluded the testimony of Bart Reid because disclosure of Reid’s testimony was not made to the state until four days before trial. In the first appeal, the defendant’s case was remanded for a finding of whether a sanction short of exclusion was available. On remand, the trial court said exclusion was appropriate because “the judge felt to do otherwise would encourage poor practice by the defense bar and the court would lose control over its docket.” 113 Or App at 240. The conviction was again reversed because there was no evidence that the state would have been prejudiced by a continuance. 113 Or App at 241. See also State v. Lindquist, 141 Or App 84, 917 P2d 510 (1996); State v. Fain, 132 Or App 488, 888 P2d 1052 (1995); State v. Clarke, 106 Or App 204, 207, 808 P2d 92 (1991).

2. Timeliness of Disclosure

In State v. Ben, 310 Or 309, 798 P2d 650 (1990), defense counsel waited until the morning of trial to disclose the existence of two witnesses to the state. When the prosecutor tried to talk to the witnesses during a noon recess, they said that defense counsel has advised them not to speak with the prosecutor about the case unless defense counsel was present. After the state rested, the trial court granted a prosecution motion to exclude the testimony of the two witnesses. The defense suggested that a recess would be an appropriate sanction. The Oregon Supreme Court held that Oregon’s reciprocal criminal discovery statutes impose an equal burden on the state and the defendant to disclose to each other the names of witnesses, and failure to perform this duty “as soon as practicable” after the filing of an indictment exposes the offending party to the possibility that a sanction will be imposed. The court held that ORS 135.835 imposes a responsibility for disclosure on the defendant and his attorney, interpreting the term “defendant” as including a defendant and his attorney. A clear discovery violation occurred because the defendant knew about both witnesses prior to trial and an additional discovery violation occurred when the defense attorney instructed the witnesses not to speak to the prosecutor unless he was present. The court also found that the witnesses could have been properly precluded from testifying as a sanction if prejudice to the state had been found; however, prejudice could have been avoided by allowing the state time to talk to the witnesses by ordering a recess. The court rejected the argument that preclusion is not appropriate where its effect would be to punish a defendant for actions that are solely the responsibility of his or her attorney. The case was reversed because the effect of the preclusion was to deny the defendant a fair trial by keeping from the jury testimony of a witness who, if believed, would have exonerated the defendant on all charges.

Practice Tip

Don’t give witnesses advice, period. If you want a witness to know whether they have to respond to that mailed subpoena that directs them to the DA’s office 30 minutes before trial (ahem), ask a fellow lawyer not associated with your office to give them that advice pro bono.

3. Protective Order

ORS 135.873 permits a party to get an order from the court protecting from disclosure information normally discoverable. This statutory section is derived in part from Rule 16(e) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure and requires a demonstrated need, such as threats against the lives of family members, before a protective order can be issued. A simple conclusive statement of fear or apprehension in an affidavit, unsupported by a showing of specific circumstances or occurrences that support the conclusion, is insufficient to justify the issuance of a protective order. State v. Pettit, 66 Or App 575, 675 P2d 183, rev den 297 Or 227, 683 P2d 91 (1984).

C. Discovery Laws Must be Complied With as Soon as Practicable

The obligation to disclose materials and information in a party’s possession and control shall be performed as soon as practicable following the filing of an indictment or information in the circuit court or the filing of a complaint charging a misdemeanor or violation of a city ordinance, ORS 135.845(1), or the filing of a traffic citation for an infraction. In State v. Harshman, 61 Or App 711, 658 P2d 1173 (1983), the Oregon Court of Appeals held that if evidence is discovered after the filing of an accusatory instrument but before or during trial, there must be prompt notification to the other party. When on the morning of trial the state gave notice of its intent to offer evidence of “other bad acts” as evidence of motive and delivered to defense counsel written material relating to the prior act, the Court of Appeals held that the trial court had abused its discretion when it failed to impose some sanction against the state. The court held that prejudice need not be shown to require imposition of a sanction, such as exclusion or a continuance, because “the effective administration of justice requires that discoverable evidence be provided much sooner than ‘moments’ before trial.”

In State v. Burdge, 295 Or 1, 664 P2d 1076 (1983), the parties stipulated that witnesses would be excluded. Defendant’s wife watched the case in the courtroom. During trial, evidence developed by the state made it important to call the wife as an impeachment witness, but the trial court refused to let the wife testify because of the exclusion order. The Oregon Supreme Court held that where a defendant does not learn a particular person or evidence may be needed for impeachment until the trial is in progress, a failure to disclose the witness’s name or the evidence prior to trial will not bar the witness from testifying or the evidence from being admitted. However, a defendant must promptly notify the state as soon as she or he learns of the additional witness or evidence, and the court may allow reasonable time for the prosecution to interview the witness or review the evidence. The court also held that violation of an exclusion order is not, of itself, sufficient to disqualify a defense witness in a criminal case. Exclusion is not proper where the violation of the order was not intentional and not procured by connivance of counsel or some improper motive.

See also State v. Dillard, 100 Or App 645, 787 P2d 1307 (1990) (no discovery violation when defendant did not learn of a photo he wished to introduce until morning of trial), and State v. Gill, 96 Or App 358, 772 P2d 957 (1989) (trial court erred in refusing to permit a witness to testify who was not disclosed during discovery without first ascertaining whether the prejudice claimed by the state could be cured by an interview with the witness, a brief recess in trial or postponement because trial court was obligated to explore other alternatives to the remedy of exclusion before it could preclude a witness from testifying).

D. No Duty to Deliver Materials

The duty to disclose does not encompass a duty to deliver the materials and information in a party’s possession and control to an adversary. It merely means that an opportunity must be afforded the adverse party to inspect or copy the material. ORS 135.805(2). Simply writing an informal letter to the district attorney demanding discovery and doing nothing more will not support the allegation that the district attorney has violated the pretrial discovery statutes if the letter writer receives no discovery. See State v. Addicks, 34 Or App 557, 579 P2d 289 (1978) (no discovery violation where state makes documents available but defendant does not inspect or copy).

In State v. Andes, 104 Or App 719, 803 P2d 273 (1990), rev den 311 Or 482, 813 P2d 1064 (1991), a defendant moved for discovery of all instances relating to the Intoxilyzer Model 4011A when an individual took multiple tests and the results varied, when there was a discrepancy in results between blood and breath tests for a single individual, or when other circumstances led an officer to question the result of an Intoxilyzer test. The Court of Appeals held that a defendant’s right to discovery does not include unlimited requests for information about cases of other individuals or other Intoxilyzer machines, and that the state had no responsibility under the discovery statutes to compile results for a defendant.

When the parties do not agree on the method of discovery to be used, a trial judge should exercise supervisory authority to ensure that the discovery proceeds properly, that the parties’ rights are not adversely affected, and that the trial is not unreasonably delayed. When the state intends to offer a videotape in evidence at trial, the trial court may order that a usable copy of the videotape be provided to the defendant at the defendant’s expense, even though the state has previously allowed inspection of the videotape. If the state fails to comply with the order, the trial court may exclude admission of the videotape. The authority for the court’s power in this case derives from ORS 135.805(2), which defines “disclose” to mean giving the adverse party the right to inspect or copy the material. State v. Kull, 298 Or 38, 688 P2d 1327 (1984).

Practice Tip

Videos are often highly exculpatory in DUII cases. They are also often full of inadmissible prior bad acts evidence (“This is my third DUII!”), hearsay (“They told me not to drive!”), and vouching (police officers discussing amongst themselves – or to themselves – how drunk defendant is). Rather than object at trial, irritating the jury during the video presentation, redact the video to exclude the inadmissible parts. Make a detailed list (on a pleading wouldn’t hurt) of what you redacted and why. I’ve had judges agree with me in theory that the state’s video has inadmissible elements, but indicate it’s coming in because I didn’t make a copy with the inadmissible parts excised.

E. No Right to Compel a Witness to Submit to Interview; Prosecutorial Interference

Once defense counsel has the names and addresses of the state’s witnesses, counsel should try to interview them. However, a defendant has no absolute right to compel an adverse witness to submit to an interview. State v. Trow, 56 Or App 478, 484, 642 P2d 1178 (“[Witness]’s decision not to talk to the defense was his own. The prosecution did not advise or suggest that he should not talk to the defense. Defendant was afforded both discovery of [witness]’s statement to police and full cross-examination at trial. He had no absolute right to compel [witness] to speak with defense counsel”), rev den 293 Or 394, 650 P2d 927 (1982). On the other hand, although the prosecutor need not make the state’s witnesses available for a defense interview, need not encourage the witnesses to consent to being interviewed, and may advise them of their right to refuse the pretrial interview by the defense, it is improper for the state to interfere with defense efforts to interview prospective witnesses by instructing them not to talk to the defense attorney or by telling them that “It would be better if they didn’t say anything.” State v. York, 291 Or 535, 632 P2d 1261 (1981). A policy that is favorable to access to witnesses and evidence and hostile to improper adversarial interference with such access is implicit in the Oregon pretrial discovery statutes, as well as the disciplinary rules of the Oregon Code of Professional Responsibility. Id. See also State v. Ben, supra.

F. Effects of Victims’ Rights Legislation

ORS 135.970 provides guidelines for discovery involving “victims” as defined by ORS 131.007 (“the person or persons who have suffered financial, social, psychological or physical harm as a result of a crime”). When interviewing someone who may be classified as a “victim” (e.g., the car owner in a hit-and-run), questioning should be prefaced with the notices described in ORS 135.970(2). Experienced investigators understand this issue, but you may need to educate legal assistants or others who may end up speaking with victims (e.g., in discussing civil compromise).

G. No Duty to Reduce Statement to Writing

The Divito trilogy, mentioned in II A, answered many questions on the interplay between the prosecutor’s office and the police department for purposes of statutory discovery (but, importantly, did not address Brady issues). Divito II made clear that it is no statutory discovery violation for police to omit important, pertinent, inculpatory information in the reports they provide to the prosecutor’s office. Cf. Kyles v. Whitley, 514 US 419, 437 (1995) (for Brady purposes “the individual prosecutor has a duty to learn of any favorable [i.e., exculpatory] evidence known to the others acting on the government’s behalf in the case, including the police”).

Divito involved a prosecutor who, the night before trial, became curious regarding the lack of 911 information in the police reports. She called 911 and the supervisor gave her the name of a witness, Dean, with whom she then spoke, and discovered he was prepared to testify obliquely (via a third party’s excited utterance) to the defendant driving that evening. This did some damage to the defense of no driving. The prosecutor left a late-night voice mail at the defense attorney’s office. At a pretrial hearing the next day, Dean testified that the investigating officer had made notes of their conversation. The trial court judge found “a discovery violation, not the DA’s office, but certainly through the police, who neglected to write a pretty critical piece of information up” in his police report and excluded the excited utterance.

Divito I was the result of a fractured en banc Court of Appeals, essentially holding that Dean’s statements were not “relevant statements” under the statute until the prosecutor decided to call him as a witness. At that point, the prosecutor properly telephoned defense counsel. State v. Divito, 152 Or App 672, 955 P2d 327 (1998) (1998) (Divito I), aff’d on other grounds, 330 Or 319, 5 P3d 1103 (2000) (Divito II), appeal after remand, 180 Or App 156, 42 P3d 918 (2002) (Divito III), rev den 334 Or 288, 49 P3d 797 (2002).

In Divito II, the Oregon Supreme Court affirmed on other grounds. The court found no discovery violation because, while Warren, supra, required prosecutors to disclose reports which already exist, it does not require prosecutors to “expand the scope of existing documents, such as police reports, to incorporate additional information of which only the police are aware.” Divito II, 330 Or at 328. Justice Durham, concurring in the result, wrote separately that although ORS 135.815(1) did not require police to include certain information in reports, it did obligate “the state to disclose [the officer’s] notes that he wrote during or after his conversation with Dean.” 330 Or at 329. “Under ORS 135.815(1), the significant fact is not that the prosecutor had no personal knowledge of Dean until the eve of trial, but that in response to defendant’s request for discovery, the prosecutor failed to disclose a police officer’s notes that were under the prosecutor’s control and that pertained to defendant’s guilt or innocence.” Id. at 330.

In Divito III (worth reading just for an exposition of the state’s inability to make a proper offer of proof), the Court of Appeals cited Justice Durham’s concurrence for the proposition that the [OR] Supreme Court never reached the issue of whether the state committed a discovery violation by failing to disclose the officer’s notes of his conversation with Dean. Ignoring Justice Durham’s quite clear opinion on the matter, a three-judge panel of the Court of Appeals held, again, that the notes were not “relevant statements” until they became actually known to the prosecutor and she intended to call Dean as a witness (the night before trial). Thus, there was no discovery violation at all. 180 Or App at 167. The state Supreme Court denied review, 334 Or 288. The Court of Appeals has since held that police officer notes generally must be disclosed, State v. Johnson, 210 Or App 733 (2007), but as a practical matter notes are often destroyed once incorporated into a formal police report.

Practice Tip

At your DMV Hearing, notice if the officer flips open a notepad in response to questioning. If so, consider a formal discovery request to the prosecutor’s office noting exactly that fact.

Earlier cases hold that neither the defense nor the prosecution is required to reduce witnesses’ oral statements to writing. State v. McKendall, 36 Or App 187, 196, 584 P2d 316 (1978) (“The statute does not provide that the state, or the defendant for that matter, under ORS 135.835(1), reduce oral statements of witnesses to writing so that they may be discovered. We decline to read such a broad requirement into the statute”), overruled on other grounds, State v. Lopez, 147 Or App 314, 936 P2d 386 (1997); but cf. Kyles v. Whitley, supra. However, Oregon courts have not yet ruled on the question of whether a trial court has the power, under the discovery statutes, to order the reduction of oral statements to writing if it determines that the oral statement was not reduced to writing in order to avoid discovery. Id.; see also State v. Curtis, 20 Or App 35, 530 P2d 520 (1975), and People v. Manley, 19 Ill App 3d 365, 311 NE2d 593 (1973).

H. What Is a “Statement”?

Once a writing exists, ORS 135.815 and 135.835 require it be disclosed if it contains the statement of a witness whom the disclosing party intends to call. Young, supra, also encourages disclosure of statements you may use to impeach – even if you have no intent to call a particular witness. This is the situation where, for example, your investigator reduces an interview with a state’s witness to writing and you intend to use those statements for impeachment.

There’s also a danger that if the state opts not to call the witness and then you wish to do so, you have then arguably committed a discovery violation by not disclosing the notes “as soon as practicable.” One could argue that, under Divito I and III, the notes were not discoverable until you subjectively intended to call the witness as your own, but it’s not a judge-friendly argument.

A writing may take many forms—it may be a fragmentary note, a rough draft, stenographer’s notes, or a final report. In State v. Bray, 31 Or App 47, 569 P2d 688 (1977), the Oregon Court of Appeals stated: “[W]e construe the statute to require production of any ‘statement’ which is intended by its maker as an account of an event or declaration fact.” The court then held that the notes penciled by an officer on the back of his citation after a traffic arrest were not “statements” within the meaning of the Oregon pretrial discovery statutes. See also State v. Peters, 39 Or App 109, 591 P2d 761 (1979). However, in State v. Johns, 44 Or App 421, 606 P2d 640 (1980), the Oregon Court of Appeals held that the notes of a Services to Children and Families caseworker were discoverable. The Oregon Court of Appeals also held that stenographer’s notes and rough drafts that are the basis for a final police report, which is a verbatim account of the shorthand notes or rough draft, are not “statements” subject to discovery under the statutes. State v. Jackson, 31 Or App 645, 571 P2d 523 (1977).

But see State v. Fritz, 72 Or App 409, 695 P2d 972 (1985), in which the Oregon Court of Appeals held that notes which a Department of Fish and Wildlife officer took of defendant’s statements during an investigation were discoverable, even though a report that was given to the defense was prepared from notes for the district attorney, because the court felt that the notes were “memoranda of...oral statements made by the defendant,” to which ORS 135.815(2) is applicable. In State v. Morrison, 33 Or App 9, 575 P2d 988 (1978), a police agent made a writing which was a complete summary of a drug transaction. When asked to write a report of the drug transaction, the police agent prepared a letter using the original writing. The Court of Appeals held that the original writing which was destroyed was not a “statement” but was more akin to notes or rough drafts. The basis for this decision appears to be that the agent had not shown the original writing to anyone but instead redrafted it. See, however, State v. Johnson, 26 Or App 651, 554 P2d 724 (1976).

In State v. Taylor, 133 Or App 503, 892 P2d 697, adhered to as modified, 134 Or App 501 (1995), the court held that police officer’s notes of statements by witnesses were not discoverable, and noted that defendant made no offer of proof as to what the notes contained, did not ask to have the notes sealed and included in the record which would have permitted appellate review, and made no showing of prejudice by the state’s failure to disclose the notes.

Practice Tip

To the extent possible, the defendant should make offers of proof, showings of prejudice, and ask to have the disputed materials sealed and included in the record.

The file of an attorney is not “work product” that is exempt from discovery if the file contains notes from the attorney’s interview with a witness. State v. Gallup, 108 Or App 508, 816 P2d 669 (1991).

The Oregon Supreme Court has not yet ruled on the question of what constitutes a statement under the Oregon pretrial discovery statutes. The reader should note that the Oregon Court of Appeals cases which restrict the definition of “statement” base their decisions on an interpretation of the ABA Standards Relating to Discovery and Procedure Before Trial, Section 2.1 (approved draft 1970). A careful reading of the ABA Standards reveals that the Court of Appeals has misstated the position of the majority of the commentators who would require broad pretrial discovery, and which may have to include “interview notes...however fragmentary...even if they are merely jottings to aid the investigator in later compiling his formal report.” ABA Standards, pp. 61–64.

Witness statements not discoverable under the pretrial discovery statute may be discoverable under other sections of the Oregon Revised Statutes. See Section IV, “Other Discovery Tools.”

I. Sanctions

ORS 135.865 sets out the sanctions that are available if a party in a criminal case fails to comply with the pretrial discovery statutes.

Upon being apprised of any breach of the duty imposed by the provisions of ORS 135.805 to 135.873, the court may order the violating party to permit inspection of the material, or grant a continuance, or refuse to permit the witness to testify, or refuse to receive in evidence the material not disclosed, or enter such other order as it considers appropriate.

When a claim of violation of the criminal discovery statutes is made during a trial, the trial court must make a record, or permit the parties to make a record, of whether or not, in fact, the discovery statutes were complied with, the explanation of noncompliance, and the impact of noncompliance. State v. Addicks, 28 Or App 663, 560 P2d 1095 (1977). Failure to comply with the Addicks procedure will result in reversal unless the failure is harmless error, State v. Burns, 30 Or App 1157, 569 P2d 671 (1977), or one of the attorneys prevents the court from determining whether the violation is prejudicial, State v. Hand, 19 Or App 514, 528 P2d 126 (1975). Although prejudice is a factor to be considered by a court in deciding what sanction is to be imposed on a party who fails to comply with the discovery statutes, it is not necessary for a party to show that it was prejudiced before invoking the sanctions set out in ORS 135.865. State v. Dyson, 292 Or 26, 636 P2d 961 (1981). One exception to this rule arises when the state seeks to exclude defense witnesses or evidence. Prejudice to the state’s case must be shown before the sanction of exclusion can be used, particularly if exclusion would violate a defendant’s Article I, section 11, right to compulsory process. See discussion of State v. Mai, supra, in Section II B. If the state is ordered to provide discovery to the defense, it has no right to appeal from the court’s order. State v. Koennecke, 274 Or 169, 545 P2d 127 (1976); State v. Langlois, 287 Or 503, 600 P2d 872 (1979) (memorandum opinion); State v. Harshman, 61 Or App 711, 658 P2d 1173 (1983).

III. Constitutional Issues

The 14th Amendment to the U.S. Constitution is the basis for Brady v. Maryland, 373 US 83, 87 (1963):

[T]he suppression by the prosecutor of evidence favorable to an accused, upon request, violates due process where the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment irrespective of the good faith or bad faith of the prosecution.

This includes evidence which may be used by the defense for impeachment purposes. Giglio v. United States, 405 US 150 (1972). When a defendant is making a motion under Brady for disclosure, dismissal or other remedies, charging that the government has withheld from the defense evidence favorable on the issue of guilt or punishment, it does not matter whether the material sought was negligently, accidentally or maliciously withheld. Hanson v. Cupp, 5 Or App 312, 320, 484 P2d 846 (1971). It may or may not matter whether the evidence favorable to the defendant and in the hands of the state was or was not requested by the defendant. Id.

In United States v. Agurs, 427 US 97 (1976), the Supreme Court fashioned a three‑tiered standard under which the degree of materiality required to overturn a conviction for Brady violations varies with the specificity of the defendant’s request for disclosure. If the prosecutor knew, or should have known, that the government’s case contained perjured testimony, a conviction should be reversed for nondisclosure if there was “any reasonable likelihood that the false testimony could have affected the jury’s judgment.” Id. at 103–104. If the defendant makes a specific request for Brady material and the government fails to disclose it, reversal is required whenever there is reason to believe that the nondisclosure “might have affected the outcome of the trial.” Id. at 104. Finally, if a defendant has made only a general Brady request, or no discovery request at all, a court may reverse only if the nondisclosed evidence would create “a reasonable doubt that did not otherwise exist.” Id. at 112.

Subsequently, a plurality of the U.S. Supreme Court held that in situations where a Brady violation occurs whether there has been no request for discovery, a general request for discovery, or a specific request for the material, a reversal is warranted only if there is a reasonable probability that the result of the trial would have been different if the nondisclosed evidence had been disclosed to the defense prior to trial. A “reasonable probability” is a probability sufficient to undermine the confidence in the outcome. For example, if the government uses perjured testimony, the conviction must be set aside if there is any reasonable likelihood that the false testimony could have affected the judgment of the jury. United States v. Bagley, 473 US 687 (1985). “[T]he individual prosecutor has a duty to learn of any favorable [i.e., exculpatory] evidence known to the others acting on the government’s behalf in the case, including the police.” Kyles v. Whitley, 514 US 419, 437 (1995). This is true notwithstanding the reality that “no one doubts that police investigators sometimes fail to inform a prosecutor of all they know. But neither is there any serious doubt that ‘procedures and regulations can be established to carry [the prosecutor’s] burden and to insure communication of all relevant information on each case to every lawyer who deals with it.’ ” Kyles, 514 US at 438 (citing Giglio, supra). The Court concluded that “any argument for excusing a prosecutor from disclosing what he does not happen to know about boils down to a plea to substitute the police for the prosecutor, and even for the courts themselves, as the final arbiters of the government’s obligation to ensure fair trials.” Kyles, 514 US at 438.

A. The Duty to Preserve Evidence

The duty to disclose evidence in the hands of the government that is favorable to the defendant includes a duty to preserve evidence prior to trial. State v. Michener, 25 Or App 523, 532, 550 P2d 449 (1976); State v. Hockings, 23 Or App 274, 285, 542 P2d 133 (1976); State v. Lance, 48 Or App 141, 616 P2d 546 (1980); but see Arizona v. Youngblood, 488 US 51(1988), discussed post. In State v. Wright, 87 Wash 2nd 783, 557 P2d 1 (1976), and People v. Gomez, 596 P2d 1192 (CO Sct, 1979), the Washington and Colorado supreme courts recognized the necessity of setting guidelines for investigative agencies to follow when they have obtained evidence they wish to destroy because of a belief that it has no value or which may be destroyed during scientific testing. Failure to preserve evidence in Wright and Gomez resulted in defense victories in a murder case and a drug case. The Oregon courts, however, have not yet seen the need to impose similar guidelines. State v. Kersting, 50 Or App 461, 475 n. 4, 623 P2d 1095 (1980), aff’d 292 Or 350, 638 P2d 1145 (1982); State v. Hendershott, 131 Or App 531, 887 P2d 351 (1994), rev den 320 Or 587, 890 P2d 993 (1995).

B. How Does a Defendant Show Suppressed Evidence Is Favorable?

In order to show a violation of the constitutional rights set forth in Brady, a defendant must demonstrate, to the extent possible under the circumstances, that the evidence suppressed or destroyed is both favorable and material to an element of her or his defense or to punishment. State v. Zinsli, 156 Or App 245, 966 P2d 1200 (1998) (video of FSTs); State v. Koennecke, 274 Or 169, 545 P2d 127 (1976); State v. Mower, 50 Or App 63, 622 P2d 745 (1981). The scope of the required showing of favorableness is gauged against what would be possible to show under the circumstances. State v. Mower, id.; State v. Kersting, supra, at 474. Simply alleging that the state has withheld evidence favorable to a defendant is not enough to invoke sanctions for violations of the due process clause. A defendant must make some showing that the evidence sought will be favorable to the defense. State v. Hockings, supra.

Where evidence sought to be disclosed has been functionally destroyed but was subjected to scientific testing by the state prior to its destruction, a defendant must show that a retest of the material would have been possible and must challenge the state’s results, either by attacking the manner in which the test was conducted or by other evidence. On the other hand, if the state has not tested an item of evidence before its loss or destruction, and no other facts indicate the test result might have proved unfavorable to the defendant, little more may be required than a showing that the test could have been performed and results obtained which, in the context of the defendant’s version of the facts, would prove exculpatory. State v. Kersting, supra. However, the U.S. Supreme Court recently held in Arizona v. Youngblood, supra, that the failure to preserve evidence, absent a showing of bad faith on the part of the government where the exculpatory value of the evidence was highly speculative at best, did not constitute a due process violation.

C. Alcohol Breath Tests

Breath tests are used by the police to determine the alcohol content of a person’s blood. The primary breath testing device currently in use in Oregon is the Intoxilyzer 8000. Documents relating to the testing, certification, design, and accuracy of the machines are discoverable, although there is probably no requirement for the police to retain any breath sample which may have been obtained from a defendant, under the federal Due Process Clause. State v. Simpson, 40 Or App 83, 594 P2d 425 (1979); California v. Trombetta, 467 US 479 (1984). If a blood sample is taken, the sample is discoverable, under ORS 135.815(4)(b). The government may also be required to preserve the sample, and failure to do so may constitute a Brady violation, upon the appropriate evidentiary showing. In State v. Michener, supra, the court held that the chemical-filled ampules used to trap a subject’s breath for testing by a different type of breath testing device are discoverable under Brady and a duty to preserve them exists, if the defendant can make a showing that a retest of the ampule would produce evidence favorable to him or her, i.e., a reading that would show or suggest that the defendant was not under the influence. This showing can be made by producing witnesses who can testify that the defendant was sober, by showing a videotape made by the police which would indicate that the defendant was not under the influence, by a technical challenge to the accuracy of the machine, or by other evidence. But see Youngblood, supra. See also State v. Kersting, supra; State v. Mower, supra; State v. Ketchersid, 36 Or App 97, 586 P2d 119 (1978), affirmed on remand 52 Or App 931, 630 P2d 1312 (1981).

D. Audio and Videotapes

If the police make a video or audiotape of your client, it is discoverable under ORS 135.815(1). State v. Peters, 39 Or App 109, 591 P2d 761 (1979). If the tape is destroyed and a showing can be made that it would have been favorable to the defendant, there has been a Brady violation. State v. Zinsli, supra (videotape evidence [of FSTs] is unique because it would provide defendant with an objective video replay of the events from which a jury could draw its own conclusions); State v. Smith, 42 Or App 543, 600 P2d 949 (1979). Unfortunately, the Zinsli court held that dismissal was too extreme a sanction for destruction of the tape; rather, the state should simply be barred from introducing evidence of the FSTs. 156 Or App at 255; cf. Smith, 42 Or App at 546 (dismissing DUII prosecution as Brady sanction for destruction of similar tape).

IV. Other Discovery Tools

ORS 40.375 (Oregon Evidence Code, Rule 612) gives a party the right to see a writing used to refresh the memory of a witness. So notes, which might not be discoverable “statements” under the pretrial discovery statutes, will be discoverable if the witness refreshed his or her memory with them.

If a client claims that he or she was abused physically or verbally by the arresting police officer, it will be useful to find out if the officer has acted previously in a similar fashion. A defendant should seek discovery of the officer’s personnel file to see if complaints have been filed against the officer. State v. Fleischman, 10 Or App 22, 495 P2d 277 (1972). A defendant must make a showing that evidence in the file will assist him and cannot rely on pure conjecture. State v. Sagner, 18 Or App 464, 525 P2d 1073 (1974); see also State v. Christopher, 55 Or App 544, 555, 639 P2d 642 (1982). If a complaint was filed regarding police conduct arising out of your particular case, the police department internal investigation of the incident may be discoverable as well. ORS 135.815(1); State ex rel Wilson v. Thomas, supra; City of Portland v. Rice, 308 Or 118, 775 P2d 1371 (1989) (police internal affairs investigation file subject to public disclosure because investigation did not result in discipline).

Oregon’s public records law, ORS chapter 192, can be used to get accident reports, overtime pay records of officers, mug shots, Intoxilyzer maintenance and repair records, medical records at jails (if your client signs a release), and countless other items. Police dispatch tapes and 911 tapes are also discoverable. ORS 135.815(1). Use of the Public Records Inspection Act, ORS 192.410–192.500, as a discovery tool was specifically discussed in State v. Spada, 286 Or 305, 309–310, 594 P2d 815 (1979). The court in Spada held that evidence should not be suppressed if available to the defendant through either a subpoena duces tecum or through Oregon’s Public Records Inspection Act. In light of Spada, it is essential for the DUII practitioner to become familiar with procedures for acquiring documents and evidence outside of the normal discovery process. It is important to both understand Oregon’s Public Records Inspection Act and to develop relationships with law enforcement records personnel. For instance, in Multnomah County one can acquire booking photos and jail records with a letter like that in Appendix C; and booking videotapes are available with a letter like that in Appendix D.

The state cannot invoke a claim of privilege concerning evidence relevant to guilt or innocence or punishment when it chooses to prosecute a person for a crime, even when otherwise such evidence might be privileged against disclosure. See State v. Fleischman, supra, at 32, and cases cited therein; State v. Warren, supra [DHS files]; City of Portland v. Rice, supra. In State ex rel Kerns v. Read, 11 Or App 48, 53–54, 501 P2d 82 (1972), rev dismissed as improvidently granted, 266 Or 382, 513 P2d 1160 (1973), the Oregon Court of Appeals held that:

When, in a criminal trial, the state persists in a claim of privilege that has been overruled by the judge the state should be given an election: disclose the information or dismiss the indictment…

Contempt is ordinarily not the sanction to be imposed. But see State ex rel Beach v. Norblad, 308 Or 429, 781 P2d 349 (1989).

State v. Hartfield, 290 Or 583, 624 P2d 588 (1981), held that after a witness has testified on direct examination by the state, the defendant is entitled to hear an existing tape recording of that witness’s testimony given in the grand jury proceeding which led to the indictment upon which the trial is held. This does not, however, extend to clerk or grand juror notes. State v. Cox, 87 Or App 443, 742 P2d 694 (1987). This may be helpful in felony DUII prosecutions, or misdemeanor DUII prosecutions that were taken to the grand jury (e.g., manslaughter cases).

V. Additional Practice Tips

It is not necessary to file a discovery motion in order to obtain discovery. Brady and the Oregon statutes require the state to disclose all discoverable material, regardless of the demand for it. However, as discussed above it is advisable to file a written demand for discovery, as well as a written motion to compel discovery, if you do not receive everything you have requested. If a demand for discovery or a motion requesting specific material is on file, the district attorney cannot claim that the material was never requested or that he or she did not know defense counsel wanted the material.

Pursuant to ORS 136.580, the defendant may subpoena books, papers or documents relevant to his trial prior to the trial. Thus, the subpoena may require that the documents be returned to the court before the trial begins and allow for the inspection and copying of what is requested. In the DUII realm, a subpoena of the arresting officer’s police reports from the three prior and three subsequent DUII arrests may reveal helpful information—such as each defendant mysteriously misses the letter “H” in the alphabet or they each stumble on step nine on the walk-and-turn.

A practitioner should always consult local court rules regarding discovery because they are different in different counties. It is also a good idea to consult with other practitioners in the county to find out how those local rules are actually practiced. For example, in Multnomah County it is virtually impossible to obtain any sanction for a discovery violation unless a motion to compel has previously been filed. Do not rely on the assertions of the district attorney’s office that “we do not have it” or “it is not discoverable.” If there is any question in your mind, file your motion and ask for a hearing. If there is doubt about the existence or discoverability of information in various files, an in camera inspection of the material is the preferred method of resolving the question, the decision to be made by the trial court. The file can then be copied and sealed for appellate review of the trial court ruling. State ex rel Dooley v. Connall, 257 Or 94, 475 P2d 582 (1970); State v. Warren, supra; State ex rel Beach v. Norblad, 308 Or 429, 781 P2d 349 (1989).
