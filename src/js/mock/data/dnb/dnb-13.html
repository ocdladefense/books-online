MJOA / Mistrial and Objections

By Joshua Pond & Ben Eder

Motions for Judgment of Acquittal

I. Introduction

The Motion for Judgment Of Acquittal (MJOA) is the defense’s chance to ask the judge, “Please don’t let the jury decide guilt in this case when you know the state has not proven my client is guilty beyond a reasonable doubt.” In essence, it is your chance to keep an unreasonable, unjust result from happening. For this reason, the chance for you to do so occurs only after the state has put on their case-in-chief. This is not just the law, but makes logical sense. If what you are saying is “Look at what the state has presented, your honor; it’s simply not enough for a reasonable jury to base a conviction upon,” then it is only logical you would not be able to make such an assertion until the state has indeed presented their case. Consider it the mercy rule of litigation. For this reason, you can make this motion both just after the state has completed their case-in-chief and after you have presented your defense.

Since jeopardy has already attached by the time you can make such a motion, the state may not re-charge your client and is barred from future prosecution by double jeopardy. As such, judges decide this motion in a light most favorable to the state. This, mixed with the reverence judges give to juries and their verdicts, makes winning an MJOA a very tough task indeed. That said, anyone who thinks defending the accused is easy is probably sitting in law school orientation right now or has no real idea what criminal defense truly is. Though a difficult task, it is your job to be your client’s greatest advocate and, as such, you must be well aware of the bases for getting MJOA’s granted and the means through which you may do so. If this is a chance for you to save your client from demise and future prosecution for the alleged crime(s), it is your duty to know the ins and outs of MJOA’s like the back of your hand and to make a genuine, robust pitch for the judge to grant the motion. It is time for you to go to the river, pick up your stones and sling and face down that giant.

II. Statutory Bases

A. ORS 136.415

Oregon Revised Statute (ORS) 136.415 says we citizens are to be considered innocent of any alleged crime unless and until the state proves we are guilty of that alleged crime, and they must not only prove it, but prove it beyond a single reasonable doubt. The legislature used the wonderful word “entitled” to say that a citizen is entitled to an acquittal if the case was not proven beyond a reasonable doubt. What this means is that we do not simply have a right to such an acquittal, but we are full-on entitled to one if the state fails to prove the allegations beyond a reasonable doubt. To this end, ORS 10.095(6) says the court must, in every single jury trial, instruct the jury “That in criminal cases a person is innocent of a crime or wrong until the prosecution proves otherwise, and guilt shall be established beyond reasonable doubt.”

B. ORS. 136.445

So, what happens if you find yourself in a situation in which the state has presented their case-in-chief and you verily believe the evidence provided is insufficient to support a conviction? Do you just wait to allow a jury of six to twelve people potentially convict when your client is in a situation in which they are entitled to that acquittal? That is precisely what the MJOA is for, and the legislature provided a statutory basis for such a motion. ORS 136.445 provides that a defendant may make an MJOA both at the close of the state’s case and once “all the evidence” has been presented. If the evidence that was presented is insufficient to support a conviction, then the court shall (I love it when they use that word) grant this motion and, as aforementioned, jeopardy shall bar future prosecution for the crime. The statute says a court will consider all the evidence presented in making a decision as to whether or not the evidence presented meets the burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt. Think about that for a second—it must consider all the evidence presented preceding the motion. The logical conclusion, then, is that the court will consider any evidence you yourself put on if you make an MJOA when the defense rests. Now, we’ve all had things presented during our case-in-chief that we wish we could undo, but—since we still have yet to figure out how to unring a bell—think critically about whether you want to make a second MJOA following your case or whether you want to let that dog lie where you left it, following the state’s case-in-chief. State v. Nix, 7 Or App 383 (1971). That said, an MJOA will not be granted, nor will its denial be grounds for an appeal, if you do not ask for it, so as long as you or one of your witnesses did not do the state’s job for them, make that motion early and often!

III. Constitutional Bases

A. United States Constitution

The Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments to the U.S. Constitution, and the corresponding counterparts in Article I of the Oregon Constitution, require the state to prove each element beyond a reasonable doubt before a citizen accused of a crime may be convicted thereof.

B. Article I, Section 11, of the Oregon Constitution

The Oregon Constitution requires all material elements be proven beyond a reasonable doubt where life or liberty is at stake. The Oregon Supreme Court upheld this notion, that every material element must be proven beyond a reasonable doubt in criminal cases, in cases like State v. Quinn and progeny. State v. Quinn, 290 Or 383 (1981).

IV. Procedural Aspects of the Motion for Judgment of Acquittal

A. Timing

As mentioned before, an MJOA may be made either at the close of the state’s case-in-chief or at the end of the presentation of all the evidence. If such a motion can only be made during these two stages of a trial, it follows that this motion cannot be made after the jury or judge has reached a verdict. This logical conclusion was reached by the Oregon Supreme Court in State v. Metcalfe, in which they held an MJOA can only be made at the close of “the state’s evidence or of all the evidence.” State v. Metcalfe, 328 Or 309 (1999).

Outside of very rare occasions where one can point to a miscarriage of justice, one’s failure to raise an MJOA waives any error on the court’s part, so make those motions! State v Moore, 238 Or 117 (1964). Likewise, the court cannot wait until a verdict is rendered before ruling on the MJOA, it must make a ruling before the jury returns a verdict. State ex rel Haas v. Schwabe, 276 Or 853 (1976). This means the timing requirements are binding on both the parties and the court and, as such, you must object to their failure to make a ruling when the time is right to do so.

The court’s oral ruling on an MJOA is not binding on the court until reduced to writing and filed with the clerk. Therefore, if the court grants an MJOA, the attorney should get the court to immediately enter a written order and file it with the clerk to prevent the trial judge from revisiting that decision.

B. Standards Under Which the Court Will Consider the Motion

The standard a state court will apply to an MJOA is commonly known as “a light most favorable to the state.” To use the words of the Oregon Supreme Court, “In reviewing a trial court’s denial of a motion to acquit, the court must treat all conflicts in the evidence as having been resolved in the favor of the state and give the state the benefit of all inferences that can be reasonably drawn from the proof.” State v. Cervantes, 319 Or 121 (1994). In other words, where there is a question, they will answer that question in a light most favorable to the state. Now, the court makes it clear it is not deciding whether or not the state proved the defendant’s guilt beyond a reasonable doubt but, rather, “whether the evidence is sufficient for a jury to so find.” Id at 121.

As the court’s job, in the context of MJOA’s, appears to be resolving questions as to whether or not the state proved the material elements of the charge sufficiently, the court requires counsel to state specifically where, why and how the state failed to meet its obligations. This means, part of the standard is specificity. One cannot simply say, “The state did not do their job,” but, rather, must be specific in stating the grounds for granting the MJOA. Cf. State v. Martin, 15 Or App 498 (1973). Not only must one be specific in stating the grounds for an MJOA, but those grounds must be specifically substantive in nature. The court may not grant an MJOA on procedural grounds, including, but not limited to, deficiencies in the charging instrument. That is not to say you may not have motions relevant thereto, just that the weapon you may use to attack things on procedural grounds is not the MJOA. State v. Wolfs, 312 Or 646 (1992).

V. Substantive Grounds for the Motion for Judgment of Acquittal

A. Inferences

Not to be a Negative Nellie, but it is worth remembering here, before delving into the substantive grounds for winning an MJOA, that judges will view the facts that have been presented in a light most favorable to the state. This cannot be overstated and should not be minimized. To defeat your enemy, you must know your enemy. Not only may a judge interpret the facts that were presented in a way that is slanted toward the state, but this “light most favorable to the state” shines evenly upon facts that were not presented. You heard me right. Facts can even be inferred and need not actually have been presented during the state’s case-in-chief. Again, I do not say all this to discourage you from making these motions. Quite to the contrary; in fact, I am encouraging you to know that enemy well and set the standard for not just making these motions, but winning them.

Take this inference example: If you simply heard what I said and left it at that, your task of winning MJOA’s may well seem insurmountable. Instead, know that a judge may rely on inferred facts only if he or she knows that these facts meet the two-part test set forth in State v Rainey: “The jury may be left free to infer that fact only when two requirements are satisfied: 1) sufficient evidence has been offered of the existence of the fact(s) giving rise to the inference to allow a rational factfinder to find the underlying fact(s) beyond a reasonable doubt, and 2) a rational factfinder could find that the inferred fact follows more likely than not from the fact(s) giving rise to the inference.” State v. Rainey, 298 Or 459 (1985).

So now you know, but also know that judges are not given carte blanche to infer whatever they please and, while the standard may be to give the state a good deal of the benefit of the doubt, they still must meet that pesky burden of proof beyond a reasonable doubt and the inference must be one that a rational factfinder would make. Tough, yes, but no longer insurmountable.

This inference example is just one example of the hurdles you will have to clear at the substantive level, so let’s get into it, shall we?

B. Venue

Alas, the failure of the state to prove venue is no longer grounds for an MJOA, as the court held venue is no longer a material element of a criminal charge. State v. Mills, 354 Or 350 (2013).

C. Identity of Defendant

Here, the task can often be quite tough. One must not only show the state failed to directly prove identity, but even the inference that the defendant is the person in question was insufficient. Meeting this inference is not especially hard for the state, as the court has held that having the same name is enough to meet the burden. See OEC 311(1)(r). Now, that said, it’s worth noting that the courts have interpreted this latter example as requiring the names be identical and not just similar. In other words, if they are relying on the “same name” inference, it must literally be the exact same name. State v. Garrett, 281 Or 281 (1978). That’s not much but it is something, and when one does not have much ammunition in this area, something is certainly better than nothing.

D. Driving

Operating a motor vehicle is defined by ORS 801.370 and says, simply, that operation means “any operation, towing, pushing, movement or otherwise propelling.” While driving is not statutorily defined, Oregon law equates “operation” with “driving.” In other words, if you operate that car, you drove that car.

The general, established rule is that driving cannot be inferred but must be specifically proven. State v. Harris, 288 Or 703 (1980). That said, be aware of State v Bilsborrow, which seems to hold that one, in fact, may infer driving. State v. Bilsborrow, 230 Or App 413 (2009). In this case, an MJOA was not granted where the evidence showed the defendant was intoxicated, in the driver’s seat of the car which was at the curb, parked, with the engine running, in gear, no parking brake set and defendant foot on the brake. While Bilsborrow did not directly overturn Harris, their holding sure seems to indicate that a jury may infer that the defendant drove. So, argue Harris, but be ready for Bilsborrow. Although a close read of Bilsborrow shows that the Court of Appeals will review the entire record, not just the evidence presented in the state’s case-in-chief. The defendant in Bilsborrow testified and that evidence was also evaluated when ruling on the JOA issue.

E. Motor Vehicle

ORS 801.360 defines a motor vehicle as any vehicle self-propelled or even designed for self-propulsion. So, next time you go on that pub crawl, bring your skateboard, not your bike.

F. While

They must be intoxicated while they were driving. This is generally clearly shown when it is proven the defendant drove and was intoxicated, but keep this word while in mind when handling cases in which your client is found asleep at the wheel or was not found by the police until after he drove. For example, if your client is found by the police later at her home and she is intoxicated at that point, has the state proven she was intoxicated while she was driving?

G. Under the Influence

This may be inferred and often is, especially considering no chemical analysis need be done to convict someone of DUII. What is or is not sufficient to prove whether or not one is intoxicated is the topic of all but constant discussion and debate, and to answer that question here would take a tome of information. Suffice it to say that as a DUII practitioner, a major part of your job is coming to an intimate understanding of what it means for one to be “intoxicated.” So, get out there and learn it backwards and forward and challenge the state to an MJOA in nearly every case. Talk to veteran DUII practitioners, get to know scientific experts and DREs, partake in a “wet lab” at the Oregon State Police academy, attend seminars and CLEs, become a licensed NHTSA trainer, study the case law on point, do it all and know what “under the influence” means more than anyone else in the building before you step into that courthouse for trial.

H. Premises Open to the Public

Whether or not the area where the driving occurred was “open to the public” is very context specific. I once heard John Henry Hingson say he visits the scene of every DUII case he tries. You may want to do the same. Often, whether or not the particular premises were open to the public can be easily inferred from the setting itself. For example, is I-5 open to the public? Well, yes, there is not much question there. Let us say, however, that you went to the scene of your client’s DUII and saw that the entire area in which he or she was driving was within an area plastered with No Trespassing signs, what then? Well, you may have proven your client was guilty of Trespass, but he or she is not guilty of DUII. State v. Mulder, 290 Or 899 (1981). I am certain you can think of other such examples. Visit the scene, find out whether or not the area was, in fact, open to the public at the time of the alleged driving and make your pitch.

Motions for Mistrial

I. Introduction

A motion for a mistrial is essentially a tool of last resort, when no other remedy will undo the damage done. Due to double jeopardy and due process considerations, a motion for a mistrial is very rarely granted when the moving party is the prosecution. This section will focus on those instances when the defense is the moving party.

II. Procedure

Object promptly, move to strike, then move for a mistrial. Skip a step or take that step too late and you may be out of luck.

Failing to object fails to preserve error in denial of a motion for a mistrial. State v. Latta, 246 Or 218 (1967); State v. Cooney, 36 Or App 217 (1978); State v. Evans, 19 Or App 345 (1974). If you are denied an opportunity to present the objection and motion for a mistrial then your motion is preserved under State v. Veatch, 223 Or App 44 (2008).

Not moving to strike objectionable testimony fails to preserve error in denial of motion for mistrial. State v. Keith, 2 Or App 133 (1974).

The objection and motions must be made promptly, at the time in the case when the error occurs. State v. Thompson, 22 Or App 146 (1975); State v. Melton, 112 Or App 648 (1993); State v. Tetukzvich, 121 Or App 449 (1993).

If the motion fails, move for a curative instruction.

III. Grounds

A. Prosecutorial Misconduct Resulting in Jeopardy Barring Retrial

The key case to get jeopardy is a three-part test articulated in State v. Kennedy, 295 Or 260, 666 P2d 1316 (1983).

1. The official conduct is so prejudicial to the defendant that it cannot be cured by means short of a mistrial. The court so found and granted defendant’s motion for mistrial. Defendant would not be able to have a fair trial after the improper testimony. It would be a good idea to put on the record why the conduct is so prejudicial and why an instruction from the court cannot cure the harm.

2. The official knows that the conduct is improper and prejudicial. If there were stipulations or orders by the court or past mistrials on the same issue with the prosecutor, this is all information that can help solidify this point. Make sure you get evidence the prosecutor or police officer actually knew it was improper.

3. The prosecution is indifferent to the resulting mistrial or reversal. Build a record on the experience and knowledge of the prosecutor/police officer. The age of the case law. Police/DA training. Try and get the court to inquire into this on the day of the mistrial so there is a good record about what he or she knew.

B. Prosecutorial Misconduct That May or May Not Result in Jeopardy

It is the prosecutor’s duty to ensure an accused citizen receives a fair trial. Hold them to it! State v. Pointer, 106 Or 589 (1923). The following (non-exhaustive) list presents scenarios in which prosecutorial action should lead to a mistrial:

  • Where the prosecution argued a defendant’s request for a lesser-included offense instruction constituted an admission of guilt. State v. Johnson, 55 Or App 98 (1981).

  • Where prosecutor commented on the veracity and intent of the defendant by stating, during an objection to defense counsel’s request to reserve opening statement, that doing so would permit defendant to “make up a story while the case is going on.” State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336 (1971).

  • Where the prosecution elicited evidence of defendant invoking the right to silence after they were placed in custody. Douglas v. Cupp, 578 F2d 266 (1978).

  • Where the prosecution uses excessive emotional appeals to confuse the issues and incite what I like to think of as borderline bloodlust in the jurors. Commonwealth v. Watts, 412 A2d 474 (1980).

  • Where the prosecution, in his or her opening statements, comments on what the defendant will say later in the trial and the defendant never testifies. State v. Turner, 433 A2d 397 (1981).

  • Where the prosecution offers statements about the percentage of defendants entering a plea or being found guilty. State v. Flores, 31 Or App 187 (1977).

  • Where the prosecution improperly shifts the burden of proof and invites adverse inferences by commenting on the defense’s failure to call witnesses that the prosecution was capable of calling as well. State v. Valentine, 587 SW2d859 (1979).

  • Where the prosecutor fails to correct false testimony. State v. Towns, 432 A2d 688 (1981).

  • Where the prosecutor implies or states overtly the defense was trying to mislead jury is grounds for a mistrial where no curative instruction is provided. Carter v. State, 356 S02d 67 (1978).

  • Where the prosecution repeatedly attempts to offer inadmissible evidence for the purpose of making the defense look as though it is attempting to keep information from the jury. State v. Nichols, 236 Or 521 (1964).

  • Where the prosecution implants “within a question any statement concerning his personal knowledge of the truth of any disputed fact in issue where the burden of proving that fact rests upon the party he represents.” State v. Matson, 120 Or 666 (1927).

  • Where the prosecutor, in argument, implies or states overtly that he or she has evidence that would implicate the defense but which the court has ruled inadmissible. State v. Newburn, 178 Or 238 (1946).

  • Where the prosecutor makes reference to other bad acts of the defendant that were not admitted into evidence during argument. Note, however, that a prosecutor may speak generally to the illicit nature of the acts that were allowed into evidence. State v. Blodgett, 50 Or 329 (1907).

  • Where the prosecutor comments on the defendant’s credibility by noting prior convictions not admitted into evidence. State v. Treit, 29 Or App 461 (1977).

  • Where the prosecutor referred to the fact that a factfinder had previously ruled adversely to the defendant on the same matters to be determined by the jury. State v. Ray, 36 Or App 375 (1978).

  • Where the prosecution refers to conviction during cross-examination that it cannot prove. State v. Rollo, 221 Or 428 (1960).

  • Where the prosecutor asks questions designed to belittle and hold up for contempt the defendant or the defendant’s witnesses. State v. Motley, 127 Or 415 (1928).

  • Where the prosecutor elicits evidence that defendant, by words and conduct, refused to voluntarily submit to the officer’s inspection of a package. Powell v. State, 660 SW2d 842 (1983).

  • Where the prosecutor elicits evidence of the accused exercising his or her right to counsel. People v. Fabert, 127 Cal App 3d 604 (1982); Zemina v. Solem, 573 F2d 1027 (1978).

  • Where the prosecutor informs the jury that the defendant’s lawyer was present when search warrant was executed. US v. McDonald, 620 F2d 559 (1980).

  • Where the prosecution implies the accused is guilty because he hired a private attorney. Bruno v. Rushen, 721 F2d 1193 (1983).

  • Where the prosecutor rhetorically asked the jury to let its verdict answer the question of whether or not it would drive on the same road as the defendant on the evening in question. State v. Pineau, 463 A2d 779 (1983).

  • Where the prosecutor or the police officer draws a correlation between defendant’s actions and his or her blood alcohol content. State v. Ross, 147 Or App 634 (1997).

  • Where the prosecutor asks any witness whether he or she agrees or disagrees with any other witness’s testimony. State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621 (1988).

  • Where the prosecutor elicits testimony about defendant asserting a constitutional right and there is any likelihood an adverse inference can be drawn from his or her doing so. State v. McCartney, 65 Or App 766 (1983).

  • Where the prosecutor tells the jury the defendant requested a phone call during defendant’s interaction with the police. State v. Veatch, 223 Or App 444 (2008).

  • Where the prosecutor, in his or her case-in-chief, comments on defendant’s choice to remain silent during his or her interaction with the police, be that silence before or after defendant received Miranda warnings. State v. Marple, 98 Or App 662 (1989).

  • Where the prosecutor comments on defendant’s failure to explain unanswered questions, which impermissibly implies that it is defendant’s burden to prove any facts at issue. State v. Macomber, 18 Or App 163 (1974).

  • Where the prosecutor tells the jury the state’s evidence was never contradicted or impeached in a trial in which the defendant was the only possible witness who could have done so. State v. Buck, 239 Or 577 (1965).

C. Judicial Misconduct

  • Where the trial judge makes a disparaging remark about the ethnicity of the defendant. State v. Medina, 39 Or App 467 (1979).

  • Where the court, during a retrial following appeal, informed the jury the defendant had previously been convicted. State v. Meyer, 56 Or App 175 (1982).

  • Where a judge’s “personal and official influence is exerted to the unfair advantage of one of the parties, with a corresponding detriment to the other.” State v. Lucas, 24 Or 168 (1893).

  • Where the judge “not only received, but he acted upon the information secretly conveyed to him by the members of his staff.” Bestel v. Bestel, 153 Or 100 (1936).

  • Where the trial judge asked questions for the prosecutor, suggested questions for the prosecutor, and generally assisted the prosecutor in its efforts. US v. Singer, 710 F2d 431 (1983).

  • Where the judge allows testimony from the officer correlating HGN evidence with defendant’s BAC. State v O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995); State v. Fisken, 138 Or App 396 (1996).

  • Where the judge allows the officer to speculate as to BAC at the time of driving. State v. Johnson, 219 Or App 200 (2008).

  • Where the judge allows the DRE officer to testify as to the DRE protocol and make conclusions as to what controlled substances defendant may or may not have been intoxicated by and the DRE did not complete the mandatory 12-step protocol. This is the case, even if only one of the 12 steps was not completed. All 12 must be completed or a foundation cannot be laid for the DRE’s expert testimony. State v. Aman, 194 Or App 463 (2004).

  • Where the judge allows the police officer to testify as to what defendant’s BAC would have been had the defendant taken the breath test. State v. Jacobs, 109 Or App 444 (1991).

D. Jury Misconduct

  • Three jurors’ attendance at a DEA seminar in a marijuana trial required a mistrial. State v. Mapel, 54 Or App 795 (1981).

  • During its deliberations, one of the members of the jury stated that if she had had as much to drink as the defendant she would have been drunk and the other jurors agreed. McGary v. State, 658 SW2d 673 (1983).

Other Similar Motions

I. Motion for New Trial & Motion in Arrest of Judgment

Once a verdict is rendered, an MJOA is no longer an option, but your client will be entitled to a new trial if you can show the evidence “lay so heavily against a verdict” a reasonable jury simply could not have reached the result it did. US v. Martinez, 924 F Supp 1025 (1996).

The grounds for a motion for a new trial are found in ORS 19.430 and ORCP 64 A, B, and D through G and ORS 136.535(4). Straight to the point, ORCP 64 B(5) provides that “[i]nsufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict or other decision” is a ground for moving for a new trial.

Even if you think you are making an MJOA and phrase it as such, the court is permitted to treat your MJOA as a motion in arrest of judgment. There are two key differences between the two kinds of motions.

First, an MJOA is squarely focused on whether or not the state adequately proved each material element of the law in question. A motion in arrest of judgment says the matter should not go to the jury because of problems with the law itself, such as situations in which the statute is unconstitutionally vague or violates aspects of due process.

The second – and more important – difference is to the rights and consequences of your client. If the court grants an MJOA, the state cannot appeal and future prosecution for that offense is barred. This is not the case with a motion in arrest of judgment. State v. McKenzie, 307 Or 554 (1989). See also ORS 135.630. McKenzie allows the court to treat an MJOA as a motion in arrest of judgment, so just because you call a chicken a duck, does not make it a duck.

The grounds for a motion in arrest of judgment are found in ORS 135.630(1) and (4). A judge may only grant this motion if it finds the grand jury lacked authority to indict the crime charged or the facts as presented did not constitute an offense.

II. Motions to Dismiss with Prejudice

Just like with the MJOA, a dismissal, even with prejudice, lacks its full weight until it is reduced to writing.

In fact, motions for dismissal require a step further than an MJOA. Not only must a dismissal with prejudice be reduced to writing, but it also must be written as an acquittal before it bars future prosecution or reversal and retrial following appeal. The sole exception is dismissal with prejudice of a Class B or C misdemeanor. See ORS 136.130.

So, if you make that motion for dismissal on a Class A misdemeanor or any level of felony, try to get it dismissed with prejudice and when you win on that, make sure it is not just reduced to writing but written as an acquittal. Not only must you do this to make sure the boomerang never returns, but the courts have held your failure to do so actually preserves the state’s right to appeal for them. Not good. State v. Gunder, 154 Or App 332, 964 P2d 265 (1998).

Objections — Significant Case Law

By Ben Eder

One of the best things you can have in your trial notebook is a list of cases to cite when the prosecutor makes an improper argument or the police officer offers inadmissible evidence. After you object, it is important to be able to point the judge to the authority for your objection or motion for a mistrial. The objections need to come immediately when the inadmissible evidence is offered, and you need to ask for a mistrial at that time if that is the remedy you are seeking. Here are some of the issues that have arisen during my DUII trials. — Ben Eder

Evidence of a previous arrest with similar conduct is very prejudicial.

State v. Reyes, 143 Or App 594 (1996)

The fact that the defendant had previously failed HGN in an unrelated matter should not have come in as evidence that defendant was therefore intoxicated by failing HGN in the present case. Erroneous admission of the past arrest was prejudicial and not harmless.

A police officer can’t correlate actions with a blood alcohol level.

State v. Ross, 147 Or App 634 (1997)

The prosecutor can’t ask if defendant’s actions were consistent with BAC level. In Ross, the prosecutor asked the police officer if the defendant’s actions corroborated the .08 breath test result. The court reversed because a police officer is not qualified to give that opinion no matter how many DUII arrests he or she has made.

State v. Johnson, 219 Or App 200 (2008)

The police officer can’t speculate as to BAC at the time of driving. In Johnson, the police officer testified that it appeared Mr. Johnson was sobering up at the time of the breath test therefore the .07 breath test translated to .08 or .09 at the time of driving. The Court of Appeals relied on Ross and affirmed that blood alcohol levels must be proven by a chemical test.

State v. Jacobs, 109 Or App 444 (1991)

The police can’t testify as to what defendant’s BAC would have been had the defendant blown. Similar to Ross and Jacobs, the only way the state can prove a blood alcohol level over the legal limit is with a chemical test, not based on a police officer’s observation and experience.

State v. Fisken, 138 Or App 396 (1996)

A police officer is not allowed to testify that your client was over the legal limit or likely over the legal limit because there were four or more clues observed on the Horizontal Gaze Nystagmus test. This is reversible error, so ask for a mistrial if the state offers evidence of blood alcohol over the legal limit with HGN clues.

State v. Ohotto, 261 Or App 70, 323 P3d 306 (2014).

The police officer is not qualified to do retrograde extrapolation because she or he lacks a scientific foundation.

The prosecutor is not allowed to ask any witness whether he or she agrees or disagrees with another witness’s testimony. State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621 (1988)

This is not a DUII case, but this is an important case to know for all trials where the prosecutor may ask your witness if she agrees or disagrees with another witness. Commonly they ask if they think the arresting police officer is lying. Citing Middleton, the court in Milbradt stated that in Oregon a witness, expert or otherwise, may not give an opinion on whether he believes a witness is telling the truth. An opinion that a person is not deceptive, could not lie without being tripped up, and would not betray a friend (to wit: the defendant) is tantamount to the same thing.

The prosecutor can’t tell the jury that the verdict will/should be the same as if the jurors would want the defendant on the road that evening.

State v. Pineau, 463 A2d 779 (Me 1983)

The prosecutor can’t use the instinctive need to do what is necessary to survive danger as a means for convicting someone. In this case, the prosecutor rhetorically asked the jury to let its verdict answer the question whether it would drive on the same road as the defendant on the evening in question. The conviction was reversed. Although this was not an Oregon case, I think it has persuasive authority for arguing that the state is not using the legal standard set forth by the legislature.

The prosecutor can’t infer that the state has other evidence that is inadmissible.

State v. Newburn, 178 Or 238, 241 (1946)

Don’t let the prosecutor suggest that evidence is suppressed or forbidden by the evidence code; it is reversible error.

Where the prosecutor referred to the fact that a judge had previously ruled adversely to the defendant on the same matters to be determined by the jury.

State v. Ray, 36 Or App 375 (1978)

The prosecutor can’t suggest guilt because most of those arrested plead guilty.

State v. Flores, 31 Or App 187 (1977)

Stating that ninety percent of those indicted plead guilty requires at least curative instruction. I would ask for a mistrial. If the judge gives a curative instruction rather than a mistrial, I would ask the judge to state that the prosecutor’s comment was improper, disregards the presumption of innocence, and that innocent people have to plead not guilty to have their day in court, too.

A comment on any exercise of a constitutional right is usually reversible error.

State v. McCartney, 65 Or App (1983)

You can ask for a mistrial or curative instruction if the prosecution elicits testimony of defendant asserting a constitutional right if there is any likelihood an adverse inference can be drawn by the reference. The most common comments on constitutional rights are commenting on defendant’s silence or requesting an attorney.

State v. Veatch, 223 Or App 444 (2008)

The court found it was improper for the prosecutor to tell the jury that the defendant wanted to make a phone call because the inference is that he would be calling for legal advice.

State v. Marple, 98 Or App 662 (1989)

It was reversible error to comment on defendant’s silence in the state’s case in chief. In Marple, the defendant said, “I’d rather not say” when the police officer asked what a small bag of drugs was. The right to remain silent applies before Miranda so the state should not have been able to offer Mr. Marple’s refusal to say what was in the baggie.

State v. Mullenberg, 112 Or App 518 (1992)

Prosecutor commented about tape recording where officer spoke, but defendant would not speak; cannot be cured by an instruction.

State v. Macomber, 18 Or App 163 (1974)

Improper to state, “defendant never explained” or “how does the defendant explain.” This happens in closing argument when the prosecutor doesn’t understand that the defense has no burden to present evidence or testify.

Douglas v. Cupp, 578 F2d 266 (1978)

Where the prosecution elicited evidence of defendant invoking the right to silence after they were placed in custody.

[ State v. Buck, 239 Or 577 (1965)]

It is improper for the prosecutor to tell the jury that the state’s evidence is “uncontradicted” or “unimpeached” if the defendant himself is the only possible witness who could contradict the evidence.

The prosecutor shouldn’t infer that the defendant will make up a story while the case is going on.

State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336 (1971)

Where prosecutor’s statement to the jury that the defense counsel’s request to reserve opening statement would permit defendant to “make up a story while the case is going on.”

The prosecutor can’t disparage a defense attorney by calling him a pimp or unethical.

State v. Lundbom, 96 Or App 458 (1989)

If the prosecutor argues that defense lawyers try and hide the truth or that they are unethical because they distort the facts then you can object and ask for a mistrial. Similarly, the prosecutor should not refer to the defense attorney as a pimp using a “hired gun” for an expert.
