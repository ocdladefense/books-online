I. Introduction

If your client is arrested for DUII and provides a breath alcohol sample that is less than 0.08%, there is a good chance the police will not apologize and release them from custody but will instead bring in a “drug recognition expert” or DRE. A DRE is a police officer that has attended a specialized training program purported to enable them to tell whether a person is impaired by drugs and, if so, which ones. The police report will likely say something like: defendant’s blood alcohol level was not consistent with the level of impairment so I brought in a DRE. A second officer is typically brought in even if the arresting officer is a certified DRE to create the illusion of impartiality. An important point about the DRE process to be aware of is that the DRE most likely knows the answer before beginning the test. By this point the arresting officer will have grilled your client relentlessly about the last time they smoked cannabis or used other drugs and will have a pretty good idea of what they are looking for. Sometimes your client will be in possession of drugs which gives the DRE a strong indication of what substance will be in your client’s urine to confirm the DRE’s opinion.

Do not be intimidated by a DRE DUII. This is a practice area ripe with opportunities to mount a strong defense against voodoo science and provide alternative explanation for observations made by the DRE. The DRE DUII jury trial provides a wonderful opportunity to educate the jury about the DRE program and why it cannot prove beyond a reasonable doubt what, if any, drugs your client was on at the time of driving. Think of yourself as a professor educating the jury on what the DRE program is, what observations the DRE made, and why those observations don’t go very far in determining with certainty whether there was impairment at the time of driving. You may want to use expert witnesses to help educate the jury, or you may choose to rely on the state’s own witnesses to support your theory of defense.

Practice Tip

Whenever possible, use the state’s own witnesses to support your theory of defense. This will have a much greater impact on the jury since they may believe defense expert witnesses are hired guns who will say whatever we want them to. There can be little chance of bias if the state’s witnesses are providing testimony we can use in closing to support our theory of defense. A common theory of defense in a DRE DUII case is, “My client was not under the influence of a controlled substance at the time of driving.” Bring out every piece of evidence in the state’s case-in-chief that supports this theory, and every piece of evidence that contradicts the state’s theory.

We will briefly review polydrug use and how a determination is made when a defendant is accused of being on multiple drugs. We will then review the DRE 12-step protocol and case law that supports the DRE program as scientific evidence. We will then explore the DRE matrix, focusing on the four drug categories that are identified 98% of the time (cannabis, CNS depressants, CNS stimulants, and narcotics). This section includes a deep dive into the cannabis category, which made up 40% of the Oregon DRE calls in 2018. We will resurface with some general defense strategies and practice tips to help you prepare for your DRE DUII trial. At the end of the chapter, you will find a glossary of useful terms that will help you analyze your DRE case, a list of sample cross-examination questions to ask the arresting officer, DRE, and lab technician. Finally, we have provided an independent peer-reviewed study that calls into question the accuracy and validity of the DRE program. DRE DUII enforcement is an area of law that casts a particularly wide net. Innocent people are often captured who may have used controlled substances at some point in the past but were not impaired at the time of driving. We hope you find this chapter to be a valuable resource to defend those accused of this crime and being prosecuted by the use of this arbitrary, pseudo-scientific process.

II. History of the DRE Program

According to the Los Angeles Police Department (LAPD) website1, in the 1970s the following scenario was regularly played out on the streets of American communities. While on routine patrol, an officer makes a stop and completes a mental checklist: bloodshot eyes, check; slurred words, check; forgetting about the driver’s license, check; car smells like a brewery, check. “I think this guy might be deuce,” the officer thinks. “Deuce” is a slang term, primarily used in California, for a person who drives under the influence of alcohol. “Deuce” is derived from 502, a former California penal code for DUII. The LAPD article goes on to lament how the officer loses the deuce because the person blows 0% BAC and they do not have the evidence to convict for driving while under the influence of drugs. LAPD was frustrated at being unable to prosecute these cases and therefore developed the first DRE program. Mixing together a potent cocktail of law enforcement, medicine, psychiatry, physiology, and toxicology, they developed a drug classification and identification program to secure more DUII convictions.

According to the International Association of Chiefs of Police (IACP) website, two LAPD sergeants collaborated with various medical doctors, research psychologists, and other medical professionals to develop a simple, standardized procedure for recognizing drug influence and impairment. Their efforts culminated in the development of a multistep protocol and the first drug recognition expert (DRE) program. The LAPD formally recognized the Drug Recognition Expert program in 1979.2

The National Highway Transportation Safety Administration (NHTSA) took an interest in the LAPD program and collaborated with the LAPD to create the current Drug Evaluation and Classification Program (DECP) in 1984. In 1987, the Highway Safety Committee of the IACP partnered with NHTSA to roll out the DECP nationwide. In the 1980s, NHTSA sponsored three studies to validate the DECP and allow them to be used in court. These studies were reviewed by a neutral third party in 2013 and found to be flawed, biased, and unscientific. See Kane study in Appendix C.3 Oregon eventually adopted the DRE program in 1997, and as of 2018 Oregon had 212 certified DREs and 38 DRE instructors (IACP 2018 annual report)4.

An ordinary officer can become a DRE after about two weeks of classes and a few field practice sessions. This is not the most sophisticated expert in the courtroom. The certification process includes a 16-hour “Pre-School,” a 56-hour DRE school (using the 2018 DRE Course Participants’ Manual), and then the officer must conduct at least 12 drug influence evaluations while under the supervision of a DRE instructor. The DRE student must pass a final written exam and show an accuracy of at least 75% based on toxicological confirmation (Session 30, pg 10). The DRE must then be re-certified every two years by showing they have completed at least four acceptable drug evaluations, completed at least eight hours of approved recertification training, and presenting an updated CV and rolling log (Session 30, pg 17). To maintain certification the DRE must maintain a rolling log that demonstrates a minimum 80% accuracy rating when compared with the toxicology results.5 To be considered an accurate opinion, toxicological confirmation proceeds as follows: for one drug category, the category called must come back positive; for two drug categories, at least one category called must come back positive; for three categories called, at least two categories called must come back positive, and so on.6

You may have had some deuces involving drugs come in that you want to beat at trial and may be wondering, how can the state prove whether my client was impaired? Being under the influence of a drug is a subjective experience, a modification of the user’s ordinary experience of consciousness that is only experienced by the individual on drugs. The DRE program has developed “signs and symptoms” that are commonly observed when a person is high on a particular drug. However, since Oregon generally tests urine rather than blood, there will be little hard evidence of impairment at the time of driving. Urine is the waste bin of the body and does not tell the jury anything about impairment at the time of driving. In cases where there was a blood draw, was there a check for quantity of controlled substance? Were there other substances in the blood or urine that could also cause the police observations? More on this can be found in Chapter 11, Breath, Blood, & Urine.

When discrediting state witnesses with the profoundly unscientific nature of this program, be kind. A big part of winning DUII cases at trial is helping the jury like you and your client and helping them not like the police. So many California juries refused to convict possessors of cannabis since the 1960s that they effectively nullified the cannabis part of the controlled substances laws. As J. IV. Polysubstance Use

Most drug users mix different substances depending on what is available and to get the desired effect. Sometimes drugs can have a synergistic effect when used together. In the 1985 Los Angeles field study, 72% of the suspects had two or more drugs on board (Session 24, pg 6). Some of the most common combinations of drugs are alcohol and cannabis, heroin and cocaine, and coffee and cigarettes. The Participant Guide says that use of alcohol and cannabis together can strengthen the effect and lead to a condition called “greening out.” (Session 24, pg 5). I have not heard that term in the Pacific Northwest but have heard this commonly referred to as being cross-faded. Polysubstance use presents a problem for the DRE because the signs and symptoms they may expect from one drug may be offset or amplified by another drug. The DRE program has taken account of this problem and implemented a method for accounting for polysubstance use in their determination by asserting that drug combinations result in four possible effects: null effect, overlapping effect, additive effect, or antagonistic effect.

A. Null Effect

If neither drug affects a particular indicator of impairment, their combination will also not affect that indicator. For example, if the person is suspected to be using cocaine and heroin and we are interested in what effect that would have on HGN the answer would be: HGN not present for cocaine + HGN not present for heroin = HGN not present. The DRE would not expect to see HGN with this combination (Session 24, pg 11).

B. Overlapping Effect

If one drug affects an indicator and the other drug does not, the DRE considers this to be an overlapping effect. For example, if the person is suspected to be using alcohol and cannabis and we are interested in HGN, the answer would be: HGN present for alcohol + HGN not present for cannabis = HGN present (Session 24, pg 15).

C. Additive Effect

If two drugs independently affect some indicator in the same way, the DRE will expect to see a reinforced action. For example, if the person is suspected of using cocaine with LSD and we are interested in pupil dilation, the answer would be: pupil size dilated for cocaine + pupil size dilated for LSD = dilated pupils, potentially extremely dilated pupils (Session 24, pg 19).

D. Antagonistic Effect

When one drug would cause one action, and the other drug would cause an opposite action, the DRE manual states that the results would be unpredictable (Session 24, pg 23). The Participant’s Manual further states that whichever drug is more psychoactive at the time determines what will be observed. This is based on the potency of the drug, the quantity ingested, the time of ingestion and the duration of effects. The manual also purports that there is not an antagonistic effect for HGN, VGN, LOC, and Reaction to Light, most likely because these are either present or not, rather than up or down (Session 24, pg 24). For example, if the person is suspected of using MDMA and Xanax and we are interested in muscle tone, the answer would be: flaccid muscle tone due to Xanax + rigid muscle tone due to MDMA = rigid, normal or flaccid muscle tone. This is a situation where the DRE can basically state any answer is correct.

V. The 12-Step DRE Protocol

The DRE program requires a 12-step protocol that must be precisely followed for evaluation results to be used as scientific evidence at trial. If the 12-step protocol is not properly followed, the results cannot be testified to as scientific evidence but may come in as nonscientific expert opinion testimony. For example, if the police do not get a urine sample, which is part of the twelfth step, the officer’s observations may come in but the officer cannot make the claim that the opinion carries the weight of expert testimony.9 The entire evaluation process is standardized and must be administered the same way to all subjects and the same way by all officers (Session 3, pg 6). At trial, you may want to point out that during Step 2, the officer interviews the arresting officer and will know whether any drugs were found or admissions made regarding drug use. This is how the DRE finds out the answer they are looking for prior to starting the test.

The evaluation is standardized in that it is administered the same way every time (Session 4, pg 4). If standardized protocol is not followed, move to exclude the DRE evaluation. Reasons for standardization listed in the Participants Manual include ensuring that no mistakes are made, promoting professionalism among DREs, and securing acceptance in court. In cases where protocol was not met, you may want to have the officer recite to the jury why standardization is important (Session 4, pg 5).

If the DRE does not obtain a urinalysis (UA) or blood test to support the evaluation as the twelfth step of the process, the test lacks standardization and scientific credibility. The 2018 Participant Guide says that if the subject refuses to submit to a chemical test they are “denying us of scientific evidence of drug influence… In that case, conviction or acquittal may hinge on the officer’s observations and expertise as a DRE” (Session 3, pg 7). Use this comment to point out to the DRE that the only scientific evidence obtained in the DRE process is the chemical test of BAC.

A. DRE Case Law

When making challenges to the DRE protocol, it is good to be familiar with the following three cases: State v. Sampson, State v. Aman, and State v. Rambo.

State v. Sampson, 167 Or App 489 (2000), was the first case to rule that DRE evidence comes in as scientific evidence only when the DRE evaluation is completed to protocol. “The state must make a foundational showing ‘that the officer who administered the test was properly qualified, the test was administered properly, and the test results were recorded accurately.’” Id. at 322. If any step is left incomplete, the DRE evidence is not considered scientifically valid.

State v. Aman, 194 Or App 463 (2004), is authority for the proposition that an incomplete DRE evaluation does not come in as scientific evidence. In Aman, the DRE failed to obtain a urine sample in the twelfth step. “Without the corroborating evidence of the urinalysis called for in the twelfth step, ‘the DRE protocol cannot be considered complete.’” Id. at 510. Absent the complete protocol, the Court of Appeals found that DRE evidence failed the foundational test for scientific evidence approved in State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995), and State v. Brown, 297 Or 404 (1984).

In State v. Rambo, 250 Or App 186 (2012), the Oregon Court of Appeals further refined what evidence can be proffered by the state when there is an incomplete DRE evaluation. In Rambo, a certified DRE conducted an evaluation but did not obtain a valid urine sample as the twelfth step of the protocol. The defense moved to suppress the results of the evaluation based on the officer’s failure to follow the 12-step protocol. The court ruled that the officer’s testimony was allowed to come in as nonscientific expert opinion evidence. Id.

In distinguishing Aman from Rambo, the court stated that based on the officer’s “training and experience, police officers can—and frequently do—testify as to their opinions of whether an individual was under the influence of alcohol or a controlled substance.” Id. “The fact that they may rely in part on independently admissible scientific evidence, such as blood alcohol content and HGN test results, to reinforce their opinions, does not render those opinions inadmissible as unqualified scientific evidence. Although the line we draw may be fine, it is not artificial. Specialized expert opinion evidence based on a witness’s training and experience draws its force from that training and experience, but not necessarily from the mantle of science.” Id.

Practice Tip

When the state relies on Rambo to argue that the incomplete DRE evidence is admissible as nonscientific evidence of drug impairment, you may argue that a jury will view the evidence as scientific regardless of how it is admitted. Then, argue that it is inadmissible under OEC 403 because the jury will allot it greater weight as “science” when the state has failed to establish its scientific validity.

B. 12-Step DRE Protocol Detailed View

Become familiar with the 12-step protocol checklist used by DREs on the previous page. A summary of the 12 steps follows.

1. A blood alcohol content (BAC) analysis is done. If the subject’s BAC exceeds 0.08 percent, the DRE protocol ends.

2. The DRE officer interviews the arresting officer to elicit information about the subject’s behavioral and physical symptoms.

3. The DRE officer conducts a preliminary physical examination: he or she checks the subject’s eyes for synchronization and pupil size, checks the pulse for the first time, and asks general health questions. This step determines whether the subject is impaired by a medical condition.

4. The DRE officer conducts four standard eye examinations developed to detect intoxication: Horizontal Gaze Nystagmus (HGN), Vertical Gaze Nystagmus (VGN), and Lack of Convergence (LOC).

• HGN—present with CNS depressants, inhalants, and dissociative anesthetics.

• VGN—present with CNS depressants, inhalants, and dissociative anesthetics (indicates a high dose for that individual).

• LOC—present with CNS depressants, inhalants, dissociative anesthetics, and cannabis (20–30% of general public cannot converge).

5. The DRE officer conducts four field sobriety tests (FSTs): the Romberg-balance test, the walk-and-turn test, the one-leg-stand test, and the finger-to-nose test.

6. The DRE officer checks the subject’s pulse a second time, blood pressure, and body temperature.

• Pulse rate—normal range 60–90 BPM.

• Blood Pressure—normal range systolic 120–140 mmHg, diastolic 70–90 mmHg.

• Body temperature—normal range 98.6 +/- 1 degree.

7. The DRE officer measures the subject’s pupil size under three light conditions (near total darkness, room light, and direct light), and inspects the nose and mouth for signs of drug ingestion.

• Near total darkness—normal range 5.0–8.5 mm.

• Room light—normal range 2.5–5.0 mm.

• Direct light—normal range 2.0–4.5 mm.

8. The DRE officer checks the subject’s muscle tone for extreme flaccidity or rigidity.

9. The DRE officer inspects for injection sites and checks the pulse for a third time.

10. The DRE officer conducts a focused interrogation and observation of the subject’s behavior.

11. Considering the results of all the foregoing procedures, the DRE officer develops a formal opinion identifying the drug the subject took.

12. The DRE officer obtains a urine sample for toxicological testing. The test is used to corroborate the DRE officer’s opinion and to provide a learning tool for the officer.

C. DRE Opinion

During Step 11 of the protocol, the DRE is asked to form an opinion as to whether the subject is under the influence at the time of the evaluation and, if so, what categories of drugs are influencing the subject.

If the subject is not under the influence of any drugs at the time of the evaluation, the DRE is to enter an opinion indicating “not under the influence.” If the DRE believes a medical condition is present that mimic’s alcohol or drug influence, the DRE should indicate a medical rule-out in Step 11. Medical conditions that mimic alcohol or drug influence are: head trauma, stroke, diabetes, conjunctivitis, shock, and multiple sclerosis, among others.

VI. The Matrix

A copy of the May 2018 DECP DRE matrix is provided on the previous page. These are updated regularly so make sure you are using the current version. The state may have an enlarged version on markable foamboard, you may want to bring your own. “Major Indicators” of drug category impairment are listed along the top nine rows. Below the major indicators are the general indicators. General indicators are the officer’s off-hand observations, but not involuntary symptoms of intoxication, like the major indicators (from the 2018 Participant’s Manual). These general indicators are not “scientific” or clinical clues that police look for. Rather, they constitute a laundry list to substantiate probable cause.

There are seven rows—one for each of the seven drug categories. We will review the four drug categories that make up 98% of the DRE calls: cannabis, depressants, stimulants, and narcotics. The process at trial is to methodically go through each of the “major indicators” and each of the “general indicators” and show the jury how 1) many of the results of these indicators support your theory of defense, and 2) even if these indicators do support the state’s theory of defense, the whole process is an untrustworthy way to determine whether someone is under the influence of a controlled substance. Mention that the 2018 Participant Manual states that the only “scientific” evidence of impairment is chemical analysis of the blood or urine (Session 3, pg 7) and hammer on the idea that if we had a blood analysis we would have a much better idea of what substance was in the system at the time of driving. The OSP lab technician can educate the jury on what blood shows versus urine and that the state did not expend the resources to test the blood even though they have the ability. VII. Cannabis

Of all drug categories, cannabis is a great one for trial. Of the nine indicators, five of them are normal plus pupil size can be normal. It’s a game of gotcha that your client can not win, and your job is to make the jury see that. I like to talk about normal muscle tone being an indicator of cannabis impairment. You want to make the police lose credibility with the jury.

In jury selection be frank and open with questioning and do all you can to have a jury composed of experienced drug users. If a person has never used cannabis they may believe it when the police tell them it impairs a person for 24 hours.

Is it cannabis or marijuana? You may want to use the term “cannabis” rather than “marijuana” when referring to the drug. There is some interesting history around why the word marijuana has been used in popular culture, as described in the following excerpt from an April 13, 2016, column published in Seattle’s weekly newspaper, The Stranger:

In 1930, Harry Anslinger, the head of the brand-new Federal Bureau of Narcotics, was unsatisfied with regulating only cocaine and opium. When he went in front of a congressional panel in 1937 to push his pot prohibition bill, he said, “We seem to have adopted the Mexican terminology, and we call it marihuana.” While he sounds all innocent there, like he just picked up the word from who knows where, many surmise that he was actively using the term to focus the discussion on recreational use. His terminology distanced the plant as much as possible from its common medical and industrial uses, where it was more often referred to as cannabis or hemp. A. Cannabis Defined

Post-legalization, the Oregon legislature chose cannabis as the legal designation in DUIIs. ORS 813.010. However, the legislature did not define cannabis, they only defined cannabinoid as follows: “For the purposes of the Adult and Medical Use of cannabis Act, ‘Cannabinoid’ means any of the chemical compounds that are the active constituents of marijuana.” ORS 475B.015(1). The legislature further defined marijuana as “the plant cannabis family Cannabaceae, any part of the plant cannabis family Cannabaceae and marijuana seeds.” ORS 475B.015(17). When drafting rules for recreational cannabis, the Oregon Liquor Control Commission defined “Delta-9-Tetrahydrocannabinol” (Δ9-THC; ) as the principal psychoactive constituent (the principal cannabinoid) of cannabis. OAR 845-025-7000 (19). (Link to the full Division 25 Recreational Marijuana Rules.)

Cannabis was also specifically removed from the definition of “Controlled Substance” under Oregon law in 2015. ORS 475.005(6). The Oregon legislature vested the State Board of Pharmacy with the authority to make changes to or make additions to the definition of a controlled substance. ORS 475.035(1). When the Board decides, the Oregon definition of “controlled substance” will vary from the federal definitions by order of rule. ORS 475.035(2). The State Board of Pharmacy memorializes its rules under the Oregon Administrative Rules Chapter 855, Division 80. Cannabis was also removed from the Pharmacy Board’s list of controlled substances.

Practice Tip: Metabolites Are Not Controlled Substances

For the purposes of cross-examination on the toxicology/urinalysis report it is important to note that 9-Carboxy-THC and 11-Hydroxy-THC are not classified and do not appear in all of Division 80. OAR 855-80. These are metabolites that do not have the psychoactive character of Delta-9-THC.

B. The Cannabis DUII Stop

In this section we take a deeper dive into the possible challenges to the DRE DUII using cannabis as an example. Cannabis was chosen because it is the intoxicant most often called in DRE evaluations in Oregon. The principles applied in the context of a cannabis DUII carryover to other drug categories.

At the roadside stop, the arresting officer will often rely on odors, paraphernalia in plain sight, and client admissions of historic cannabis use to develop probable cause to request field sobriety tests.

Cannabis odors fall into two categories, unburned or “green” and burned cannabis. The case law surrounding probable cause based on both categories is currently fluctuating to adapt to post-legalization realities. For either category, it will be worth testing the limits of the Arreola-Botello ruling to question whether or not the odor of cannabis provides an “independent constitutional justification” to continue the stop. State v. Arreola-Botello, 365 Or 695 (2019).

1. Green or Unburned Cannabis

One should argue that the odor of “green” or unburned cannabis is insufficient to reach probable cause to request field sobriety tests (FSTs) because cannabis is no longer contraband under Oregon law. Smalley asserts that the federal prohibition upholds cannabis’s status as contraband to form the basis for probable cause. State v. Smalley, 233 Or App 263 (2010). However, in our post-legalization reality, this would be practically akin to requesting a driver to perform FSTs because they were seen placing a six-pack in their trunk at the grocery store.

The courts are recognizing that cannabis is not contraband and cannot be treated as such. The Oregon Court of Appeals has held that the general odor of marijuana in a vehicle does not give rise to objectively reasonable suspicion that a passenger possessed marijuana in State v. Martinez, 275 Or App 645, 650 (2015). In the Northern District of California, “a strong odor of marijuana emanating from the open car window” did not give rise to a “fair probability that contraband or evidence of a crime” would be found in the vehicle. United States v. Maffei (N.D. Cal. 2019).

Following passage of the 2019 Farm Bill legalizing industrial hemp, there has been a proliferation of hemp farms nationwide. In response, the Oregon State Police Crime Lab issued an advisory letter on July 19, 2019, that the Forensic Services Division would no longer examine plant material identified as “marijuana” because it is indistinguishable from industrial hemp. By the same letter, industrial hemp is defined as a cannabis plant with a delta-9-THC content of no more than 0.3% by weight.

2. Burned Cannabis

It is worth arguing that the residual odor of burned cannabis is insufficient to reach probable cause. In an older yet uncontroverted case, the Oregon Court of Appeals disagreed with the proposition that an officer witnessing the frequent lighting of a pipe gave rise to reasonable suspicion of smoking marijuana in a vehicle, even though the officer smelled burned marijuana when approaching it. State v. Chambers, 69 Or App 681 (1984).

Further, the same argument above for unburned cannabis should be made for burned cannabis in that if unburned industrial hemp is indistinguishable from cannabis containing psychoactive amounts of THC according to Oregon State Police forensic scientists, it stands to reason that field officers are not receiving any training to distinguish the burned odors of the two, which is likely impossible.

C. Cannabis FSTs

The field sobriety tests (FSTs) are validated only as an estimator of blood alcohol content (BAC), not impairment from cannabis or other controlled substances. Even so, the consumption of cannabis has been shown to lead to impaired driving. The National Highway Transportation Safety Administration, however, has not performed studies to specifically validate the Standardized Field Sobriety Tests (SFSTs) for cannabis impairment, only BAC level.12

Independent studies have shown that performance on the SFSTs provides only a moderate predictor of driving impairment following the consumption of THC, ranging from 65.8% and 76.3% detection rates.13 However, the SFSTs produce a false positive rate of up to 26%.14

The 2018 Field Sobriety Test Manual provides very little guidance for the patrol officer to identify cannabis impairment.

Practice Tip: Cannabis Use

Credibility of witness testimony is great to bring up in closing argument, as in: the testimony of this officer is not credible, he would have you believe that smoking weed causes your tongue to turn green and that the high lasts up to 24 hours. You may want to ask the officer if he has used cannabis. Now that it is legal I expect police will start admitting this, as easily as they will admit that they have drunk beer, or had some amount of beer and driven a car.

D. Cannabis DRE

The DRE is trained to look for expected results of the evaluation that are broken into two categories considered to be “Observable Evidence of Impairment” from cannabis. The “Major Indicators” in the DRE Matrix are a combination of the “Clinical Indicators” and “Symptoms” of cannabis use. The “General Indicators” are general observations of the subjects appearance and behavior.

1. Major Indicators

First attack the “Major Indicators” or “Clinical Indicators.” The first thing to notice about this category is that of the nine major indicators, five of them are normal. This is great to point out to the jury. The DRE manual includes a “Cannabis Symptomatology Chart” that summarizes this in the list of physical features regarded as indicating cannabis impairment.

Practice Tip: Involuntary Conditions

On cross and closing, emphasize the nature of the clinical indicators as involuntary physical conditions that the client could not control or fake.

The major or clinical indicators are grouped into three categories: the dexterity tests, eye activity, and vital signs.

Dexterity Tests are the familiar tests from the FSTs. Performance is expected to be impaired on the walk-and-turn test, modified-Romberg-balance test, one-leg-stand test, and finger-to-nose test.

The modified-Romberg test and the finger-to-nose test are not part of the “standard” battery of FSTs. In the modified Romberg, officers are trained that +/- 5 seconds in estimating the passage of 30 seconds is outside the range, and the officer will generally emphasize swaying by the subject. In the finger-to-nose test, it is helpful to refer to the DIE sheet to see where the officer is marking the missed touch. For example, if the officer testifies that the third touch was low and their mark on the DIE is close to the tip of the nose, that detail can be used to impugn credibility on cross-examination.

Eye Activity Tests are HGN/VGN, Lack of Convergence, and observations relating to pupil size. HGN and VGN will not detect cannabis impairment, though remember the VGN is generally not admissible outside the DRE. State v. Bevan, 235 Or App 533 (2010).

Lack of Convergence (LOC) is often considered the hallmark of cannabis consumption. LOC is simply checking for whether a person can cross their eyes. An inability to cross eyes occurs naturally for some people. If your client was unable to cross their eyes and the DRE is using that as a sign of impairment point out Session 5, pg 15, of the 2018 Participant Manual which says “this test may not be as reliable as the other eye tests because some people may have an inability to cross their eyes normally.” This presents yet another “gotcha” moment for the DRE where another “major indicator” of cannabis impairment is a naturally occurring condition. If this is the case with your client, consider bringing in a medical doctor to testify. When LOC is not present, emphasize its status as a “clinical indicator” in DRE training and compare to the involuntary movement of the eyes in HGN and VGN as a clue the client could not control or fake.

Pupil Size measurements are detailed in the 12-Step Protocol section above. In the cannabis DUII, Pupil reaction to light will be normal. Pupil activity is not a reliable clue for the DRE. The DRE matrix indicates that pupil size is “Dilated (6)” and when you refer to (6) it says “(6) pupil size possibly normal.” This is the quintessential example of the gotcha game. You can’t win. If your pupils are dilated, it is a “major indicator” of cannabis impairment. If your pupils are normal it is also a “major indicator” of cannabis impairment.

Rebound Dilation is related to pupil size. DREs report rebound dilation is present in subject impaired by cannabis. “Rebound dilation” is a period of apillary constriction followed by a period of apillary dilation where the pupil steadily increases in size and does not return to its original constricted size. However, see pupil size above, DREs also expect cannabis to not reliably affect pupils, which makes pupil activity a perfect example of the gotcha game of the cannabis DRE.

Vital Signs are medical measurements the DRE performs on the subject’s body. These are fairly straightforward, and one of the three are normal. Following cannabis consumption, pulse is generally elevated, blood pressure is generally elevated, and muscle tone is normal. Always consider other reasons that may elevate your client’s pulse rate or blood pressure when under arrest and being “examined” by a DRE.

Practice Tip: Using Medical Doctors

Bring in a medical doctor to testify. The client’s primary care physician may be most useful. The doctor may be able to testify to your client’s specific conditions that affect pulse and blood pressure, as well as general physiological reactions to stress. If your client is testifying, ask if they were nervous and how long they had been under arrest when these tests were performed. Then emphasize that when they were scared and in jail their numbers were up.

With no HGN present, no VGN present, reaction to light normal, body temperature normal, muscle tone normal and pupil size possibly normal, we are left with only three of the nine major indicators being utilized to determine cannabis impairment: heart rate, blood pressure, and lack of convergence. This is where the matrix really starts to break down.

2. General Indicators

After reviewing the major indicators, you will want to spend time on the general indicators. These indicators are a lengthy list of general observations. The DRE will likelly testify that, given the totality of the circumstances and in their training and experience, these observations lead them to believe your client was impaired by cannabis.

  • Odor of marijuana

  • Possible paranoia

  • Relaxed inhibitions

  • Lack of concentration

  • Impaired memory

  • Alterations in thought formation

  • Drowsiness

  • Sedation

  • Mood changes

  • Panic reactions

  • Paranoia

  • Dry mouth and throat

  • Fatigue

  • Paranoia

  • Acute anxiety

  • Possible psychosis

New to the 2018 DRE manual are the general indicators of acute anxiety attacks and possible psychosis. These additions recall the prohibitionist scare tactics used in the time of the 1936 propaganda film Reefer Madness15. The use of these over-the-top characterizations can be used to delegitimize the DRE in the eyes of the jury, when our current reality of legalization does not cast cannabis as a “dangerous” narcotic.

My favorite non-credible indicator of marijuana impairment is the green tongue. There is absolutely no science to prove this. See the referred article out of USA Today’s “York Daily Record” which provides some history on this concept.16 Apparently, the photo below was included in a handbook called “Identifying the Marihuana User,” published in 1986 for the California Highway Patrol. Even though there was no science to back this up, the IACP and DECP program ran with it and it is still included on the DRE matrix to this day.

Practice Tip: Using the State’s Witness

The key is using the state’s witnesses to prove your defense, if you can get this from their forensic scientist. But your own expert witnesses are also great. Doctors can talk about it, and if you can use the client’s primary care physician, they might have more credibility with the jury than a “hired gun” expert witness.

E. Addressing Roadside Admissions

If your client made an admission to use prior to driving, you will want to develop a timeline that establishes when the use occurred and when the driving occurred. Then, determine the onset and duration of effects to decide whether you will be able to show the jury that your client was no longer under the influence of the substance.

For example, if your client makes an admission that they “smoked a bowl this morning around 7 a.m.” and the stop occurred at 4 p.m., do this analysis: cannabis ingestion within a few minutes; peak effect 10–30 minutes after use; duration 2–3 hours user continues to feel and exhibit effects, 3–5 hours user feels “normal” (Session 21, pg 17). Then do the math: stop occurred at 4 p.m., which was nine hours after cannabis ingestion, which means the client was no longer impaired at the time of the stop. But be aware of the 1990 Stanford University airline pilot study that concluded that seven of nine pilots showed some degree of impairment in specific behaviors at 24 hours after smoking cannabis, while only one of those pilots reported any awareness of the effects.

This is why you want a jury of people that have used cannabis. If the officer testifies that the effects of cannabis last up to 24 hours, the jury can use their own experience and common sense to counter the credibility of the testimony. If there are jurors on the panel that have never used cannabis, they may believe noticeable effects last up to 24 hours. Also, be aware that the duration of edibles can be much longer than when it is smoked.

F. Analytical Report

As soon as an analytical report is received from the crime lab, send a discovery request directly to the Oregon State Police (OSP) Crime Lab by fax and to the district attorney by your usual methods. At a minimum request the following: case notes, conversation logs, and chain-of-custody report.

A police officer may obtain a chemical test of the breath or blood to determine the amount of alcohol in any person’s blood, or a test of the person’s blood or urine, or both, to determine the presence of cannabis. ORS 813.140 (Oregon Revised Statutes (2019 Edition)).

In blood or urine reports, you will see findings of 9-Carboxy-THC and 11-Hydroxy-THC, but these are not the principal psychoactive components of cannabis. 9-Carboxy-THC has not been found to have any psychoactive effect. 11-Hydroxy-THC has been found to associate with lingering euphoria and is eliminated from the blood within six hours. The presence of either metabolite in urine is not an indicator of present-sense impairment at the time the sample was collected. Make the OSP lab tech confirm this on cross-examination.

The Oregon Court of Appeals recognizes that the urine test performed during cannabis DREs has very little evidentiary value: “At most, if the ‘positive’ result is assumed to be correct despite the lack of a confirmatory quantification test, the evidence could demonstrate that defendant had used drugs some time before the accident.” State v. Jayne, 173 Or App 533 (2001). In Jayne, the Court of Appeals found a “lack of direct correlation between the urinalysis test results and impairment at the time of the accident.” Id. The court was consistent in holding that you “cannot judge or cannot tell impairment from a drug test[,] … [only] that the drug was used at some point in time.” State v. Moody, 201 Or App 58 (2005).

In light of these decisions, one should attack the Analytical Report at every stage of the case. Urine tests for cannabis impairment should not be admissible as scientific evidence.

The first line of attack is to move to suppress the Analytical Report under State v. O’Key. The Analytical Report fails the first prong requiring scientific evidence to be “pertinent to the issue to which it is directed.” State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285, 303 (1995). In a cannabis DUII case, it must be pertinent to whether or not a driver was “adversely affected … to a noticeable and perceptible degree” at the time of driving. Uniform Criminal Jury Instruction No. 2702.

The question for the court will be can the legislature dispense with applicable foundational requirements for scientific evidence in ORS 813.131? The Court of Appeals previously concluded that “the legislature, in enacting ORS 813.131, did not intend to dispense with the otherwise applicable foundational requirements for scientific evidence for a urinalysis performed pursuant to the implied consent law.” State v. Tripathi, 226 Or App 552, 562 (2009).

If the legislature did intend to dispense with the “otherwise applicable foundational requirements for scientific evidence,” then ORS 813.131 violates due process. The Oregon Supreme Court recognized that scientific evidence is linked to defendants’ rights to due process in O’Key. In O’Key, the court held that trial courts must exclude “‘bad science’ in order to control the flow of confusing, misleading, erroneous, prejudicial, or useless information to the trier of fact.” Id. at 306. The Analytical Report showing someone used cannabis at some time in the past is just the kind of useless information the O’Key court characterized as “bad science.”

By abrogating the burden on the state to prove that scientific evidence must be pertinent to the issue, ORS 813.131 violates due process. Rather, such legislative action “alters the rules of evidence in a one-sided way that makes conviction of the defendant more likely.” State v. Fugate, 332 Or 195, 213 (2001). Thus, creating a new rule that operates only in favor of the prosecution violates due process by removing the judicial tests for scientific evidence under O’Key without substituting nearly the same protection. Therefore, you should ask the trial court to find that ORS 813.131 violates due process because removing the protections provided by the O’Key tests will allow the confusing, misleading, erroneous, prejudicial, and useless Analytical Report to be admitted into evidence.

Practice Tip: Subpoena Lab Technicians

Do not let the state avoid the issue by relying on the statute. Subpoena lab technicians to suppression hearings to testify that 9-Carboxy-THC and 11-Hydroxy-THC are not indicators of present impairment.

In a 4-to-3 opinion, the Court of Appeals declined to address the issue of introducing 9-Carboxy-THC as evidence of intoxication because the issue was not properly presented. However, the lengthy dissenting opinion would have found that the evidence of 9-Carboxy-THC was “not pertinent to the issue to which it is directed – whether the measurement of the total cannabinoids in plaintiff’s urine indicated that he was impaired at the time of his injury.” Mitchell v. Mt. Hood Meadows Oregon, 195 Or App 431, 468 (2004) (Wollheim, J., dissenting).

It is useful to question the lab technician on the Selective Ion Modeling methodology used to test for cannabis metabolites; make it look as unreliable at indicating present intoxication as it really is.

XI. Trial Preparation and Typical Defense Arguments

A. Trial Preparation

Use expert witnesses for consult or testimony and familiarize yourself with their curriculum vitae (CV), the police report, the drug influence evaluation report and the DRE report.

Preparation is key. Thoroughly review the reports and any video from the scene or DRE evaluation. Review all of the laboratory documents that accompany the Analytical/Toxicology Report.

1. DRE Rolling Logs

This is a critical piece of discovery and a valuable way to impeach the DRE. Every DRE is required to maintain a document that records all of their DRE arrests, which drugs the DRE determined the defendant to be impaired by, and which drug (if any) showed up in the urine or blood. Be sure to make a specific discovery request and get it before trial so you have time to review it. Check that it has been updated with recent lab results and, if not, consider filing a motion to compel. Then look through it for instances when the DRE called a certain controlled substance that is not supported by the urinalysis, particularly when it is the controlled substance your case involves. You will want to highlight those specific cases on cross-examination to show the jury that the DRE is incorrect a certain amount of the time. You may want to redact defendant names and provide this to the jury as an exhibit.

2. Toxicology

Always request laboratory case notes, conversation logs, and chain-of-custody reports. With the help of an expert, these documents can help you spot mishandling of specimens, errors in test results, or multiple tests with contradictory results. See Chapter 11, “Breath, Blood and Urine.”

B. Typical Defense Arguments

The manual lists the defense arguments below to train the DRE how to respond to cross-examination (Session 28, pg 15). Use this list to develop your own arguments and be prepared to cross an officer that’s had this training.

1. Missing Symptoms & Normals

You will want to carefully review the DIE and evaluation and make note of all symptoms that deviate from what would be expected by the drug category called. Elicit statements on cross-examination that build your theory of defense by showing the contradiction. For example, if the muscle tone is normal in controlled substance depressant cases, ask the questions: “CNS depressants cause a flaccid muscle tone? Mr. Bigg’s muscle tone was normal?”

2. Alternative Explanations

a. Medical conditions may mimic drug impairment (Session 6, pg 47). Bring in a medical doctor to testify that, for example, your client’s high blood pressure at the time of driving may have been due to ongoing hypertension they are being treated for, not use of a controlled substance. Other medical conditions or historic injuries may also impact balance and dexterity in the evaluation.

b. Natural conditions may also affect vital signs: exercise, excitement, fear, anxiety, and depression (Session 6, pg 48).

c. Use of caffeine or nicotine may affect clinical indicators.

3. Defendant’s Normals

Normal is described on Session 6, pg 5 of the manual. The manual tells you that normal is a range, and what is normal for one person may not be normal for another. Clarify that to the jury.

4. Doctor Cop / Just a Cop

a. A DRE is not a doctor. You may want to call a doctor as an expert witness and really distinguish the level of expertise of a medical doctor versus the DRE (“doctor cop”). Carefully review the DRE’s CV before trial and compare and contrast it with your doctor’s CV. A DRE is an officer trained by other officers on medical issues used to determine impairment. As stated in the physiology chapter of the Participant’s Manual, a DRE does not diagnose anyone, they render an opinion that is a judgment based on special knowledge and experience (Session 6, pg 7). The manual distinguishes opinion from diagnosis, which is a legal and medical conclusion reached by someone with medical experience and expertise. A 56-hour police training that includes coverage of human physiology using the acronym MURDERS, INC, to aid memorization of body systems (muscular, urinary, respiratory, system, endocrine, reproductive, skeletal, integumentary, nervous, and circulatory) is a long way from medical training, especially considering this is being used with a veil of expertise to tell a jury why a defendant should be convicted. Some medical topics briefly covered in Session 6 include homeostasis, neurotransmitters, voluntary and autonomic nervous systems, metabolites, and use of sphygmomanometers. This is truly a case of giving someone just enough information to be dangerous.

b. Push back by bringing your own learned treatises that contradict the DRE’s testimony. There is a nice list at Session 13, pg 17. One of my favorites is A Primer of Drug Action by Robert M. Julien.

c. The officer is conducting a medical evaluation even though they have not had any medical training. Suggest that this type of medical evaluation would be best performed by a medical professional. The original LAPD DRE program was a collaboration with medical professionals; now, officers are being trained to do all the medical work on their own.

5. The Unknown

An officer can recite what they have memorized out of the Participant’s Manual but will most likely be unable to explain how or why the information they were taught is accurate. For example, you can ask: “You have no idea how a stimulant causes the pupils to dilate?” or “You cannot explain in medical terms the physiological process that causes a person’s blood pressure to decrease after using a depressant?”

6. Guessing Game

The DRE process is so subjective it defies our sense of moral honesty. A DRE makes a semi-educated guess about what substances a person has used and then conveys this to the jury with an air of certainty. Let the jury know this DRE actually has no idea what drugs your client consumed or when.

7. Tolerance Argument

In Session 6 on page 43, the 2018 Participant’s Manual states that a habitual user must externally supply some of the drug just to feel like a typical, non-drug-using person would. Argue that your client just took a maintenance dose to feel normal and that they were not “under the influence.” And in Session 17, page 17, the manual states that a narcotic user who has developed tolerance and is using his or her “normal” dose of the drug may exhibit little or no evidence of intellectual or physical impairment. If there is no evidence of impairment, is there impairment?

XII. Conclusion

We hope that the information contained in this chapter will help you assess and dismantle the state’s case in a DRE DUII. Don’t be intimidated by the scientific nature of the DRE and toxicology report. There are plenty of avenues to attack each piece of the state’s evidence. With persistence and careful attention to details, you can show the jury that a DRE’s opinion does not mean much.

Glossary of DRE Terminology

''' These are terms you will want to be familiar with and understand to effectively defend a DRE DUII case. These are the police definitions and all are taken from Session 1 of the 2018 DRE Participant’s Manual.

Addiction – Habitual, psychological, and physiological dependence on a substance beyond one’s voluntary control.

Additive Effect – One mechanism of polydrug interaction. For a particular indicator of impairment, two drugs produce an additive effect if they both affect the indicator in the same way. For example, cocaine elevates pulse rate and PCP also elevates pulse rate. The combination of Cocaine and PCP produces an additive effect on pulse rate.

Alkaloid – A chemical that is found in, and can be physically extracted from, some substance. For example, Morphine is a natural alkaloid of Opium. It does not require a chemical reaction to produce Morphine from Opium.

Analgesic – A drug that relieves or allays pain.

Analog (of a drug) – A chemical that is very similar to the drug, both in terms of molecular structure and in terms of psychoactive effects. For example, the drug Ketamine is an analog of PCP.

Antagonistic Effect – One mechanism of polydrug interaction. For a particular indicator of impairment, two drugs produce an antagonistic effect if they affect the indicator in opposite ways. For example, Heroin constricts pupils while Cocaine dilates pupils. The combination of Heroin and Cocaine produces an antagonistic effect on pupil size. Depending on how much of each drug was taken, and on when they were taken, the suspect’s pupils could be constricted, or dilated, or within the DRE Average range of pupil size.

Bad Trip – A hallucination where the user becomes panic‐stricken by what he/she is seeing or hearing, and may become uncontrollably excited, or even try to flee from the terror.

Blood Alcohol Concentration (BAC) – The percentage of alcohol in a person’s blood.

Breath Alcohol Concentration (BrAC) – The percentage of alcohol in a person’s blood as measured by a breath testing device.

Blood Pressure – The force exerted by blood on the walls of the arteries. Blood pressure changes continuously, as the heart cycles between contraction and expansion.

Bruxism – Grinding the teeth. This behavior is often seen in persons who are under the influence of Cocaine or other CNS Stimulants.

Cannabis – This is the drug category that includes Marijuana. Marijuana comes primarily from the leaves of certain species of cannabis plants that grow readily all over the temperate zones of the earth. Hashish is another drug in this category, and consists of the compressed leaves from female cannabis plants. The active ingredient in both Marijuana and Hashish is a chemical called delta‐9 tetrahydrocannabinol, usually abbreviated THC.

Carboxy-THC – A metabolite of THC (tetrahydrocannabinol).

Central Nervous System (CNS) – A system within the body consisting of the brain, the brain stem, and the
spinal cord.

CNS Depressants – One of the seven drug categories. CNS Depressants include alcohol, barbiturates, anti‐anxiety tranquilizers, and numerous other drugs.

CNS Stimulants – One of the seven drug categories. CNS Stimulants include Cocaine, the Amphetamines, Ritalin, Desoxyn, and numerous other drugs.

Conjunctivitis – An inflammation of the mucous membrane that lines the inner surface of the eyelids caused by infection, allergy, or outside factors. May be bacterial or viral. Persons suffering from conjunctivitis may show symptoms in one eye only. This condition is commonly referred to as “pink eye”, a condition that could be mistaken for the bloodshot eyes produced by alcohol or cannabis.

Curriculum Vitae (CV) – A written summary of a person’s education, training, experience, noteworthy achievements and other relevant information about a particular topic.

Dissociative Anesthetics – One of the seven drug categories. Includes drugs that inhibits pain by cutting off or disassociating the brain’s perception of pain. PCP and its analogs are considered Dissociative Anesthetics.

Downside Effect – An effect that may occur when the body reacts to the presence of a drug by producing hormones or neurotransmitters to counteract the effects of the drug consumed.

Dysphoria – A disorder of mood. Feelings of depression and anguish.

Expert Witness – A person skilled in some art, trade, science or profession, having knowledge of matters not within the knowledge of persons of average education, learning and experience, who may assist a jury in arriving at a verdict by expressing an opinion on a state of facts shown by the evidence and based upon his or her special knowledge. (NOTE: Only the court can determine whether a witness is qualified to testify as an expert.)

General Indicator – Behavior or observations of the subject that are observed and not specifically tested for. (Observational and Behavioral Indicators)

Hallucination – A sensory experience of something that does not exist outside the mind, e.g., seeing, hearing, smelling, or feeling something that isn’t really there. Also, having a distorted sensory perception, so that things appear differently than they are.

Hallucinogens – One of the seven drug categories. Hallucinogens include LSD, MDMA, Peyote, Psilocybin, and numerous other drugs.

Horizontal Gaze Nystagmus (HGN) – Involuntary jerking of the eyes occurring as the eyes gaze to the side.

Hydroxy-THC –  A metabolite of THC (tetrahydrocannabinol).

Hypertension – Abnormally high blood pressure. Do not confuse this with hypotension.

Impairment – One of the several items used to describe the degradation of mental and/or physical abilities necessary for safely operating a vehicle.

Inhalants – One of the seven drug categories. The inhalants include volatile solvents (such as glue and gasoline), aerosols (such as hair spray and insecticides) and anesthetic gases (such as nitrous oxide).

Insufflation – One method of ingesting certain drugs. Insufflation requires that the drug be in powdered form.
The user rapidly draws the drug up into the nostril, usually via a paper or glass tube. Insufflation is also known as snorting.

Lack Of Convergence (LOC) – The inability of a person’s eyes to converge, or “cross” as the person attempts to focus on a stimulus as it is pushed slowly toward the bridge of his or her nose.

Major Indicators – Physiological signs that are specifically assessed and are, for the most part, involuntary reflecting the status of the central nervous system (CNS) homeostasis (Physiological Indicators).

Marijuana – Common term for the cannabis Sativa plant. Usually refers to the dried leaves of the plant. This is the most common form of the cannabis category.

Marinol – A drug containing a synthetic form of THC (tetrahydrocannabinol). Marinol belongs to the cannabis category of drugs, but Marinol is not produced from any species of cannabis plant.

Medical Impairment – An opinion made by a DRE based on the evaluation that the state of a suspected impaired driver is more likely related to a medical impairment that has affected the subject’s ability to operate a vehicle safely.

Metabolite – A chemical product, formed by the reaction of a drug with oxygen and/or other substances in the body.

Narcotic Analgesics – One of the seven drug categories. Narcotic analgesics include opium, the natural alkaloids of opium (such as morphine, codeine and thebaine), the derivatives of opium (such as Heroin, Dilaudid, Oxycodone and Percodan), and the synthetic narcotics.

Neuron – A nerve cell. The basic functional unit of a nerve. It contains a nucleus within a cell body with one or more axons and dendrites.

Neurotransmitter – Chemicals that pass from the axon of one nerve cell to the dendrite of the next cell, and that carry messages across the gap between the two nerve cells.

Null Effect – One mechanism of polydrug interaction. For a particular indicator of impairment, two drugs produce a null effect if neither of them affects that indicator. For example, PCP does not affect pupil size, and alcohol does not affect pupil size. The combination of PCP and alcohol produces a null effect on pupil size.

Nystagmus – An involuntary jerking of the eyes.

Overlapping Effect – One mechanism of polydrug interaction. For a particular indicator of impairment, two drugs produce an overlapping effect if one of them affects the indicator but the other doesn’t. For example, cocaine dilates pupils while alcohol doesn’t affect pupil size. The combination of cocaine and alcohol produces an overlapping effect on pupil size: the combination will cause the pupils to dilate.

Parasympathetic Nerve – An autonomic nerve that commands the body to relax and to carry out tranquil activities. The brain uses parasympathetic nerves to send “at ease” commands to the muscles, tissues, and organs.

Physician’s Desk Reference (PDR) – A basic reference source for drug recognition experts. The PDR provides detailed information on the physical appearance and psychoactive effects of licitly‐manufactured drugs.

Piloerection – Literally, “hair standing up”, or goose bumps. This condition of the skin is often observed in persons who are under the influence of LSD.

Polydrug – Use Ingesting two or more different drugs.

Psychedelic – A mental state characterized by a profound sense of intensified or altered sensory perception sometimes accompanied by hallucinations.

Psychophysical Tests – Methods of investigating the mental (psycho‐) and physical characteristics of a person suspected of alcohol or drug impairment. Most psychophysical tests employ the concept of divided attention to assess a suspect’s impairment.

Rebound Dilation – A period of pupillary constriction followed by a period of pupillary dilation where the pupil steadily increases in size and does not return to its original constricted size.

Resting Nystagmus – Jerking of the eyes as they look straight ahead.

Sinsemilla – The unpollinated female cannabis plant, with a relatively high concentration of THC.

Standardized – Conforming to a model in comparative applications.

Standardized Field Sobriety Testing (SFST) – There are three SFSTs, namely Horizontal Gaze Nystagmus (HGN), Walk and Turn (WAT), and One Leg Stand (OLS). Based on a series of controlled laboratory studies, scientifically validated clues of impairment have been identified for each of these three tests. They are the only Standardized Field Sobriety Tests for which validated clues have been identified.

Synapse (or Synaptic Gap) – The gap or space between two neurons (nerve cells).

Synesthesia – A sensory perception disorder, in which an input via one sense is perceived by the brain as an input via another sense. An example of this would be a person “hearing” a phone ring and “seeing” the sound as a flash of light. Synesthesia sometimes occurs with persons under the influence of hallucinogens.

Tachycardia – Abnormally rapid heart rate.

Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) –The principal psychoactive ingredient in drugs belonging to the cannabis category.

Tolerance – An adjustment of the drug user’s body and brain to the repeated presence of a drug. As tolerance develops, the user will experience diminishing psychoactive effects from the same dose of the drug. As a result, the user typically will steadily increase the dose he or she takes, in an effort to achieve the same psychoactive effect.

Vertical Gaze Nystagmus (VGN) – An involuntary jerking of the eyes (up‐and‐down) which occurs as the eyes are held at maximum elevation. The jerking should be distinct and sustained.
