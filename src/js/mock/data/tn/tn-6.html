I. Definition and Timing Requirements of Demurrers and Similar Motions

A. Motion to Dismiss on Grounds of Former Jeopardy

This motion applies when a former prosecution bars the current prosecution. ORS 135.470.

Must be made at arraignment, within 10 days of arraignment, or later, upon a finding of good cause. ORS 135.520. Implicit in the language of the statute, a judge who did not find good cause for a filing more than 10 days after arraignment could refuse to consider the motion on its merits.

B. Motion to Set Aside the Indictment

This motion applies when the charging instrument fails to comply with ORS 132.360, 132.400 to 132.430 and 132.580, or when there is a failure to include the names of the grand jury witnesses at the foot of the indictment. ORS 135.510.

The timing issues are the same as with a motion to dismiss on grounds of former jeopardy. ORS 135.520.

A successful motion to set aside is not an automatic bar to refiling the indictment, unless the basis for the motion was former jeopardy. ORS 135.560.

C. Demurrer

A demurrer is a challenge to either the entire complaint, or an individual charge within the complaint. It is applicable only when the charging instrument is flawed on its face. ORS 135.630. ''See also State v. Pinnell, 319 Or 438 (1994).

Practice Tip

If you need extraneous evidence to show why the indictment is flawed, such as a police report or witness testimony, it is almost a certainty that a demurrer is not a proper vehicle for your challenge to the indictment. The one possible exception is a demurrer based on a complaint that is indefinite and uncertain. The state may use the discovery to show that it cures any ambiguity in the complaint, or the defendant may use the discovery to show that it does not. At present, no case has explicitly held whose burden it is to show that discovery does or does not cure the problem. Defense counsel should assume the burden is on them, and if not actually offer the discovery into evidence, at least make an offer of proof (e.g., an affidavit regarding what the discovery does and does not show).

A demurrer “shall be entered either at the time of the arraignment or at such other time as may be allowed to the defendant for that purpose.” ORS 135.610. “The words ‘such other time’ in [../../../../Publications/Trial%20Notebook/2013%20edition%20-%20TNB/'13%20revised%20chapters%20/chapters%20ready%20for%202013/ch%2006%20demurrers/htt%0D%0Ap://www.lexis.com/research/buttonTFLink%3F_m=0344f5c9403e8995f51319dad0a189ab&_xfercite=%3Ccite%20cc%3D%22USA%22%3E%3C%21%5BCDATA%5B315%20Ore.%20103%5D%5D%3E%3C%2Fcite%3E&_butType=4&_butStat=0&_butNum=69&_butInline=1&_butinfo=ORCODE%20135.610&_fmtstr=FULL& ORS 135.610] establish a discretion in the trial court to permit the filing of a demurrer at times other than arraignment. This court will not disturb the trial court’s exercise of that discretion except in a case of clear abuse.” State v. Tucker, 252 Or 597, 451 P2d 471 (1969).

The Oregon Supreme Court stated in State v. Wimber, 315 Or 103 (1992), that “[we] do not wish to be understood as approving of the practice of raising demurrers at trial. As this court stated in State v. Montez, 309 Or 564, 597, 789 P2d 1352 (1990):’ The proper time for defendant’s objection to the indictment was before trial. A pretrial demurrer would have given both the court and the state a timely opportunity to review the indictment and to make any necessary corrections before trial.’” Wimber at 112. The Wimber court also noted that the defendant – at arraignment – had “reserved” his right to move against the charging instrument at any time, and that no objection was made by the state or the arraignment judge. This would seem to be good practice, although it did not have a bearing on the Wimber holding.

That said, two bases for demurring – when the facts stated do not constitute an offense or when the court lacks jurisdiction – may be taken as a motion in arrest of judgment. ORS 135.640.

Practice Tip

When determining when to file a demurrer (or a motion in arrest of judgment), it helps to know the local rules (written and unwritten) of the county in which it would be filed. Some counties – and some judges – will not consider a demurrer filed after jeopardy has attached if the demurrer is based strictly on statutory grounds. Unless it’s a demurrer that can be refiled as a motion in arrest of judgment, a judge’s refusal to consider a demurrer that was filed more than 10 days after arraignment may effectively defeat the demurrer, leaving no grounds for appeal.

A defense attorney should ask a number of questions before deciding when to file the demurrer:

1. Will the prosecutor be able to fix the problem?

2. If the prosecutor believes he or she will have to reindict, will the demurrer nevertheless strengthen my hand in negotiations?

3. Will it get my client out of custody, even if he or she will be reindicted later?

4. Will the judge refuse to consider a late demurrer, thereby depriving my client of any benefit of filing it?

5. If there is a 90-day speedy trial issue, will the state be able to reindict and get the case to trial in time to satisfy the 90-day demand?

6. Will a judge refuse to grant my demurrer on the merits, thereby giving my client an issue on appeal he or she might otherwise not have had?

There is often no right answer on when to file the demurrer: it depends on your client, the grounds for demurring, the facts of the case, the prosecutor, the county, and the judge. As the best defense attorneys have long said about the evidence code, “I may not object to the introduction of evidence that I could have objected to, but it’s my choice; I always know when I can object, and the grounds for doing so, even when I choose not to.” The same is true for demurrers and the decision regarding when to file. Do what you think is best, but know the reasons why.

II. Requirements

A demurrer must be in writing and signed by the attorney or the defendant, and it must be specific. ORS 135.610. An oral demurrer is not worth the paper it is written on.

III. Hearings on the Demurrer

The issues raised by a demurrer are supposed to be heard either “immediately or at such time as the court may direct.” ORS 135.650.

Practice Tip

If you wait until the morning of trial to argue the demurrer, the prosecutor may be able to reindict that same day (even before your client gets out of custody), since all of the state’s witnesses will be there for the trial. Consider setting the hearing on the demurrer well before trial to avoid immediate re-indictment, and if doing so is contrary to the local court rules, direct the court’s attention to ORS 135.650.

IV. Grounds for Demurring

A demurrer can be filed when:

1. The charging instrument fails to comply with any of a number of procedural statutes listed in ORS 135.630(2).

If the accusatory instrument is an indictment, that it does not substantially conform to the requirements of ORS 132.510 to 132.560, 135.713, 135.715, 135.717 to 135.737, 135.740 and 135.743.

Some of the requirements of ORS 132.540, which, if not complied with, can result in a successful motion to set aside:

An allegation that the crime was committed within the jurisdiction of the court, either because it occurred within the county or is otherwise triable within. ORS 132.540(1)(b).

An allegation that the crime occurred prior to the finding of the indictment and within the time allowed by the statute of limitations. ORS 132.540(1)(c).

The indictment shall not contain allegations that the defendant has previously been convicted of the violation of any statute which may subject the defendant to enhanced penalties, except where the conviction constitutes a material element of the crime charged. ORS 132.540(2).

However, see State v. Upton, 339 Or 673 (2005), which holds that sentencing enhancement facts are now material elements under ORS 132.540(2). Though intended to enhance the sentence, the allegation of “persistent involvement in prior offenses” would not be a basis for a motion to set aside since the “material element” exception now applies.

2. When the grand jury had no legal authority to inquire into the crime, because the crime was not triable in the county in which the grand jury was empaneled. ORS 135.630(1).

3. When the accusatory instrument charges more than one offense not separately stated. ORS 135.630(3).

An argument was once made that alleging DCS – Commercial Drug Offense (CDO) and DCS – Substantial Quantity (SQ) in the same count violated this provision. The Oregon Court of Appeals ruled otherwise in State v. Merrill, 135 Or App 408 (1995). The reason the Merrill court gave is that CDO and SQ factors are not elements, but alternative grounds for enhancing the sentence.

Practice Tip

There are times when losing a demurrer is in your client’s best interest. Some demurrers, if successful, will simply result in the state reindicting. But losing the demurrer, then winning on appeal, will often mean it’s too late under the statute of limitations to reindict.

Also, if the legal argument is sound but the judge decides a demurrer is not the proper vehicle, the judge may indicate a willingness to reconsider the argument at trial or at sentencing. In that situation, you may have improved your bargaining position, even though you have lost the demurrer.

Another time losing the demurrer can benefit your client is when you lose the demurrer for improper joinder. See Appendix C. If you lose because the judge finds the two felonies joined in the indictment are from the same criminal episode (e.g., failure to register and felon in possession), your client now gets the benefit from being from the same criminal episode, including the Buchholz/Miller, shift-to-I, 200% and 400% rules.

4. When the facts stated do not constitute an offense. ORS 135.630(4).

a. This is one of the grounds that can also be filed as a motion in arrest of judgment or even a motion for judgment of acquittal (''see State v. Hankins,342 Or 258, 151 P3d 149 (2007), in which the Oregon Court of Appeals held that a motion for judgment of acquittal based on a failure to state an offense is in effect a premature motion in arrest of judgment. However, in affirming, the Oregon Supreme Court strongly suggested that a trial court should wait until after verdict, in case an acquittal moots the issue.) Also, in State v. Touchstone,188 Or App 45 (2003), in an analysis which would apply just as much to demurrers, a charge of harassment was improperly alleged and remanded to the trial court for entry of a conviction for attempted harassment. In that case, the defendant wisely filed a motion in arrest of judgment rather than a demurrer because the prosecutor would have fixed the complaint if alerted to the issue by way of a demurrer.

Case law indicates that jeopardy does not attach, even after trial, when a motion in arrest of judgment is granted on the basis that the facts do not constitute an offense. State v. Wolfs, 312 Or 646 (1992). In other words, the defendant could go through a trial, be convicted, be sentenced, successfully file a motion in arrest of judgment and the state could still reindict. At present, however, all appellate cases dealing with this issue have dealt with single count indictments, and the same analysis may not hold true when there are counts from the same criminal episode which were adjudicated at trial and survived the motion in arrest of judgment.

Jeopardy would still attach to those additional counts, which should result in a different result than the one in Wolfs.

b. Although there is no explicit statutory language allowing the defendant to demur to indictments with constitutional defects, the appellate courts have ruled that when a statute is constitutionally “invalid,” a charging instrument based on that statute does not “constitute an offense.” SeeState v. McKenzie, 307 Or 554 (1989) (a vague statute is subject to a motion in arrest of judgment because – if vague – the statute is “invalid” and therefore fails to state an offense).

At first blush, it might make sense to apply the same analysis to – for example – a pretrial demurrer to a statute that would inevitably result in a constitutionally disproportionate sentence, given that a disproportionate statute is “invalid.” State v. Shumway, 291 Or 153 (1981). But case law is ambiguous on this point:

Defendant also contended before the trial court and argues again on appeal that the classification of delivery of marijuana for no consideration as a Class A felony in this case, while the delivery of marijuana for consideration is classified a Class B felony, violates the proportionality clause in Article I, section 16, of the Oregon Constitution. However, that argument is not a proper consideration at this time under ORS 135.630 because its effect is to challenge the length of sentence that the trial court would be authorized to impose only if defendant is convicted. ''Cf. State v. Fanus, 336 Or 63, 79 P3d 847 (2003) (holding that a facial challenge to the constitutionality of Oregon’s sentencing statute for aggravated murder was the proper subject of a pretrial demurrer).

State v. Brown, 232 Or App 472, 480 (2009).

Regardless, a demurrer is not the proper vehicle when the constitutional defect does not appear on the face of the indictment. State v. Pinnell, 319 Or 438 (1994).

If there is no statutory remedy anywhere for a constitutional defect, the constitution itself may serve as the authority for a hearing on the issue. Se,e for example, State v. Sutherland, 329 Or 359 (1999), where the Oregon Supreme Court found that in the absence of statutory authority allowing the defendant to challenge the “bail minimum” statute, the Oregon Constitution’s bail provisions could serve as authority for a hearing on a violation of that provision. See Appendix B for an example.

5. When the facts in the charging instrument constitute a legal justification or other legal bar to the alleged crime. ORS 135.630(5).

Up until September 29, 1991, the crime of sexual penetration with a foreign object – former ORS 163.411 – specifically excluded from its scope penetration by any part of the actor’s body.The 1991 legislature amended ORS 163.411 to include digital penetration within the conduct prohibited by the statute.The amendment took effect on September 29, 1991. As a result of that change, it is not unusual to see indictments that describe the foreign object as, “to wit: the defendant’s finger.” However, because the statute of limitations on sex crimes has been lengthened (in some cases more than 20 years), the state may now indict the crime of sexual penetration, using the “to wit: the defendant’s finger” language, when the crime occurred long enough ago (before September 29, 1991) that “the finger” was actually a defense to the crime.

6. That the accusatory instrument is not definite and certain. ORS 136.630(6).

This provision codifies the requirement under Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution, which provides: “In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall have the right…to demand the nature and cause of the accusation against him.…” This section has been interpreted to require specificity in accusatory instruments. State v. Smith, 182 Or 497, 188 P2d 998 (1948); State v. Sanders, 280 Or 685, 572 P2d 1307 (1977).

In State v. Cooper, 78 Or App 237 (1986), the court decided that a complaint charging a person with promoting gambling was insufficient when it merely mirrored the language of the statute and contained no identifying facts or acts. This may be particularly useful when the indictment includes sentence enhancement allegations, such as “harm substantially greater than typical,” without giving any specific facts either to the harm or what is typical.

What is the purpose of a complaint/indictment?

What is the purpose of a complaint/indictment? Is it merely to give notice to the defendant? Or is it to ensure that the defendant is only tried for crimes found by the grand jury? The following quotes are taken from State v. Anderson, 233 Or App 475 (2010):

“Defendant acknowledges both the general principle that an indictment in the language of a statute generally is adequate and the holdings in Reynolds, Hulsey, and Burns. Regardless, he argues, each of those cases stands only for the proposition that the indictment was sufficient to fulfill the ‘notice’ objective of indictments. Defendant emphasizes that one of the functions of an indictment is to ‘ensure that the defendant is tried only for an offense that is based on facts found by the grand jury.’ Burnett, 185 Or App at 415. He argues, ‘The fact that the indictment identified the [felon in possession] statute in the caption and alleged ‘unlawfully and knowingly’ may establish that the indictment was sufficient to give defendant notice, but it does not establish that the grand jury considered, much less found, that defendant knew, or was reckless or criminally negligent in failing to know, that he was a felon.’ Defendant relies on Burnett to support his argument.”

* * * *

“Most directly relevant to defendant’s argument in this case, we concluded, while agreeing that the indictment satisfied most of the functions of an indictment, that it did not satisfy the requirement that a defendant be tried only for an offense that is based on facts found by the grand jury indicting him. ‘[W]ithout the inclusion in the indictment of the elements of this offense set forth in ORS 811.540(1)(b), we stated, ‘we cannot say with certainty that the grand jury based the indictment on facts that satisfy this element of the crime.’ Id. at 416. Defendant asserts in this case that, under Burnett, the state’s failure to allege a culpable mental state regarding his status as a felon means that the indictment was deficient because it did not ensure that the grand jury ‘considered, much less found, all the elements of the offense.’ “Defendant is attempting to place more weight on the holding in Burnett than it can bear, for several reasons. We read Burnettas stating a narrow exception to the established rule that the primary function of an indictment is to provide notice to a defendant as to what crime he is being prosecuted for. Burnett held only that, when an indictment completely lacks language regarding an essential element of a crime, that indictment is insufficient. But when, as in Reynolds and Burns, the element is alleged--in this case, that defendant was a felon--but reference to other language in the indictment is needed only to ‘round out’ the allegation, it is governed by the more general rule that an indictment is sufficient if it is framed ‘in such manner as to enable a person of common understanding to know what is intended[.]’ ORS 132.550(7).”

V. Effect of Allowance of Demurrer

1. Like a motion in arrest of judgment, a demurrer puts the defendant in the same position he or she would be in prior to the filing of the charging instrument.

2. If a demurrer has been granted, one might think that ORS 135.670(2) bars the case from being re-filed or re-submitted if the state has not done so within 30 days of when the court submitted the order granting the demurrer. However, ‘if re-filing does not occur within 30 days after the order allowing a demurrer…further prosecution is not barred.’ ” State v. Harrison, 125 Or App 472 (1993), ''citing State v. Moline, 104 Or App 173 (1990).

3. An indictment against which a defendant has successfully demurred shall not be refiled unless the trial court believes the indictment can be fixed. ORS 135.670(1).

VI. Denial of a Demurrer

In a very disappointing holding, the Oregon Court of held that, even assuming the demurrer for failure to state a crime was wrongly denied, it would be harmless error since the jury was properly instructed on the elements of the crime. State v. Eberhardt, 225 Or App 275, 201 P3d 915 (2009), review denied, 347 Or 608, 226 P3d 43 (2010).
