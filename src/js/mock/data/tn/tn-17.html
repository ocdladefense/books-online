Chapter 17. Jury Instructions

By Robert S. Raschio

I. Introduction

Interestingly, litigators often spend a great deal of time and energy on the stages of trial before the jury instructions and then allow the court to deflate the jury with the Uniform Criminal Jury Instructions. Even the Bar Committee advises the Uniform Instructions are a framework and the court and counsel rely too much on them. Jury instructions are important weapons in a defendant’s arsenal. An order from the judge to the jury compels the jurors to act in a particular way. If the judge accepts your instruction, she will be ordering the jury to follow your interpretation of the law. Convictions are overturned on appeal because of the failure of a judge to give a properly requested defense instruction or because of the judge giving an improper instruction.

Start working on your jury instructions when you start work on your case. When you draft an instruction, you are applying the law that governs your case. The instructions should be connected to the opening statement, the theory of the case and the closing argument.

Jury instructions are your final message to the jury.

II. Statutes and Local Rules

ORS 136.320—Questions of fact are for the jury to decide and the court instructs on all matters of law.

ORS 136.325—No jury instructions regarding punishment except in murder and insanity cases.

ORS 136.330—Refers to the ORCPs and the polling of juries.

ORS 10.100—Views of premises by a jury.

ORCP 58(B–D)—Jury selection, instruction of duties and jury questions format. ORCP 58(A) is not specifically authorized for use in criminal cases. ORS 136.330.

ORCP 59—Refers to the process of jury instructions and deliberations.

UTCR 6.060—Proposed jury instructions and verdict forms.

UTCR 6.070—Jury Instructions.

Practice Tip

Uniform Trial Court Rules and Supplementary Local Rules

Uniform Trial Court Rules should be consulted when you are preparing your jury instructions. Rereading the rules helps keep instructions to the jury in focus. Local trial court rules should also be consulted as they may affect timing, preparation or contents of jury instructions. There is further discussion of each of the above rules throughout this chapter.

III. Requesting Instructions

A. Draft Your Own Special Instructions

Legal research leads to new or different interpretations of the law. Good writing skills can clarify and make dry, uniform instructions more interesting. Change commonly used uniform instructions such as the vague mental state instructions. Replace terms, such as “alibi,” that have strong negative connotations. Use federal court jury instructions as a way to get judges to start thinking “outside the box.” The court, however, is not required to use the exact language requested by counsel. State v. Charles, 293 Or 273, 647 P2d 897 (1982),reversed on other grounds, State v. Sandoval, 342 Or 506, 156 P3d 60(2007). Note the language in UTCR 6.060(4) regarding form requirements.

State v. Sandovalis a case study on how the law is changed through jury instructions. The Uniform Jury Instructions, along with a special instruction from the state objected to by the defense, lead to an abrogation of the state supreme court’s former opinion on the duty to retreat and a reevaluation of said duty, finding there is none under Oregon law. Timely objections and special instructions can change the lay of the land.

First, prior to reading the Uniform Criminal Jury Instructions, review the law of your case, decide on your defense and write the jury instructions of your dreams. Specifically, write a jury instruction directing the jury to the law that wins your case. Outline the defense in clear terms. If you can write it with supporting law, the court has to read it. Once read, the court has told the jury to follow your defense.

Second, review the Uniform Criminal Jury Instructions.

Third, modify the Uniform Criminal Jury Instructions to meet the law of the case that accurately messages your theory of the case. See the Appendix for a modified instruction on serious physical injury. The instruction incorporates the language of the UCrJI and the language of State v. Higgins, 165 Or App 442, 998 P2d 222 (2000), to communicate to the jury that the physical injury needs to be more profound than the state would have it.

Fourth, review your defense again. Go to the statutes and see if there is supporting language there for an instruction. When you use the language of the statute, the court’s discretion is limited only to the question of whether there is evidence in the record to support the instruction. Since you prepared the instruction, you will make sure the evidence is in the record!

Finally, when writing the instructions remember to:

• Use short, direct sentences.

• Avoid negative sentences.

• Avoid legalese—use concrete, not abstract words.

There is a wealth of special instructions already written on the Library of Defense page; use them or write your own. https://libraryofdefense.org/legal/jury-instructions.

B. When Proposed Instructions Should Be Submitted

In general, proposed instructions should be submitted by the parties at the commencement of trial. UTCR 6.060(6). Prompt submission of jury instructions is especially important if the litigator is asking the court to deliver some instructions before opening statements. Proposed instructions shall be in writing. UTCR 6.060(1). Proposed instructions upon questions of law developed by the evidence, which could not be reasonably anticipated, may be submitted at any time before the court has instructed the jury. ORCP 59A. If the proposed instruction is not from the Uniform Instructions, there must be a statement citing the statute, decision or other legal authority which supports the requested instruction. UTCR 6.060(4)(f). No identifying information or authority can be on the copy presented to the jury. UTCR 6.070.

An instruction first requested after the parties have concluded their closing arguments may be refused. State v. Radford, 101 Or App 665, 793 P2d 324 (1990).

C. Form of Instructions

The original plus one copy of the written jury instructions and verdict form should be submitted to the court. UTCR 6.060(2). Remember to check local rules for variations. Do not request instructions that may be relevant to the case but are not helpful to the defense. If you request an instruction, you cannot object to it later. Be careful.

It is normally appropriate to list requested Uniform Oregon Jury Instructions by number and title rather than copying them verbatim. However, if the uniform instruction has blanks that need to be filled in, the person preparing the instruction should type up the uniform instruction with the blanks filled in. UTCR 6.060(3). Copies of key instructions in your file may assist you in preparing opening statement and closing argument.

Practice Tip

Counsel should consider challenging or drafting alternatives to the Uniform Oregon Jury Instructions. They are advisory and not always accurate reflections of the current state of the law. The Oregon Supreme Court recently commented on the Reckless instruction, UCrJI 1036, stating, “the current Uniform Criminal Jury Instruction defining ‘recklessly’ exhibits the same deficiency that we discuss in this opinion. The bare terms ‘particular result’ and ‘particular circumstance’ do not adequately convey to a jury the required link between reckless conduct and the results or circumstances specified in the underlying criminal statute. Furthermore, a trial court should not instruct the jury that the mental state relates to both a result and a circumstance, unless that is accurate in the context of the particular crimes.” State v. Crosby, 342 Or 419, 154 P3d 97 (2007).

Counsel should challenge UCrJI’s that do not accurately state the law or context of the law. Counsel can use the opportunity to propose alternative instructions that set out the defense theory of the case or narrow the state’s theory of criminal conduct. State v. Sandoval, supra; see also State v. Lang, 215 Or App 15, 168 P3d 310 (2007).

D. Sending Written or Recorded Instructions to the Jury

Either party may request the court to reduce the jury instructions to writing or to record the jury instructions during an oral charge to the jury and give the written or recorded instructions to the jury to use during deliberations. ORCP 59B. The request must be made prior to trial, ORCP 59B, and the party making the request has the responsibility for recording the instructions or reducing them to writing. The statutory language is mandatory and failure to comply with a party’s request may be reversible error. State v. Looper, 76 Or App 231, 234–235, 708 P2d 1190 (1985); replacing opinion 77 Or App 660, 713 P2d 1099 (1986); State v. Etchison, 142 Or App 396, 921 P2d 1333 (1996).

A party may not want the jury to have the instructions. Attorneys are frequently concerned that despite the court’s warning, the jury might attach undue significance to a particular instruction. Many judges are tape recording and providing the instructions to the jury on their own initiative. Counsel should object to the court doing the act sua sponteif there is a concern about the jury attaching too much emphasis to a specific instruction.

E. Limiting Instructions

Upon request, the court must give a jury a limiting instruction when evidence which is admissible to one party or for one purpose but not to another party or for another purpose is admitted. Oregon Evidence Code Rule 105 (ORS 40.035). A timely objection and request to limit is vital, otherwise the issue may be waived. State v. Stevens, 328 Or 116, 970 P2d 215 (1998). This situation may present a difficult tactical decision for the litigator who may not want to highlight potentially prejudicial evidence.

A limiting instruction is inadequate under the [#amendmentvi Sixth Amendment] of the federal Constitution if evidence of a non-testifying co-defendant’s confession is admitted at trial and it inculpates another defendant being tried with the non-testifying co-defendant. Bruton v. United States, 391 U.S. 123 (1968).

F. Presumptions and Inferences

An instruction requiring a jury to draw an inference from certain facts is almost always unnecessary if not improper. ORS 136.320, State v. Rainey, 298 Or 459, 693 P2d 635 (1985); State v. Short, 88 Or App 567, 571, 746 P2d 742 (1987); State v. Campbell, 100 Or App 153, 785 P2d 370 (1990); State v. McCormick, 280 Or 417, 421, 571 P2d 499 (1977); State v. Johnson, 55 Or App 98, 103, 637 P2d 211 (1981); ''but see State v. Hines, 84 Or App 681, 735 P2d 618 (1987) (allows the Inference Uniform Jury Instruction).

In Hines, Judge Young writes an impassioned dissent on why the Inference instruction is in direct conflict with the Beyond A Reasonable Doubt instruction and should not be read. Counsel should consider excepting to the instruction and citing the dissent’s logic that the instruction dilutes the standard of proof by requiring juries to apply their “common sense” to every element of the crime.

A judge may not instruct the jury to find a presumed fact against the accused. OEC 309 (ORS 40.125); State v. Rainey, supra''. Instructing on presumptions that shift the burden of proof violates due process. Sandstrom v. Montana, 442 U.S. 510 (1979).

G. Alternate Theories

When a defendant is charged with one crime under alternate theories, the jury must be instructed that to convict, the jurors must agree on the manner in which the crime was committed. State v. Boots, 308 Or 371, 780 P2d 725 (1989). However, jurors do not have to agree on a single body of evidence to find facts sufficient to convict. State v. King, 114 Or App 32 (1992). An instruction that suggests otherwise will be properly denied. Ibid.

H. Weaker and Less Satisfactory Evidence

ORS 10.095(7) and (8) require the court to instruct a jury on the conclusions it may draw if a party produces evidence that is weaker and less satisfactory than it might have produced. However, this instruction should generally not be given in criminal cases, except in those rare instances where because of an asserted defense the defendant has the burden of proof on an issue in the case. State v. Mains, 295 Or 640, 646–648, 669 P2d 1112 (1983). A defendant has the right to request an instruction that is modified, like UCrJI 1025, to apply only to the responsibilities of the prosecution. State v. Mains, 295 Or at 648 n. 5.

In State v. West,145 Or App 322, 930 P2d 858 (1996), the trial court properly refused to give this instruction even though the officer who administered the field sobriety tests in a DUII case did not testify. The defendant failed to show that the testimony of the absent officer would have been any stronger than another officer who observed the tests.

Though the instruction is rarely given, counsel should propose it when there is an inference to be made from the evidence the state did not produce that said evidence would be adverse to its position, such as the state has something to hide or it possesses or could have possessed stronger evidence and has failed to produce it for the jury. If there is evidence in the record to support the instructions, it should be given. State v. McDonnell, 313 Or 478, 837 P2d 941 (1992).

In evaluating whether the trial court abused its discretion in not delivering the less satisfactory evidence instruction, it must be determined whether the party requesting it has demonstrated that the evidence was reasonably available and regarding an issue of fact that a jury would find such evidence stronger and more satisfactory than that offered. Ibid.

I. Sequence of Instruction—Before or After Argument

The sequence of a trial is within the discretion of the trial judge. ORCP 58A. ORCP 58 sets out the order of proceedings in a jury trial and has instruction following argument, ORCP 58B(5) and (6), but a judge can exercise her discretion and instruct before argument. State v. Ness, 54 Or App 530, 535–536, 635 P2d 1025 (1981), aff’d on other grounds,294 Or 8, 653 P2d 548 (1982). This unfortunately allows the government to literally have the last word with the jury.

IV. Giving the Instructions

A. When Is it Error to Refuse to Give an Instruction?

In general, the court’s failure to give a particular instruction is not error when counsel fails to request it. State v. Pinnell, 319 Or 438, 877 P2d 635 (1994); State v. Cardwell, 22 Or App 240, 538 P2d 372 (1975).

The trial court must give a party’s requested instruction if there is evidence to support it and it correctly states the law. State v. Freeman, 109 Or App 472, 474–475, 820 P2d 37 (1991) (Self Defense), State v. Boyce, 120 Or App 299, 852 P2d 276 (1993) (Lesser Included Charges), State v. Branch, 208 Or App 286, 144 P3d 1010 (2006) (Defense of Another), State v. Dahrens, 192 Or App 283, 84 P3d 1122 (2004) (Self Defense/Defense of Property), State v. Hogevoll, 223 Or App 526, 196 P3d 1008 (2008) (Game Case Instructions connecting statute, administrative laws, and the published rules).

Note: It is not error to give instructions that are “inaccurate and incomplete.” State v. Moine, 128 Or App 530, 877 P2d 94,rev den,320 Or 492 (1994). Likewise, a judge does not have to give an instruction in the form requested, and it is not error for the court to refuse to give a requested instruction if a different instruction adequately covers the subject matter of the instruction requested. State v. Forsyth, 20 Or App 624, 633, 533 P2d 176 (1975). A requested instruction is always properly refused unless it should be given in the terms in which it was proposed. State v. Francis, 284 Or 621, 626, 588 P2d 611 (1978) (the defendant requested an instruction on “intent” when the indictment alleged “knowledge”); ''see also State v. Owens, 159 Or App 80, 979 P2d 284 (1999).

A trial judge has the discretion to instruct on all questions and issues arising from the evidence even without a request. State v. Pruett, 24 Or App 555, 559, 546 P2d 415 (1976).

B. Defendant’s Right to Have an Instruction Stating His Theory of the Case

A defendant is entitled to have his theory of the case presented to the jury if there is any evidence from which the jurors could infer that the required elements of the defense were present and if he has submitted an instruction that correctly states the law. State v. Menaugh, 152 Or App 389, 954 P2d 177 (1998); State v. McBride, 287 Or 315, 319, 599 P2d 449 (1979), State v. Smith, 107 Or App 647, 813 P2d 1086 (1991), State v. Ketchum, 206 Or App 635, 138 P3d 860 (2006), State v. Thaxton, 190 Or App 351, 79 P3d 897 (2003), State v. Dollar, 181 Or App 354, 45 P3d 1014 (2002) (right to instruction on joint ownership). Theory of the case appears to refer to affirmative defenses or lesser-included offenses that are supported by the evidence. State v. Harper,132 Or App 29, 888 P2d 19 (1994).

Harper also contains a list of cases concerning theory-of-case instructions.

Practice Tip

Preservation of Error. Make your record. If you fail to except to the court’s instructions after the jury has been sent out to deliberate, the Court of Appeals will not review the instruction even if it is a plain error in the record. State v. Rodriguez-Castillo, 210 Or App 497 (2007), reversed on different grounds345 Or 39, 188 P3d 268 (2008).

Further, if the court answers a question from the jury during deliberations attempting to clarify a jury instruction, you should except to it at the time of the reinstruction to properly preserve your issue. State v. Crosby, 342 Or 419, 154 P3d 97 (2007).

C. Required Instructions

1. A defendant has a statutory right to have the jury instructed on the presumption of innocence. ORS 10.095(6). The instruction may be constitutionally required in some cases in order to insure due process of law, Taylor v. Kentucky, 436 U.S. 478 (1978), but is not required in all cases. Kentucky v. Whorton, 441 U.S. 786 (1979).

2. A judge must instruct on proof beyond a reasonable doubt. ORS 10.095(6). UCrJI 1006 is not statutorily required, nor is any other particular definition. State v. Castrejon, 317 Or 202, 856 P2d 616 (1993). Constitutional arguments were not preserved. The Supreme Court held that the U.S. Constitution neither prohibits nor requires trial courts to give a particular definition of “reasonable doubt.” Victor v. Nebraska,511 U.S. 1, 114 S Ct 1239, 127 L Ed 2d 583 (1994). An instruction defining “reasonable doubt” as “grave uncertainty” and “actual substantial doubt” violates due process because it suggests a higher degree of doubt than is required for acquittal. Cage v. Louisiana, 498 U.S. 39, 111 S Ct 328, 112 L Ed2d 339 (1990).

“Unless a reasonable doubt instruction misleads the jury to believe it can convict on a lesser degree of proof than that required, the court will not find error.” State v. Pratt, 316 Or 561, 853 P2d 827 (1993).

3. A judge must instruct on the functions of the court and jury. ORS 10.095 (1). 4. The court must instruct the jury completely on each element of the offense charged. State v. Brown, 310 Or 347, 355, 800 P2d 259 (1990). 5. A judge must instruct about the defendant’s constitutional right not to testify if the defendant requests the instruction. State v. Johnson,88 Or App 185, 744 P2d 600 (1987).

V. Taking Exceptions (And Avoiding Pitfalls When Drafting Your Own Instructions)

Exceptions must be taken immediately after the court instructs the jury. ORCP 59H. General exceptions fail to preserve error for purposes of appellate review. State v. Davis, 52 Or App 187, 190, 627 P2d 884 (1981). Failure to except to a trial court’s instruction on a specific theory generally bars appellate relief on that theory. State v. Brown, 310 Or 347, 355, 800 P2d 259 (1990). Also state how the error will prejudice your client if that is not obvious from your specific exception. Beware of exceptions made in chambers and off the record. The following are examples of specific exception language:

Argumentative instructions are instructions that state the law in language weighted in favor of one side and are properly rejected. State of Oregon v. Story, 208 Or 441, 447, 301 P2d 1043 (1956).

Comment on the evidence. “The judge shall not instruct with respect to matters of fact, nor comment thereon.” ORCP 59E. Language that provides a hint of the court’s evaluation of the evidence is objectionable. State v. Nefstad, 309 Or 523, 542, 789 P2d 1326 (1990);

Repetitious instructions may emphasize one side’s theory of the case or confuse the jury. State v. Harper, 132 Or App 29, 888 P2d 19 (1994).

Abstract (misleading) instruction. Take exception if the instruction relates to an issue that is not before the jury. Columbia County v. Randall, 49 Or App 643, 620 P2d 937 (1980).

Complex or incomplete instructions. State v. Brazeal, 247 Or 611, 431 P2d 840 (1967).

Instructions that have the effect of directing a verdict on an element of the offense.

Inference instructions are related to comments on the evidence. They tell the jury how to treat a particular piece of evidence. State v. Rainey, 298 Or 459, 693 P2d 635 (1985). Such instructions can also be said to shift the burden of proof. State v. Hayward,327 Or 397, 963 P2d 667 (1998).

Misstates the law. Brazeal, supra, State v. Lockwood, 43 Or App 639, 603 P2d 1231 (1979).

There is no need to except if the judge fails to give your requested instruction. ORCP 59H.

For an instruction standing alone to constitute reversible error, it must be such as would have prejudiced the defendant when the instructions are considered as a whole. State v. Bird, 59 Or App 74, 79, 650 P2d 949 (1982).

VI. Lesser-Included Offense Instructions

ORS 136.460 and 136.465 are the statutory bases for lesser-included offenses. State v. Washington, 273 Or 829, 543 P2d 1058 (1975), and State v. Palaia, 289 Or 463, 614 P2d 1120 (1980), discuss the general principles that govern when lesser-included offense instructions must be given. The only limitation on the court for giving a lesser-included instruction is that there must be evidence supporting the instruction in the record. Washington, 273 Or at 495–96.

A. Attempts

Is the other crime an attempt to commit the crime charged in the charging instrument? Attempts are always lesser-included offenses.

B. Lesser Grade or Degree

Is the other crime of a lesser grade or degree? Such crimes are not always lesser-included offenses. The litigator must analyze the elements of each offense. Are the elements of the lesser crime included in the greater crime? Arson II is a lesser-included offense of Arson I. State v. Gibson, 42 Or App 575, 600 P2d 962,rev den288 Or 173 (1979). Assault 3 is not a lesser-included offense of Assault 2. State v. Connell, 129 Or App 144, 876 P2d 867 (1994). However, Assault 4 is a lesser-included of Assault 2. State v. Higgins, 165 Or App 442, 998 P2d 222 (2000). Misdemeanor DWS is not always a lesser-included of felony DWS. State v. Edwards,123 Or App 519, 860 P2d 846 (1993).

Review the elements of the offenses and related case law carefully.

C. Lesser and Included

Is the other crime lesser and included in the language of the charging instrument? Reckless Burning is a lesser-included of Arson I. State v. Merideth, 149 Or App 164, 942 P2d 803 (1997). DUII can be a lesser-included of criminally negligent homicide. State v. Devereaux, 20 Or App 358, 531 P2d 749 (1975).

D. Evidence

Is there evidence to support a finding of guilt for the lesser-included offense?

State v. Farrar, 309 Or 132, 786 P2d 161 (1990).

E. Order of Deliberations

The 1997 Oregon Legislature amended ORS 136.460 requiring juries to find a defendant not guilty of the charged offense before they could consider a lesser-included offense. The statute is not unconstitutional. State v. Horsley, 169 Or App 438, 8 P3d 1021 (2000).

Failure to give a factually appropriate lesser-included offense instruction violates the [#amendmentviii Eighth Amendment] and Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution. Beck v. Alabama, 447 U.S. 625 (1980). Lesser-included instructions must be given to the jury if the facts support it. State v. Leckenby, 200 Or App 684, 117 P3d 273 (2005).

A defendant is entitled to submit a lesser-included offense instruction even if the lesser included offense is a violation. State v. Rafal, 21 Or App 114, 117–118, 533 P2d 1397 (1975) (Possession of Marijuana); State v. Cuffee, 87 Or App 293, 742 P2d 637 (1987) (Reckless/Careless Driving).

When a defendant requests a lesser-included offense instruction, trial judges must resolve doubts about the propriety of giving the requested instruction in favor of the instruction. State v. Miller, 53 Or App 493, 498 n. 4, 632 P2d 493 (1981).

If there are facts in evidence that support giving a lesser-included offense instruction, a defendant does not have a valid objection if the prosecution asks the court to give the instruction. State v. Keaton, 15 Or App 477, 484, 516 P2d 490 (1973); State v. Goldsberry, 30 Or App 1087, 1090, 569 P2d 646 (1977).

A defendant cannot complain about the failure to instruct if a defense to the principal charge is a complete defense to the lesser-included offense. State v. Stoneberg, 15 Or App 601, 604–605, 517 P2d 333 (1973).

Under the appropriate facts, a defendant may request a lesser-included offense instruction even though the defendant denies committing the act charged. State v. Thayer, 32 Or App 193, 197–98, 573 P2d 758 (1978); ''contrast with State v. Farrar,309 Or 132, 167–68. 786 P2d 161, 184 (1990).

VII. Deadlocked Juries

A “dynamite” or “Allen” charge is an instruction to the jury “that although no juror is expected to yield a conscientiously held opinion, the jury has a duty to decide the case if it can conscientiously do so, and that if a majority of a jury is for either conviction or acquittal, the minority ought to consider whether a contrary view may be reasonable or correct.” State v. Marsh, 260 Or 416, 423, 490 P2d 491 (1971). This charge has been specifically repudiated in Oregon. Id. UCrJI 1023 is based on instructions approved in State v. Marsh, supra, and State v. Ortega, 20 Or App 345, 531 P2d 756 (1975). The instruction applies in cases involving unanimous and non-unanimous verdicts. State v. Ortega, supra. It is not a required instruction and giving it or refusing to so instruct is within the discretion of the trial judge. State v. Marsh, supra, 260 Or at 443.

The litigator must also consider the circumstances in which a jury is instructed to continue deliberating. In State v. Hutchinson,142 Or App 56, 920 P2d 1105,rev den,324 Or 654 (1997), it was error to instruct the jury to continue deliberating immediately after reviewing a note with them that made it clear they were eight to four in favor of a conviction.

Exceptions to a “dynamite” charge should be made immediately after the instruction is given and the objection should be specific. State v. Johnson/Mitchell, 17 Or App 242, 245, 521 P2d 355 (1974).

VIII. Reinstruction

ORCP 59D permits a judge, at the request of the jury, to reinstruct the jury on a point of law. The judge may instruct the jury orally or in writing, but must do so in the presence of, or after notice to, the parties or their counsel. It is within the discretion of the court as to whether the jury’s request will be honored. State v. Vaughn, 200 Or 275, 277, 265 P2d 249 (1954). The extent of reinstruction is within the discretion of the court. This discretion includes giving additional instructions beyond those requested or to repeat all of the instructions previously given. State v. Brown, 4 Or App 219, 222, 475 P2d 973 (1970); State v. Holleman, 225 Or 7, 8–9, 357 P2d 264 (1960). Reinstruction out of the presence of and without notice to counsel violates the ORCP and is error, ORCP 59D; Huntley v. Reed, 276 Or 591, 556 P2d 122 (1976), but may not be reversible error, State v. White, 55 Or App 729, 639 P2d 1291 (1982). There was no error in denying the defendant’s motion to reinstruct the jury after the jury accidentally erased tape-recorded instructions. State v. Etchison,142 Or App 396, 927 P2d 1333 (1996).
