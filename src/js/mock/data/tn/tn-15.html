Chapter 15. Expert Witnesses

By Brook Reinhard

I. Testimony by Experts – Overview

If scientific, technical or other specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue, a witness qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training or education may testify thereto in the form of an opinion or otherwise. ORS 40.410; OEC 702.

OEC 702 says, “If scientific, technical or other specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue, a witness qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training or education may testify thereto in the form of an opinion or otherwise.”

Expert testimony is generally admissible when the knowledge of an expert would help the jury better understand matters about which they already have some knowledge.

If the expert’s opinion comes from scientific principles, you must meet certain foundational requirements. Those requirements were described in State v. Brown, 297 Or 404 (1984), as:

The technique’s general acceptance in the field.

The expert’s qualifications and stature.

The use which has been made of the technique.

The potential rate of error.

The existence of specialized literature.

The novelty of the invention.

The extent to which the technique relies on the subjective interpretation of the expert.

And State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995):

Whether the theory or technique in question can be (and has been) tested.

Whether the theory or technique has been subject to peer review and publication.

The known or potential rate of error and the existence of operational standards controlling the techniques operation.

The degree of acceptance in a relevant scientific community.

Practice Tip

Consider having the court rule pretrial on the admissibility of your expert if it is something new or unusual. See Chapter 7, “Motions in Limine.”

If the court excludes your expert witness or part of the testimony, make an offer of proof. See Chapter 11, “Making a Record and Offers of Proof.”

II. Obtaining Expert Witnesses

A. Determine Why an Expert Is Needed

Experts add to case preparation and presentation:

Always consult an expert if prosecution is using one in investigation, testing or trial.

Experts can provide a conduit for otherwise inadmissible evidence. OEC 703.

Tell the accused’s story through an expert. This can be useful if the accused is inarticulate, presents poorly, or has other communication problems.

Using an expert can avoid cross-examination of the accused and his or her criminal record.

Experts can present mitigating testimony at sentencing.

Practice Tip

Danger: Do you open the door to prior convictions, prior bad acts, or other negative impact information if such is “of a type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field in forming opinions or inferences upon the subject”?

B. Areas of Expertise Are Infinite

“This rule is broadly phrased. The fields of knowledge which may be drawn upon are not limited merely to the ‘scientific’ and ‘technical’ but extend to all ‘specialized’ knowledge. Similarly, the expert is viewed, not in a narrow sense, but as a person qualified by ‘knowledge, skill, experience, training or education.’ Thus, within the scope of the rule are not only experts in the strictest sense of the word, e.g., physicians, physicists and architects, but also the large group sometimes called ‘skilled’ witnesses....” 1981 Conference Committee Commentary citing Wigmore, Evidence §1918 (3rd ed. 1940).

2. Mental Health Professionals a. Psychiatrists

Generally preferable if the case involves organic, neurological, or physiological causes for client’s aberrant behavior. They are also generally qualified to discuss medications used in their field.

b. Psychologists

In many cases, psychologists can be more valuable before a jury due to the testing materials that they use. Although there are some problems with such standardized tests, juries are impressed with their “built-in” verification questions. Many psychological tests have an inherent demonstrative evidence value that buttresses the expert’s opinion as something not guessed at or not rooted in the manipulation of a crafty criminal defendant.

c. Neuropsychologists

Exceptionally valuable in cases where organic brain dysfunction is suspected. Through the use of extremely interesting testing procedures, these experts can narrow a problem to a specific area of the brain and explain in minute detail how the organic injury impacts a person’s cognitive functions and abilities.

Practice Tip

It is important to develop a “stable” of experts you can rely on. After you use an expert, carefully evaluate that expert’s performance. Don’t be afraid to change experts in a particular field if you are not fully satisfied with the expert. Your goal is to find an expert that is persuasive to a trier of fact in the expert’s field and is “bulletproof” on cross-examination. This “stable” can be developed through years of practice

Note: Obtain curriculum vitae or the educational, academic or trade résumé.

Practice Tip

Personally locating and evaluating potential experts for your case is one of the most important things the criminal defense lawyer can do. Do not assign this task to a legal assistant or office help by saying, “Find me an expert.” Read the curriculum vitaes and learn as much as you can about these experts, and try to obtain the best and most qualified expert your client can afford.

E. Appointment of Experts

1. Retained Cases ORS 135.055(3) allow clients with retained lawyers to apply for court-appointed expenses so long as the client is eligible for appointed counsel. (“A person determined to be eligible for appointed counsel is entitled to necessary and reasonable fees and expenses for investigation, preparation and presentation of the case for trial, negotiation and sentencing.”)

2. Indigent Cases

Practice Tip

Counsel should – as part of the motion, accompanying affidavit and order—request that the contents of all ex parte documents remain sealed until the conclusion of the case, See State v. Cunningham, 161 Or App 345, 333, 985 P2d 827 (1999) (“We hold that the trial court erred in denying defendant’s motion to seal the indigent defense expense records until the conclusion of the case”).

a. Approval and authorization of payment of expenses for an expert requires making an application for preauthorization of such expenses to the Office of Public Defense Services (OPDS). The rules and procedures for making application for non-routine expenses at OPDS are found in Section 3.2.3 of Public Defense Payment Policies, which can be found on the OPDS website at http://courts.oregon.gov/OPDS/. Application must be made on Request for Preauthorization of Non-Routine Expense form, and must be completed, signed and dated by the attorney requesting the expense. It must be accompanied by a narrative stating 1) the date counsel was appointed, 2) the most serious charge (if criminal), 3) the type of service or expense requested, 4) the reason the service or expense is necessary and reasonable for proper representation, and 5) what results counsel expects to obtain with the service or expense requested. “The OPDS will authorize the use of an out-of-state expert only if a qualified in-state expert is not available or is more expensive than an out-of-state expert.” Public Defense Payment Policies, Section 3.2.3.1. The OPDS guidelines amount for an expert is $110/hour.

When application for pre-authorization is presented to OPDS, they will review the application and make a decision. In the event the application is denied, a request for reconsideration can be submitted. If this is denied, “counsel may appeal the denial to the presiding judge in the court in which the subject case is pending or the Chief Judge or Chief Justice when the request involves and appellate case. The decision of the judge is final.” Public Defense Payment Policies, Section 3.2.3.1.

Practice Tip

OPDS recommends that the following information be included in the narrative accompanying a request for pre-authorization of funds:

Is counsel court-appointed? If not, who retained counsel? Has the client been found eligible for public defense expenses by the court?

Has there been a change in the client’s financial circumstances since the completion of the affidavit of eligibility that would make the client ineligible for public defense expenses?

What are the charges or allegations? What are some of the details regarding the case?

Has counsel made a previous request in this case for similar or related services?

What will the provider do? Be fairly specific.

Why is the service needed and how will this service benefit the defense?

Are there co-defendants and might a co-defendant benefit from this particular service? Can material or services be shared?

Will the provider work for the guideline rate? If not, why is counsel requesting this particular provider versus one who will work at the guideline rate? What is special about this particular provider or service that merits the increased rate?

If an unusually large number of hours are being requested, why are so many needed?

If counsel was not able to request pre-authorization, what circumstances prevented counsel from doing so?

III. Preparing Expert to Testify

A. Submission of Materials and Documents

1. Indirect Indirect method of submitting evidence under OEC 703. Under the provisions of this rule, experts can make use of any and all information that such an expert would normally make use of in their field, notwithstanding the limitations of other rules of evidentiary exclusion. Thus, one of the principal tactical reasons for engaging an expert, particularly in the mental health field, is the ability to get before the trier of fact information that would either not be admissible or for tactical reasons is better put in through the very articulate mouth of the expert rather than the inarticulate mouth of the accused or another witness.

2. Dangers of Submitting Materials Under OEC 704, all information which an expert has made use of in coming to his or her conclusions or opinions is subject to discovery by the prosecution during cross- examination. Thus counsel must weigh the potential advantages of getting positive evidence before the jury against the potential cost of any concomitant negative information coming out. Counsel must always assume that all information turned over or made known to the expert will probably be made known to the trier of fact. Thus, counsel should carefully weigh what information counsel will relay to the expert in writing, as opposed to verbally, and the tactical advantages and disadvantages of each means of communication on a case specific basis.

3. Danger of Withholding Material I accept the dangers associated with withholding material and generally provide my expert with all police reports and investigative materials. If your expert is surprised on cross-examination with an important fact that the expert knew nothing about before coming to court, you might lose your case. Also, that expert may never work for you again. Most experts are very concerned with their reputation. They know other prosecutors are getting transcripts of their testimony. The last thing they want to do is get embarrassed and successfully impeached by information you withheld.

B. Initial Letter to Expert

1. Explain statutory and case law perimeters within which the expert must operate. This letter must include the fact that the expert is covered by the attorney-client privilege and ensures the expert fully understands the absolute necessity of client confidentiality. 2. Point out time and cost limits. 3. Be careful in discussing case and client. This letter will most certainly become available to the prosecution.

Practice Tip

There may be cases where counsel wishes to verbally brief the expert on details rather than be overly inclusive in a preliminary letter.

C. Interaction with Client

Introduce client and whatever quirks or problems the expert may encounter in dealing with the client. In addition, ensure that client is aware of who the expert is, the purpose of the expert’s participation in the defense team, when expert will see client, etc.

D. Preliminary Discussion Prior to Preparation of Any Report

No expert of any kind should be retained for the defense team without a commitment from the expert that the expert will discuss in detail with counsel the expert’s findings and opinions before they are included in any written report or materials. This is not to tell the expert what the conclusions and opinions should be but rather whether a written report should be produced. It will also aid the expert in wording any written report in a way that is both entirely consistent with the expert’s findings and most beneficial to the defense theory.

E. Detailed Discussion of Trial Presentation

It is even more essential to review in detail with the expert the information which counsel seeks to put before the trier of fact. The expert should know exactly what questions will be asked, the order of presentation, the points that will be made. The expert must be aware of and able to communicate specific statutory language used in many affirmative defenses.

F. Cross-Examination

The well-informed and prepared expert does not need a great deal of protection from cross-examination. If well prepared, it is generally best to let them face the prosecutor without interference. The expert should anticipate that the prosecutor has read any material published by the expert and may try to use that material in cross-examination.

In many cases, counsel and experts working together can formulate a game plan to lure the prosecution into questions that will bring forth devastating answers that are elicited by the prosecutor.

On the other hand, proceed with great caution when cross-examining the state’s experts. Never do so without the aid of an expert of your own. Remember that the witness knows a great deal more than you do in their particular field. However, you should learn as much as you can about the particular field and also read this expert’s published materials as a potential source of cross-examination.

IV. Testimony by Experts

A. Voir Dire

Prepare the jury for your experts.

Discover juror biases regarding experts in general and the area of expertise for your specific expert. Some jurors think some areas of expertise are not actual expertises, i.e., psychology.

Discuss expert’s area of expertise. Make him or her interesting.

In this world of future shock and information explosion it is normal and necessary to call upon an expert. The expert is not a hired gun. He or she is here to help the jury understand an important issue.

B. Evidentiary Issues with Expert Testimony

1. Scientific Evidence/Foundational Requirements In State v. Brown, 297 Or 404 (1984), the Oregon Supreme Court stated that the term “scientific” refers to “evidence that draws its convincing force from some principle of science, mathematics and the like.” In State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995), the court extended this definition to “proffered expert scientific testimony that a court finds possesses significantly increased potential to influence the trier of fact as ‘scientific’ assertions”. Id. at 293.

2. Standards for Admissibility for Expert Testimony a. If evidence is deemed “scientific,” the burden is on the proponent of the evidence to establish its admissibility. Unless the particular type of scientific evidence has been approved by statute or a prior appellate decision, the proponent of the scientific evidence must establish that it meets the standards for admissibility set forth in State v. Brown and State v. O’Key.

b. State v. Brown. In discussing the method by which the admission of novel or new scientific evidence should be scrutinized in determining admissibility, the following non-exclusive list of factors is set out:

Technique’s general acceptance in the field

Expert’s qualifications and stature

Use which has been made of the technique

Potential rate of error

Existence of specialized literature

The novelty of invention

Extent to which the technique relies on the subjective interpretation of the expert.

c. State v. O’Key. In the O’Key case, the Oregon Supreme Court incorporated the analysis of the United States Supreme Court in Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579 (1993).

Whether the theory or technique in question can be (and has been) tested.

Whether the theory or technique has been subject to peer review and publication.

The known or potential rate of error and the existence of operational standards controlling the technique’s operation.

The degree of acceptance in the relevant scientific community.

d. In Kumho Tire Co., Ltd. v. Carmichael,526 U.S. 137 (1999), the U.S. Supreme Court eliminated the distinction between scientific and non-scientific evidence for the purpose of the trial court’s gate-keeping function established in Daubert.

3. Types of Scientific Evidence For a full treatise on scientific evidence, see the new OCDLA manual by Kevin Sali, Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys, and check the Scientific Evidence section at the Oregon Library of Defense.

a. Approved Scientific Evidence

Drug recognition expert protocol (DRE) – State v. Sampson, 167 Or App 489, 502–503 (2000). However, all 12 steps must be completed or the DRE will be excluded. State v. Aman, 194 Or App 463, 473 (2004).

Horizontal Gaze Nystagmus (HGN) – State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285, 322–323 (1995).

DNA evidence using the RFLP method – State v. Herzog, 125 Or App 10 (1993).

DNA evidence using the POLYMERASE chain reaction (PCR) method – State v. Lyons, 324 Or 256 (1996).

Chemical analysis for alcohol of a person’s breath, urine or saliva – ORS 813.300.

Ballistics – State v. Henderson, 182 Or 147, 187 (1947).

Radar – State v. Ringle, 40 Or App 393 (1979).

Fingerprints – State v. Smith, 128 Or 515, 526 (1929).

Blood tests to determine paternity – ORS. 109.250-.262.

Blood enzyme tests – State v. Mower, 50 Or App 63 (1981).

Bloodhound tracking evidence – State v. Harris, 25 Or App 71 (1976).

Blood splatter analysis – State v. Proctor, 94 Or App 720 (1989).

Hair analysis – State v. Kersting, 50 Or App 461 (1981).

b. “Clear Case” Exception

In State v. Branch, 243 Or App 309, 259 P3d 103 (2011), the Oregon Court of Appeals carved out a new rule for Lidar (Laser Distance Measurement). Brown’s and O’Key’s foundation requirements do not apply to Lidar, the Court said, because it is a “clear case” where the science is universally accepted and the technology is widely used. With this type of evidence, it is the opposing party’s burden to show that the science is not universally accepted, or not widely used, or not sound for the purpose offered.

c. Eyewitness Identification

This topic should probably consume an entire chapter unto itself, thanks to the landmark ruling in State v. Lawson where the Oregon Supreme Court held that the procedure on whether to admit eyewitness ID is based on Oregon’s evidence code. Lawson, __ Or __, (November 29, 2012.) (Slip opinion can be found at http://www.publications.ojd.state.or.us/docs/S059234.pdf). Under the new procedure, once a defendant moves to exclude an eyewitness identification, it is the state’s burden to prove the identification is reliable. Reliability is based upon traditional factors under the evidence code, such as personal knowledge, and whether the information will assist the jury in making a decision. If the state is able to demonstrate reliability, the defendant must then establish the probative value is substantially outweighed by the risk of unfair prejudice. This balancing test is where an expert witness can be critical for your case. For more information, Bronson James has some helpful first impressions on Lawson and what to do about it. (Link: Library of Defense, “Thoughts on Lawson, The New Eyewitness ID Case.”)

d. Psychological Syndromes

To qualify an expert witness to testify about “syndromes” such as battered woman syndrome and rape trauma syndrome, the criteria for introducing scientific evidence in State v. Brown and State v. O’Key must first be satisfied.

e. Diagnosis of Sex Abuse/Victim’s Characteristics

Two recent cases have completely changed the analysis for evidence pertaining to diagnosis of sex abuse: State v. Southard, 347 Or 127, 142, (2009), and State v.Lupoli, 348 Or 346 (2010). In Southard, the Oregon Supreme Court held that a statement from an expert that a child had been sexually abused was inadmissible in the absence of physical evidence of such abuse. Without the physical evidence, the opinion was simply the expert telling the jury that he or she believed the victim, which obviously was improper vouching and failed the OEC 403 balancing test.

In Lupoli,decided after Southward, the defendant did not challenge the diagnosis that the victim had been sexually abused, but he challenged the testimony of the nurse practitioner, Avila, regarding the basis for her diagnosis. The court agreed that the testimony should have been excluded. “Avila’s diagnosis, given the lack of physical evidence of abuse, necessarily was based on her assessment of the child’s believability. When Avila then was asked the basis for her diagnosis, she was implicitly declaring, with each statement and description, why she had found SM to be credible. She did so explicitly as well, as when she explained that ‘the manner in which [SM] told her story was pretty compelling’ and that ‘it had an effect’ on her forming her diagnosis.”

Lupoliand Southardare applicable in bench trials, and the state cannot circumvent the cases by having their expert testify that the facts are “highly concerning for sex abuse.” State v. Davilia, 239 Or App 468 (2010). Even if there is physical evidence, it must confirm the diagnosis of sex abuse, or the testimony should be excluded. State v. Lovern, 234 Or App 502 (2010).

Note: In light of Lupoliand Southard, many of the cases mentioned below are of dubious reliability. Where none have been explicitly overturned, if the state tries to use any of them to bootstrap impermissible vouching evidence, make sure to be ready with the proper objections.

In State v. Middleton, 294 Or 427 (1983), the court held that it was proper to allow expert testimony that a sex crime victim acted similarly to other children who have been sexually abused by other family members. This holding was qualified in State v. Milbrandt, 305 Or 621 (1988), where the court held that a foundation for such evidence must be established that meets the requirements of State v. Brown. The holding was further qualified by Lupoli, in striking down an argument that State v. Odoms (infra) and Middleton compelled a different result. (“It is evident from the foregoing that neither Odomsnor Middletonis apposite. Neither case even suggests, much less compels, the conclusion that a trial witness should be permitted to give an opinion that a nonwitness complainant was telling the truth.”)

In State v. St. Hilaire, 97 Or App 108 (1989), the defendant argued that expert testimony regarding the delay in reporting of child sex abuse victims was not relevant and not supported by the scientific evidence criteria of Brown. The court affirmed the trial court’s decision allowing the testimony, although it noted that the expert, a police detective who investigated sex abuse cases, may have strayed beyond his expertise when he “offered explanations for why abused children behave the way they do.”

In State v. Dale, 75 Or App 453 (1985), the defendant objected to testimony by a CSD child sex abuse worker that the victim reacted the way that a typical five- to seven-year-old victim would, arguing that State v. Middleton’s prohibition on vouching extended to this testimony. (Middleton, 294 Or 427 (1983)). The court affirmed, holding that the CSD worker was only testifying on the reaction of typical victims, and did not express an opinion on whether this victim was being truthful.

In State v. Pettit, 66 Or App 575 (1984), the court affirmed the testimony of a doctor to testify regarding victim’s general ability to recall dates, relate details of sex abuse, tell consistent stories and report such incidents promptly. (Note: The explanation and holding are the last three sentences of the case. There is no reasoning on why this testimony was acceptable, other than that it fit within the confines of Middleton. It is impossible to reconcile any of these cases with Lupoli.)

In State v. Padilla, 74 Or App 676 (1985), the court affirmed the testimony of a sex abuse counselor regarding whether the victim could accurately perceive a sexual contact and then relate it at a later point in time. Again, the court held that the Middleton rule on vouching would only come into play if the witness was directly asked to offer an opinion on the victim’s credibility.

In State v. Leach, 169 Or App 530 (2000), the court declined to answer the question of whether scientific foundation is needed to establish evidence of “grooming behavior” in a State v. Johns motion. For more information, see the concurring opinion in the case by Justice De Muniz.

In State v. Gerard, 188 Or App 414, 420–21 (2003), the court reversed a trial court ruling that had precluded the defendant from cross-examining the victim about aspects of their relationship that were normal (that they took family vacations, that they went hunting and fishing together, and that they spent time together). The trial court had ruled the evidence was “syndrome” evidence requiring expert testimony, but the Court of Appeals held that the defendant’s questioning would have merely established that the defendant had a normal, non-abusive relationship with the victim. That sort of evidence was not syndrome evidence, and therefore no expert testimony was needed in order to cross-examine victim about it.

Compare State v. Marrington, 335 Or 555, 560 (2003), in which the court ruled that the state’s expert who testified that delayed reporting was a predominant feature of child sex abuse needed to establish that this was indeed a valid scientific theory underBrownandO’Key''.

f. Polygraph Evidence

“In State v. Lyon, 304 Or 221 (1987), the Oregon Supreme Court ruled that polygraph evidence is not admissible for any purpose in a legal proceeding. This is true even when the parties have stipulated that the polygraph evidence would otherwise be admissible.

The exception to the Lyon rule was set out in 1992 by Fromdahl v. Fromdahl, 314 Or 496, 508 (1992), where the court held that polygraph evidence should have been admitted where the relevancy was that the wife believed that the husband failed a polygraph test regarding whether he sexually abused the children. The knowledge of the polygraph result was therefore relevant to her state of mind to explain why she left the state with her children – and not to prove the truth of the matter of whether the sex abuse had actually occurred.

g. Witness Credibility

State v. Harwood,45 Or App 931 (1980). Expert testimony relating to witness credibility is admissible if it goes to the ability of the witness to perceive, remember or relate, but psychiatric evidence calling into question complainant’s personality or veracity is an invasion of the jury’s function.

State v. Middleton,294 Or 427 (1983). “We expressly hold that in Oregon, [a] witness, expert or otherwise, may not give an opinion on whether he believes a witness is telling the truth.”

State v. Milbradt,305 Or 621, 756 P2d 620 (1988). “We have said before, and we will say it again, but this time with emphasis – we really mean it – no psychotherapist may render an opinion on whether a witness is credible in any trial conducted in this state. The assessment of credibility is for the trier of fact and not for psychotherapists.” Milbradt at 629 (emphasis in original).

State v. Odoms,313 Or 76, 83, 829 P2d 690 (1992). Expert testimony cannot be excluded simply because it tends “to show that another witness is or is not telling the truth.” (Distinguished by Lupoli.)

State v. Charboneau,323 Or 38, 42&1, 913 P2d 308 (1996). A prosecution witness was impeached and DA attempted to offer written plea agreement that contained state’s “belief” that the reduced charge “accurately reflects” the role the witness played in the crime. The court said: “A witness’s testimony or an exhibit may not, explicitly and directly, contain an opinion as to a trial witness’s credibility.” At 48.

State v. Gherasim,329 Or 188, 19B, 985 P2d 1267 (1999). “In this case, we conclude that the expert’s testimony about his diagnosis of dissociative amnesia would have been helpful and that its exclusion was not harmless. The determination of defendant’s guilt primarily depended on whether the jury believed the victim’s or defendant’s version of what had occurred. Defendant’s expert would have testified that, in his opinion, the victim suffered from dissociative amnesia and that the condition affected her capacity to remember what had occurred on the night that she was assaulted. The testimony was evidence that defendant was entitled to present to the jury.”

In State v. Romero, 191 Or App 164 (2003), the court reversed the exclusion of expert testimony where the defendant sought to have the expert testify regarding his suggestibility to police interrogation techniques. The court ruled that the expert witness should have been able to testify as to the defendant’s psychological condition because he was not commenting on the defendant’s credibility – he was instead giving the jury information from which it could make inferences “regarding defendant’s claims that her confession was false.”

In State v. Leahy, 190 Or App 147 (2003), the court found that it was error to admit expert testimony by police officer that “I believe that it most definitely happened exactly the way the victim described it to me”; impermissible to allow any witness, expert or otherwise, to provide direct comment on the credibility of another witness.

Practice Tip

Be very careful when questioning an expert witness in this area. Judges are very sensitive to lawyers who try to get expert witnesses to testify that a particular state’s witness is not credible. If you stray too close to this question, you may get admonished in front of the jury by the judge. Your expert will be very unhappy with you, also.

h. Opinion Based Upon Evidence Obtained in Violation of Statute

i. For defense – admissible, State v. Milstead, 57 Or App 658, 656 P2d 63,rev. den.293 Or 483 (1982).

ii. For prosecution – excluded for all purposes. State v. Knepper,62 Or App 623, 661 P2d 560 (1983). Such opinions must be excluded and cannot be used as basis for state expert’s opinion on intoxication. This can be circumvented by the state if they produce a witness who can testify from personal perception.

i. Ultimate Issue – ORS 40.420 (OEC 704)

In State v. Middleton, supra. Testimony on the ultimate issue is not inadmissible solely on that basis, since even if it is uncontradicted expert testimony, the jury is not bound by it, for the jury alone must make the ultimate decision.
