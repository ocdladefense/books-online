Chapter 16. Closing Argument

'By Steven Sherlag''

I. Introduction

The final summation is the last word on your client’s behalf, and, because of the entertainment industry’s obsession with crime dramas and the bar set by professional actors, counsel may feel absurdly mounting personal pressure and pressure from your client to “hit one out of the park.” This infatuation with rhetoric in closing argument may in some ways be counterproductive. While there are endless tactics and styles that counsel can adopt in closing, the most important aspects of closing argument are that counsel maintain credibility with the jury, be sincere, and be prepared.

There are endless opportunities for counsel to develop new styles and techniques from closing, starting with brainstorming with peers (both in and outside the legal profession), watching other lawyers try cases, reading compelling closing arguments, including compilations of arguments, and by watching and learning from the entertainment industry’s representation of closing argument in crime-based dramas—don’t discount this. Really, it is amazing to see what a professional actor can do in two minutes. While two minutes is too forcefully truncated, it certainly sets a tone that a jury may expect and suggests that droning on for an hour or more is likely way too long, perhaps self-absorbed, and at a minimum likely well beyond the attention span of average jurors except in the most complicated scenarios.

“My profession and that of an actor are somewhat akin except that I have no scenes to help me and no words are written for me to say. There is no backcloth to increase the illusion. There is no curtain. But, out of the vivid, living dream of somebody else's life, I have to create an atmosphere—for that is advocacy.” Sir Edward Marshall Hall, KC, (Brighton, 16 September 1858 – 24 February 1927) was an English barrister who had a formidable reputation as an orator. He successfully defended many people accused of notorious murders and became known as “The Great Defender.”

II. The Law of Closing Argument

A. Defense Counsel’s Right to Closing Argument

Defense counsel has a statutory and a constitutional absolute right to make final argument. ORS 136.330; ORCP 58B(4); Herring v. New York, 422 US 853, 863, 95 S Ct 2550, 45 L Ed2d 593 (1975). The right is absolute, and the court cannot imply a waiver, even in a court trial. State v. Barajas, __ Or App __ (Dec. 14, 2011) (reversing conviction).

Closing argument provides the defense with an opportunity to persuade the jury to its point of view by reconciling conflicting facts and by interpreting the facts that have been presented, to argue conclusions based upon the facts gathered at trial, to explain how the law applies to the facts of the case, and to elicit sympathy from the jury. Id.(Dec. 14, 2011).

B. Prosecution First and Last

ORS 136.330 provides for criminal trial procedure and refers to ORCP 58B(4) that plaintiff shall commence and conclude the argument to the jury.

C. Improper Rebuttal

In general, the prosecution is prohibited from arguing on rebuttal new matter not raised in the defense argument. “The Government opportunity to have the last word must give way when that opportunity is abused.” United States v. Giovanelli, 945 F2d 479, 496 (2nd Cir 1991). Government counsel should not be permitted to develop new arguments not put forth by defense counsel. United States v. Taylor, 728 F2d 930, 937 (7th Cir 1984).

D. Waiver by Prosecution

Plaintiff may waive the closing argument. If defendant then argues the case to the jury, plaintiff has the right to reply, but not otherwise.

E. Length of Final Argument

ORCP 58B(5) provides that the whole time occupied by either party shall not be limited to less than two hours for argument.

F. Objecting and Making the Record

When the prosecutor engages in outrageous behavior during the course of trial, defense counsel must take four steps:

1. Object and Move to Strike Three great reasons for doing so:

First, it just might get granted, or even better, the judge might yell at or show scorn for the prosecutor.

Second, if you don’t object it’s not preserved. The defendant must move for a mistrial at the time the misconduct occurs and at the time the objection is made. State v. Wilson, 121 Or App 460, 855 P2d 657 (1993).

Third, the prosecutor might get a little gun shy or, in defending his comment, may just talk him or herself right into double jeopardy if the mistrial is granted.

2. Request a Hearing Outside the Presence of the Jury 3. Move for a mistrial; (possible motion to dismiss with prejudice under State v. Kennedy, 295 Or 260, 277, 666 P2d 1316 (1983)) The motion for mistrial must be timely or it will not lead to reversal and may cost you the ability to get a curative instruction. State v. Neal,143 Or App 188, 922 P2d 717 (1996). Where defendant’s motion came after the statement and the prosecutor finished his closing argument and defendant said, “I will have a matter for the court when we recess,” the claim of error was not preserved for review. State v. Williams, 322 Or 620, 912 P2d 364 (1996).

To obtain a mistrial, the defendant must establish that the right to a fair trial has been impaired. State v. Vann, 158 Or App 65, 973 P2d 354 (1999);see also State v. Smith, 310 Or 1, 791 P2d 836 (1990).

The standard for review for denial of a motion for mistrial is the face-saving “abuse of discretion” standard, since the appellate courts indicate the trial court is in the best position to determine whether a challenged act has prejudiced a defendant’s ability to receive a fair trial, State v. Nguyen, 117 Or App 219, 843 P2d 990 (1992), irrespective of that reality that many trial judges really don’t want to do the whole damn thing over, and they know if they grant a mistrial, they will.

For additional information, see Chapter 12, “Mistrial.”

4. Request a Curative Instruction Give the specific language that you wish the jury to be instructed on, but beware—you’ve got to be right on the law. Under Oregon law, there are two different types of error regarding jury instructions: (1) error in the failure to give a proposed jury instruction, and (2) error in the jury instructions that were actually given. See Bennett v. Farmers Ins. Co.,332 Or 138, 152–53, 26 P3d 785 (2001) (so indicating). An appellate court will not reverse a trial court’s refusal to give a proposed jury instruction unless the proposed instruction was “clear and correct in all respects, both in form and in substance, and … altogether free from error.” Williams v. Philip Morris Inc.,344 Or 45, 56, 176 P3d 1255 (2008);see also Hernandez v. Barbo Machinery Co.,327 Or 99, 106, 957 P2d 147 (1998) (“there is no error [in refusing to give a proposed instruction] if the requested instruction is not correct in all respects”).

G. Examples of Improper Prosecutorial Summation

Editor’s Note:The following pages contain objections for Prosecutorial Vouching in closing. For a complete discussion of vouching refer to Chapter 4 on the topic.

1. No Expression of Personal Beliefs or Knowledge Counsel should never argue to the jury personal beliefs or personal knowledge. State v. Miller, 1 Or App 460, 465, 460 P2d 874 (1969); DR 7-106 (C)(4). The utilization by the prosecutor of terms such as “I think” or “I believe” are error. State v. Miller, supra; State v. Gairson, 5 Or App 464, 484 P2d 854 (1971).

Note: In both Miller and Gairson, the defense did not object to the prosecutor’s argument. Oregon Judge’s Criminal Bench Book (1987), p. 19–2.

It is improper for the prosecutor to include within a question any statement concerning his personal knowledge of the truth of any disputed fact in issue where the burden of proving that fact rests upon the party he represents. State v. Matson, 120 Or 666, 670, 253 P 527 (1927); Burger v. United States, 295 US 78, 88 (1935).

It is improper for the court to declare a mistrial sua sponte unless it is beyond dispute that the prosecutor’s comments are so prejudicial as to deny a fair trial. State v. Fanus, 336 Or 63, 79 P3d 847 (2003). Make your objection. If it is denied, demand a curative instruction. State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336, 479 P2d 240 (1971).

2. No Vouching for Witness Credibility Any implication that a witness of the prosecution would not lie because the witness took a polygraph examination, or because an officer is in the court monitoring the testimony and the defendant could be prosecuted for perjury in the event he lied, is error. State v. Farrar,309 Or 132, 786 P2d 161 (1990); State v. Lyon,304 Or 221,744 P2d 231 (1987). Additionally, any statement, comment, remark, or insinuation where the district attorney vouches for the credibility or integrity of a state’s witness is improper. United States v. DiLoretto, 888 F2d 996 (3rd Cir 1989). The prosecutor may not interject his personal views that a witness spoke the truth. United States v. Modica, 663 F2d 1173, 1178–79 (2nd Cir 1980),cert denied, 456 US 989 (1982). United States v. Dennis, 786 F2d 1029 (11th Cir 1986). The prosecution may not imply that the government has taken steps to assure the veracity of its witnesses. United States v. Berry, 667 F2d 193, 198 (9th Cir 1980).

As a parallel, prosecutors cannot vouch for their own case on its “strength” because their office only brings “true cases.” State v. Cheney, 171 Or App 401, 16 P3d 1164 (2000).

3. No Argument Regarding Inadmissible Evidence It is reversible error for the prosecutor to argue or intimate that the state has other inadmissible evidence which implicates the defendant. State v. Newburn, 178 Or 238, 241–42, 166 P2d 470 (1946); State v. Parker,235 Or 366, 384 P2d 986 (1963).

4. No Argument Regarding Invocation of the Fifth or Sixth Amendment Rights It is never proper for a prosecutor to comment about a defendant’s failure to testify. State v. White, 303 Or 333, 341–42, 736 P2d 552 (1987); State v. Marple, 98 Or App 662, 780 P2d 772 (1989). Furthermore, any statement, remark, or insinuation regarding the defendant’s failure to testify or intention not to testify has a “presumably harmful effect.” State v. Wederski, 230 Or 57, 60, 368 P2d 393 (1962); See also State v. Halford, 101 Or App 660, 792 P2d 467 (1990).

Fundamentally, it is improper to introduce evidence of the defendant’s pre-arrest silence. State v. Mason, 53 Or App 811, 819, 663 P2d 820 (1981). A person not in custody can assert the right to remain silent under Article I, section 12. State v. Marple, 98 Or App 662, 666, n 2, 780 P2d 772 (1989); State v. Ragland, 210 Or App 182, 149 P3d 1254 (2006).

Thus, a prosecutor is prohibited from making arguments about a defendant’s invocation of silence or even counsel:

A prosecutor may not argue that evidence is uncontradicted by reason of a defendant not testifying. Griffin v. California, 380 US 609 (1965).

A prosecutor may not argue adverse inferences from a defendant’s post-arrest silence. Doyle v. Ohio, 426 US 610, 96 S Ct 2240, 49 L Ed2d 91 (1976); Oregon Constitution, Article I, section 12; State v. Alvord, 118 Or App 11, 846 P2d 432 (1993).

A prosecutor may not argue that guilt can be imputed since a defendant exercised the right to counsel. Bruno v. Rushen, 721 F2d 1193 (9th Cir 1983). The Fifth and Sixth Amendments preclude the prosecution from eliciting testimony regarding the defendant’s actions and hiring an attorney.

5. No Reference to Equally Available Missing Witnesses A prosecutor may not argue for adverse inferences from a defendant’s failure to call a witness or witnesses equally available to both parties, particularly if the issue has not been addressed to the court in advance of such argument. Forsberg v. United States, 351 F2d 242, 249 (9th Cir 1965). Furthermore, a suggestion that the failure of the defense to call an expert witness implies guilt is impermissible. State v. Walton, 311 Or 223, 248, 809 P2d 81 (1991).

6. No Attack on Defense Counsel or Defense Witness Attack Comments regarding the defense counsel may be erroneous, particularly if it is implied that the defense counsel was trying to mislead the jury throughout the case. State v. Lundblom, 96 Or App 458, 461, 773 P2d 11,rev den,308 Or 382 (1989) (defense counsel and witness called pimps). State v. Werder,112 Or App 179, 828 P2d 474 (1992).

Any suggestion that defense counsel and defendant made up a defense because they had access to discovery materials is improper. State v. Pirouzkar, 98 Or App 741, 745, 780 P2d 802 (1989).

7. No Reference to Evidence Outside the Record Comments by the prosecutor on matters outside the factual record constitute error. State v. Bolt, 108 Or App 746, 817 P2d 1322 (1991); State v. Treit, 29 Or App 461, 564 P2d 708 (1977); State v. Franklin, 11 Or App 239, 502 P2d 392 (1972); State v. Pace, 187 Or 498, 510, 212 P2d 755 (1949).

8. No Reference to Specific Notorious Crimes by Others It is proper for the prosecutor to comment upon the evils generally of the crime, but any reference to other specific alleged nefarious acts requires a mistrial. State v. Bolt, supra; State v. Blodgett, 50 Or 329, 343, 92 P 820 (1907).

9. Prosecutor May Not Appeal to Public Opinion It is error for the prosecutor to imply that the public will appreciate a guilty verdict or that the jury should be concerned with the public’s opinion if a guilty verdict is not returned. Caldwell v. Mississippi,472 US 320 (1985).

10. Prosecutor May Not Appeal to Passion – No “Sending a Message” Any prosecution argument which appeals to the passion of the jury is erroneous. State v. Wilson, 221 Or 602, 351 P2d 924 (1960). Thus, “send a message” arguments are not permissible. State v. Neal, 143 Or App 188 (1996); United States v. Solivan, 937 F2d 1146 (6th Cir 1991). Statements or insinuations that are calculated solely to evoke an emotional response from the jury are equally prohibited. Walker v. Penner, 190 Or 542, 553, 227 P2d 316 (1951). Remarks that are “highly likely to incite or inflame the jury to decide a case on an improper basis” require a mistrial. Warren v. Baldwin, 140 Or App 318, 915 P2d 1016 (1996).

11. No Non-Rebuttal/Matters Not Addressed by Defense The prosecutor may not argue new matters or points in closing summation as this summation is solely a rebuttal argument. Defense counsel should not argue matters in closing summation which will open the door for rebuttal; some things are best left unsaid. State v. Miller, supra; see also State v. Guritz, 134 Or App 262, 894 P2d 1235 (1995); see Hempel v. Palmateer, 187 Or App 70, 66 P3d 513 (2003).

12. Improper to Argue Sentence in Most Situations Any reference (except in the penalty phase of a capital case) to imposition of sentence, including probation or parole, is improper. State v. Wampler, 30 Or App 931, 935, 569 P2d 46 (1977).

13. No Argument that Mistakes will be Corrected on Appeal It is error for the prosecutor to infer that the jury need not worry about its decision because any error will be corrected on appeal. Caldwell v. Mississippi,supra.

14. No Argument re Sexual Innuendo Unrelated to Case Any reference to the sexual preference or sexual proclivities of a defendant when neither is an issue in the case constitutes error. United States v. Birrell, 421 F2d 665 (9th Cir 1970); State v. Rollo,221 Or 428, 351 P2d 422 (1960).

15. No Argument re Inferences by Request for Lesser-Included Instruction Any inference that a defendant lied that is drawn by noting the request by counsel for a lesser-included instruction is at odds with the testimony is error. State v. Johnson, 55 Or App 98, 637 P2d 211 (1981),revden, 292 Or 722 (1982).

16. Improper to Argue Deceased Victim Is a “Silent Witness” It is improper for a prosecutor to refer to a deceased victim as the state’s silent witness. State v. Leland, 190 Or 598, 642, 227 P2d 785 (1951).

17. Improper to Argue the Law The law is for instruction by the court; arguing erroneously may be error. If the prosecutor states the law incorrectly, possibly confusing the jury, the court must either instruct the jury so as to remove the confusion or grant a mistrial. State v. Dyer, 16 Or App 247, 514 P2d 363 (1973). Of course, arguing incorrect interpretations of the law is equally prohibited. State v. Molatore, 3 Or App 424, 428, 474 P2d 7 (1970).

18. No Argument that the Defendant Committed Other Crimes Suggesting that the defendant committed crimes other than those alleged in the indictment is impermissible. State v. Blodgett, 50 Or 329, 343, 92 P 820 (1907); ''State v. Treit, supra (improper reference to prior record).

19. No Argument Regarding Beliefs of the Grand Jury It is impermissible for the state to make reference to the beliefs of the grand jury. State v. Miller,supra ''(“The state is here. The state believes there is evidence. The grand jury believes there is evidence. I believe there is evidence…”).

20. No Reference to Prospective Jurors Who Have Been Victims of Crime Reference during the state’s closing to a prospective juror who had been a victim of crime is impermissible. State v. Bolt, supra.

21. No Reference to the Defendant Being Less Than Human It is improper for the prosecutor to refer to the defendant as:

• an “animal”; State v. Wilson, 69 Or App 569, 573, 687 P2d 800 (1984).

• a “crook”; State v. Kennedy, 295 Or 260, 277, 666 P2d 1316 (1983).

• “diabolical”; United States v. Steinkoetter, 633 F2d 719, 720 (6th Cir 1980).

• a “devil”; United States v. Chavez-Vernaza, 844 F2d 1368 (9th Cir 1987).

• a “vicious murderous pig”; State v. Gairson, 5 Or App 464, 484 P2d 854 (1971).

• a “hoodlum”; United States v. Goodwin, 492 F2d 1141, 1147 (5th Cir 1974).

• a “deviant homosexual”; State v. Swink, 225 SE 2d 646, 647 (1976).

• a “cheap, scaly, slimy crook”; Volkmor v. United States, 13 F2d 594, 595 (6th Cir 1926).

• “a junky, rat, and sculptor with a knife”; People v. Hickman, 34 A2d 831 (1970).

• a “hippy, an anti-Christ and a communist”; Renn v. State, 495 SW 2d 922, 924 (Tex Crim App 1973).

• a “blood-crazed animal who hovered over the victim’s grave”; People v. Martinez, 377 NE2d 1222, 1229 (1978).

• a “pervert, a weasel and a moron who raped his mother’s friend, would rape a dog and would rape each and every member of the jury”; People v. Garreau, 189 NE 2d 287, 289 (1963).

• “Mr. Cocaine”—a slip of the tongue; United States v. Hickey, 917 F2d 901 (6th Cir 1990).

• a “liar”; United States v. Nickens, 955 F2d 112, 121 (1st Cir )cert denied506 US 835 (1992); United States v. Rodriguez Estrada, 877 F2d 153, 159 (1st Cir 1989); United States v. Garcia-Guizar, 160 F3d 511, 521 (9th Cir 1998).

• “Saddam Hussein,” “a bully and a thug”; Harris v. People, 888 P2d 259 (1995).

Note: This case contains excellent Berger analysis of the prejudice associated with misconduct by the prosecutor, finding that the misconduct leads to a partial jury, who is not basing its verdict on evidence but on prejudice and bias.

The following are also improper:

• Comparing defendant’s actions to those of Hitler’s brown shirts, Mussolini’s people in Italy, and the KKK; People v. Bedolla, 94 Cal App 3d 1, 8 (1979).

• Allowing defendant to be impeached by evidence of an unsuccessful not guilty plea in unrelated prosecution, and allowing prosecutor to argue that the defendant was dishonest based upon that plea, was error; United States v. Norris, 910 F2d 1246 (1990).

• The independent counsel stating that Oliver North and McFarland were up to “Adolph Hitler” tactics. The court ruled that this was just “poor judgment;” United States v. (Oliver) North, 910 F2d 843 (DC Cir 1990).

• The prosecutor referring to defendant as an animal, coward, beast, sadist, maniac, or any other generic term designed to subject the defendant to personal abuse or convey to the jury that defendant is less than human; Richardson v. State, 257 SW 2d 308 (Tex Crim App 1953); Marks v. State, 150 SW 2d 1040 (Tex Crim App 1941).

22. Improper to Argue About the Defendant’s Off-the-Witness-Stand Behavior Reference to nervous habits of the defendant, or any off-the-stand behavior is improper. United States v. Pearson, 746 F2d 787 (11th Cir 1984); United States v. Wright, 489 F2d 1181 (DC Cir 1973) (comment on off-the-stand behavior requires reversal). Unless the defendant takes the stand, the defendant’s personal appearance or conduct at the trial is irrelevant to the question of guilt or innocence. If the defendant remains stoic during the testimony of the accuser, the defendant is only conforming to the standards of decorum that are expected in the trial process. Cunningham v. Perini, 655 F2d 98 (6th Cir 1981); United States v. Carroll, 678 F2d 1208 (4th Cir 1982); United States v. Pearson, supra.

23. Burden Shifting Is Improper Any statement that places the burden of proof on the defendant is improper. State v. Walton,311 Or 223, 809 P2d 81 (1991). A prosecutor’s argument that “[t]he defense…has to convince you” in summation was “indefensible.” United States v. Cruz, 797 F2d 90 (2nd Cir 1986).

24. Logical Connectivity There must be some logical connection between the criminal charge and the evidence discussed in closing. State v. McBean, 189 Or App 235, 74 P3d 1127 (2003). Inference must flow from the predicate facts. State v. Schlender, 199 Or App 253, 110 P3d 653 (2005). If they do not, the argument is improper. State v. Branch, 208 Or App 286, 144 P3d 1010 (2006).

III. Practice Tips

In the introduction to William Safire’s fantastic book, Lend Me Your Ears: Great Speeches in History, the author analyzes great speeches for what makes them great. To Safire, there are ten rules of great speeches:

The first rule is simple—smile. Or, if circumstances dictate, cry. In other words use sincere passion to your advantage, for you are persuading people, and people are persuaded by emotion and connection.

Then comes “shapeliness”—thematic anatomy or structure. This is the simple organizational principle of tell them what you’re going to tell them, tell it, and tell them what you told them.

Third, have a pulse, a changing rhythm, and a sense of movement that gets the audience “tapping its mind’s foot.” For rhythm use the anaphora, a repeated beginning. Examples are plentiful and well known: Jesus—“Blessed are the poor…Blessed are the meek…Blessed are the peacemakers.” Or Martin Luther King, Jr.—“I have a dream…I have a dream…I have a dream.” You—“Do not engage in guesswork…Do not engage in conjecture…Do not engage in speculation.”

Fourth, you need “occasion”— this is given. You deliver a closing to jurors who need to hear it, who need to be unified in the quest for freedom, who need spiritual uplift.

Fifth, you need “forum” — also a given, for jurors must listen, if you give them reason.

Sixth, you need “focus.” Say the right thing, at the right time, when the audience is most fitted to hear it. For example, counsel may want to repeat a key phrase from testimony that focuses on your theory. This could be the first sentence of closing.

Seventh rule — “purpose.” “A great speech is made for a high purpose—to inspire, to ennoble, to instruct, to rally, to lead.”

Eighth, you need a “theme.” “In the end, you must answer in a word or sentence the question of the person who couldn’t be there: What was the speech about?”

Rule number nine is to use phrases with appropriate metaphors. What you lack in depth, you can make up in zest.

Last but not least—delivery. Beware of undeliverable words, for they are sure to make you stumble or fly over the heads of the audience. Or, as Safire puts it, “Embrace the thin word.”

If you follow these rules, you may not win but at least your client will know you believe in their cause and will believe they’ve had their day in court. Pericles, who had a reputation as an orator and liked to speak of himself in the third person, said of Demosthenes: “When Pericles speaks, the people say ‘How well he speaks.’ But when Demosthenes speaks, the people say ‘Let us march!’ ” Your goal is to get the jury marching, to embolden them to be better than they really are, to instill bravery, to shun cowardice, and to make them feel that for once the government is beholden to their commands and not the other way around.

A. Entertaining the Jury

The preeminent trial lawyer of his generation and perhaps any generation once commented:

“Unless a speaker can interest his audience at once, his effort will be a failure.”

—Clarence Darrow, as quoted in The Art of Public Speaking,S.E. Lucas(5th ed. McGraw-Hill 1995).

Counsel cannot overlook the notion that a bored jury is a jury that is shut down. Respect them by entertaining them—make them want to hear what you are going to say. This starts in voir dire and continues every time you rise to speak throughout the entire trial.

B. Structure of Closing Argument

“If I had more time, I would have written a shorter letter.”

—Variously attributed to Ben Franklin, Blaise Pascal, Robert Sayre, Mark Twain

Respect the jury by preparing your closing before trial and all trial long. Repeat the theme of opening, or if you’ve abandoned it or modified it, hit your theme clearly, succinctly, and preferably in an entertaining manner. An organized closing is shorter, punchier, more entertaining, and more effective. When you have to give a long closing, unnecessary repetition disrespects the jury and tells them to shut down.

One good technique is to keep a yellow pad (or file) that contains only snippets for closing as they arise all trial long. That way your closing writes itself, or at least you’ve got the pithy stuff pulled together.

1. KISS Keep It Simple Stupid. That’s what the jury wants. If you have some complicated technical evidence to discuss, you better have a simple way of explaining, breaking it down, which began in opening, direct, cross, and which you can now short hand in closing.

Never talk down to the jury — they are allies and should be treated as such.

Keeping it simple shows the jury you respect them.

2. Break it Down into Headings and Subheadings Ever watch Fox News? Well, you better, because chances are that a significant portion of your jury prefers “Fair and Balanced” news. According to recent studies, eight out of ten of the most watched cable news shows are on Fox. Is it because Fox has the evidence on its side? The facts? Does it matter?

It doesn’t really matter. Fox News is brilliant because it takes complicated issues and makes them simple, breaking them down into simple little bites which are irresistible to jurors and which compel an answer. Use the same techniques in closing. Outline your closing by topic and facts that support your theory, and use short, memorable and emotionally appealing phrases that direct an acquittal. Rewrite your outline at least once if not several times. Throw out the redundant, the unnecessary, and distill it to what is needed.

Consider giving the jury your outline—i.e., your roadmap to closing—at the beginning of closing, especially through PowerPoint or even with a marker on a white paper at the easel. If you do they will know where you are going, are more likely to remember the salient points, and, if necessary, they’ll tolerate a longer closing. They’ll tolerate a longer closing because they’ll have an idea where you’re going with it, and as you knock off topics, they’ll have a sense of accomplishment, a sense of what’s coming next, and a sense of how much longer it will be until they get to re-enact “Twelve Angry (Not All)

Men.”

3. Notes? No Notes? Write Out the Whole Closing? Read the Entire Closing? Many seasoned advocates will argue for notes, many against, many more against writing out and reading the entire opening.

“No notes” may be highly desirable in theory, but it’s very risky because it’s fraught with the very high danger of forgetting an important point. It’s also risky because you may deliver an important point with less than optimal persuasive impact. Generally, this should be rejected unless you’ve got a great memory, you have a very simple closing, or you are prepared to memorize most, if not all, of your closing. These opportunities or abilities are rare.

“Notes” are what most of us mortals, strapped for time between the last witness and closing, rely upon. If you can create an outline, redraft it a few times, and put it into a PowerPoint, you’ll give the jury an effective roadmap and one that might just look like you are giving it extemporaneously.

“Writing out the whole closing” is highly desirable but few are the times that criminal defense lawyers have the luxury. However, if you can do it, it will help shape, craft, reshape, and recraft, and maximize your delivery.

“Read the entire closing” is very rare, and few would ever advocate it. I’ve only seen it done once in a long and complicated trial, conducted by highly skilled counsel, and let me say it was one of the very best closings I’ve ever had the privilege to watch in all respects. So don’t rule it out if you can do it, but reading a closing takes its own set of skills. In very complicated cases I write out my entire opening, re-craft and re-draft. I started doing this because my openings were too argumentative, and then I realized that when I wrote them out they started to get much better than my ad-lib renderings. But when reading my openings, I’ve spent so much time writing them that I practically have them memorized and even though I’m reading it, it’s more like a teleprompter delivery, and it probably looks to the jury like I’m just looking at notes. Tips when reading it are:

  • Break your closing down

C. Delivery of Closing Argument: Pace, Passion, Attitude and Emotion

There is no single uniformly correct manner of delivery. Every case can be tried beautifully many different ways by the same or different lawyers.

Natural, sincere emotion is a powerful appeal, a call to arms, a call to individual jurors to fight for your client to the end. I’ve heard stories from my trials that the jury’s initial vote was 11-1 to convict, and that one passionate juror turned them around to 10-2 acquittal. Appeal to their conscience, their sense of morality, to the finality of a conviction. But remember that an emotional appeal is exhausting and can strain credibility if it goes on too long. Save it for one or two really important points.

Consider the volume at which you speak. When you modulate it, you make your speech more dramatic and add emotion, not only by getting LOUDER but by getting softer during a particularly vulnerable, emotional moment.

Pace of your closing is important. Varying your pace keeps it interesting. There is a tendency that lawyers have to race and become fast talkers in closing, but if you go too fast you will risk coming across as a salesman—insincere, lacking in confidence, and nervous. More importantly, there is only so much that anyone can hear and absorb if you are going too fast.

Practice Tip

The Power of the Pause. Think of times when you have gone out to a fine restaurant and your server rolls off 2-3 “specials of the day” in about 35 seconds. Your co-diner scratches her head and asks, “How are they preparing the fish?” You reply, “Fish? I didn’t hear the server even mention the fish, I thought he said veal.”

How did you miss it? Were you paying attention? Sure you were, but the server went so fast that you never even heard the server describe the fish because you were still thinking about the cream of squash soup with roasted peppers.

Same thing happens in trial. Make your points too fast and they’ll get lost, scrambled, jumbled and not remembered.

Pauses can be used for dramatic effect. Pauses are punctuation marks. Pauses give you a chance to make eye contact and connect with jurors. Pauses give the jurors the chance to hear, remember what you said, and to connect the dots.

D. Credibility

If you’re not credible throughout the trial, and not credible in closing, you will not be effective. This is far more than just a straight-faced test. You’ve got to give in fully to the trial psychosis, a true believer in your client’s complete innocence, even if you’re not arguing innocence. It’s that suspension of (the likely) reality that will give you the extra boost, to look the jurors in the eye and argue against a permanent strike against your client.

E. Persuasion Techniques

There are many different techniques that are effective and can be employed in various circumstances. Borrow from colleagues, borrow from TV and the movies, borrow from any effective storyteller. Here are a few ideas:

1. Drama The start of a closing is inherently dramatic. Jurors will sit up and listen. Grab their attention. Remember the concepts of Primacy (what they hear first they are more likely to remember, Recency (what they hear last they are more likely to remember), and use catchy phrases or themes. Remember Fox News.

2. Quotes and Humor Mine the collective works of Google. Here’s a few:

“To a man with a hammer, everything looks like a nail.”

—Mark Twain

“A lie that is half-truth is the darkestof all lies.”

—Tennyson, Alfred

“There are some things one remembers even though they may never have happened.”

—Harold Pinter, Old Times

“Son, always tell the truth. Then you’ll never have to remember what you said the last time.”

—Sam Rayburn

A little bit of humor, when appropriate and not mocking of the prosecution or witnesses, opens the soul, brings you and the jury closer together. Do not underestimate that, when appropriate, humor can be very effective. Self-deprecating humor can be especially effective.

3. Stories The use of stories and parables in the course of your narrative simplifies your theme. In this endeavor,Jury Argument in Criminal Casesby Ray Moses is indispensable; it is filled with specimens of narrative brilliance. Make the story personal to you, and if possible self-deprecating ones can be very effective if relevant to your theme. The possibilities are endless, and they don’t have to be true! Just make up an event and tell it like it’s true.

Tell a good story about how you mistook someone for your wife/husband at a dark bar in an identification case.

Tell a good story about how your daughter got caught in a lie in a child sex case.

Tell a good story about how your mom and your aunt have completely different recollections of an important childhood event—which is ever so helpful because neither one is lying, both are equally adamant that they are right, and you were stuck on who to believe more likely than not, let alone beyond a reasonable doubt.

4. Humanizing the Defendant Throughout the entire trial your client is never “my client,” “the defendant” or “the accused,” but Mr. or Mrs. So-and-so—unless you are making a specific point (such as “Mr. So-and-so is only the accused of crimes, where are the facts?”). As you tell the story of your client throughout the trial, connect with him or her in closing, too. Look at your client and show genuine concern and affection. The jury will see it, and if they jury likes you and sees that you are not afraid of your client, they will be open to seeing your client as a human being, and it’s a lot harder for a jury to convict a human being than an anonymous “defendant.”

In the middle of one jury trial, a now-retired judge criticized me for putting my arm around and touching my client, saying he thought it made the jury uncomfortable. Really? How uncomfortable was the judge when my client, who had impeachable priors and testified at trial, was acquitted of robbing and assaulting his crippled girlfriend?

How much will it harm your client if you sit as far away from them at counsel table as possible, never interact, and act like you don’t care for them at all but just technicalities? Jurors are human, let their hearts lead their heads to the right verdict.

5. Empower the Jury Guilty verdicts are routine and boring. Acquittals show that the ultimate power resides not with the government, not with the police, not with the court, but with the jury. Jurors have real power, however, and they need to use it to work for the betterment of humankind.

6. Demonstrative Evidence/PowerPoint Use visual aids and make them BIG ENOUGH to be seen by every juror. If there is a list of facts that you want the jury to focus on write them out in BIG letters. Ask the jurors who are taking notes to write down what you are printing on the easel. That way, an organized and concise list of facts that establish reasonable doubt will be in their hands when they go into the jury room. The fact that the jury reads what you write, then writes it down themselves, will place those facts in the forefront of their memory better than expecting the jurors to extract the material thrusts of your closing from your narrative. LARGE, LEGIBLE TEXT respects the jury.

Markers — bring your own to every trial and make sure they have plenty of ink. At art supply stores you can get markers that are refillable for a small investment. Lawyers look disorganized at the easel when they search for markers that aren’t dried out and look stupid if they blame the staff for poor supplies.

PowerPoint works. Who cares if the jury might think you’re slick. They are going to know that you care enough to be prepared, and a prepared closing is more effective, more organized, and more powerful. And when you use PowerPoint, you may be able to drop the notepad altogether since your outline is right there in front of you.

Moreover, if the prosecutor uses visual aids and they make any mistakes, pound them for it. Early in my practice a prosecutor hand wrote the entire jury instruction for Resisting Arrest and dropped the word “clearly” from “behavior clearly intended to prevent being taken into custody.” Pound their mistakes home.

7. Analysis of the Evidence Of course, but it can be overdone. Often, offering a few examples are more effective than an overbearing, droning analysis of every nit. But the jury is going to be looking for substance in addition to style.

F. Specific Topics

1. Thanking the Jury, the Court, and Opposing Counsel Many seasoned lawyers believe that any competent closing includes brief but sincere courtesies to the court, opposing counsel, court staff and the jury, without whom the system cannot function. It cannot hurt to remind the jury that you appreciate their service, the vital civic function that they perform, the sacrifices they, their families, and their employers have made, and remind them that their decision, rendered on the jury verdict form, will live in the courts forever, long after the participants are gone.

2. Presumption of Innocence The “presumption of innocence” must be emphasized. Jacob Stein gave a fabulous example of putting flesh on the concept of reasonable doubt when he called it the “state’s first witness”:

I take it that presumption is not some idle sound. I understand it to mean a vital, a living, a real prospection. I do not mean it as a shelter for a man accused, I do not mean it as a retreat from which he must be bombarded by truth, but I mean it as a presumption…that innocence is the first witness that is ever introduced in every case where a human being is charged with an offense. It is a venerable witness… It stands before the accused when the indictment is read, and the charge is made. It lifts its hand above him, and as each witness speaks it pronounces the words “Not Guilty” over his head to all the world. It is a witness who no man has ever dared impeach. It is a venerable witness whose character no man has ever dared assail, and this witness has stood in all Anglo-Saxon countries, in all Anglo-Saxon tribunals, with his hand lifted above the accused, pronouncing the charges as slander, denouncing his accusers as falsifiers, until the evidence becomes so overwhelming that, bowed and dejected with the weight of shame, he is bound to make his way from the forum, and leave the accused exposed to the consequences of his conduct.

(Ray Moses, Jury Argument in Criminal Cases: A Trial Lawyer’s Guide (1996)).

3. Reasonable Doubt “Reasonable doubt,” its concept and its illumination, generally takes up at least five minutes of my closing argument. The fact that no one ever applies the standard of “beyond a reasonable doubt” to anything in one’s life, that we all survive without ever having to use such a standard in any decision we ever make, forces the jury to ruminate on just how high the standard really is, how permanent their verdict really is.

Think about it. Most people buy their house, their most important investment in their life, knowing that they can still sell it and probably not get dinged too badly—it’s reversible, but a conviction is not. We take medications merely because a doctor has told us that they are safe, yet we take it on faith even though there isn’t another doctor there to test their conclusion—we take it on faith. In criminal trials, there is no such thing as faith. There are only facts. More importantly, in most cases, the absence of facts is where we find reasonable doubt.

Besides, beyond a reasonable doubt is not a shield that protects just defendants, it also protects jurors so that when they make a permanent decision, one that your client will have to live with for the rest of their life, they can safely say with a clear conscience, the government just didn’t do enough.

How many reasonable doubts does the jury need, though? Just one, just one doubt, and sometimes I’ll create a list of all of the reasonable doubts they should have and rattle them off, one after another in short staccato fashion, a short pause between them, with so many that no way can a jury really believe that the government has carried its burden of proof, and hopefully giving at least every juror a different one (or two, or three…) so that when they walk into the jury room they see the mountain of doubt as insurmountable.

4. Burden of Proof A criminal trial is never a balancing of witness, or “Who are you going to believe,” yet there is a moral element to it, and that is an element that if your client is convicted they will be branded a criminal and their life will be tarnished, so the jury better believe it beyond a reasonable doubt down in the jury room and know that for the rest of their lives they will be living with this verdict and can never undo it once they leave the jury room. When the defense calls no witnesses, it is thought that it is easier for a jury to understand the burden of proof, but it is worth emphasizing often and convincingly what the burden is and who bears it. There are many ways to do this. Talk with colleagues and determine what works for you, within your personality and style.

5. Defendant’s Silence While it’s never proper for the prosecutor to comment on a defendant’s silence, see above, and a jury instruction helps the defense out in this regard, be aware that jurors often expect a defendant to testify and deny the charges against them—it’s human nature. Thus it’s rare that it’s enough to simply rely on the instruction.

Defense counsel should be prepared to address silence in closing and should do so in a way that makes sense in the context of the case and your client. You can tell the jury that your client has already spoken in many ways: 1) by pleading not guilty, 2) through counsel, 3) in their exculpatory statements to the police and actions that demonstrate silence, and 4) by remaining silent, thus telling the jury that the government’s case is weak.

If you anticipated your client not testifying, this probably should have been brought up in voir dire and perhaps opening, and maybe restate the promises that were made by jurors not to hold it against your client, or remind them that not all people could take the stand and whither the blistering attack of the prosecutor—and that is a solemn individual right. Some counsel can effectively wax eloquent regarding the solemn right to remain silent and the star chambers of the past. Whatever your tactic, it’s important to do something with silence in closing.

6. Confessions — the “So-Called Confession” If your client “confessed,” there is seldom a better tool in the defense lawyer reserve than Oregon’s wonderful jury instruction regarding “Defendant’s Statements”:

DEFENDANT’S STATEMENTS

When a witness testifies about statements made by the defendant, you should consider such testimony with caution.

In reviewing such testimony, you should consider, among other things, the following:

(1) Did the defendant make the statement, and, if so, did the defendant clearly express what [he/she] intended to say?

(2) Did the witness correctly hearand understandwhat the defendant said?

(3) Did the witness correctly rememberand relatewhat the defendant said?

(4) Did the witness intentionallyor mistakenly altersome of the words used by the defendant, thereby changing the meaningof what was actually said?

If, after weighing such factors, you conclude that the defendant said what [he/she] intended to say and that the witness to the statement correctly understood, remembered, and related to you what the defendant said, then you are authorized to consider such statements for what you deem them to be worth.

[Emphasis supplied.] This instruction, blown up on foam board or put into a PowerPoint, is tremendous and creates substantial fodder for counsel to recreate the setting of the “so-called confession” and explain why it’s simply “so-called” and not a confession. Look at all of the cautionary words—“caution,” “clearly,” “correctly,” “mistakenly” and “what you deem it to be worth”—enough to justify just about any theory why the confession is simply so-called in at least two or three different ways.

Furthermore, defense argument can be illuminated by occurrences from the trial where witnesses or counsel have misspoke “in the light of day and witnessed by the jury” (or even better from the prosecutor’s first closing), or illuminated in a self-deprecating story from your life where you made a mistake, or illuminated by contrasting the so-called confession, unrecorded, and differing from written contracts. Confessions are not the end of the world and you can win a case when one comes in, you just have to have a theory and a decent explanation.

7. Witness Credibility a. Attacking Witnesses — “Liar” v. “Mistaken”

It is rare that calling a witness a “liar” is effective and does not unwisely risk alienating the jury. When the evidence shows it, when you’ve really got the goods on a witness or a cop, you can do it forcefully, but you don’t have to. A subtle discussion of the lies, giving the jury an excuse for the witness, an understandable motive, can be very effective too. But if you are going to call someone a liar, you had better set forth the solid facts, because the stakes are high and so is the risk.

What is often the better tactic is to argue that the witness is mistaken, didn’t have the chance to form a clear memory, or that their memory has been unintentionally impacted. This happens to all of us in our lives in so many ways. You’re watching the Civil War game, you’re a Duck and your best friend is a Beaver. Close play in the end zone and do you agree with your best friend? Is either of you lying? While this example is trite and simple, there are many more ways to convey this exact notion to the jury with less risk then calling a witness a liar.

b. The Child Witness

Child witnesses can tear a case apart for a defense lawyer, especially in child sex cases, yet every person who has been near or close to a child knows that children, especially young ones, are easily manipulated and their memories influenced, often completely innocently and unintentionally as the child tries to “please” their parent, the prosecutor, or a police officer. It is appropriate to acknowledge the difficulties the jury will have in disbelieving a child and how they must certainly sympathize with them, but yet can safely tell the court that the government’s case did not meet the standards.

c. Responding to the Prosecutor’s First Closing

Often prosecutors focus only on limited aspects of witnesses’ testimony, i.e. that which supports their theory, and ignore the inconsistencies between witnesses and in the witnesses’ own statements. Point that out, how the prosecutor wants to put a gloss on their case, but how just in their case alone there is reasonable doubt.

Some experienced counsel never respond to a prosecutor’s first closing, thinking that only gives the prosecutor’s arguments power, showing what you’re concerned about. Others feel it necessary to dissect the prosecutor’s first closing, so that there is little for the prosecutor to say in their final closing that cannot be anticipated by the jury. What’s right in your case depends on your case, your prosecutor, your client, and your theory of defense. There is no right or wrong.

d. Anticipating the Prosecutor’s Final Closing

Again, there is no right or wrong, but some experienced lawyers tell the jury the reason the prosecutor gets two arguments is because the burden of proof is so high, eliminating all reasonable doubt—although I don’t think this argument gets too much traction.

Yet it cannot hurt to remind the jurors that you have no sure rebuttal, and ask them when they listen to the prosecutor’s rebuttal to carefully ask themselves: “What would defense counsel say in reply?” or “What evidence contradicts the prosecutor’s argument?”

e. Missing Evidence, Missing Witnesses

Arguing missing evidence and missing witnesses may open the door to the prosecutor arguing, “Well, they were equally available to both parties.”

But jurors, based on the circumstances, may know that this is not practical, especially when the witnesses really are more allied with the prosecution, and when they were interviewed by the prosecutor or the police. It’s still their damn burden—let the jurors know it.

Editor’s Note: I have included this outline for two reasons. First, the vouching issue arises in almost every trial. This document will help you form your objection for any circumstance where the prosecution attempts to insert personality over facts. Second, this is a fine, fine outline. As you develop your case, you should outline similarly for all issues that might arise in your trial. Being able to stand up and click out the law is impressive and gives the court confidence you are right. Be prepared.

I. Vouching Defined and Why It Is Prohibited

A. Defined

Vouching includes many kinds of improper conduct, but generally falls into five categories:

1. Providing personal opinion or assurances of the prosecutor.

2. Implying that only the government knows, or can know, the truth.

3. Implying that government has out-of-court support for its case.

4. Aligning prosecutorial case with authority of the court.

5. Plea agreements – with or without polygraphs conditions.

Each of these is illustrated in turn.

1. Providing Personal Opinion or Assurances of the Prosecutor • Improper to communicate personal opinion, even by implication, that prosecutor could or did verify truthfulness of witness. United States v. Brooks,508 F3d 1205, 1210 (9th Cir. 2007).

• Suggestion that senior government attorneys (or investigators) believed there was probable cause to issue wiretap warrant, or believed that defendant was guilty. United States v. Brooks, supra, at 1210 (nor is testimony as to court’s authorization of wiretap warrant proper, not part of foundation for recorded statements; Id., at 1211.

• Prosecutor’s assertion of fact that “life sentence” never really means life sentence absolutely improper, reversible error where personal opinion compounded by falsity of statement. Sechrest v. Ignacio, 549 F3d 789, 810 (9th Cir. 2008).

• Prosecutor’s false statement that “life sentence” never really means life sentence was flagrant misconduct, in part because personal opinion was enhanced by reference to his professional position. Sechrest v. Ignacio,supra, at 810.

• Stating, “I can’t prosecute a case unless I believe defendant is guilty, and charges true” is improper vouching. Cheney v. Washington, 614 F3d 987, 992 (9th Cir. 2010) (denial of habeas review, adequacy of representation in question).

• Improper vouching for prosecutor to refer to his own experience, saying he’s seen all elements of this defense used before by guilty defendants. United States v. Wright, 625 F3d 583 (9th Cir. 2010).

• Improper vouching to offer own opinion whether one should have child pornography on one’s own computer, as well as stating the defense is “remarkable.” United States v. Wright,supra, at 610–11.

• Improper vouching for prosecutor to say “I think” as to issues in question during argument. United States v. Wright,supra, at 611.

• Personal opinions of government prosecutor are particularly harmful. United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 1146 (9th Cir. 2005):

Vouching of that sort is dangerous precisely because a jury may be inclined to give weight to the prosecutor’s opinion in assessing the credibility of witnesses, instead of making the independent judgment of credibility to which the defendant is entitled.

There is no legitimate basis for a prosecutor’s “flat-out statement of his or her personal opinion as to witness credibility in a system that, like ours, vests the sole determination regarding such credibility (like all other factual determinations) with the jury.”

Id.,at 1148.

• Prosecutor’s vouching with personal opinion of guilt was improper, despite defense counsel’s argument. United States v. Young, 470 US 1, 17 (1985) (prosecutor’s statement of his “personal impressions” of defendant’s guilt was an improper response to defense counsel’s improper argument).

• Personal opinion of culpability, relative culpability of defendants, seriousness of crimes or moral culpability – all improper vouching. United States v. McKoy, 771 F2d 1207, 1211–12 (9th Cir. 1985):

But the prosecutor may not express his opinion of the seriousness of the defendant’s crimes before the jury, any more than he may express his opinion of the defendant’s guilt.... By expressing his opinion of the moral culpability, he may distort the jury’s decision on the question of the defendant’s guilt or innocence.

• Improper vouching to place prestige of the government behind a witness through personal assurances of that witness’ veracity. United States v. Necoechea, 986 F2d 1273, 1276 (9th Cir. 1993).

• Improper vouching for prosecutor to give his personal opinion of defendant’s guilt. United States v. McKoy,supra, at 1211. ''Also see: United States v. Necoechea,supra.

• Improper vouching for prosecutor to give his personal opinion of credibility of government’s witnesses. United States v. McKoy,supra, at 1211. ''Also see: United States v. Necoechea,supra.

• Assertions of personal knowledge by prosecutor weighted with substantial prejudice. Berger v. United States, 295 US 78, 88 (1935):

It is fair to say that the average jury, in a greater or less degree, has confidence that these obligations, which so plainly rest upon the prosecuting attorney, will be faithfully observed. Consequently, improper suggestions, insinuations, and especially, assertions of personal knowledge are apt to carry much weight against the accused when they should properly carry none. (88)

• Improper for prosecutor to provide evidence, [through testimony, here] that prosecution believed defendant the guiltiest of all defendants, that the cooperating informants were less guilty, or that government had very strong case. United States v. McKoy,supra, at 1211–12.

• Personal appraisal of witness’ credibility is improper vouching. State v. Parker, 235 Or 366, 377, 384 P2d 986 (1963), reversed on other grounds,385 US 363 (1966), improper vouching for prosecutor to state:

In this particular case this Bostwick girl was telling the truth. We wouldn’t have her if she wasn’t. It is impossible for her to have been lying. it is impossible for her to have told that story.

''Also see: United States v. Necoechea, 986 F2d 1273, 1276 (9th Cir. 1993).

• Prosecutor portraying self as one of team conducting the criminal investigation is improper vouching. Prosecutor’s use of terms “we” and “us” are improper where such a conclusion can be drawn from them. United States v. Hermanek, 289 F3d 1076, 1098–99 (9th Cir. 2002).

• Informing jury that prosecutor involved in discovery of evidence is improper vouching. “When a prosecutor is personally involved in the discovery of a critical piece of evidence [and] when that fact is made evident to the jury...the prosecutor’s participation in the trial of the defendant constitutes a form of improper vouching.” United States v. Hermanek,supra, at 1098–99.

• Prosecutor stating his own interpretation of evidence is vouching. In absence of testimony on this particular point: “The only drug that you’d rock up nine of, sack up in powder and cook is cocaine... This conversation is indisputably about cocaine.” United States v. Hermanek,supra, at 1100–01, noting that this could have been construed as the prosecutor’s own expert testimony.

• Improper vouching for prosecutor to place prestige of the government behind the witness, by providing personal assurances of credibility. United States v. Kerr, 981 F2d 1050, 1053 (9th Cir. 1992).

• Improper vouching for prosecutor to tell jury his individual impressions of the evidence. United States v. Kerr,supra, at 1053:

Because he is the sovereign’s representative, the jury may be misled into thinking his conclusions have been validated by the government’s investigatory apparatus.

• Test for [one type of] vouching: whether jury could reasonably believe that prosecutor was indicating a personal belief in the witness credibility. United States v. Roberts, 618 F2d 530, 537 (9th Cir. 1980).

2. Implying that Only the Government Knows, or Can Know, the Truth • Vouching to claim that prosecution pursues only those it believes are guilty.State v. Cheney, 171 Or App 401, 404; 16 P3d 1164, 1165 (2000):

Prosecutor stated during closing: “I cannot just advocate for whatever my client tells me to say. And whether it’s true or not. I cannot get up here and say that. My job, by law, is I – I can only advocate for cases where I believe that it’s true, where I believe that it happened. If I think it’s a close case, if I think it’s a case I could win, and I still don’t feel good about it, I’m required by law not to go through with it. That’s a very different job than [defense counsel] has. Very different. And what is the job of the police in this particular case? What did they tell you? There are many cases where we do not recommend prosecution. There are many cases that we find unfounded and we don’t go ahead with those. And it’s only on true cases that we are required to recommend prosecution.”

“At the outset, we emphasize that we do not endorse or condone the prosecutor’s ‘vouching’ remarks. Those remarks went well beyond the bounds of proper argument.”

4.Aligning Prosecutorial Case With Authority of Court – an Especially Egregious Form of Vouching 

• Of several kinds of vouching in this case, most serious was implying, through witness questioning, that government and courts monitored defendant’s conduct, through issuance of court-approved wiretap, and both affirmed his guilt.United States v. Brooks, 508 F3d 1205, 1212 (9th Cir. 2007).

• Invoking authority of court especially egregious form of vouching. United States v. Kerr, 981 F2d 1050, 1053 (9th Cir. 1992):

A prosecutor must not abuse his position and his duty to see justice done by invoking the authority of the court. “That particular form of vouching goes beyond the mere proffer of an institutional warranty of truthfulness; rather, it casts the court as an active, albeit silent, partner in the prosecutorial enterprise.” [Cit. omitted.]

• Plain error where vouching was personal, and on behalf of the court.United States v. Smith, 962 F2d 923, 933 (9th Cir. 1992). Prosecutor, in closing, stated that the cooperating witness could not “get up here and say whatever he wanted to say,” or he would be prosecuted for perjury. He also said that his job was not to seek a conviction but rather to guarantee a fair trial and turn over any favorable evidence to the defense. Later, he said that the government’s job is to ferret through all the smoke screens and lead you to the truth. Finally, he said, “If I did anything wrong in this trial I wouldn’t be here. The court wouldn’t allow that to happen.”) Id., at 933–34.

• Prosecutor may not abuse position and his obligation to see justice done by imputing such satisfaction to the court.United States v. Smith,supra. Prosecutor, in closing, stated, “If I did anything wrong in this trial I wouldn’t be here. The court wouldn’t allow that to happen.” Id., at 933–34.

• Invoking court is more serious violation, qualitatively different from other types of vouching. Id.,at 936.

• “We simply cannot permit” prosecutor placing the imprimatur of the judicial system itself on witness’ credibility. Id., at 936.

• Implying that court would determine truthfulness of witness’ testimony (prior to her sentencing) is vouching. United States v. Ortiz, 362 F3d 1274, 1278–79 (9th Cir. 2004). The vice is in the implication that the court, as well as law enforcement can, has and will monitor truthfulness of witness.

Here, witness suggested that court monitors veracity by ultimately deciding whether to grant Rule 35 motion if witness tells the truth. And also in mentioning that witness received Rule 35 reduction suggested that court had previously determined that testimony was indeed true.

5. Plea Agreements—With or Without Polygraph Conditions a. Plea Agreements With Polygraph Conditions

• Potentially reversible error to introduce plea agreement with polygraph condition: same danger as vouching. There is a danger that a jury might give evidence of passing a polygraph examination great weight and even view it as a complete answer to the question of the credibility of a witness. This type of evidence is inherently prejudicial. State v. Middleton, 668 P2d 371, 295 Or 485, 492 (1983) (but harmless error where defendant admitted act, claimed diminished responsibility, and witness in question did not significantly contradict defendant).

• Polygraph mention improper bolsters witness credibility, implying guarantee of veracity.State v. Snider, 674 P2d 585, 296 Or 168, 172 (1983):

Evidence of a plea agreement containing a provision that the state’s witness must take and pass a polygraph examination to verify trial testimony constitutes improper bolstering of the witness’s credibility.

The vice of such evidence is that a jury might give special credence to the testimony of the state’s witness because of the binding force of the plea bargain, implying a guarantee of the witness’s veracity.

The jury might well infer that the contract contained a mechanism, to wit, the polygraph examination, to keep the witness honest.

Given the awesome powers which the prosecution may wield in order to insure veracity of its witnesses’ statements, we strongly condemn this superfluous practice. Prosecutors who introduce such evidence in the future to obtain convictions should expert reversal.

(Cit. omit.) Id., 296 Or 173.

• Even where requirement to take polygraph is prospective, improper vouching to introduce plea agreement containing polygraph condition. State v. Snider,supra, at 171.

• Polygraph may not be used to bolster credibility of witness. United States v. Brown, 720 F2d 1059 (9th Cir. 1983):

Our unbroken history of rejection of polygraph evidence when offered for its substantive value buttresses our holding here that admission of the polygraph recitals was error, and that the use made of them by the Government constituted impermissible vouching for the credibility of its witnesses. [720 F2d at 1072.]

We hold that it was improper for the court to allow the Government to argue, and the jury to hear, read, and infer, that the threat of exposure of any falsity by means of such an instrument assured the accuracy of the Government’s key witnesses.

Id.,at 1073.

Note:Polygraph results are not admissible in state or federal courts, generally. Object to any reference to them which is or may be harmful to your client on substantive grounds as well.

B. Rationale for Rule Against Vouching

Introduction: There are several reasons why vouching is prohibited, based on both the great disparity in power between the defendant and the state, and upon the inherent ethical demand that the state pursue justice through just means.

• Reasons why vouching is improper. United States v. McKoy, 771 F2d 1207, 1211 (9th Cir. 1985):

Presumption of “outside” evidence:Jury might conclude that evidence not produced at trial confirms prosecutor’s opinion;

Implied expert knowledge:Jury could construe statements as opinion of “expert testimony” based on his personal knowledge and prior experience in other cases;

Jury may yield judgment to prosecution:Jury may be inclined to give weight to the prosecutor’s opinion in assessing the credibility of witnesses, instead of making the independent judgment of credibility to which the defendant is entitled.

• Vouching is dangerous to independence of jury deliberations. Vouching is dangerous because the jury may be inclined to give weight to a prosecutor’s opinion instead of rendering their own independent judgment of witness credibility to which the defendant is entitled. United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 1147 (9th Cir. 2005). Or it may induce the jury to trust the government’s judgment on how to analyze the evidence, rather than trusting its own view. Id., at 1147.

• Danger of prosecutorial vouching. United States v. Young, 470 US 1, 18–19 (1985) (prosecutor’s vouching for the credibility of his witness, and expressing personal opinion concerning guilt of the accused carries two dangers: (1) such comments can convey the impression that evidence not before the jury supports the charges against the defendant, thus jeopardizing the defendant’s right to be tried solely on the basis of evidence before the jury, and (2) it carries the imprimatur of the government and may induce the jury to trust the government’s judgment rather than its own).

• Government, as a sovereign, is obligated to provide fair trial, to do justice, and has a duty to refrain from improper methods calculated to produce a wrongful conviction. Berger v. United States, 295 US 78, 88 (1935):

The United States Attorney is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty whose obligation to govern impartially is as compelling as its obligation to govern at all; and whose interest, therefore, in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done. As such, he is in a peculiar and very definite sense the servant of the law, the twofold aim of which is that guilt shall not escape or innocense suffer. He may prosecute with earnestness and vigor – indeed, he should do so. But, while he may strike hard blows, he is not at liberty to strike foul ones. It is as much his duty to refrain from improper methods calculated to produce a wrongful conviction as it is to use every legitimate means to bring about a just one.

• Use of FBI agent’s testimony is very powerful form of vouching; danger is jury might identify prosecution’s case with the integrity of the United States.United States v. Rudberg, 122 F3d 1199, 1204 (1997) (“...together with vouching through testimony which included the testimony of a very experienced FBI agent, a person whose position the jury might easily identify with the integrity of the United States, presents vouching in a very powerful form”).

C. What Vouching Is Not

Many kinds of misconduct may be objectionable on other grounds, but are not vouching, such as the following:

1. Circumstantial buttressing of credibility of another witness through factual corroboration is not impermissible vouching. Alcazar v. Hill, 195 Or App 502, 98 P3d 1121, 1125 (2004). Witnesses often bolster the credibility of their witnesses indirectly and implicitly through the presentation of corroborating evidence. This is not impermissible vouching.

2. Argument from which jury may infer that an attorney believes his/her witnesses, without providing personal opinion or other form of vouching, is permissible.State v. Charboneau, 323 Or 38, 48; 913 P2d 308, 314 (1996) (evidence will often tend to show that a witness is or is not telling the truth; likewise, it is permissible for the jury to infer – from argument that the jury should find facts in accordance with that witness’ testimony – that an attorney believes his/her witnesses). Bockting v. Bayer, 399 F3d 1010, 1039 (9th Cir. 2005) (stating that witness’ testimony was unembellished, straightforward, and candid).

3. Improper, tasteless or inappropriate comments; (while these may not be vouching they may provide grounds for mistrial if they result in a denial of the right to a fair trial). State v. Bockorny, 124 Or App 585, 594; 863 P2d 1296, 1301 (1993).

4. “I submit witness is lying” is not vouchingas long as no personal assurance is made.

United States v. Necoechea, 986 F2d 1273, 1279 (9th Cir. 1993).

5. Arguing inferences from evidence, if fair, not vouching. United States v. Necoechea,supra, at 1279; Bockting v. Bayer,supra, at 1040 (not vouching to ask jury to find complainant credible based on evidence, or to argue one side is lying); United States v. Gooch, 506 F3d 1156, 1160 (9th Cir. 2007) (not vouching to refer only to evidence in record and ask jury to assess witness’ credibility).

6. Prosecutor has some latitude arguing reasonable inferences.United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 1147 (9th Cir. 2005).

7. Testimony as to demeanor of detective is not vouching.Alcazar v. Hill, 195 Or App 502, 98 P3d 1121, 1125. After defense claimed that interrogating detective improperly pressured defendant, witness called by state simply recounted what she heard and observed as to the detective’s demeanor, as any other witness under subpoena would be obliged to do.

8. Perjury case, where materiality is relevant, not vouching to call [civil] prosecutor in criminal case to testify as to materiality. United States v. McKenna, 327 F3d 830, 842–43 (9th Cir. 2003). Court rejected defense argument that it was up to the jury to decide materiality, so therefore it was vouching to call civil AUSA to testify how false statements affected, and were material in, civil proceeding. Court emphasizes that, as civil AUSA, this witness testified only as a witness with personal knowledge of the statements, in light of factual issues at stake in the civil litigation. Materiality may be shown by use of transcripts, testimony from a member of the grand jury or jury, or through witness summaries as well. Id. 842–43.

9. Not vouching for defense to use witness’s plea agreement to attack witness credibility. United States v. Schoneberg, 396 F3d 1036, 1043 fn 13 (9th Cir. 2004).

10. Tangential comments that “I think” not vouching.Bockting v. Bayer, 399 F3d 1010, 1039 (9th Cir. 2005) (stating that witness “testified as candidly as I think is possible”). United States v. Inzunza, 580 F3d 894 (9th Cir. 2009.

11. Quoting Dept. of Justice motto that “The United States wins when justice is done” is not vouching, when in response to defense argument that case was politically motivated, or prosecution abusive. United States v. Inzunza, 580 F3d 894, 910 (9th Cir. 2009).

12. Statement “we know” may or may not be vouching.Not vouching when statement, “We know that case was delivered,” was made only to argue inferences from evidence. United States v. Inzunza, supra, at 910–11; but “we know” is generally improper form of vouching. United States v. Younger, 398 F3d 1179, 1191 (9th Cir. 2005).

13. Expert testimony which conflicts with defense witness testimony, from which conclusion that they are lying could be drawn, is not vouching.United States v. Moran, 493 F3d 1002, 1009 (9th Cir. 2007) (but here, experts never referred to a single witness or discussed the veracity of witnesses, his conclusion was merely at odds with the defense case).

D. Vouching Can Appear Anywhere In, Or Even Before, Trial

1. Argument, the most common form.''See, e.g. Bockting v. Bayer, 399 F3d 1010, 1039 (9th Cir. 2005); United States v. Roberts, 618 F2d 530, 534 (9th Cir. 1980).

2. During questioning.United States v. Combs, 379 F3d 564, 574 (9th Cir. 2004).Even on redirect examination, after the defense has attacked the witnesses credibility on cross-exam-ination, the prosecutor’s asking a witness to agree that his plea agreement required him to tell the truth is still vouching. United States v. Brooks, 508 F3d 1205, 1210 (9th Cir. 2007). But in that context, the court’s willingness to find impropriety, or to assign any consequences to any impropriety, are lessened. See, e.g. United States v. Inzunza, 580 F3d 894, 910 (9th Cir. 2009) (defense cross-examination resulted in only “mild” vouching on redirect, not plain error).

3. When elicited during testimony. United States v. McKoy, 771 F2d 1207, 1211 (9th Cir. 1985) (prosecutor called another AUSA to stand to testify as to his “state of mind” in negotiating plea agreement with cooperating witness, which AUSA then testified how strong he believed the government’s case to be, ad nauseam).

Note: When vouching comes in as evidence, prejudice greatly increased. United States v. McKoy,supra, at 1213. In that case, the prosecutor’s improper remarks from the witness stand were presented to the jury as sworn testimony. “In sum, we believe that in this case prejudice to the accused is so highly probably that we are not justified in assuming its nonexistence.” Citing Berger v. United States, 295 US 78, 89 (1935).

4. Vouching can be disguised as a question.In United States v. Sarkisian, 197 F3d 966, 989 (9th Cir. 1999), the prosecutor asked on closing argument: “And somebody in the defense group said that the Government…whined to Mr. Bedrik that they didn’t have enough evidence on Mr. Bugriev, and he had to help them out. Not only have you seen the witnesses on the stand day after day, but you’ve seen the lawyers in the courtroom day after day. Do you really think Mr. Bender or myself would have such a conversation with a witness?” Court held that this question in effect asked the jury to rely on prosecutor’s credibility, and consequently on the prestige of the United States, in bolstering Bedrik’s testimony. [Emphasis added.] Id., 989–90.

5. In documents, such as plea agreements.State v. Charboneau, 323 Or 38, 48; 913 P2d 308, 314 (1996).

6. Even by judicial action: judicial reliance on off-record “facts” about prosecutorial motive, held to be vouching, conviction reversed. In State v. Ross, 154 Or App 121, 961 P2d 241 (1998), the trial court rejected African-American defendant’s Batson objection to jury selection. Trial court relied in part on its personal knowledge of the prosecutor in having race-neutral reason to challenge jurors. Reversed, finding that trial court vouched for credibility of prosecutor:

It is apparent that the trial court did not confine its credibility fact finding role to the voir dire proceeding. Rather, it relied, in part, on its personal knowledge about the prosecutor...we cannot affirm a finding based on a court’s personal, out-of-court, off-the-record knowledge about the prosecutor. To do so injects into the decision-making process an improper component because the trial court acted both as a character witness, vouching for the credibility of the prosecutor, and as the fact finder.

154 Or App at 128.

7. Possibly even in grand jury instructions.In United States v. Navarro-Vargas, 408 F3d 1184 (9th Cir. 2005), the court examined the propriety of the grand jury charge that “If past experience is any indication of what to expect in the future, then you can expect candor, honesty, and good faith in matters presented by the government attorneys.” The defense claimed this improperly vouched for the prosecution at the grand jury, but the court found the instruction not to be improper, given the other instructions stressing the independence of the grand jury. Id., at 1187.

8. Even at voir dire. Sechrest v. Ignacio, 549 F3d 789, 808 (9th Cir. 2008) (statements made by prosecutor during voir dire formed part of extensive vouching, aggravated by falsity of statements, resulting in reversible error).

E. Ethical Basis: Oregon Rules of Professional Conduct (ORPC) and Case Law

Introduction: Case law goes beyond the narrower proscriptions of the ORCP, due to constitutional rights to due process and a fair trial as reflected in the case law.

1. Oregon Rules of Professional Conduct Introduction:The ORPC and case law are based on overlapping concerns. When making an objection, cite any ethical violation, as well as case law and constitutional considerations (be sure to federalize constitutional objections!). Citing the ethical rule violated by the particular form of vouching in question provides the court with an unequivocal basis to provide an immediate remedy.

Rule 3.4Fairness to Opposing Party and Counsel

A lawyer shall not:

(e) in trial, allude to any matter that the lawyer does not reasonably believe is relevant or that will not be supported by admissible evidence, assert personal knowledge of facts in issue except when testifying as a witness, or state a personal opinion as to the justness of a cause, the credibility of a witness, the culpability of a civil litigant or the guilt or innocence of an accused.

Rule 3.5Impartiality and Decorum of the Tribunal

A lawyer shall not:

(a) seek to influence a judge, juror, prospective juror or other official by means prohibited by law.

Rule 8.4Misconduct

(a) It is professional misconduct for a lawyer to:

(1) violate the Rules of Professional Conduct...

(4) engage in conduct that is prejudicial to the administration of justice.

Rule 3.7.Lawyer as Witness

(a) A lawyer shall not act as an advocate at a trial in which the lawyer is likely to be a witness on behalf of the lawyer’s client.

2. Case Law on Ethics • Advocate-witness rule – attorney may not appear as both a witness and advocate in the same litigation – forms part of basis of vouching prohibition. United States v. Prantil, 764 F2d 548, 552-53 (9th Cir. 1985). ''Compare withORPC 3.7(a).

• Ethical underpinning, requirement of high standard of conduct of prosecutor: Berger v. United States, 295 U.S. 78, 88 (1935):

The United States Attorney is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty whose obligation to govern impartially is as compelling as its obligation to govern at all; and whose interest, therefore, in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done. As such, he is in a peculiar and very definite sense the servant of the law, the twofold aim of which is that guilt shall not escape or innocense suffer. He may prosecute with earnestness and vigor – indeed, he should do so. But, while he may strike hard blows, he is not at liberty to strike foul ones. It is as much his duty to refrain from improper methods calculated to produce a wrongful conviction as it is to use every legitimate means to bring about a just one.

It is fair to say that the average jury, in a greater or less degree, has confidence that these obligations, which so plainly rest upon the prosecuting attorney, will be faithfully observed. Consequently, improper suggestions, insinuations, and especially, assertions of personal knowledge are apt to carry much weight against the accused when they should properly carry none.

Id., at 88.

• Still the law 76 years later:United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 1148 (9th Cir. 2005), in the context of discussing the impropriety of a prosecutor asserting his personal belief as to witness credibility:

In that respect we stress that the ethical bar is set higher for the prosecutor than for the criminal defense lawyer–a proposition that has been clear for at least seven decades (see Berger v. United States, 295 US 78, 88, 55 S Ct 629, 79 L Ed 1314 (1935); and see also such cases as United States v. Modica,663 F2d 1173 (2d Cir. 1981) as well as the ABA Standards for Criminal Justice § 3-5.8(b)).

• Ethical basis: Prosecutor has special obligation to avoid improper suggestions, insinuations, and assertions of personal knowledge. United States v. Roberts, 618 F2d 530, 533 (9th Cir. 1980). Compare withORPC 3.4(e), 3.5(a).

• Prosecutor’s duty includes ensuring fair trial.State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336, 338; 479 P2d 240 (1971).

• Improper argument violates professional ethics. United States v. Young, 470 US 1, 7 (1985):

The line separating acceptable from improper advocacy is not easily drawn; there is often a gray zone. Prosecutors sometimes breach their duty to refrain from overzealous conduct by commenting on the defendant’s guilt and offering unsolicited personal views on the evidence. Accordingly, the legal profession, through its Codes of Professional Responsibility, and the federal courts, have tried to police prosecutorial misconduct.

Compare ORPC 3.4(e).

3. Ethical Proscriptions Against Vouching Apply to Both Parties...to Some Extent • Ethical obligations of proper argument apply to both parties. United States v. Young,supra:

It is clear that counsel on both sides of the table share a duty to confine arguments to the jury within proper bounds. Just as the conduct of prosecutors is circumscribed, the interests of society in the preservation of courtroom control by the judges are no more to be frustrated through unchecked improprieties by defenders. [Cit. omit.] Defense counsel, like the prosecutor, must refrain from interjecting personal beliefs into the presentation of his case. [Cits. omit.] Defense counsel, like his adversary, must not be permitted to make unfounded and inflammatory attacks on the opposing advocate.

A personal attack by the prosecutor on defense counsel is improper, and the duty to abstain from such attacks is obviously reciprocal.

Id., at 10. See ORPC 3.4(e).

• Rule against vouching restricts conduct of prosecution, not defense, in criminal case, one of the asymmetries between what prosecution and defense may do in a criminal trial. United States v. Schoneberg,supra. [Note, however, that this is in the context of backing up testimony with the integrity of the government. Personal vouching, by one’s personal opinion, is still improper for defense counsel. ORPC 3.4(e)].

Practice Tip

While it is true that ethical rules, including that prohibiting vouching, apply equally to both parties, vouching inflicts a substantially heavier prejudicial impact upon the defense than upon the prosecution. See cases above on rationale of vouching prohibition and the so-called “invited response” issue, infra.

• Defense use of witness’s plea agreement to attack witness credibility does not violate vouching rule. United States v. Schoneberg,supra, at 1043 fn 13 (9th Cir. 2004).

• Prosecution’s higher ethical bar is no excuse for defense to vouch, either. United States v. Weatherspoon, supra, at 1148: no lawyer should put his own credibility on the line by expressing his own opinion of witness credibility.

F. Related Issues Often Accompanying Vouching

The scope of this outline is prosecutorial vouching, but vouching can be equally damaging – and equally improper – when done by witnesses. A prosecutor who willingly engages in vouching may also violate other related rules of conduct, including those listed below.

1. Middleton rule that a trial witness cannot testify as to the credibility of another trial witness. State v. Charboneau,323 Or 38, 47; 913 P2d 308, 313 (1996):

In this case, no trial witness gave an opinion as to Smith’s credibility. Nonetheless, what happened here through the plea agreement is analogous. The state could not have called the investigating detective to testify: ”Based on Smith’s statements and on my investigation, I believe that the charge of felony murder accurately reflects the role Smith played in the crime. Moreover, I believe Smith’s representation that Largo’s death was primarily accomplished by others.”

2. Impropriety of asking one government agent whether another is telling truth in out-of-court statement. United States v. Sanchez, 176 F3d 1214, 1220 (9th Cir. 1999) (reversible error found). This adds the element of a de facto agent of the state vouching for another witness.

3. Prosecutorial misconduct to elicit opinion of defendant’s out-of-court statement to police. This infringes upon the jury’s right to make credibility determinations. United States v. Sanchez,supra, at 1220–21.

4. Wide variety of improper argument denigrating defense function.For example, in United States v. Wright, 625 F3d 583, 611 (9th Cir. 2010), the court found that while referring to the defense as “a sham” may not technically be vouching, it is improper.

5. Expert may not give opinion on credibility of another witness.State v. Milbradt, 305 Or. 621, 629, 756 P2d 620 (1988). The task of assessing credibility is for the fact finder alone, no matter how qualified a witness is as an expert: “We expressly hold that in Oregon a witness, expert or otherwise, may not give an opinion on whether he believes a witness is telling the truth. An opinion that a person is not deceptive, could not lie without being tripped up, and would not betray a friend (to wit: the defendant) is tantamount to the same thing.” [Subquotes omitted.]

6. Expert conclusions, even if only based on out-of-court credibility assessments, are improper vouching. State v. Lupoli, 234 P3d 117, 124; 348 Or 346, 361 (2010) finally dealt directly with the so-called “expert” testimony presented in so many of these cases, in which the “expert” diagnoses child sex abuse based upon the credibility of the child alone. Lupoli called this what it was: one witness vouching for the credibility of another, and reversed the convictions based on such testimony. It is important to note that the witness’ testimony need not bedirect'' vouching (“she was credible”); it is improper to allow a witness to testify to their own conclusions, which are based on their credibility assessment. Id., at 234 P3d 122; 348 Or 357.

See also: State v. Bainbridge, 241 P3d 1186, 238 Or App 56 (2010); State v. Davilia, 244 P3d 855, 239 Or App 468 (2010) (this case involved a non-jury trial, and rejected theCafarelli'' rule allowing appellate courts to ignore improperly admitted comments on witness credibility); State v. Cordova-Contreras, 245 P3d 147, 239 Or App 279 (2010).

II. Authority/Obligation of Court to Protect Defendant from Vouching

• Trial court has broad discretion in control of arguments. State v. Bockorny, 124 Or App 585, 594; 863 P2d 1296, 1301 (1993).

• Trial court has broad discretion to control argument, but its discretion is not unbounded. State v. Bolt, 108 Or App 746, 749; 817 P2d 1322, 1325 (1991).

• Discretion to control argument not unbounded: court has obligation to preserve right to fair trial. State v. Lundbom, 96 Or App 458, 461; 773 P2d 11 (1989), where prosecutor called defense attorney a “pimp” on closing, the court held that where (1) properly challenged, (2) if a remark was likely to influence the jury, the court’s failure to take action may be grounds for reversal.

• Court’s inaction can be a factor in degree of harm, appellate remedy.In Sechrest v. Ignacio, 549 F3d 789 (9th Cir. 2008), a case in which the prosecutor’s egregious vouching, by falsely repeating that a “true life” sentence in a homicide case would not really result in a “true life” sentence, the appellate court found reversible error, and noted the significance of the trial court’s inaction in the face of the false statements by the prosecutor. Id., at 811.

• Court’s failure to act on improper comments suggests approval.State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336, 339; 479 P2d 240 (1971) (improper comment by prosecutor on role of defense counsel). “The court’s inaction was tantamount to judicial approval of the statement.”

• Trial court has obligation to promptly deal with improper argument. United States v. Young, 470 US 1, 9 (1985) (the kind of advocacy shown by this record has no place in the administration of justice and should neither be permitted nor rewarded; a trial judge should deal promptly with any breach by either counsel).

We emphasize that the trial judge has the responsibility to maintain decorum in keeping with the nature of the proceeding: the judge is not a mere moderator, but is the governor of the trial for the purpose of assuring its proper conduct. The judge must meet situations as they arise and be able to cope with the contingencies inherent in the adversary process.

[Citations and related punctuation omitted.] Id., at 10.

• Trial court sometimes has obligation to stop improper comments without waiting for an objection. Viereck v. United States, 318 US 236 (1943). Here prosecutor argued, during WWII prosecution on charges of violation of Act requiring registration of foreign agents:

In closing, let me remind you, ladies and gentlemen, that this is war. This is war, harsh, cruel, murderous war. There are those who, right at this very moment, are plotting your death and my death; plotting our death and the death of our families because we have committed no other crime than that we do not agree with their ideas of persecution and concentration camps. This is war. It is a fight to the death. The American people are relying upon you, ladies and gentlemen, for their protection against this sort of a crime, just as much as they are relying upon the protection of the Men who man the guns in Bataan Peninsula, and everywhere else. They are relying upon you, ladies and gentlemen, for their protection. We are at war. You have a duty to perform here.

Id., at 248, fn 3.

The court held that the trial court should have stopped counsel’s discourse without waiting for an objection:

The United States Attorney is the representative not of an ordinary party to a controversy, but of a sovereignty whose obligation to govern impartially is as compelling as its obligation to govern at all, and whose interest, therefore, in a criminal prosecution is not that it shall win a case, but that justice shall be done. As such, he is in a peculiar and very definite sense the servant of the law, the twofold aim of which is that guilt shall not escape and innocence suffer. He may prosecute with earnestness and vigor – indeed, he should do so. But, while he may strike hard blows, he is not at liberty to strike foul ones. It is as much his duty to refrain from improper methods calculated to produce a wrongful conviction as it is to use every legitimate means to bring about a just one.” [Emphasis added.]

Citing Berger v. United States, 295 US 78, 88 (1935).'' Id., at 248.

• Serious prosecutorial misconduct not adequately addressed by sustaining objections and telling jury to disregard improprieties: stern rebuke, repressive measures, possibly mistrial may be called for.

Berger v. United States, 295 US 78, 84 (1935) (where prosecutor engaged in serious and repeated misconduct, court responded only by sustaining objections and telling jury to disregard improprieties):

But the situation was one which called for stern rebuke and repressive measures and, perhaps, if these were not successful, for the granting of a mistrial. It is impossible to say that the vile influence upon the jury of these acts of misconduct was removed by such mild judicial action as was taken.

• Trial court can also limit scope of testimony, e.g. instructing witness to limit testimony on truth-telling provision in plea agreement to avoid vouching.United States v. Schoneberg, 396 F3d 1036, 1043 fn 13 (9th Cir. 2004).

• Prejudice analysis – important in all cases – depends in part on trial court’s response to improper vouching. Factors include whether a curative instruction was given, and the strength of that instruction; whether the trial court corrected sua sponte the improper vouching; whether the court addressed the specific improper statements or gave a weak general instruction that attorney’s arguments are not evidence. United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 1150 (9th Cir. 2005).

III. Responding to Vouching at Trial

A. Pervasive Prejudice of Vouching

Introduction:The prejudice that flows from improper vouching is pervasive, having an effect far beyond the words or actions that constitute the vouching themselves. In arguing for a mistrial, or on appeal, be sure to note that case law recognizes that it is not only proper, it is necessary, to look at the potential, unseen impact of vouching. This is where you have to be creative.

1. Look not just to terms of statement, but to “inferences” resulting therefrom.State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336, 339; 479 P2d 240 (1971) (improper prosecutorial comment about role of defense counsel created “inference that the prosecutor believed the defendant was going to falsely manufacture and tailor a defense...”).

2. Improper “suggestions” and “insinuations” are at the heart of improper vouching.United States v. Kerr, 981 F2d 1050, 1053 (9th Cir. 1992). And in United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142 (9th Cir. 2005), the court found reversible error in the prosecutor’s statements that his witnesses would face legal and professional repercussions if they had lied. The court noted that the implication that such consequences would occur was sufficient, the prosecutor need not give firm assuranceof such negative results. [Emphasis added.] Id.at 1146.

3. Implication that government has “waiting in the wings” [not admitted] evidence which can prove truth of its witnesses is improper vouching. United States v. Brown, 720 F2d 1059, 1074 (9th Cir. 1983):

Admission in this case of the reference to the polygraph waiting in the wings, and the use in argument of its provisions combined to create errors which we believe infected the whole case.

But, taken as a whole, the plea-bargain agreement, like a covenant of faith, told them that the prosecution stood ready at all stages to bring the witnesses to account should they stray from the truth. It gave assurance not only that the witnesses were truthful when the prosecution decided to put them on the stand, but also that their revelations to the jury could be believed because of the threat of certain exposure.

Id.,at 1074.

4. Look to what is “intimated,” not merely to words themselves: United States v. Brown,supra:

[Re: polygraph] “It also opens the intimation that the Government knows that its witnesses have told and are telling the truth because otherwise they would not have been offered as witnesses. Further, the jury may be led to think that should their jury testimony later be found false even after leaving the witness stand, the Government surely would know and surely would act to purge any falsity from the record.”

720 F2d 1074.

And it was the imprimatur, as well as the direct assertion of support of the government for its witness’ credibility which was underlying basis for reversible error in United States v. Weatherspoon,supra, at 1147.

5. Look at how evidence could have been construed by the jury.Prosecutor argument to jury, in absence of any specific testimony on the point, that “The only drug that you’d rock up nine of, sack up in powder and cook is cocaine...This conversation is indisputably about cocaine.” In finding this improper vouching the court, in United States v. Hermanek, 289 F3d 1076, 1100(9th Cir. 2002), noted that this could have been construed as the prosecutor’s own expert testimony.

6. What jury could reasonably have understood prosecutor’s comments to mean is the damage of vouching, not just the words themselves. United States v. McKoy, 771 F2d 1207, 1211 (9th Cir. 1985):

In the context of this trial, the jury reasonably could have understood the former prosecutor’s testimony as an expression of his belief that the government witness, Greenmayer, was telling the truth and the defendant, McCoy, was lying.

7. Cumulative effect of statements to be analyzed.United States v. Brooks, 508 F3d 1205, 1212 (9th Cir. 2007); United States v. Smith, 962 F2d 923, 934 (9th Cir. 1992).

B. Curative Instruction Should Be Immediate, Specifically Address Vouching

• Standard end-of-trial jury instructions unlikely to neutralize prejudice of vouching. United States v. Combs, 379 F3d 564, 575 (9th Cir. 2004): Where standard jury instructions – (statements and arguments of counsel not evidence; jury was to decide case solely on evidence; credibility determination within jury’s province) – did not specifically address improper vouching, insufficient to cure damage of improper vouching. Simpson v. Coursey, 197 P3d 68, 73; 224 Or App 145, 155 (2008),rev den346 Or 184; 203 P3d 1058 (2009) (curative instruction must, at a minimum, tell the jury to disregard totally the improper testimony).

• But jury instruction to review with greater care testimony of informant, or that attorney arguments are not evidence, may dilute prejudice. United States v. Necoechea, 986 F2d 1273, 1280 (9th Cir. 1993).

• Curative instruction suffices if immediate curative instruction, only tangential impact of vouching. United States v. Sanchez, 944 F2d 497 (9th Cir. 1991). During closing, prosecutor stated, “That is the truth, ladies and gentlemen,...he told me about this.” Court interrupted, admonished government attorney and gave cautionary instruction about what prosecutor said. Again, during final instructions, court reiterated instruction against vouching. Comment held to be “tangential” to the evidence, curative instruction deemed sufficient.

• Beware judicial Catch-22: standard instructions may be sufficient where defense counsel does his/her job properly. In United States v. Necoechea,supra, the court found no reversible error where standard instructions were given, because error of improper vouching was found to be in part balanced when the credibility of the witness was “forcefully challenged at trial” by the defense. Id., at 1280–81.

Practice Tip

The Necoechea rule apparently means that where counsel does his/her job well, the “forceful challenge” to the witness’ credibility somehow lessens the adverse impact of improper vouching. This is absurd: if the issue arises in your case, argue that the “forceful challenge” to credibility actually increases the prejudice of vouching, because it elevates the importance of the witness credibility. Why? “Vouching is especially problematic in cases where the credibility of the witnesses is crucial.” United States v. Molina, 934 F2d 1440, 1445 (9th Cir. 1991). ''Also see: United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 1148 (9th Cir. 2005) (vouching more damaging in cases where credibility is critical issue).

• But some courts get it right:In Simpson v. Coursey, 197 P3d 68, 224 Or App 145 (2008)rev den346 Or 184; 203 P3d 1058 (2009), when the lead detective vouched for complainant’s credibility, saying she was “very honest” and “very straightforward,” and defense counsel only objected to the characterization of “honest” but made no motion to strike or for a curative instruction, the court found that the defendant’s rights to effective assistance of counsel and a fair trial [under Article 1, §11 Or. Const. and 6th Am. U.S. Const.] were violated. Id.,at 197 P3d 72; 224 Or App 152.

• At a minimum, move to strike, request curative instruction to disregard testimony, and consider moving for a mistrial. Simpson v. Coursey, supra, at 197 P3d 72, 224 Or App 153.

• Curative instruction may lessen prejudice and render mistrial unnecessary. In State v. Cheney, 171 Or App 401; 16 P3d 1164 (2000), the trial court did not order a mistrial, but gave a cautionary instruction. “Although such instructions do not inevitably remedy prejudice from improper argument, it is by no means ‘beyond dispute’ that the choice of a curative instruction in lieu of a mistrial was inadequate to remedy any prejudice, or, similarly, that the choice was beyond the bounds of reasonable discretion.” Cheney, 171 Or App at 412; 16 P3d at 1170.

• Not necessary to ask for curative instruction to preserve error.United States v. Roberts, 618 F2d 530, 534 (9th Cir. 1980) (court noted that it would have been helpful had defense counsel asked the trial court to give the jury a curative instruction, but such a request is not necessary when the error is brought to the court’s attention and curative action is clearly called for).

• Failure to seek curative instruction or mistrial not always error, where trial court actions make it obvious it is not disposed to grant either. In State v. Lundbom, 96 Or App 458, 462, 773 P2d 11 (1989), where the prosecution called defense counsel a “pimp,” defense counsel objected at least twice, the court overruling both. Court held that failure to request curative instruction or mistrial – note the word “or” – was not error, as the trial court’s responses to objections made it obvious that it was not disposed to grant either.

Practice Tip

Do you really want a “disregard that,” unring-the-bell curative instruction? If you’ve objected to serious misconduct, it is probably better to move for a mistrial, leaving the compromise of a curative instruction for the court to suggest, as most judges will readily do. If the judge comes up with the typical feckless instruction, demand something far stronger, arguing that the prejudice will not be eliminated with the judge’s weak proposal. Make your record! Remember, appellate court’s love curative instructions, and tend to accept even the worst of them.

• Curative instruction should mention the specific statement, and be timely.United States v. Kerr, 981 F2d 1050, 1054 (9th Cir. 1992), where vouching was egregious, invoking the authority of the court. Timely means immediate: State v. Shafer, 222 Or 230, 235; 351 P2d 941 (1960) “The time to move for a mistrial is when the allegedly prejudicial act occurs, not after the incident has been allowed to pass by, for then it is too late for the trial judge to caution the jury and mend the harm.”

• Curative jury instructions: must be immediate, deliver strong cautionary message, and address the particular statement. United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 1151 (9th Cir. 2005).

As for objections, some were overruled, those that were sustained did not produce any meaningful alternation of the prosecutor’s arguments, and the manner in which such objections were sustained unfortunately did not deliver the strong cautionary message....

• “Such failures to correct the improper statements at the time they were made cannot [be] salvaged by the later generalized jury instruction reminding jurors that a lawyer’s statements during closing argument do not constitute evidence.” (Emphasis added.) Weatherspoon, supra, at 1151.

The curative instructions offered here did not neutralize the harm of the improper statements because they did not mention the specific statements, and were not given immediately after the damage was done. [See citation.] Id., at 1151.

• More substantial measures than general credibility jury instructions are needed to cure misconduct where prosecutor invokes authority of the court. United States v. Kerr, 981 F2d 1050, 1053 (9th Cir. 1992).

• In cases where truthfulness provision in plea agreement is admitted, curative instruction to avoid vouching is advisable. United States v. Roberts, 618 F2d 530, 536 (9th Cir. 1980) (court should consider carefully the phrasing and content of the promise in the plea agreement and “decide whether an instruction to the jury would dispel any improper suggestions”).

C. Mistrial Motion

1. Sua Sponte Ordering of Mistrial

Practice Tip

Don’t hold your breath waiting for this to happen.

• What about court’s sua sponte obligation to order mistrial?Mistrials ordered sua sponteare disfavored because of the former jeopardy problems they may create. State v. Montez, 309 Or at 564, 601; 789 P2d 1352 (1990).

• Some case support, however, for trial court’s obligation to sua sponte order a mistrial. State v. Montez, 324 Or 343, 356–57, 927 P2d 64 (1996); State v. Simonsen, 329 Or 288, 300–01, 986 P2d 566 (1999), cited in State v. Cheney, 171 Or App 401, 409; 16 P3d 1164, 1168 (2000), referring to Montez: “The court implicitly assumed that a trial court’s failure to grant a mistrial sua sponte based on an improper argument could be reversible plain error – that is, that, in appropriate circumstances, the court could be obligated to declare a mistrial sua sponte.” By “appropriate circumstances,” Cheney meant when a prosecutor’s comments are so prejudicial as to deny a defendant a fair trial. Id.''

• Take the offensive and object to failure of the trial court to sua spontedeclare a mistrial. Simpson v. Coursey, 197 P3d 68, 70, fn. 1; 224 Or App 145, 149 fn. 1 (2008), noting that the trial court failed to sua sponte declare a mistrial or explicitly strike the vouching testimony and give a curative instruction, “nor did counsel object to the trial court’s failure to do so.”

• Court’s failure to correct prejudice may result in reversible error: In State v. Lundbom, 96 Or App 458, 461; 773 P2d 11 (1989), prosecutor called defense counsel a “pimp” on closing. Court held that the trial court’s failure to take appropriate action to counteract prejudice resulting from improper remarks likely to influence the jury could be grounds for reversal. The court’s actions in correcting improper vouching is one of the factors in determining the extent of prejudice resulting to the defendant. United States v. Weatherspoon,supra, at 1150.

• Influence on jury key consideration in whether trial court abuses discretion in allowing improper remarks. State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336, 479 P2d 240 (1971) (improper comment on role of defense counsel). “The test to be applied is whether the defendant was prejudiced by the conduct or remark, i.e., whether the jury was likely influenced thereby.”

2. Move for a Mistrial!

Practice Tip

When misconduct occurs, move for a mistrial, immediately, if you plan on ever doing so. Don’t wait for the court to step in and take corrective measures. Above all, don’t expect the Court of Appeals to do for your client what you failed to do.

That said, it is recognized that at times strategic considerations may suggest it is better not to ask for a mistrial (such as reliance on a surprise defense which won’t work on retrial, a key state witness on vacation during the trial, etc.)

• Mistrial motion must be made at time of objectionable comment, or issue lost. State v. Williams, 912 P2d 364, 322 Or 620, 630–31 (1996). Defense counsel interposed an immediate objection on comment improperly shifting burden of proof to defense. Then – likely in keeping with that particular judge’s practice – stated, “I will have a matter for the court when we recess.” Held: motion for mistrial not timely, claim of error not preserved for review, court declined to consider argument. ''See also: State v. Shafer, 222 Or 230, 235; 351 P2d 941 (1960). “The time to move for a mistrial is when the allegedly prejudicial act occurs, not after the incident has been allowed to pass by, for then it is too late for the trial judge to caution the jury and mend the harm.”

• Failure to immediately move for mistrial waives issue: where defense counsel later objected and moved for mistrial, appellate court held that the motion was untimely. State v. Cheney, 171 Or App 401, 407; 16 P3d 1164, 1167 (2000), citing State v. Williams, 322 Or 620, 630–31, 912 P2d 364 (1996), for proposition that “timely” means at the time the objectionable statement is made.

• Failure to move for mistrial, or untimely motion does not preserve alleged underlying error for review. State v. Cheney, 171 Or App 401, 407; 16 P3d 1164, 1167 (2000).

Practice Tip

Time-conscious trial courts are increasingly disinclined to send the jury out for what they consider a predictable motion (and denial), and routinely require counsel to delay mistrial motions until the next recess. Do not accede to such delays. Even if the trial court assures you that your issue will be preserved, there is no assurance appellate courts will not deem the issue waived. Remember, the court exists for your client’s benefit, not the other way around.

• Don’t leave appellate counsel with a bad record! A too-high percentage of appellate cases reviewing vouching issues are decided on the highly deferential “plain error” standard. ''See, e.g., United States v. Mohsen, 587 F3d 1028, 1033 (9th Cir. 2009); United States v. Harrison, 585 F3d 1155, 1158 (9th Cir. 2009); United States v. Inzunza, 580 F3d 894, 910 (9th Cir. 2009).

Practice Tip

When you move to strike, move for a curative instruction or move for a mistrial; state your reasons with as much detail as you can; also state the harm to your case caused by the vouching and why it cannot be corrected with a remedy less than the one you ask for. This will help the trial judge understand your reasoning and will allow the appellate court to understand the concerns from the viewpoint of the trial. Constitutionalize your objections as well, with brief citations to the applicable Oregon and United States constitutional provisions. If the judge cuts you off, insist on your right to create your appellate record.

• Even if you make the mistrial motion late, object to failure of the trial court to sua spontedeclare a mistrial, and state your reasons on the record. Simpson v. Coursey, 197 P3d 68, 70, fn. 1; 224 Or App 145, 149 fn. 1 (2008) (noting that the trial court failed to sua spontedeclare a mistrial or explicitly strike the vouching testimony and give a curative instruction, “nor did counsel object to the trial court’s failure to do so”).

D. Raise Prosecutorial Misconduct; Constitutional Issues Federal and State

Introduction:While beyond the scope of this outline, it is important to note that when objecting to prosecutorial vouching, it is also important to add specific objections for prosecutorial misconduct. Also state, on the record, both state and federal constitutional rights to a fair trial. Make all of these reasons the basis for your objection and motion for mistrial. E. Invited Response Doctrine; A Grand Misnomer Which Refuses to Die Quietly

Introduction:Prosecutors and some judges are of the opinion that if defense counsel argues improperly, that opens the door to responsive improper argument by the prosecution. Some even believe that attacks on credibility of government witnesses or a fierce challenge to the veracity of the government’s case justifies vouching. They invoke the phantasm of the “invited response” or “invited reply” doctrine. But there is no such animal, at least in the vouching context.

Even when the defense commits misconduct, the prosecution is never entitled to vouch or commit any other impropriety in response.The government’s remedy, rather, is to object and ask for a curative instruction or other judicial intervention at the time of the misconduct. Still, appellate courts occasionally use the term, however inappropriately, to suggest that the prosecution’s misconduct was in part excused by previous defense misconduct. The sole occasionin which defense misconduct may properly be part of aiding the prosecution’s cause is in the assessment of the proper remedy on appeal; it is proper to consider defense misconduct as part of the overall context of the case in assessing the prejudice of prosecutorial misconduct. Supreme Court precedent specifically prohibits consideration of defense misconduct as any sort of authorization of governmental misconduct, but it does consider the former in determining the probable impact on the jury of the latter.

But law doesn’t necessarily trump reality: despite these limitations imposed by the Supreme Court, lower courts will still tend to provide “equitable” remedies in which prosecutorial misconduct will be excused because of prior defense misconduct. It is up to defense counsel to properly brief these issues to prevent this from happening.

1. “Invited response” misconstrues actual case precedent, which simply provides that determination of effect of misconduct on fair trial must consider error in context of entire trial, including any defense misconduct. United States v. Young, 470 US 1, 11–12 (1985) (The court discusses Lawn v. United States, 355 US 339 (1958), which is often cited as supporting a doctrine of “invited response” or “invited reply.” But Lawn did nothing more than apply settled law. Lawnteaches that “the remarks must be examined within the context of the trial to determine whether the prosecutor’s response would have [ ] on the jury’s ability to judge the evidence fairly. In this context, defense counsel’s conduct, as well as the nature of the prosecutor’ response, is relevant.”)

2. Prosecutor is never “licensed” to argue improperly; improper defense argument is part of context of whether unfair trial resulted from such improper argument. United States v. Young,supra, at 12 (1985):

In retrospect, perhaps the idea of “invited response” has evolved in a way not contemplated. Lawn and the earlier cases cited above should not e read as suggesting judicial approval or encouragement – or response-in-kind that inevitably exacerbates the tensions inherent in the adversary process. As Lawnitself indicates, the issue is not the prosecutor’s license to make otherwise improper arguments, but whether the prosecutor’s ‘invited response,’ taken in context, unfairly prejudiced the defendant.

3. Prosecutor’s remedy is objection at the time of defense misconduct, not to respond inappropriately. United States v. Young,supra, at 14 (1985):

We share the Court of Appeals’ desire to minimize ‘invited responses’ and we agree that the prosecutor’s response constituted error....the prosecutor’s comments crossed the line of permissible conduct established by the ethical rules of the legal profession, as did defense counsel’s argument...and went beyond what was necessary to ‘right the scale’ in the wake of defense counsel’s misconduct. Indeed the prosecutor’s first error was in failing to ask the District Judge to deal with defense counsel’s misconduct.

4. Ethical bar – and related requirements – higher for prosecutor than defense counsel; defense violation no excuse for government violation. United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 1148 (9th Cir. 2005):

In that respect we stress that the ethical bar is set higher for the prosecutor than for the criminal defense lawyer – a proposition that has been clear for at least seven decades. [Citations omitted.]

Although to be sure no lawyer, either public or private, should lay his or her own credibility on the line by expressing his or her own opinion about a witness’ believability, the difference is that a private lawyer’s impropriety in that respect carries no implication of official governmental support.

Id.,at 1148.

5. Invited response doctrine does not open door to improper vouching; government has an obligation to object to perceived defense misconduct as well. United States v. Weatherspoon,supra, at 1150. Here, some of improper statements preceded defense argument, and prosecutor never objected to improper comments by defense:

Despite all that has been said to this point, the prosecutor urges that his statements should be considered proper because he was simply making ‘invited replies’ to arguments made by defense counsel during closing argument. Quite apart from the fact that some of the prosecutor’s improper statements preceded that closing argument so that no ‘invitation’ then existed, plus the added fact that the prosecutor never objected to any of the now-complained-of arguments by defense counsel, that attempted explanation wholly misunderstands the doctrine of ‘invited response’ as applied inYoung, 70 US at 12.''”[Emphasis added.] [Id.,at 1150.]

But that does not at all mean that whenever a defense counsel attacks the credibility of witnesses the prosecutor can respond with vouching statements. To the contrary, we have concluded that “[a]ttacks on the credibility of a defense witness are legitimate tools of advocacy and do not, standing alone, trigger the invited response rule.” United States v. Smith, 962 F2d 923, 934 (9th Cir. 1992). And that is particularly true when, as here, the defense attacks were grounded in inferences from the evidence rather than defense counsel’s personal assurances.”

Id., at 1150.

6.  Even if defense argument improper, government may not vouch.United States v. Weatherspoon,supra, at 1150:

Moreover, even if any defense statements were somehow viewed as opening the door to a prosecutorial response, the particular response chosen here would still be inappropriate because “the prosecution is not allowed to use improper tactics even in response to similar tactics by the defense.” (United States v. Sarkisian, 197 F3d 966, 990 (9th Cir. 1999); see also Smith, 962 F2d at 934). Prosecutors must understand the different – and special – place they occupy in the criminal justice system. (1150)

Though we do not of course countenance, let alone encourage, excesses on the part of defense counsel, the prosecutor’s role as representative of the United States (the named plaintiff in every federal criminal prosecution) demands the exercise of far better restraint and better judgment than was exhibited here. In short, nothing about the invited response doctrine rescues the prosecutor’s statements from impropriety – even to the extent that some (thought not all) of them might be viewed as invited, the statements did far more than simply ‘right the scale.’”[Emphasis added.]

Id., at 1150.

7. Vouching is improper even when “invited” by defense argument. United States v. Combs, 379 F3d 564, 574 (9th Cir. 2004): “It is well settled that the prosecution is not allowed to use improper tactics even in response to similar tactics by the defense.” [Subquotes omitted.] Citing United States v. Sarkisian, 197 F3d 966, 990 (9th Cir. 1999).

8. Attacks on credibility of government witnesses are legitimate tools of advocacy and do not, alone, justify prosecutorial vouching. United States v. Kerr, 981 F2d 1050 (9th Cir. 1992).

9. Witness credibility contest between parties (i.e., effective defense representation in action) does not open door to vouching. United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 1148 (9th Cir. 2005):

On that score, it is wholly beside the mark to suggest that a witness’ challenge to the credibility of another witness during the trial somehow validates the prosecutor’s repeated statements of his own belief as to such credibility in the course of closing argument, as though such statements were other than constitutionally prohibited vouching.

10. Prosecution is not entitled to use improper tactics to respond to defense tactics! United States v. McKoy, 771 F2d 1207, 1212 (9th Cir. 1985).

11. But prosecution may have some leeway to “right the scale of justice” and defense conduct may be relevant to context of entire trial. United States v. McKoy,supra, 1212, citing United States v. Young, 470 US 1, 12–13 (1985).

12. Even if reply is “invited,” no excuse for vouching.United States v. Sarkisian, 197 F3d 966, 990 (9th Cir. 1999). (The statement was an invited reply to defense counsel’s argument that Bedrik manufactured evidence. “Nevertheless, the prosecution is not allowed to use improper tactics even in response to similar tactics by the defense.”)

13. Defense counsel’s behavior may be factor in determining prejudice and error analysis. United States v. Sarkisian, 197 F3d 966, 990 (9th Cir. 1999) (in applying a harmless error analysis, this court must determine whether it is more probable than not that the prosecutor’s conduct materially affected the fairness of the trial). Here, the court determined the error to be harmless, but did not attribute the determination to defense counsel’s conduct, instead focusing on tangential nature of witness’ testimony and other corroboration of the witness’ testimony. In dicta, the court then did add that the response was in part in response to defense counsel’s comments. Id., at 990. ''Also see: Cheney v. Washington, 614 F3d 987, 997 (9th Cir. 2010) (invited response concept relevant in determining what effect a prosecutor’s improper statements had on the trial as a whole, to which defense conduct is also relevant, citing Young).

14. Limited justification for prosecutor arguing credibility: when defense counsel invites it by attacking credibility of prosecutor or suggesting he suborned perjury or withholding evidence. United States v. Smith, 962 F2d 923, 934 (9th Cir. 1992).

15. Last but not least: Improper so-called “invited responses” can be discouraged by prompt judicial action against perceived defense misconduct. United States v. Young, 470 US 1, 13 (1985):

“Invited response” can be effectively discouraged by prompt action from the bench in the form of corrective instructions to the jury and, when necessary, an admonition to the errant advocate.

Plainly, the better remedy in this case, at least with the accurate vision of hindsight, would have been for the district Judge to deal with the improper argument of the defense counsel promptly and thus blunt the need for the prosecutor to respond. Arguably defense counsel’s misconduct could have warranted the judge to interrupt the argument and admonish him, [citation omitted], thereby rendering the prosecutor’s response unnecessary.

Id., at 13.

IV. Appellate Issues and Problems

Introduction: The best advice about how to deal with vouching on appeal is to make a thorough record at trial. Object and do so timely. Provide the reasons for your objection, including state and federal constitutional provisions. Follow up with a written briefing with case authorities if you don’t have them at your fingertips during trial, and file them to amend the record. Make sure you argue the prejudice of vouching at the time of your objections. Move for a mistrial and incorporate the same grounds as for your objection. If you object and thoroughly state your factual and legal grounds, you’ll be able to avoid the “plain error” review standard and provide the appellate court with a respectable record. When briefing appellate issues, educate the court as to the importance of improper vouching.

• Harmless Error Standard of review, when defense counsel objects at trial.United States v. Weatherspoon, 410 F3d 1142, 50 (9th Cir. 2005).

• Harmless Error Standard: reversal if more probable than not that misconduct materially affected trial fairness. United States v. Younger, 398 F3d 1179, 1190 (9th Cir. 2005). When counsel objects to alleged acts of prosecutorial misconduct, we review for harmless error. A claim of prosecutorial misconduct is viewed in the entire context of the trial, and reversal on this basis is justified only if it appears more probable than not that prosecutorial misconduct materially affected the fairness of the trial.

• Plain Error Standard of review, if counsel fails to object at trial.United States v. Mohsen, 587 F3d 1028, 1033 (9th Cir. 2009); United States v. Harrison, 585 F3d 1155, 1158 (9th Cir. 2009); United States v. Inzunza, 580 F3d 894, 910 (9th Cir. 2009); United States v. Weatherspoon,supra.

• Plain Error analysis most restrictive; allows correction of only the most egregious errors which seriously affect fairness, integrity or public reputation of trial. United States v. Young, 470 US 1, 14–15 (1985):

The plain error doctrine of Rule 52(b) tempers the blow of a rigid application of the contemporaneous-objection requirement. It authorizes the Courts of Appeals to correct only particularly egregious errors, those which “seriously affect the fairness, integrity or public reputation of judicial proceedings.”

The plain error exception to the contemporaneous objection rule is to be used sparingly, solely in those circumstances in which a miscarriage of justice would otherwise result.

• Where vouching isn’t serious, with only tangential impact, curative instruction will usually suffice (here, harmless error standard).United States v. Sanchez, 944 F2d 497 (9th Cir. 1991). (During closing, prosecutor stated, “That is the truth, ladies and gentlemen,...he told me about this.” Court interrupted, admonished government attorney and gave cautionary instruction about what prosecutor said. Again, during final instructions, court reiterated instruction against vouching. Comment held to be “tangential” to the evidence, curative instruction deemed sufficient.)

• Failure to timely object results in condemnation to “plain error” standard on appeal. United States v. Combs, 379 F3d 564, 568 (9th Cir. 2004).

Practice Tip

Object! Object! Object! Make your record! Be obnoxious, if need be. Cases are not won by appeasing judges who wish nothing more than to be rid of you and your case as soon as possible. State your reasons, take your time! The court exists for your client at that moment, never forget that. Provide not just ethical rules and case law, but constitutional provisions. Name all of them, if you can’t remember which Article or Amendment applies. Do not forget to federalize your objections! Make your record! Make your record! Make your record!

• Requirements for reversal under “plain error” standard. Defendant must prove (1) error, (2) that error was plain, and (3) the error affected substantial rights. Reversal is proper only if “viewed in the context of the entire trial, the impropriety seriously affected the fairness, integrity, or public reputation of judicial proceedings, or where failing to reverse a conviction would result in a miscarriage of justice.” United States v. Combs,supra.

• Other factors in “plain error” review. “When reviewing for plain error, we then balance the seriousness of the vouching against the strength of the curative instruction and closeness of the case.” United States v. Combs,supra, at 576.

• Standard of review where constitutional error established: Chapman harmless-error analysis. United States v. Schoneberg, 396 F3d 1036, 1044 (9th Cir. 2004):

“Though constitutional error has been established, it is not sufficient to compel a result. Under Delaware v. Van Arsdall, [475 U.S. 673 (1986)], a confrontation clause violation does not result in automatic reversal, but rather is subject to the Chapman harmless-error analysis.” The Supreme Court held in Van Arsdall, that the correct inquiry is whether, assuming that the damaging potential of the cross examination were fully realized, we might nevertheless say that the error was harmless beyond a reasonable doubt. (1044)

• Appellate review: analysis must consider entire context of trial, including defense conduct. United States v. Young,supra, at 11 (a criminal conviction is not to be lightly overturned on the basis of a prosecutor’s comments standing alone, for the statements or conduct must be viewed in context; only by so doing can it be determined whether the prosecutor’s conduct affected the fairness of the trial).

• No bright-line rule for reversibility: factors determining when vouching requires reversal. The court in United States v. Combs,supra, set forth the following factors to consider:

1. The form of the vouching

2. How much the vouching implies that the prosecutor has extra-record knowledge of or the capacity to monitor the witness’s truthfulness

3. Any inference that the court is monitoring the witness’s veracity

4. The degree of personal opinion asserted

5. The timing of the vouching

6. The extent to which the witness’s credibility was attacked

7. The specificity and timing of a curative instruction

8. The importance of the witness’s testimony and the vouching of the case overall

9. Strength of the curative instruction and closeness of the case.

379 F3d at 575.

Practice Tip

“Balancing tests” are often excuses for courts to avoid enforcing constitutional rights, while hiding behind pretenses of mathematical certainty or “majority wins” sophistry. Find a good case that fits your situation and argue it, but prepared to argue the balancing test because the prosecution will. When did the government start having constitutional rights, anyway?!

• Plain error where vouching included prosecutorial assurances and implicated authority of the court; a rare reversal. United States v. Smith, 962 F2d 923, 933 (9th Cir. 1992). (Prosecutor, in closing, stated that the cooperating witness could not “get up here and say whatever he wanted to say,” or he would be prosecuted for perjury. He also said that his job was not to seek a conviction but rather to guarantee a fair trial and turn over any favorable evidence to the defense. Later, he said that the government’s job is to ferret through all the smoke screens and lead you to the truth. Finally, he said “If I did anything wrong in this trial I wouldn’t be here. The court wouldn’t allow that to happen.”) Id., at 933–34.

• Appellate court will consider strength of evidence in determining prejudice. State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336, 338; 479 P2d 240 (1971): “If proof of defendant’s guilt is strong, the challenged conduct or remark will not be presumed prejudicial. [Citation omitted.] However, an objectionable remark, properly challenged but left uncorrected, may be ground for reversal.

• Vouching especially problematic when credibility of witness is critical. United States v. Necoechea, 986 F2d 1273, 1276 (9th Cir. 1993).

• Cumulative effect of statements to be analyzed. United States v. Smith,supra.

• Where credibility if particularly important, prejudice of vouching exacerbated.United States v. Weatherspoon,supra, at 1151 (9th Cir. 2005).

• Proper to consider cumulative effect of vouching with other misconduct.United States v. Combs,supra (improper questioning which forced defendant to testify that agent was lying combined with improper vouching for reversible error). Also see: United States v. Sanchez, 176 F3d 1214 (9th Cir. 1999) reversing conviction based upon cumulative effect of improper cross-examination of defendant and impermissible vouching, among other errors, andUnited States v. Richter'', 826 F2d 206 (2nd Cir. 1987) reversible error where prosecutor forced defendant to testify that government’s agent’s testimony was not true, then vouched for agent’s truthfulness in closing.

• Judicial sophistry, or just poor reasoning.Last but not least is the twisted reasoning, the broken logical trail and the outright sophistry which some judges are willing to employ – consciously or not – to arrive at a conclusion which keeps your client in jail. For example, in Bockting v. Bayer, 399 F3d 1010 (9th Cir. 2005), the court determined that the risk of prejudice of prosecutorial vouching was diminished by the general jury instruction that lawyer’s statements are not evidence. Id., at 1040. But this misses the point entirely: the fact that lawyer’s statements are not evidence does nothing whatsoever to diminish the damage done by vouching. And there are many cases which repeat the tired refrain: “We affirm, while not condoning the vouching” or which resort to the popular “harmless error” excuse. ''See, e.g.: United States v. Brooks, 508 F3d 1205 (9th Cir. 2007).

Until appellate courts get as serious about prosecutorial misconduct as they apparently are about misconduct of citizens charged with crimes, vouching will continue. As defense attorneys, we need to do our part to educate judges and make a good record for appeal.
