Chapter 10. Cross-Examination

By Bob Thuemmel'''1

This cross-examination section of the OCDLA Trial Notebook has been broken down into two parts. You will find the substantive law on cross-examination following this section.

This section deals with the “practical tips” of cross-examination. It has been written with the less-experienced trial lawyer in mind, though it will contain a tip or a reminder for the more seasoned. It only represents things that I have learned and that work for me, gathered over many years of mistakes and many trials and errors.

In thinking about cross-examination, the following tips come to mind.

I. Plan Your Cross Examination Carefully

This first tip may be the most important one of all. Plan your cross-examination in upcoming trials more carefully than any other part of the trial. Not only does this help organize what it is you want do, but it forces you to get to know the testimony of the state’s witnesses inside and out. Write yourself an outline. Some of us use notes in cross-examination, some of us don’t. Even if you don’t use notes, make yourself notes showing you a map of where you want your cross to go and why you want to get there.

Many experienced lawyers will admit that for every hour they spend in preparing for trial, they spend at least 45 minutes of that hour planning out cross-examination. It is that important.

II. Why Are You Asking That Question?

In many ways this can be just as important as your outline. For every question you want to ask on cross, ask yourself, “Why am I asking that question?”

Cross-examination should accomplish a few basic things. Hopefully, it should help to:

Discredit the witness and impeach the testimony given on direct

Produce your client’s version of events and/or your theory of defense

Create doubt about the state’s case.

Cross-examination is not the time to show the jury that you are a smart or clever lawyer. In today’s jury climate, getting and maintaining the trust of members of the jury is a critical thing for the defense attorney to do. If cross-examination questions are not carefully designed to get to the issues that are important in the case, you can hurt yourself in your effort to connect with the jury.

III. Less Is More

Many lawyers find that the more trials they have done, the fewer questions they will ask on cross-examination. After a while, you will go in with questions only on where you want or need to go, and you won’t spend time on things you don’t need to ask. But this comes only from experience. The more you do this work, the more refined your sense of where you are going in cross will become.

This is not to say that you shouldn’t take your time and be thorough in all the areas that are important to your case. You should take all the time you need to do that, including repeating some questions with a slightly different spin to emphasize the answer you are getting. But going to areas where you don’t need to go is an obvious waste of time. Example: “Officer, you are getting paid overtime for being here today, aren’t you? So these trials are good for you because they increase your salary, don’t they?”

Remember, too, that with some witnesses, you should not be afraid to say the two magic words: “No questions.” Before beginning the cross of a minor witness in the state’s case, you should always ask yourself:

Did this witness say anything that hurts my case?

Can I get them to say anything at all that would be helpful to my case?

If the answers to both are “no,” then don’t cross-examine the witness. If you do, you may find yourself wading into an area of testimony you really wish you hadn’t opened up. Everyone who has tried a case has had this happen to them, and it can be uncomfortable!

IV. Make It Interesting

Many times, jurors will be bored by the routine and monotone presentation of the state’s case. Prosecution trial mechanics can be downright boring, and jurors can easily fall into that mood. That’s when it becomes your job to pick up the pace and make it interesting.

You should feel like it is your duty to make your cross-examination interesting to those people who are stuck in the box, getting paid $10 per day to listen to what you have to say. Keep it lively whenever you can, assuming the subject matter provides for that. Keep it human, and down to earth, and relatively friendly whenever you can. Give the jurors a reason to sit up and pay attention. Of course, every trial lawyer has to develop their own style of courtroom presence. Remember, this stage of the trial should be interesting.

V. It’s Your Courtroom, Control It

When it’s time for cross-examination, you are in charge. This doesn’t mean you should disregard courtroom protocol, or wrestle for control with the trial judge or the deputy district attorney, you should not. It does mean, however, that you can and should control the witness on the stand.

The best cop in the world may well be a good police officer out on the street. She knows how to develop probable cause, knows how to interview eyewitnesses, knows how to fill out a search warrant affidavit, or knows how to conduct field sobriety tests. But when that officer goes into a courtroom and becomes a witness and when it comes time to cross-examine her, she is not in control of how that goes, you are.

This may seem easier said than done. But with some practice, it gets easier to do. In order to do it, first of all get back to your outline. An experienced trial attorney will have a v-e-r-y skillful police witness on the stand saying some very damaging things about their clients. The conversation in those lawyers’ heads will go something like this: “Boy, this guy is good! How am I going to lay a glove on him?” The answer is usually: stick with your game plan. When it is your turn, don’t feel that you need to begin your cross by running around throwing water on the fires that the witness started with his direct testimony. That is usually a disorganized way to attack the witness. Worse, it can give the clear message that you are upset and struggling with what the witness has just been saying. You don’t want to do that. Some of us feel that the old adage “never let ’em see you sweat” applies with greater force in a courtroom. When a witness has just spent some time clobbering your client, start your cross by going exactly where you wanted to start when you prepared your cross-examination. What was important to you when you planned this all out in your office is still important to you, and you’ll find ways of dealing with any new issues that were brought up on direct. You should not be “chained” to your planned cross, but you shouldn’t throw it out and just start taking jabs at a witness who was effective against your client. It will not work.

VI. Have the Witness Answer Your Questions

This tip is about control of the cross-examination in your trial. The other way of saying this is: don’t let the witness use your question as the place to give the speech that he or she wants to make instead of answering your question.

It seems so often these days that we see witnesses who come into court “loaded for bear,” and by that I mean witnesses who want to take it upon themselves to speak out forcefully in favor of their position regardless of what they are being asked. Their answers are non-responsive, and pretty repetitive. They look at every one of your questions as an opportunity to slip in a mini-argument about the strength of their case.

You have to develop your own ways of dealing with this. It depends on the style that you use in the courtroom. However, there are some basic pointers that you can use, such as:

Always bring the witness back to your question. Ask it again. Ask the witness if he understands the question. Ask the witness if they can answer the question. Then, ask them for the answer.

Repeat this each time the witness wants to “go off” in his own direction. Soon, you may find that the witness is taking your lead on cross a little more than they were at the outset.

If necessary, ask the judge to remind the witness to be responsive to your questions.

Allow the jury time to see what’s going on. Most of them will not appreciate it. I will often say, “Mr. Officer, I am not here to waste the jury’s time and, hopefully, neither are you. Is there some reason you are not able to give us an answer to the question that I have asked you?” I have found this approach often gets the job done, and you may discover the witness is now responding directly to your questions and the rest of the cross will go just as you want.

VII. Nail the Witness Down on Everything They Have Written About the Case

It stands to reason that a person’s memory and reports near the time of an event will be better and more comprehensive than what they are saying now, months or years later. I begin every cross-examination by referring to police reports, telling jurors through the witness what they are, having the witness agree with me that they tried to write down everything important at the time and that they have been trained to write comprehensive reports. I will then get the witness (usually police witnesses) to agree with this: “Without the reports in front of you, you can’t even tell us the name of another person that you dealt with in the month of (month of your client’s arrest), can you officer?” They will always answer, “Nope, not without some reports in front of me, I can’t.” When that happens, the jury knows that police reports are important to police witnesses and any discrepancies, omissions, or “recently developed memory pieces” from this witness may be a little suspect. You have effectively set the stage for those things as you go into the rest of your cross.

Another variation will take place in multi-cop cases where only one has written a report. When the non-writing officer is on the witness stand, she will generally have papers with her. I will begin my cross, knowing that this witness has no notes or report of her own, by asking the following:

“What’s that in front of you?”

The answer will be, “That’s Officer Jones’ police report”—the primary, report-writing officer.

“Where’s your report?”

“I didn’t write one.”

“I’ve read Officer Jones’ report, there are no quotes from you in that report, are there?”

“No.”

“You have a notebook. You sometimes make notes in the field that do not turn into police reports? You keep your notebooks? So...where are your notes?” (Of course by this time, you know there are no notes!)

Here, your mission is to show the jury that this witness is only giving them what Jones thinks about the case, and they have already heard from Jones. This makes the jury wonder about the testimony of the non-reporting officer that is helpful to you.

VIII. Common Beginner Mistakes

If you were to survey deputy district attorneys and judges, asking them, “What is the most common mistake made by defense attorneys on cross-examination?” the answer would be the same: “Most defense attorneys just go over and over what the state has brought out on direct, hurting their case.” You want to avoid that, even taking the “no questions” approach if your only option with the witness is to elicit something not helpful. Hopefully your organization and use of your outline as a roadmap for your cross will help you.

Another mistake is not listening. Often, lawyers will get so caught up in what they are doing that they will forget to l-i-s-t-e-n to what is happening from the witness stand. This is a skill that we all need to work at improving all the time. It is the skill that is the most important one a lawyer can have.

IX. Know the Rules of Impeachment and Don’t Get Caught Up in Them

If you want to use a prior inconsistent statement, or a treatise, or prior testimony, or impeach from the police report or the manual by which this officer was trained: Know how to do it. The mechanics involved in these skills should come automatically to you as a courtroom trial lawyer, and if you have any questions about them, relearn them from the books you learned them from originally, or just sit and practice with some friends. Do them often enough that doing them is routine. You do not want to be caught on the wrong side of an “improper foundation” argument or objection in front of the jury.

X. Why Ask Why?

Probably the most often overlooked rule in cross-examination is this one. We risk getting carried away in what we are trying to do, and if we’re not careful we can venture into the most dangerous area in all of cross-examination: the open-ended question. This can be your own courtroom death sentence, and most skilled witnesses will do everything they can to make it just that:

“So, Officer, you wanted to be completely thorough in your investigation of this burglary, didn’t you? Why then did you not give my client even the chance to explain to you what you thought were suspicious circumstances before you simply arrested him?”

“Counselor, I’ve been doing my job for 20 years. I don’t like arresting people unless I am completely convinced that they are guilty. In fact, I often lie awake at night tormented about the possibility of an innocent person being wrongly convicted, so because of that I am always so careful in my investigations. And so far in my long career, I have never testified against an innocent person, thank God. In this case, among all the cases I have handled, the evidence was simply overwhelming. And again, the reason I say that is because of these things...”

You get the point. If cross-examination is about controlling the witness and taking him where you want him to go, it never makes sense to let him off the lead to go where he wants to go. You will always regret it, because the skillful witness will always take the chance to run away from you and make you sorry you are a lawyer in that courtroom.

There is only one time that you can get away from this rule, and that is when you have effectively set the stage for the “any answer helps” opportunity. By that I mean when you are in a position to gain advantage from any direction the witness wants to go in his answer. “Do you agree a witness should always be truthful?” This is a good example of an open-ended question that can rarely hurt you. There aren’t many answers to that which hurt you. But before you give a witness the floor on an open-ended or a “why” question, stand back and imagine all the ways that they could feed you an answer you would choke on. If you can think of a way, so can they.

So always stay away from the open-ended or “why” question on cross. Remind yourself again and again of this rule. You only want to get out in cross what you want to get out. Don’t hand the witness the microphone and essentially say, “Go ahead, say the worst thing you can think of against my client and it’s okay if you make me look foolish while you’re doing it, too.”

XI. What To Do When You Don’t Know What To Do

As lawyers begin trying more cases, they often run into a major witness for the state and they don’t know what to ask. “I need to impeach this witness; what can I ask?”

In this situation, there are some basic areas that will often help your case. Some of them include:

Take a look at the charges your client is facing. Imagine all of the things that a person who was guilty of that crime would do, or would say, or would have with them—all the things you’d expect to be in that type of a case. Now, make a list of the pieces that are missing in your case. Work them into your outline of cross-examination and ask the witness about the pieces that are not there.

What do you think an arsonist/burglar/drunk driver should be like? What should they have with them? What characteristics would they typically have? What is the picture you see of that person when you close your eyes and imagine them? Jot down all of your observations, then go back through the police reports and see if those pieces are present in your case. If not, you will want to exploit their absence by pointing it out to the jury, since many of them have the same expectations, too.

Defense attorneys everywhere have noticed that juries today are expecting more of the state’s case because they watch TV shows that are takeoffs on police science and detective work, and they learn from those programs to expect a lot from police investigations. We should use these expectations in the courtroom.

It is never a waste of time in a courtroom to carefully inquire into the parts of the investigation that are missing, or are lacking, or did not happen, or got lost or misplaced, or can’t be retested or re-examined for some reason. Most jurors have a “limit” to how shoddy an investigation is that they are willing to put up with, and if your case can be shown to fall at or below that limit your chances of an acquittal go up. Think carefully about ways to demonstrate that the police work in this case was not what people can normally expect, and frame your questions to lead the witness through the topic. Remember, you do not want to let the witness simply explain as they go why things are not up to snuff.

Talk up your case. One of the lawyers I have admired most along the way once told me, “Every time you talk to somebody about your case, you learn something about it.” That observation has proven itself true time and time again over the years. Sit down with your friends, colleagues, family members—anybody who will listen—and talk about your case. You will get fresh insight into the weaknesses of the state’s case. You can get ideas on why your case is strong and of new things that you will want to cross-examine on. It is never a waste of time to run your case past as many people as you can when trial approaches and when it’s time to plan out your cross-examination. This is very helpful to you when you are making your mental list of the “things that are not in the reports” as suggested above. You’ll be surprised by the gems others will give you. Hopefully, you have the chance to bounce your case off of as many non-lawyers as possible.

XII. Anger: Use Sparingly

When we prepare our cases for trial, we get to know the facts very well. Often, we know some of the state’s witnesses, and we come to know their characteristics in and out of the courtroom. We learn to see subtle nuances or inconsistencies in the evidence, or in procedures used, or in statements made in reports or from the stand. Many of those things can make us mad, convinced that we have a lying, or at least an exaggerating, witness on our hands and that it is our job to expose them to the jury for what they are. How do you do it?

One of the best tips I ever got on this subject was to “not get mad at a witness in the courtroom until every juror knows why you are getting mad.” This means, before you show your feelings, contempt for the person or the evidence that you believe is false, lay the groundwork. Jurors want to assume the best about every witness who comes in front of them in the state’s case. That’s a given. It is ingrained in the system we work in. If you go off on a mission in cross-examination starting with your contempt for the person on the stand, you will lose jurors. They will decide early that you are picking on a witness for no reason and turn you off. On the other hand, if the jurors can “connect the dots” on the inconsistencies, or sloppy police work, or suspect testimony, those parts that make you angry, then they may well react to it the same way you do. They may disbelieve and reject not just that evidence, but other things the witness says. It is your job to slowly lay out the groundwork to expose the fabrication or inconsistency. Remember that many of the things we react to are very subtle nuances in the testimony, things that lay people might not ordinarily see or be concerned with. It becomes the trial lawyer’s job to point them out, to point out the significance of them and what they say about the evidence brought to court by the witness.

Be slow and cautious before revealing distaste for the witness or what the witness is trying to accomplish in court. If you show your feelings at the right time, it can be very effective. If you do it too early, it can cost you your bond with the jury.

XIII. Conclusion

The topic of cross-examination is overwhelming and there are shelves full of books written on it. I recommend many of those to you if you would like to learn more, but most of all I recommend doing cross-examination as often as you can. Try your cases whenever possible, cross-examine as much as possible, try as many new approaches as you can and, sure, make your own mistakes. You can not cross-examine without making a few mistakes. I hope the ideas presented have been useful to you in your effort to become a skilled trial lawyer. They are not intended to be a treatise on the subject, but I hope they can be a working outline for you as you refine your skills and habits in the courtroom. Good luck.

Cross Examination—Substantive Case Law

I. Introduction

In preparing for trial, defense counsel should know the statutory and constitutional law supporting the right to cross-examine witnesses. The statutory right lies primarily in Article VI of the Oregon Evidence Code. The constitutional right to confrontation of witnesses comes from Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution and in the [#amendmentvi Sixth Amendment] to the United States Constitution. Kirkpatrick’s Oregon Evidence (5th ed., 2011) is the best resource for state evidentiary law. For federal authority, the most useful treatise is Weinstein’s Federal Evidence (Weinstein, et al, Matthew Bender & Co: 1997). A discussion of prior inconsistent statements may be found at the end of this chapter.

The Library of Defense also has a section on Cross Examination:

https://libraryofdefense.org/legal/cross

II. Scope of Cross-Examination

A. Oregon Evidence Code

Rule 611 generally limits the scope of cross-examination to the subject matter of the direct exam and matters affecting credibility of the witness.

B. Federal Constitutional Right of Cross-Examination; Bias and Interest

1. In Davis v. Alaska, 415 U.S. 308, 94 S Ct 1105, 39 L Ed2d 347 (1974), the Court rendered its seminal pronouncement on the right to cross-examine: Cross-examination is the principal means by which the believability of a witness and the truth of his testimony are tested. Subject always to the broad discretion of a trial judge to preclude repetitive and unduly harassing interrogation, the cross-examiner has traditionally been allowed to impeach, i.e., discredit, the witness… A more particular attack on the witness’s credibility is effected by means of cross-examination directed toward revealing possible biases, prejudices, or ulterior motives of the witness as they may relate directly to issues or personalities in the case at hand. The partiality of a witness is subject to exploration at trial, and is always relevant as discrediting the witness and affecting the weight of his testimony. … We have recognized that the exposure of a witness’s motivation in testifying is a proper and important function of the constitutionally protected right of cross-examination.

Id. at 316, 94 S Ct at 1110 (citation omitted).

2. The right to cross-examination includes cross-examination about the witness’s plea-bargain. Delaware v. Van Arsdall, 475 U.S. 673, 106 S Ct 1431, 89 L Ed2d 674 (1986); Weinstein’s Federal Evidence at 607[03]). 3.The right to cross-examination is satisfied by an opportunity to ask questions, regardless of whether the witness is able to answer. United States v. Owens, 484 U.S. 554, 108 S Ct 838, 98 L Ed2d 951 (1988) (admission of testimony concerning prior, out-of-court identification by witness unable to explain its basis because of memory loss held not to violate Sixth Amendment Confrontation Clause, which protects the “opportunity” to cross-examine). 4. Olden v. Kentucky, 488 U.S. 227, 109 S Ct 480, 102 L Ed2d 513 (1988) (trial court’s limitation on cross-examination, in sexual assault case, as to cohabitation of complaining witness with third party at time of trial violated the defendant’s Sixth Amendment right of confrontation, and was not harmless error). Olden is an extremely important case! 5. “[E]xposing a witness’s reasons for fabrication in a specific case is at the heart of the Confrontation Clause.” Sussman v. Jenkins, 636 F3d 329, 353 (7thCir. 2011). 6. United States v. Abel, 469 U.S. 45, 52, 105 S Ct 465, 83 L Ed2d 450 (1984). (“Proof of bias is almost always relevant because the jury, as finder of fact and weigher of credibility, has historically been entitled to assess all evidence which might bear on the accuracy and truth of a witness’s testimony.”) 7. When a “star” government witness is involved, the importance of cross-examination is amplified. United States v. Wesevich, 666 F2d 984 (5th Cir. 1982). See also United States v. Morales-Quinones, 812 F2d 604 (10th Cir. 1987) (where the testimony of a witness is crucial to the government’s case, the defendant has a right to attack the witness’s credibility by a wide-ranging cross-examination). 8. Evidence that a prosecution witness is subject to reindictment by prosecution is probative of potential bias, and is therefore a proper subject of cross-examination under the confrontation clause, even without evidence of a cooperation agreement. United States v. Anderson, 881 F2d 1128 (D.C. Circuit 1989).

C. The Oregon Constitution and the Right to Confrontation—Article I, Section 11

1.The right to confrontation guaranteed to a criminal defendant by the Oregon Constitution includes the right to engage in effective cross-examination. State v. Kitzman, 323 Or 589, 920 P2d 734 (1996). 2. The leading Oregon case adopting the rule of Davis v. Alaska is State v. Hubbard, 297 Or 789, 688 P2d 1311 (1984). Hubbard holds that: “The discretion of the trial judge to control the scope of cross-examination pursuant to OEC 611(2) does not allow the exclusion of evidence offered to impeach a witness for bias or interest.” Id. at 799. The Hubbard court further stated that the defendant has great latitude in cross-examining a prosecution witness, particularly in cases where the witness and the defendant give sharply conflicting accounts of the facts and the outcome of the case rests heavily on the credibility of witnesses. Id. at 794. State v. Najibi, 150 Or App 194, 945 P2d 1093 (1997), continues the rule requiring considerable latitude when cross-examining a prosecution witness for bias or interest.

Beware of the appellate trend toward harmless error. Curtailing the defense cross-examination may be harmless if the jury had an adequate opportunity to assess witness credibility, or if witness credibility was not important to the outcome. State v. Titus, 328 Or 475, 982 P2d 1133 (1999); State v. Najibi, supra.

Practice Tip

Obviously the right to confront witnesses has been expanded greatly by the United States Supreme Court’s decision Crawford v. Washington, 541 US 36, 124 S Ct 1354, 158 LE2d 177 (2004). The principle overturning the Roberts analysis under the Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution for hearsay statements was applied in Oregon in State v. Page, 197 Or App 72, 104 P3d 616 (2005),rev denied, 340 Or 673 (2006). Counsel should keep abreast of the ever changing law around the right to confront witnesses in Oregon. Further, counsel should make all objections under the state and federal confrontation clauses in light of the Oregon State Supreme Court’s decision in State v. Cook, 340 Or 530, 135 P3d 260 (2006) upholding the Roberts analysis for hearsay under the Oregon constitutional analysis under Article I, §11.

D. Confrontation in non-criminal proceedings

The constitutional rights to confrontation and compulsory process only apply in criminal trials. Similar, albeit reduced, rights exist in DMV hearings (Cole/Dinsmore v. DMV, 336 Or 565, 87 P3d 1120 (2004)), probation-revocation hearings, State v. Gonzalez, 212 Or App 1, 157 P3d 266 (2007), and FAPA hearings, Hemingway and Maurer, ___Or App___, ___P3d___ 2012 WL 19453 (Or App) (January 5, 2012).

E. Keep the Door Closed

When the trial judge curtails your cross-examination, assert that the court is denying your client’s rights under Article I, section 11, and the Sixth Amendment right to confront the witnesses against him. Make an offer of proof.

Also, watch the scope of questioning. Questioning that is too broad may open the door on redirect examination for damaging evidence which would otherwise have been inadmissible. ''See State v. Renly, 111 Or App 453, 457, 58, 827 P2d 1345 (1992); State v. Wolfe, 56 Or App 795, 798, 643 P2d 404 (1982).

Some cases on “opening the door” (also called curative admissibility, or, in early cases, invited error) permit the admission of otherwise inadmissible evidence. State v. Adonri, 143 Or App 298, 923 P2d 658 (1996); United States v. Cromer, 389 F3d 662, 678–79 (2004). Be careful, and ask for a ruling out of the presence of the jury if opening the door is possible.

C.f. Howard v. Walker, 406 F.3d 114 (2d Cir. 2005) (error to hold that cross-examination of state’s expert will open the door to otherwise-inadmissible evidence)

The single best guide to effective cross-examination is Larry Pozner and Roger Dodd’s comprehensive treatise entitled Cross Examination: Science and Techniques(Lexis Law Publishing).

III. Impeachment for Bias or Interest

OEC 609(1) provides:

The credibility of a witness may be attacked by evidence that the witness engaged in conduct or made statements showing bias or interest. However, before this can be done, the statements must be related to the witness and the conduct described, with the circumstances of times, places and persons present, and the witness shall be asked whether the witness made the statements or engaged in such conduct, and, if so, allowed to explain. If the statements are in writing, they shall be shown to the witness.

The Commentary to OEC 609(1) explains that “bias” takes the form of friendly feeling or hostility toward a party, while “interest” commonly takes the form of having a stake in the outcome of the litigation. See also Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence, p 491–513 (5th ed 2011), ''see also State v. Phillips, 314 Or 460, 840 P2d 666 (1992).

In State v. Brown, 299 Or 143, 150, 699 P2d 1122 (1985), the Oregon Supreme Court noted, “Completely aside from OEC 609(1), bias due to friendship, family relationship, etc., and interest in the form or amount of expert witness fees, etc., continue to be viable forms of impeachment even though no conduct or statement is involved.”

Moreover, “[t]he Oregon appellate courts have held impeachment for bias or interest to be proper under the following circumstances: [1] Where immunity had been granted to a prosecution witness. State v. Cox, 87 Or App 443, 742 P2d 694 (1987); [2] Where a prosecution witness was facing a pending theft charge. State v. Presley, 84 Or App 1, 733 P2d 452 (1987); [3] Where the victim was contemplating a civil action against the defendant. State v. DeLucia, 40 Or App 711, 596 P2d 585 (1979); [and 5] Where the victim was potentially subject to criminal prosecution himself. State v. Sheeler, 15 Or App 96, 514 P2d 1370 (1973).” Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence, 5th ed. at 520. Rule 609 (1) of the Oregon Evidence Code also covers impeachment pertaining to bias or interest.

Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence, 5th ed. at 520–21 has a comprehensive list of proper areas for impeachment for bias or interest.

Practice Tip

Remember that OEC 806 allows you to impeach a hearsay declarant just as if the declarant had testified as a witness. State v. Phillips, 314 Or 460, 840 P2d 666 (1992). State v. Dishman, 148 Or App 404, 939 P2d 1172 (1997).

IV. Use of False Names

A witness’s use of false names or false identities is a proper subject of cross-examination. United States v. Mansaw, 714 F2d 785 (8th Cir. 1983).

V. Placing the Witness in His or Her Setting

The cross examiner may place the witness in her proper setting identifying her with her environment. Alford v. United States, 282 U.S. 687 (1931), quoted and relied on in Smith v. Illinois, 390 U.S. 129 (1968). In Alford, the Court stated:

It is the essence of a fair trial that reasonable latitude be given the cross-examiner, even though he is unable to state to the court what facts a reasonable cross-examination might develop. Prejudice ensues from a denial of the opportunity to place the witness in his proper setting and put the weight of his testimony and his credibility to a test, without which the jury cannot fairly appraise them.

Be creative. If the witness comes from a negative setting or environment, use it to your advantage.

VI. Contradiction

A witness may be impeached by evidence contradicting her or his testimony. State v. Burdge, 295 Or 1, 664 P2d 1076 (1983); State v. Gibson, 338 Or 560, 113 P3d 423 (2005).

In State v. Clark, 98 Or App 478, 779 P2d 215 (1989), the court held that evidence that a codefendant had been convicted for the same transaction, although not admissible as substantive evidence of defendant’s guilt, was admissible to contradict the defendant’s testimony when the defendant opened the door to such evidence.

Note: This method of impeachment is subject to the qualification that a witness cannot be impeached on a collateral matter. State v. Thompson, 28 Or App 409, 559 P2d 1294 (1977); see also Gibson, ibid.

VII. Prior Conviction of a Crime

This area of impeachment is covered by Rule 609 of the Oregon Evidence Code. The federal rule is Federal Rules of Evidence 609. All felony convictions not more than 15 years old are admissible. This includes misdemeanors involving false statements or dishonesty. The Oregon Legislature has decided that some crimes involve false statements by their very nature. A partial list of these crimes is found in the commentary to Rule 609, Oregon Rules of Court, West Publishing, as well as in other publications concerning the Oregon Evidence Code. The inchoate forms of these crimes are also admissible to impeach the witness. A class C felony that was treated as a misdemeanor is still admissible because it was punishable as a felony. State v. Smith, 298 Or 173, 691 P2d 89 (1984).

A trial judge has no discretion under OEC 403 to exclude a felony conviction admissible under OEC 609 in a criminal case. State v. King, 307 Or 332, 768 P2d 391 (1989). However, disclosing the sentence imposed in addition to the conviction can raise irrelevant and possibly prejudicial issues. In State v. Gallant, 307 Or 152, 764 P2d 920 (1988), the state conceded error when the prosecution brought up the sentence of probation and a probation revocation for the prior crime used to impeach the witness. The court, however, found that the error was harmless.

In State v. Weinstein, 108 Or App 486, 814 P2d 565 (1991), the state again conceded that it was prejudicial error for the trial court to refuse to allow the defendant to impeach an assault victim by showing that the victim was on probation and therefore had a motive to lie about the details of a fight, ''but see State v. Pratt, 316 Or 561, 853 P2d 827 (1993) (defense could not introduce documents showing the government witness was on probation for the crime he was being impeached for, the terms of his probation, and that he was in violation of that probation); and State v. Archer, 150 Or App 505, 947 P2d 620 (1997) (the state was allowed to mention the felony status of defendant’s driving while suspended conviction).

VIII. Character and Conduct of a Witness

Impeachment based on the character and the conduct of a witness is covered under Rule 608 of the Oregon Evidence Code. Counsel should also review the character evidence rules set forth in OEC 404 and 405. Such impeachment does not generally include specific instances of witness conduct.

IX. Inadequate Perception and Lack of Memory

A. Mental Impairment

In State v. Gherasim, 153 Or App 313, 956 P2d 1054 (1998), the defense was allowed to present expert testimony regarding a witness who suffered dissociative amnesia.

In United States v. Robinson, 583 F3d 1265 (10th Cir. 2009), the trial court erred by precluding cross-examination of an informant on his mental health history and his use of prescription medication.

''See also State v. Longoria, 17 Or App 1, 520 P2d 912 (1974) (witness may be impeached by defect of memory).

B. Use of or Dependency on Intoxicants

The general rule regarding impeaching a witness by evidence of use of or dependency on intoxicants is found in State v. Batchelor, 34 Or App 47, 50, 578 P2d 409 (1978): “The rules supported by the majority of cases seem to be that for the purpose of discrediting a witness evidence is not admissible to show that he is a user of opium, morphine or similar drugs, or to show the effect of such drugs, unless it is proven that the witness was under their influence at the time of the occurrences as to which he testifies, or at the time of the trial, or that his mind or memory or powers of observation were affected by the habit.”

X. Impermissible Areas of Impeachment

A. Vouching

A witness (expert or otherwise) may not give an opinion on whether he or she believes another witness’s testimony in court is truthful. State v. Middleton, 294 Or 427, 438, 657 P2d 1215 (1983); State v. Southard, 347 Or 127 218 P3d 104 (2009). A federal case stating this rule is United States v. Akitoye, 923 F2d 221 (1st Circuit 1991). An expert witness can testify about another witness’s ability to perceive, if the expert does not directly comment on the witness’s trustworthiness. See State v. Gherasim, 153 Or App 313, 956 P2d 1054 (1998), and State v. Stringer, 292 Or 388, 639 P2d 1264 (1982).

See the section in Chapter 16 titled, “The Ethical and Legal Problems of Prosecutorial Vouching” for more detail.

B. Improper Bolstering

A prosecutor cannot create a foundation for rehabilitation of a witness’s character for truthfulness by asking defendant if witness has lied. State v. Carr, 302 Or 20, 725 P2d 1287 (1986); see also State v. Charboneau, 323 Or 38, 913 P2d 308 (1996) (reversible error to allow prosecutor to rehabilitate a witness by admission of a plea agreement containing a condition that the witness would be truthful);State v. Middleton'', 295 Or 485, 668 P2d 371 (1983) (while an opposing party may offer evidence to rebut an inference of bias or interest on part of a witness, the fact that the witness was required to pass a polygraph test before entering a plea agreement is inadmissible to rebut a charge of interest or bias); State v. Walker, 140 Or App 472, 915 P2d 1039 (1996) (police officer cannot vouch for credibility of informant).

XI. Impeachment with Prior False Allegations of Sexual Offenses

As a general rule, impeachment with specific instances of the witness’s conduct is inadmissible under OEC 404(2) and 405(10). State v. Marshall, 312 Or 367, 373, 823 P2d 961 (1991).

State v. LeClair, 83 Or App 121, 720 P2d 690 (1986),rev den303 Or 74 (1987), held that, notwithstanding the Evidence Code’s exclusionary rules, when a defendant lays the proper foundation, impeachment of the complainant with evidence that on a prior occasion the complainant falsely alleged a similar crime against another person, such impeachment is constitutionally mandatory. State v. Arellano, 149 Or App 86, 941 P2d 1089 (1997), however, requires the defense to present very clear and concise evidence of prior falsehoods by the victim.

Redmond v. Kingston, 240 F3d 590 (7thCir. 2001). In this case, the court found a confrontation violation where the defendant had been prohibited from cross examining the alleged rape victim with respect to a prior false claim that she had been forcibly raped. The evidence was relevant to her motive to falsely accuse the defendant. In an important passage, the court noted that “[t] false charge ‘exception’ to the rape shield statute is not really an exception, but rather a reminder of the limited meaning of “sexual conduct” as defined by the statue.” Id. at 592.

United States v. Stamper, 766 F Supp 1396 (1991),aff’d959 F2d 231 (4th Cir. 1992), holds that, where evidence is relevant to a legitimate defense theory, the [#amendmentvi Sixth Amendment’s Confrontation Clause] trumps statutory exclusionary rules such as the Rape Shield Law, FRE 412. In Stamperthe defendant did not offer the evidence to show that the complainant had a “mere propensity to fabricate.” Rather, he intended to use it for the legitimate purpose of proving “a contrived ulterior motive or plan.” Id. at 1402.

Commonwealth v. Bohannon, 376 Mass 90, 378 NE2d 987, 990–21 (1978) (holding that excluding such evidence denied the defendant a fair trial).

See also Fowler v. Sacramento County Sheriff's Dep’t, 421 F3d 1027 (9th Cir. 2005) (habeas relief granted when trial court precluded cross-examination of the victim relating to prior accusations: “a jury very well might reasonably have concluded that [the complainant] tended to misinterpret or misperceive the conduct, overreact, and then perhaps unconsciously, exaggerate or embellish in recounting the conduct so as to fit her subjective misinterpretation or misperception.”Id''. at 1038–39.

State v. Cox, 298 Md 173, 468 A2d 319, 324 n2 (1983) (prior false allegation evidence generally is admissible; fact that complaining witness made only one, rather than multiple such allegations is no basis for excluding evidence of the single falsity).

''But see State v. Driver, 192 Or App 395, 86 P3d 53 (2004) (prior recantation of accusation against another person is not admissible by statute and not required under the Oregon or federal confrontation clauses).

United States v. Smith Grading & Paving, Inc., 760 F2d 527, 531 (4th Cir. 1985) (“Rule 608(b) should not be read so broadly as to disallow the presentation of extrinsic evidence that is probative of a material issue [on a defendant’s guilt]”), cert den, 474 U.S. 1005 (1985).

Annotation: “Impeachment or Cross-Examination of Prosecuting Witnesses in Sexual Offense Trial by Showing that Similar Charges Were Made Against Other Persons,” 71 ALR 4th 469 (1989 and Supp. 1997).

XII. Cross of the Defendant Who Testifies at Trial

When the accused in a criminal trial testifies in his own behalf, he may be cross-examined to the same extent as any other witness. State v. Kelly, 118 Or 397, 247 P 146 (1926). If the defendant does not submit to cross-examination, the court may strike the direct testimony. State v. Cox, 337 Or 477, 98 P3d 1103 (2004).

The federal rule is similar. As Weinstein points out: “A defendant taking the stand is open to questioning on any matter relevant to the case. For example, in one case, the court noted that proffered testimony, limited as it might have been, was intended to prove that the defendant was not guilty. ‘The implications of the proffered testimony were very broad and any question which would have elicited testimony that was reasonably related to the inferences that might reasonably be drawn from his direct testimony would be possible.’ The court also noted that regardless of the limits on cross-examination Rule 611(b) grants discretion to the trial judge to inquire into additional matters as if the witness were on direct examination. The court suggested that this could subject the defendant witness to very broad questions on relevant matters.” 4 McLaughlin, Weinstein’s Federal Evidence, §611.04[1][e] (2000) (quoting United States v. Raper, 676 F2d 841, 846 (D.C. Circuit 1982)).

XIII. Constitutional Limitations on Cross-Examination of the Accused

Counsel should pay extra attention in this area. Federal and state authorities sometimes conflict and sometimes don’t.

The Oregon Constitution prohibits impeachment of the defendant’s trial testimony with prior inconsistent statements elicited through illegal questioning. State v. Isom, 306 Or 587, 761 P2d 524 (1988).

Under the United States Constitution, the prosecution may use statements procured without administering Miranda warnings to impeach the defendant’s direct testimony. Harris v. New York, 401 U.S. 222 (1971). Involuntary statements cannot even be used for impeachment. Mincey v. Arizona, 437 U.S. 385 (1978).

The prosecution may use illegally obtained evidence to impeach the defendant’s testimony, even where the evidence is not admissible in the government’s case-in-chief. United States v. Havens, 446 U.S. 620 (1980).

Illegally obtained evidence cannot be used to impeach a defense witness other than the defendant. James v. Illinois, 493 U.S. 307 (1990).

Prior Inconsistent Statements

I. Introduction/Sources of Law

A. OEC 613 (ORS 40.380) Prior Statements of Witnesses

1. Examining witness concerning prior statement. In examining a witness concerning a prior statement made by the witness, whether written or not, the statement need not be shown nor its contents disclosed to the witness at that time, but on request the same shall be shown or disclosed to opposing counsel.

2. Extrinsic evidence of prior inconsistent statement of witness. Extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement by a witness is not admissible unless the witness is afforded an opportunity to explain or deny the same, and the opposite party is afforded an opportunity to interrogate the witness thereon, or the interests of justice otherwise require. This provision does not apply to admissions of a party-opponent as defined in Rule 801 (ORS 40.450).

B. OEC 801(4)(a) (FRE 801(d)(1)(A))

Statements which are not hearsay. A statement is not hearsay if:

Prior statement by witness. The declarant testifies at the trial or hearing and is subject to cross-examination concerning the statement, and the statement is::

(A) Inconsistent with the testimony of the witness and was given under oath subject to the penalty of perjury at a trial, hearing or other proceeding, or in a deposition;

(B) Consistent with the testimony of the witness and is offered to rebut an inconsistent statement or an express or implied charge against the witness of recent fabrication or improper influence or motive; or

(C) One of identification of a person made after perceiving the person.

C. Related Rules

1. OEC 106 When part of a transaction proved, whole admissible. 2. OEC 607Who may impeach. 3. OEC 609-1Impeachment for bias or interest (No FRE!). 4. OEC 611Mode and order of interrogation and presentation. 5. OEC 612Writing used to refresh memory. 6. OEC 613Prior statements of witnesses. 7. OEC 805Hearsay within hearsay. 8. II. Law of Prior Inconsistent Statements

A. General

Prior statements are generally inadmissible hearsay if offered for truth. If prior statement is inconsistent with witness’s present testimony, it is admissible to impeach witness. Court must give limiting instruction if asked. Query: Does anyone really believe juries distinguish between substantive and impeachment evidence?

B. Types of Statements: Examples

1. Witness’s Prior Sworn Statement • deposition

• grand jury or preliminary hearing

2. Witness’s Prior Signed Statement • letter

• report (police or otherwise)

3.Third-party Statement, Signed or Adopted by Witness • witness reads document and initials or adopts it as accurate

4. Third-party Statement, Not Signed or Adopted by Witness • defense investigator’s report recounting witness’s statement

• police report, etc...

5. Prior Oral Statement of Witness • another person heard witness say

C. Inconsistent

The word “inconsistent” is not defined by rule or statute.

1. Generally The word “inconsistent” is not defined by rule or statute. Generally, it “need not be a contradiction in plain terms; it is enough if the proffered testimony as a whole by what it says or omits affords some indication that the fact was different from the testimony.” United States v. Barrett, 539 F2d 244, 254 (lst Cir. 1976).

2. Material Variance The Oregon Supreme Court has held that a statement is inconsistent if there is any material variance between the statement and the direct testimony of the witness. Rigelman v. Gilligan, 265 Or 109, 506 P2d 710 (1973). The court further advised trial judges to lean toward receiving such statements in case of doubt to aid evaluating the testimony. This liberal view of inconsistency is the preferred view. See McCormick, Evidence §34 at page 74 (3d ed., 1984); Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence p. 553 (5th ed., 2011).

3. Omission v. Commission Prior statements may be used to impeach as inconsistent as a result of what they don’t say as well as what they do say. “Inconsistent statements have been held to include omissions of material facts from earlier statements, earlier statements that the witness lacked knowledge of the subject, and silence under circumstances where the witness would reasonably have been expected to speak if the facts were as he or she now testified.” Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence p. 553, citing McCormick, supra, at 74–75.

4. Opinion McCormick also points out that a prior expression of opinion may be seen as a prior inconsistent statement even though it does not directly contradict the fact asserted at trial. Thus a witness who testifies to particular facts incriminating a defendant in a self-defense assault case might be impeached by the prior general statement, “it wasn’t his fault.” See generally, McCormick, Evidence, §35 page 76.

5. Lack of Recollection If a witness claims not to remember a material fact (e.g., “I don’t remember who ran the red light”), he or she may be impeached with a prior statement as to that fact (i.e., “the red car ran the red light”). Similarly, the witness may be impeached if denying memory of the prior statement. See Annot “Denial of Recollection As Inconsistent with Prior Statement so as to Render Statement Admissible,” 99 ALR 3d 934 (1980).

D. Foundations for Impeachment

1. OEC 613(1)—Examining the Witness (FRE 613(a)) This rule dispenses with the requirement that the witness must be shown the statement before being asked about it. The only mandatory safeguard is that the cross-examining lawyer must disclose the statement to opposing counsel if requested.

2. OEC 613(2)—Extrinsic Evidence of Statement (FRE 613(b)) This rule dispenses with the requirement that any particular litany be repeated with the witness before the inconsistent statement can be introduced. In fact, the inconsistent statement can be introduced as extrinsic evidence before the witness is ever asked about it. The rule does impose two safeguards:

a. The witness must have an opportunity to explain or deny the statement.

b. Opposing counsel must have the opportunity to interrogate the witness on the statement.

Practice Tip

If you intend to introduce the inconsistent statement with extrinsic evidence rather than on cross-examination, it is important to make sure the witness will be available at a later time.  You should request the court keep the testifying witness under the court’s subpoena when that witness is done testifying in the first instance.

E. Exceptions to OEC 613 Foundation Requirements

1. Prior Conduct If the witness’s trial testimony is inconsistent with prior conduct rather than a prior statement, the prior conduct could be proven by extrinsic evidence without the safeguards of 613(2). See Legislative Commentary to OEC 613; Weinstein & Berger, Evidence, §613, page 6 (1991).

Counsel must be careful that the conduct being introduced is not a “statement” under Rule 801, which includes “nonverbal conduct of a person, if it is intended by him as an assertion....” ''See United States v. Praetorius, 622 F2d 1054 (2nd Cir. 1979),cert denied449 U.S. 860 (1980) (Rule 613(b) applies to witness’s failure to identify the defendant at an earlier trial because that failure could be termed a statement rather than conduct).

2. Admissions of Party Opponent Admissions of party opponents are not hearsay and are specifically exempted from the requirements of OEC 613.

3. OEC 806—Impeaching Hearsay Declarant If a hearsay statement has been admitted against a party, that party can impeach the hearsay declarant just as if the person had actually testified. This includes use of prior inconsistent statements and the foundation requirements of OEC 613 are not required.

4. Interests of Justice This provision was placed in the rule to give the court some discretion to dispense with a foundation in unusual circumstances, such as when a party discovers an inconsistent statement after a witness has become unavailable. Don’t count on generous application of this rule.

5. Bias or Interest: Oregon Rule If the prior inconsistent statement goes to the bias or interest of the witness, OEC 609(1) provides a different and more extensive foundation before impeachment is allowed. It resembles the Queen Caroline’s cases rules. 609(1) is meant to take precedence over 613 if both apply. See Legislative Commentary to 609(1). There is no equivalent federal rule.

F. Problem Areas

1. Limits on Extrinsic Evidence The cases and commentators talk about the court’s power to limit impeachment on “collateral” matters. There is no general agreement as to what this means or whether this “rule” should be seen as reformulated under Rule 403 as a relevancy and balancing question. See discussion in McCormick, supra, §36, page 77–78; Kirkpatrick, supra, p. 554.

Generally, if the statements concern facts which are relevant to the issues in the case, impeachment will not be “collateral.” Additionally, if the statements are otherwise provable to discredit the witness’s testimony, i.e. to show bias, interest, or lack of knowledge, then they will be admitted on that basis. (Impeachment for bias or interest is never collateral!)

2. OEC 607—Impeaching Own Witness Traditional rules of evidence did not permit a party to impeach his own witness. Rule 607 specifically permits this practice. This creates a potential problem when a party calls a witness for the sole purpose of eliciting a statement and then introducing the prior statement in the guise of impeachment, which statement the party really hopes will be accepted as fact. See discussion in Weinstein & Berger, Weinstein’s Federal Evidence §607[01]; Kirkpatrick, supra, text accompanying §607, p. 475. In State v. Staley, 165 Or App 395, 995 P2d 1217 (2000), the state could not impeach a victim with prior statements to police simply because she testified she could not remember the incident at trial.

3. Impeachment Using Unconstitutionally Obtained Statements Under federal law, a defendant may be impeached using statements that were obtained in violation of constitutional rights. Harris v. New York, 401 U.S. 222 (1971); Michigan v. Harvey, 494 U.S. 344, 110 S Ct 1176 (1990). The Oregon Constitution has been interpreted to disallow impeachment use of statements seized in violation of Article I, section 12. ''See State v. Isom, 306 Or 587 761 P2d 524 (1988).

4. Extrinsic Evidence When Witness Admits Statement When the witness has been cross-examined about a prior inconsistent statement and has admitted making it, can the proponent of the statement still introduce it as extrinsic evidence? Both Kirkpatrick and McCormick indicate that the answer is unclear.

G. Prior Inconsistent Statements As Substantive Evidence—OEC 801(4)(a)(A) (FRE 801(d)(1)(A))

If the prior inconsistent statement was given under oath subject to the penalty of perjury, the rule changes prior law to allow the statement as substantive evidence by declaring that it is not hearsay. The rule requires the statement to have been under oath at a trial, hearing or other proceeding, or in a deposition.

The rule requires that the witness testify at the trial and be subject to cross-examination about the statement. However, the rule does not require that the prior statement had been one subject to cross-examination. Therefore, grand jury testimony inconsistent with a witness’s trial testimony may be received as substantive evidence.

Note: This rule, in combination with OEC 607 on impeaching one’s own witness, creates a special type of problem if the prosecution in a criminal case attempts to convict based on the recanted sworn testimony of their own witness. See Kirkpatrick, supra at VII-21 to 22; Weinstein & Berger, supra, 801–107; Goldman, “Guilt By Intuition: The Insufficiency of Prior Inconsistent Statements to Convict,” 65 North Carolina Law Review 1 (1986).

III. The Use of Prior Statements to Impeach

A. Some Tactical Considerations

After understanding the legal framework outlined above, the advocate must decide how to use the impeachment material. The rule allows the lawyer to ignore the witness and introduce the inconsistency through other witnesses. Before choosing that path, however, you must determine if that is the most effective way to present the impeachment. Listed below are some of the questions which may be important in choosing your method.

1. Is the inconsistency important to my case? How does it help? How does it fit in to my “theory of defense?” 2. Will the witness deny the prior statement? Can I make the witness eat the statement if he or she does try to deny it? How? 3. Will the impeachment impress the jury more if I do it with the witness on the stand or by introducing the extrinsic evidence? 4. How will the witness try to explain the inconsistency? How will the prosecutor try to rehabilitate the witness and can I anticipate that in my cross-examination? There are no automatic answers to any of these questions. Obviously, the advocate needs to have identified and organized any inconsistencies prior to making the decision whether or not to cross-examine and impeach the witness. As a general rule, it would seem that impeachment of the witness on the stand with a powerful inconsistency would be more impressive drama and more likely to reach the jury than a contradiction introduced by another witness sometime later in the case. With that in mind, a general approach for cross-examination is set forth below.

B. Examples

1. Police Reports. When the inconsistency is in a police officer’s report, it is quite simple to go through an extended litany with the officer about his training and experience in writing reports and how important that aspect of his training and job are. The officer has to concede that he writes accurate reports and includes important details and knows how important the reports are to other people as well as to himself when he later needs to refresh his memory before testifying. 2. Memory. Whenever the prior statement was relatively close to the event you may want to attempt to get the witness to concede how memory works. Most people will admit that one’s memory is fresher closer to the event. Some attorneys prefer to establish the time sequence and leave the fresh memory argument for closing. 3. Deposition. When the prior statement is from a deposition, you can and should set the entire scene for the deposition: the fact that there was a court reporter who administered an oath to the witness; that the opposing attorney told the witness that if any question was unclear the witness should request clarification; that the attorney asked whether the witness was experiencing any mental or physical problems that would interfere with his ability to recall events or understand the questions, and that the witness assured the attorney there were none; that the reporter prepared a transcript and gave the witness a copy to review; and that the witness signed the transcript after reviewing it without making any changes.

IV. Impact on Other Parts of Defense Case

A. Trial Preparation & Discovery

Gear your investigation and preparation toward finding as many statements as possible. Use the discovery laws and procedures to get as many reports as you can. (See Chapter 2, Discovery.) Any inconsistent statements by state witnesses should be available under Brady, in addition to the Jencks Act and ORS 135.815.

Have your investigator interview all potential state witnesses and write reports. You may consider employing a procedure where the investigator reads back the witness’s statement to him from the report so that he can verify that it is accurate. If the witness initials the report or orally adopts it as correct, there will be much less room for him to wriggle off the hook if he changes his story at trial.

B. Voir Dire

If you know you will be using prior inconsistent statements during the trial, you may want to inject the issue into the case during voir dire. Caveat: Proceed with caution if your client is going to testify and is on his or her third version of events. You might broach the issue by asking some of the jurors why they think somebody might change his or her story. You might suggest some answers to them, but will often find that they can think of plenty of appropriate answers themselves.

C. Choice of Language

During the course of the trial, you will probably not want to use the words “prior inconsistent statement” when trying to communicate anything to the jury. Choose non-legal language such as the witness “changed the story,” “couldn’t keep his story straight,” “first version and second version,” “the witness changed her tune,” etc.

D. Demonstrative Evidence

Use charts to illustrate the difference between the two statements. You can use such devices during the impeachment, during argument, or both.

E. Substantive Evidence Instruction

If you have used a prior inconsistent statement which was admissible as substantive evidence under OEC 801, ask the court to instruct the jury at the time the evidence comes in and/or at the close of the case that this evidence is not just hearsay but is admissible for the truth. Who knows? Somebody on the jury might understand or just be impressed.

F. False-in-part Jury Instruction

UCrJI 1023 is a standard instruction about a witness being distrusted if testimony is found to be false in part. See also ORS 10.095(3). You will want to get this instruction and preview it for the jury during closing argument.
