I. The Motion (on the Threshold of Trial)

A. Definition of a Motion in Limine

A motion in limine is a pretrial motion that seeks to limit or control the admissibility of certain evidence. There is no specific rule in the Oregon Evidence Code allowing or disallowing such motions. The Latin term in limine does not signify any special form of pleading, but simply means “preliminary” or “on the threshold.”

Any request for an evidentiary ruling that is made in advance of trial which seeks an order to exclude or regulate the production of potentially inflammatory evidence before the jury, and which seeks relief on the ground that suggestive or uncontrolled revelation of that evidence to the jury may unfairly prejudice the determination of the case, may be regarded as a motion in limine. 20 Am Jur Trials441, 445 (1973).

Oregon courts began taking an open view to such pretrial determinations of evidentiary matters in such cases as State v. Foster:

The pretrial motion to limit evidence, commonly called a “motion in limine,” provides a legal procedure to flush out problems to be encountered during the trial, before a jury is contaminated with the evidence. An objection to evidence, with a motion to tell the jury to disregard it, is a poor alternative. The old cliché, “you can’t unring a bell,” still applies.

State v. Foster, 296 Or 174, 182, 674 P2d 587 (1983).

Motions in limine can be used to move to exclude reference to particular evidence or facts in all phases of the trial, including voir dire and closing argument.

B. Authority for a Motion in Limine

Motions in limine are permitted at the trial court’s discretion under the omnibus hearing statute, ORS 135.037. State v. Madison,290 Or 573, 575–76, 624 P2d 599 (1981); State v. Troen,100 Or App 442, 444, 786 P2d 751 (1990).

The relevant parts of ORS 135.037 read as follows:

(1) At any time after the filing of the accusatory instrument in circuit court and before the commencement of trial thereon, the court upon motion of any party shall, and upon its own motion may, order an omnibus hearing. (3) The court, at the time of the omnibus hearing, may also consider any matters which will facilitate trial by avoiding unnecessary proof or by simplifying the issues to be tried, or that are otherwise appropriate under the circumstances to facilitate disposition of the proceeding.

Depending on the rules of the particular jurisdiction this hearing may happen in the days and weeks prior to trial or may occur immediately before the jury selection.

Note that a court is not required to rule on all possible evidentiary issues at the time of the motion in limine. State v. Cheshier, 41 Or App 141, 145–146, 597 P2d 839 (1979); State v. Staley, 142 Or App 583, 588, 923 P2d 650 (1996).

C. The Form of a Motion in Limine

In an ideal world with unlimited time to prepare for each part of a case, the motions in limine would be written in order to provide notice to the opposing party and to the court. In practice, however, there may be reasons to not inform the prosecutor ahead of time of the defense intent to challenge certain evidence. Some issues may be better raised immediately before trial.

Non-written oral motions may simply serve as a reminder or an opportunity to clarify the court’s and prosecutor’s understanding of the rules of evidence. For instance, defense counsel might orally move the court to limit reference to certain topics. “Your honor, the defense would move in limine to exclude reference to the defendant’s prior convictions by the police officer. I anticipate that may be a problem because of several paragraphs in the police report discussing how the booking photos from a previous burglary case were of great use in allowing the civilian witness to make a possible ID. I want to be clear that testimony regarding the previous charges should not be mentioned in front of the jury.” This gives the court an opportunity to consider the issue of how the photos would be introduced without references to prior bad acts.

II. Types of Motions in Limine

There are two main types of motions in limine. The first main type of motion in limine is a motion seeking to prohibit certain evidence from being presented to the jury. The second type seeks a permissive order by informing the court of a particular issue and seeking permission to elicit certain evidence.

A. Prohibitive Order

A motion in limine seeking a prohibitive order regarding evidence is one in which the movant asks the court to prevent the opposing party from eliciting certain evidence. It is a request to forbid the other party from raising a certain issue with witnesses or forbidding the opposing party from moving to admit certain evidence. For example, the defense attorney may ask to prevent the prosecutor’s use of excessively gory photos of an injury as too prejudicial. Perhaps there are other similar photos that are less graphic and are appropriate. The defense attorney should seek pretrial to prohibit the use of the more graphic photos as exhibits.

A pretrial motion seeking such a prohibitive order should be done to keep such evidence away from potential jurors. Certain issues may lend themselves to pretrial decisions while in other cases the court might reserve a ruling until later in trial to see how the evidence develops.

B. Permissive Order

As an alternative to asking to prohibit evidence, other motions in limine may seek the court’s permission to elicit particular testimony or admit certain physical evidence. An attorney may want to avoid committing error or causing a mistrial or may want to essentially warn the court that the opposing party may object to certain evidence and the question is best dealt with pretrial.

An example involving testimony evidence might be the question of a hearsay exception under OEC 404(3) showing a prior bad act to prove motive, opportunity, intent, preparation or absence of mistake. For instance, the prosecutor may wish to inform the court that he intends to offer evidence to show that the defendant had been convicted of three previous shoplifting charges at a particular store to show absence of mistake. By getting the court’s permission pretrial, the prosecutor allows the defense attorney to make the appropriate objection and record and limit the range of the questioning on the previous act instead of objecting at the time of testimony in front of the jury. In such a situation the state would seek a permissive order while the defense would seek a prohibitive order.

An example involving physical or demonstrative evidence might be when there is evidence that is logically relevant but for which there may be an apparent question of prejudice. For example, a defense attorney may wish to subpoena a K-9 dog handler to bring the animal to court to demonstrate the size of the animal and the handling techniques in a case in which the defendant is accused of resisting arrest. There may be a logical relevance, but the defense attorney may wish to clarify the court’s wishes pretrial regarding such atypical evidence.

There are numerous, less unusual examples in which a defense attorney may seek pretrial permission or guidance on the admission of such evidence as recordings, videos, photos or other potentially prejudicial evidence.

III. The Hearing

The hearing on the motion in limine may involve detailed evidentiary proceedings or simple verbal offers of proof by counsel. The court normally has no authority to order a witness to testify on the substance of the case until the witness is called during the normal course of the trial. State v. Nefsted,99 Or App 12, 17, 781 P2d 358 (1989). It is not necessary to reveal the defense case prior to trial. Instead, at this hearing the movant must only show:

1. Anticipation that the adversary will offer evidence that is the subject of the motion in limine.

2. That determination of inadmissibility must be capable of being made prior to the time that the evidence is offered before the jury.

3. Grounds and authority for excluding evidence.

IV. The Ruling

The court may, after a hearing on the motion in limine, come to two types of decisions: an absolute ruling that will be the final determination of the admissibility of such evidence in the trial or a preliminary or contingent motion. Counsel should ask the court to clarify the nature of the court’s ruling.

A. Absolute Rulings

An absolute ruling would be one that decides an issue for the rest of the trial. For instance, after considering the proposed evidence, the court might make a determination that some photos are far too prejudicial to be admitted and that only certain other photos showing substantially the same thing should be admitted instead. This order will control throughout the trial and will not be subject to change based on the course of other testimony or evidence.

B. A Preliminary Ruling

A preliminary ruling might suggest the court’s inclination on a certain issue and informs counsel that the issue should be raised outside the presence of the jury at a later point in the trial in order to allow the court to make a final determination. This allows the court to reserve its ruling until there is a more complete body of evidence before it to allow for a more considered decision and a more thoughtful weighing of the probative versus prejudicial value of the evidence. This may be advantageous or disadvantageous depending on how the trial progresses.

Counsel should be sure to ask to address the issue at a later time outside the presence of the jury in order to preserve any objections.

V. Advantages of Motions in Limine

There are many advantages to dealing with evidentiary matters pretrial. There are numerous reasons a motion in limine may be appropriate, and the advantages or disadvantages will depend on each individual case. Consider the following general reasons to use a motion in limine.

A. Limit Prejudicial Evidence

Dealing with these evidentiary matters pretrial will help insulate the jury from prejudicial evidence. Once the bell is rung, it cannot be unrung. By addressing a matter pretrial, the defense avoids the risk that a witness would blurt out something before counsel is able to object.

B. Eliminate the Need for Impractical or Useless Admonitions

Motions in limine replace ineffective, unrealistic and psychologically invalid admonitions to the jury. Despite the judge’s instructions to a jury to disregard testimony they just heard, the jury will of course already have heard the information and the admonition will only pique their interest. Sometimes you can’t unring the bell no matter how hard the judge tries.

C. Avoid Objections in Front of the Jury

By dealing with objections through a motion in limine, the objecting attorney does not suffer from appearing to hide the truth. If the matter has been dealt with pretrial, the jury is not left wondering what the answer to the question might be should the judge sustain an objection. Counsel does not have to choose between permitting prejudice to be planted by opposing counsel or stimulating the prejudice by objection.

D. Uncover the Opponent’s Case

Pretrial attack at sensitive evidentiary areas may force the prosecutor to disclose what evidence she may offer as well as the legal theory behind that evidence.

E. Lock Witnesses into Statements/Fishing Expedition

By having the witnesses testify beforehand, not only can the defense discover more specifically what the witnesses will testify to, but the witnesses then become bound by their statements.

F. Gain a Better Bargaining Position

A prohibitive motion that disarms the opponent or a permissive one that strengthens the defense case can suddenly make things negotiable.

G. Give the Court More Time to Consider Legal Issues

A judge may have more time to consider evidentiary issues if a complex or lengthy issue is raised pretrial rather than in the middle of a trial. A court is much more likely to consider the evidentiary issues fully and consult the proper legal sources to come to a thoughtful decision if counsel raises the matter before a jury is sitting in a jury room and the witnesses are lined up ready to testify.

H. Streamline the Presentation of the Case

By dealing with evidentiary matters pretrial, the defense attorney will prevent the need for numerous objections and arguments over admissibility and allow the judge and jury to focus on the main issues. This may increase the strength of the defense case and will allow counsel to keep the jury’s focus on the primary issues. (However, depending on the case, the defendant may want the confusion and delays created by frequent objections).

I. Eliminate the Risk that Counsel Will Miss the Issue When it Arises in Trial

By addressing the issues in an organized manner pretrial, the defense attorney will avoid the risk of falling asleep at the switch and missing an objection or allowing a witness to blurt something out before an objection can be raised.

J. Preserve Issues for Appeal

Filing a motion in limine allows the issue to be preserved for appeal later down the line. However, depending on the nature of the judge’s ruling on the motion, it may also be necessary to re-raise the objection at trial. Even if the ruling is absolute, it does not hurt to overpreserve an issue. Counsel can simply say, “Judge, I would like to re-object based on the reasons I argued in my pretrial motion.”

In addition, if the defense lost on the motion in limine and then the evidence comes in at trial in a somewhat different way, it is essential to object by re-raising the issue and making a slightly different argument that corresponds to the change in evidence.

VI. Disadvantages of Motions in Limine

There are also a number of reasons not to make a motion in limine and rely instead on objections to evidence in the course of a trial. A motion in limine may:

A. Waste Time on Nonissues

A motion in limine may force attention at pretrial stage to evidentiary issues that may otherwise not even come up at trial because the evidence might not be offered.

B. Be Denied or Be Untimely

A motion in limine does not allow the court to fully evaluate the evidence in the total trial context. By considering the evidence out of context, the court may engage in piecemeal and fragmented analysis.

C. Deprive the Defendant of a Defense

In State v. Troen, 100 Or App 442 (1990), an animal rights activist was forbidden from raising evidence that would support a choice-of-evils defense after the state moved in limine to exclude photos of lab experiments that the defendant intended to offer as evidence of a choice of evils. The result of the pretrial arguments on this issue led to the denial of the defendant’s choice-of-evil defense.

D. Allow an Appeal By the State

ORS 138.060(3) permits the state to appeal from a pretrial order suppressing evidence. This statute extends to any pretrial order rejecting evidence that the state intends to offer. “When a defendant raises an objection to evidence in advance of trial, he takes the risk that the state may have an opportunity to obtain review of an adverse decision it would otherwise be deprived of if the objection were made during the course of the proceeding.” State v. Hoare, 20 Or App 439, 444–45, 532 P2d 240 (1975). ''See, e.g., State v. Hess,342 Or 647,654, 159 P3d 309(2007); State ex rel. Carlile v. Frost, 326 Or 607, 616, 956 P2d 202 (1998).

E. Suggest Proof to Opposing Counsel that She Had Not Yet Considered

Defense counsel wants to avoid raising an issue if it is not clear that the prosecutor is aware of the issue or proof in question. Failure to prevail after calling such proof to the attention of opposing counsel strengthens her case.

F. Allow the Prosecutor Time to Fix Flaws or Gaps in His Case

A major reason not to make a motion in limine is because the defense attorney may give the prosecutor a chance to fix the problems in the state’s evidence. A defense attorney may want to wait for jeopardy to attach to raise an objection. For example, depending on the experience or sophistication of the prosecutor, defense counsel may want to save an objection until well into a trial. If a prosecutor fails to properly lay a foundation for some piece of evidence or discovers that they are not able to get a particular statement or fact into evidence, the defense will have gained by not giving them the chance to set the case over or get a continuance to gather the appropriate witness or learn the proper method of submitting the evidence. In the appropriate case the defense may want to surprise them with an objection when their problem is too late for them to fix.

VII. Matters Which Must Be Raised by Motions in Limine

A. OEC 412 (Rape Shield Law)

If a defendant charged with any degree of rape, sodomy, sexual abuse, or any attempt of those crimes wishes to offer proof of the victim’s past sexual behavior for the purpose of establishing bias, refuting scientific or medical evidence, or for some other relevant purpose, he must file a motion requesting admission of such evidence, together with a written offer of proof, at least 15 days before trial. OEC 412(4)(a).

The rule does not allow admission of opinion or reputation evidence regarding the sexual behavior of the victim. OEC 412(1)(a).

When the evidence is newly discovered or the issue making the evidence relevant is newly raised, the 15-day requirement may be excused. In addition, the notice and hearing requirements cannot be interpreted to conflict with the right of confrontation in Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution. State v. Rieter,65 Or App 304, 307, 672 P2d 56 (1983); Lajoie v. Thompson,201 F3d 1166 (9th Cir. 2000).

The state may appeal before trial an order allowing admission of the evidence. OEC 412(4)(c); State v. Wright,97 Or App 401, 405, 776 P2d 1294,rev den,308 Or 593 (1989).

B. OEC 803 18(a)(b) (Child Hearsay—Sexual and Domestic Violence Complaints)

This rule creates a hearsay exception for statements of children under the age of 12 who are victims of sex abuse and domestic violence. The exception applies in civil, criminal, and juvenile court proceedings. The statements cannot be admitted unless it is made known to the opposing party at least 15 days before trial the moving party’s intention to offer the statements and the particulars of the statement. OEC 803 18(a)(b).

The Court of Appeals has “previously recognized that, if evidence satisfies the criteria for admissibility under OEC 803(18a)(b), it does not violate a defendant’s confrontation rights under Article I, section 11.” State v. Shelton, 218 Or App 652, 657, 180 P3d 155 (2008)(referencingState v. Reed,173 Or App 185, 192, 21 P3d 137,rev den,332 Or 559, 34 P3d 1177 (2001); Statev. Renly,111 Or App 453, 461, 827 P2d 1345 (1992).

Note: Keep in mind that the constitutionality under the Sixth Amendment of this type of hearsay, as well as all cases involving hearsay exceptions, should be thoroughly researched since Crawford v. Washington, 541 US 36, 124 SCt 1354, 158 LEd2d 177 (2004) modified the basis for admissibility to nontestimonial statements. Determining “whether an unavailable child victim’s statement is testimonial almost invariably ‘will depend on a variety of considerations.’ ” State ex rel Juv. Dept. v. S. P.,218 Or App 131, 149, 178 P3d 318 (2008). “The major considerations include the primary purpose of the questioner who elicited the statement, the primary purpose of the declarant, and the ‘nature and extent of police or prosecutorial involvement.’ ” Shelton, 218 Or App at 658.

C. DHS Files

In State v. Wattenbarger,97 Or App 414, 416–17, 776 P2d 1292,rev den,308 Or 331 (1989), the Court of Appeals held that the trial court should have conducted an in camerainspection of CSD [now DHS] files to determine whether exculpatory evidence exists which should be given to the defense. (See Chapter 2, Discovery.) ''See also State v. Warren,304 Or 428, 434–35, 746 P2d 711 (1987). Under ORS 135.873, a trial judge is prohibited from delegating his statutory duty to examine a file to the prosecutor, State ex rel Dugan v. Tiktin, 313 Or 607, 617, 837 P2d 959 (1992), or defense counsel. State ex rel Carlile v. Lewis, 310 Or 541, 544–45, 800 P2d 786 (1990).

VIII. Matters Recommended to Raise by Motions in Limine

A. Scientific Evidence Objections

“When proffered scientific evidence raises issues of scientific validity, those issues should be addressed by the trial court in a separate OEC 104(1) [preliminary questions] hearing, preferably in advance of trial.” State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285, 307, 899 P2d 663 (1995). One of the main reasons to deal with this issue pretrial is to make certain that the judge performs the “gatekeeper” function by “…ensur[ing] that the trier of fact does not attach an undue aura of reliability to ‘scientific’ evidence that is not scientifically valid.” Id.at 302. Some recent issues that have arisen are challenges to vertical nystagmus tests, State v. Bevan, 235 Or App 533, 233 P3d 819 (2010), as well as challenges to diagnoses of sexual abuse absent physical evidence, State v. Southard,347 Or 127,218 P3d 104 (2009).

Advantages to raising these issues pretrial include:

winning the admissibility argument at the trial court level, developing and preserving a basis for an appeal, and previewing the State’s evidence and potentially weakening it at trial. (As to this last objective, many of the issues affecting the admissibility analysis will also provide useful cross material at trial, and in the admissibility hearing without the jury present you can ask probing questions with comparatively little risk even if you don’t know the answers.)

Kevin Sali, Challenging Scientific Evidence: Choosing Your Strategy, at Oregon Library of Defense.

However, there are some disadvantages to challenging the evidence pretrial. Filing a motion in limine allows the state to prepare better against challenges to their evidence. It also gives them a right to appeal the judge’s decision where they would not be able to during trial. Sali, Challenging Scientific Evidence, supra.

IX. Stipulations

A. Entering into Stipulations

A stipulation is an agreement of the parties that a particular piece of evidence or fact should be considered by the fact finder without the usual need of a party to present the evidence through a witness.

Stipulations can sometimes help the defense by limiting the threats of prejudicial evidence and effective government witnesses. Once the parties have entered into a stipulation, however, the defense counsel must remain vigilant. Additional proof of the stipulation is generally left to the discretion of the court. If the offered proof is prejudicial to the defense and not probative of anything more than the stipulation, then the evidence is inadmissible and there should be an objection. State v. McKendall, 36 Or App 187, 198, 584 P2d 316 (1978), ''overruled on other grounds by State v. Lopez,147 Or App 314, 936 P2d 386 (1997); State v. Zimmerlee, 261 Or 49, 54, 492 P2d 795 (1972); State v. Turner,40 Or App 285, 290, 594 P2d 1304 (1979).

A further tool to limit additional evidence that supplements the facts stipulated too is found in the evidence code. OEC 403 reads:

Although relevant, evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury, or by considerations of undue delay or needless presentation of cumulative evidence.

So if there is already a stipulation to a fact then an attorney should consider utilizing OEC 403 to limit additional information about that fact as unfairly prejudicial or cumulative.

B. Bilateral Stipulations

Common bilateral stipulations include such matters as scientific reports, chain of custody, prior convictions and reports of physical or mental examinations.

“A stipulation is the functional equivalent of a judicial admission; indeed, the two terms are sometimes used interchangeably.” State v. Porter, 202 Or App 622, 626, 123 P3d 325 (2005) (referencingState v. Anderson, 137 Or App 36, 42, 902 P2d 1206,rev den, 322 Or 362 (1995); In re Marriage of Kathrens, 47 Or App 823, 827, 615 P2d 1079 (1980)). As such, it is binding on the party entering into the stipulation. Bailey v. Mead, 260 Or 410, 415, 492 P2d 798 (1971). However, if additional evidence is introduced which is at variance with the stipulation and is not objected to, it is permissible to argue that the fact at issue is not proved. State v. Knoke, 14 Or App 187, 194, 512 P2d 1353 (1973).

Parties cannot stipulate to admit evidence that would otherwise be excluded by statute or case law. For instance, polygraph test results are inadmissible even when stipulated to by the parties. State v. Lyon, 304 Or 221, 231, 744 P2d 231 (1987).

C. Stipulated Facts Trials

A stipulated facts trial is often preferable to a guilty plea. For example, in order to preserve certain issues for appeal, such as the denial of a motion to suppress, a guilty plea will not suffice. Trials are required, and a stipulated facts trial is sufficient to preserve legal issues. State v. Wright, 109 Or App 495, 497, 820 P2d 824 (1991) (“…one purpose of stipulating to the facts, rather than simply pleading guilty, is to preserve issues for appeal…”). Specific issues raised in the motion to suppress must be included in the stipulated facts in order to be preserved for appeal. For example, if the evidence suppressed was found in a house, but the stipulated facts mentioned only a car, then the issue raised in the motion to suppress may not be preserved for appeal.

If a written jury waiver is not executed by the defendant prior to trial, reversal is usually mandated. ORS 136.001; State v. Huntley, 112 Or App 22, 24, 827 P2d 918 (1992). Although stipulating to facts is not the same thing as pleading guilty, the due process issues are similar. The trial court must apply the same test to a motion to withdraw stipulated facts that it applies to a motion to withdraw a guilty plea. Wright, 109 Or App at 497. Depending on the judge and jurisdiction, there may be a certain form for a waiver of jury trial and motion for a stipulated facts trial.

Occasionally, a careless prosecutor will submit a case on stipulations, having omitted an essential element. See State v. Turner, 235 Or App 462, 234 P3d 993 (2010) (grant of judgment of acquittal was appropriate where stipulated facts made no direct mention of venue and contained no information from which venue could be inferred). Alternatively, the facts might be agreed on but the question of whether the facts constitute a crime is still at issue and the judge is free to acquit, given the stipulated facts. See State v. Williams, 117 Or 238, 243 P 563 (1926). In such cases, counsel should move for a judgment of acquittal.

For further consideration on stipulated fact trials, see the discussion of conditional pleas in the November/December 2006 and January/February 2007 issues of The Oregon Defense Attorneypublished by OCDLA.

X. Exclusion of Witnesses from the Courtroom

As a general rule, defense counsel will want to move to exclude witnesses from the courtroom under OEC 615. State witnesses should be excluded when possible from all pretrial hearings and prior to the start of voir dire examination. An attorney should be sure that any such motion to exclude is always on the record and be sure to state the rule the motion is based upon. Defense counsel should request that the court’s order be communicated to the state’s witnesses as it is often difficult to monitor who is coming in and out of a courtroom.

There may be strategic and tactical reasons not to exclude witnesses from the courtroom. The defense witnesses may be sympathetic or show ongoing family or community support for the defendant. Likewise, a state’s witness might be unsympathetic and their behavior or presence in the courtroom may help the defense position in the eyes of the jury. A decision and the appropriate motion should be made at the very beginning of the trial process.

A. Statutory Basis for Exclusion

OEC 615 (ORS 40.385) reads in its entirety:

At the request of a party, the court may order witnesses excluded until the time of final argument, and it may make the order on its own motion. This rule does not authorize exclusion of: (1) A party who is a natural person; (2) An officer or employee of a party which is not a natural person designated as its representative by its attorney; (3) A person whose presence is shown by a party to be essential to the presentation of the party’s cause; or (4) The victim in a criminal case.

This rule authorizes the court (at the request of a party or on its own initiative) to exclude witnesses until final argument. The language of the rule could indicate to some readers that the court must exclude all witnesses, so be aware that defense witnesses may be subject to the same exclusion.

According to the legislative commentary to OEC 615, the time period of the exclusion potentially includes voir dire, opening statements and offers of proof. It was enacted to prevent a witness from being influenced by hearing the testimony of a prior witness. State v. Vosika, 83 Or App 298, 302, 731 P2d 449 (1987), modified on recons 85 Or App 148, 735 P2d 1273 (1987). The exclusion rule also helps prevent a witness from learning the types of questions the opposing lawyer may ask on cross-examination. State v. Larson, 325 Or 15, 26, 933 P2d 958 (1997). The rule is a modified version of Federal Rule 615. The federal rule specifically excludes witnesses “[…] so that they cannot hear the testimony of witnesses[…].”

In a criminal case, the trial court is required to exclude witnesses at the request of a party unless the opposing party objects. State v. Cetto, 66 Or App 337, 339, 674 P2d 66 (1984); Legislative Commentary to OEC 615.

If the opposing party objects, the court must exercise its discretion and weigh the reasons offered by the objecting party against the policy favoring exclusion. In State v. Bishop, the court noted:

Thus, if the record contains some showing of good cause for not excluding the witnesses, and if the trial court made a reasonable choice between the good cause shown and the policy favoring exclusion, its decision will not be disturbed on appeal. But if the record contains no reason for not excluding witnesses, or an insufficient reason, then the trial court has abused its discretion.

State v. Bishop, 7 Or App 558, 564, 492 P2d 509 (1972).

However, even where the trial court errs or fails to exercise its discretion, it is not reversible error. The record must show that the defendant was prejudiced. State v. Larson, 325 Or 15, 28, 933 P2d 958 (1997).

B. Exceptions to the Exclusion Rule

1. Under OEC 615(2), the court may not exclude witnesses who are “an officer or employee of a party which is not a natural person designated as its representative by its attorney.” This is more likely to be an issue in civil trials in which there may be a need for a single individual to act as both witness and corporate representative in a trial. In criminal cases, however, the state is a party and is not a “natural person” and therefore may have its designated representative/ witness present during all phases of the trial.

In State v. Cooper, 319 Or 162, 874 P2d 822 (1994), the Oregon Supreme Court held that the state may designate a city police officer, who investigated and filed state criminal charges, as its “officer” representative under exemption to the rule governing exclusion of witnesses for “officer” designated as party’s representative. Presumably this could be applied to other representatives of the state such as DHS caseworkers, probation officers or other representatives of investigating state agencies.

As explained in footnote 2 of Cooper, the “purpose of the order of exclusion under OEC 615 ‘is to prevent any possibility that the testimony of one witness may be influenced or tainted by what he learned from hearing the testimony of another.’ State v. Burdge, 295 Or 1, 9, 664 P2d 1076 (1983).”

The state’s witness can be excluded if allowing the state’s witness to remain in the courtroom would unduly impair defendant’s right to cross-examination; however, “a criminal defendant is [only] ‘guarantee[d] an opportunity for effective cross-examination, not cross-examination that is effective in whatever way, and to whatever extent, the defense might wish.’ ” State v. Alexander, 105 Or App 566, 569, 805 P2d 743 (1991) (quoting Delaware v. Fernsterer,474 U.S. 15, 20, 106 S Ct 292 (1985)). Since the Confrontation Clause is implicated, counsel could consider objecting to testimony of the state’s representative as a violation under Crawford v. Washington,supra, if the testimony of the officer could be influenced by the testimony of other witnesses. If the officer does not testify first or is called for redirect, the objection may have an impact since their testimony could be tainted by the prior witnesses. Counsel should brainstorm ideas regarding how the non-exclusion of the state’s representative may impact your client’s rights and raise them at trial.

The criminal defendant is a “natural person.” There is no exception under this subsection that would apply to any defense witness.

2. Under OEC 615(3), the court may not exclude witnesses “whose presence is shown by a party to be essential to the presentation of the party’s cause.” The Oregon Court of Appeals has held that allowing a police officer witness to remain in the courtroom despite exclusion of other witnesses did not violate the defendant’s rights to cross-examination and confrontation under Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution because the state had established that his presence was essential to assist the prosecution under OEC 615(3). State v. Alexander,supra.

A defense investigator witness could presumably qualify under this section if that investigator is providing ongoing help or analysis during the trial. A defense attorney relying on this exception will have to establish that the investigator was essential to the defense presentation of the case to the court.

3. Under OEC 615(4) the court may not exclude a witness who is “the victim in a criminal case.” Who is a victim? ORS 131.007 defines a victim as:

[T]he person or persons who have suffered financial, social, psychological or physical harm as a result of a crime and includes, in the case of a homicide or abuse of corpse in any degree, a member of the immediate family of the decedent and, in the case of a minor victim, the legal guardian of the minor. In no event shall the criminal defendant be considered a victim.

In State v. Stookey, 119 Or App 487, 850 P2d 1167 (1993), a victim’s fiancée was not a “victim” entitled to remain in the courtroom during testimony of other witnesses. The error, however, was harmless.

The right of the victim to be present is found in Article I, section 42(1)(a), of the Oregon Constitution which gives victims the right “to be present at […]any critical stage of the proceedings held in open court when the defendant will be present.” Article I, section 42(6)(c), places the determination of who is a victim under the authority of the district attorney.

3a. The prosecuting district attorney or the police agency that initially responded to or is investigating the case is responsible for notifying the victim of the various stages of the criminal proceedings. ORS 147.417(1), (4). ORS 147.510(2) (which does not apply to juvenile proceedings) requires the state to notify the court “[a]t the beginning of each critical stage of the proceeding” if the victim is present, and if not, whether the victim has requested advance notice of the proceedings.

Failure to notify a victim is not grounds for overturning a conviction or adjudication and does not affect the validity of a guilty plea. Article 1, section 42 (2), ORS 147.417 2(a)-(b).

However, State v. Barrett, 350 Or 390, 255 P3d 472 (2011), held that remanding the defendant’s case for resentencing was an acceptable remedy to the violation of the victim’s right to be notified of, and present at, sentencing. The victim initially invoked her rights in a timely manner and before defendant had been sentenced; therefore, she was entitled to her proposed remedy of resentencing, provided it did not violate the defendant’s federal constitutional rights. Per the Supreme Court’s interpretation of United States v. DiFrancesco,449 US 117, 101 S Ct 426, 66 L Ed 2d 328 (1980), “sentences [are] not comparable to acquittals for double jeopardy purposes,” Barrett,350 Or 390. and remanding the case for resentencing was constitutional under the Double Jeopardy Clause. Nevertheless, in a footnote the Court noted that “had the prosecutor and the trial court engaged in the colloquy required by ORS 147.510, this constitutional violation likely would have been avoided.” Id.

C. Timing of Motions to Exclude Witnesses

OEC 615 does not specify a time when counsel should move to exclude witnesses, but since the purpose is to keep witnesses from hearing testimony discussed at all phases of the trial, it should be made as early as possible. It appears that the motion should be allowed at any time if it is not opposed. See State v. Bishop,supra. However, if an attorney waits too long, the trial court may be able to justify its denial of the motion, and the denial will not be deemed an abuse of discretion on appeal. Larson, 325 Or at 17.

XI. Sequestration

While the exclusion of witnesses from the courtroom may prohibit witnesses from hearing the testimony of other witnesses with an ear toward conformity and consistency, such an order alone will not prohibit the witnesses from talking in the hall and communicating during the course of the trial. Therefore, a “sequestration” order prohibiting the witnesses from any contact with other witnesses may be necessary to ensure the fairness of the proceedings. See State v. Burdge,295 Or 1, 664 P2d 1076 (1983). This preserves the witness’ memory and testimony and avoids tainting the testimony by knowledge of other witnesses’ specific and actual testimony.

It is important to note, however, that there is no clear authorization for such an order under the rules of evidence or Oregon case law. Defense counsel should be prepared to justify such a motion through argument.

A. Stipulation of Parties

The best way for counsel to get an order forbidding witnesses from communicating with each other is a stipulation with the opposing party. If both parties stipulate to such an order, the court can simply agree to enforce the agreement of the parties. The “practice that [the Oregon Supreme Court] encourages is that the witness exclusion/sequestration agreements be formalized with the court prior to the commencement of trial.” Burdge,295 Or at 5 n1.

Burdge is the case generally relied on for justification of such orders. An inquiry into sanctions for violation of such an order in a trial in which the parties stipulated to a sequestration order may be necessary. This is the cleanest way to obtain the order since the method has a foundation in case law.

B. Oregon Rules of Evidence and Statutory Basis for Sequestration Orders

As stated above, there is no specific rule allowing a court to issue such orders. However, ORS 1.010(5) may be a partial justification for such an order. The statute reads, in part:

Every court of justice has power…:

(3) To provide for the orderly conduct of proceedings before it or its officers.

(4) To compel obedience to its judgments, orders and process, and to the orders of a judge out of court, in an action, suit or proceeding pending therein.

(5) To control, in furtherance of justice, the conduct of its ministerial officers, and of all other persons in any manner connected with a judicial proceeding before it, in every matter appertaining thereto.

Defense counsel should argue that the court has the inherent power to order the witnesses (who are persons connected with a judicial proceeding before it) to not discuss their testimony with each other.

Furthermore, the purpose of the exclusion orders clearly authorized by OEC 615 will be undermined if the witnesses are permitted to discuss testimony in the hall outside the courtroom.

C. Federal Case Law

There is a slightly larger body of case law in the federal system containing arguments justifying such a sequestration order. For a short discussion of the federal cases on this issue, a suggested source is Oregon Evidence by Laird Kirkpatrick, at section 615.03[6] (5th ed, 2007).

The federal cases focus on the undermining of the purposes of the exclusion order. Defense counsel should argue that the exclusion order can be made meaningless if the witnesses can step from the courtroom and discuss their testimony and the attorney’s lines of questioning with the other witnesses.

XII. The Rights of a Criminal Defendant

The trial court can refuse counsel an opportunity to consult with the client during a short recess in the trial in the middle of the defendant’s direct examination and before the beginning of cross-examination. Perry v. Leeke,488 US 272, 283–84, 109 S Ct 594 (1989). However, for things like an overnight recess, such an order would unconstitutionally interfere with the defendant’s right to consult with an attorney. Geders v. United States, 425 US 80, 96 S Ct 1330 (1976).

If there is a recess in the trial in the middle of testimony by a witness, defense counsel may want to make a special motion requesting that the prosecutor not discuss testimony or facts of the case with the interrupted witness during the recess.

Remember that a sequestration order would likely apply to all witnesses including defense witnesses, so be sure to inform defense witnesses as needed. Counsel should inform defense witnesses that the order applies both inside and outside the courthouse and that they could be courting trouble if they discuss their testimony during a recess. Depending on local courthouse layout and the local neighborhood, a violation of such an order may be witnessed by state witnesses, jurors or even the judge herself.

XIII. Enforcement and Sanctions of Exclusion and Sequestration Orders

A. Enforcement

Keep in mind that the witnesses need to be informed of exclusion or sequestration orders. Counsel will want to ask the judge to directly address the witnesses and have the court inform the witnesses of the court’s order. This will make a judge more likely to severely sanction any offenders should there be a violation. Such notice also helps avoid any misunderstandings or unintentional violations.

The best practical way to help enforce such orders may be to place an observer with the witnesses waiting outside a courtroom. Consider stationing a legal assistant or nonwitness investigator in the hallway to monitor conversations between state witnesses.

B. Sanctions

Rule 615 does not address the sanctions available for violation of an exclusion order. The appropriate remedies are within the discretion of the trial court, with consideration to be given to whether the witness or parties acted in bad faith and the extent of any resulting prejudice. There is no presumption of prejudice. State v. Larson,139 Or App 294, 911 P2d 953 (1996).

Counsel should suggest possible sanctions and request that the court:

• refuse to allow the witness to testify;

• declare a mistrial;

• allow the violation to be brought out on cross-examination of the witness;

• instruct the jury to weigh the credibility of the witness in light of the witness’s knowledge of earlier trial testimony;

• hold the witness in contempt.

In State v. Burdge, the court noted that the sanction of precluding a witness’s testimony in a criminal case was “too grave a sanction” when the violation “was not intentional and not procured by a connivance of counsel.” 295 Or at 14. The court, also, stated that the trial court should review its sanction options “in an escalating manner, first considering whether contempt is a sufficient sanction, or proceeding by instructing the jury as to the witness’s violation of the order or, ultimately, the preclusion sanction.” Id. at 15 n 11.

XIV. Preservation of Error and Final Note

Motions in limine are limited only by the rules of evidence and the imagination of the moving attorney. If there is a basis for exclusion of evidence, a defense attorney can ask for exclusion through a motion in limine.

If it is the defense’s own evidence that is excluded by a preliminary motion in limine, be sure to make an offer of proof to preserve the record for appeal. State v. Busby,315 Or 292, 298-300, 844 P2d 897 (1993). See also Chapter 11, Making a Record and Offers of Proof.

Perhaps the most valuable use of a motion in limine is that it avoids the jury hearing objections to evidence that should be excluded, therefore the evidence is not highlighted for them. If the motion is granted, the jury does not hear the evidence. If it is denied, nothing is lost, you just make the objection at trial. See State v. Foster,296 Or 174, 182, 674 P2d 587 (1983); State v. McClure, 298 Or 336, 338–41, 692 P2d 579 (1984).
