Motion for a New Trial

Rankin Johnson, IV

I. Introduction

A motion for a new trial is the reexamination of an issue of fact in the same court after a judgment. ORCP 64A (Note: Rule 64 of the Oregon Rules of Civil Procedure is set out as an appendix at the end of the chapter). In a criminal case, only the defendant may move for a new trial. ORS 136.535. A new trial motion is only appropriate following a jury trial, ORCP 64B, but after a bench trial the court can take new evidence and return a different verdict, ORCP 64C, which, in the author’s humble opinion, looks a lot like a new trial.

II. Procedure and Time Limits

ORCP 64 provides the timing and procedure for moving for a new trial. A motion for new trial must be filed no later than ten days after filing of the judgment, unless the trial court allows additional time. ORCP 64F. The motion may be filed before the judgment is filed. State v. Howard,205 Or App 408, 134 P3d 1042 (2006). The court cannot allow additional time beyond the 55-day limit within which the motion must be decided, as discussed below.

The motion must be accompanied by any necessary affidavits. Affidavits are required when the basis of the motion is found in ORCP 64B(1)–(4), namely: irregularity in the proceedings or abuse of discretion, jury or government misconduct, accident or surprise, or newly discovered evidence. ORCP 64D.

The state has ten days after the filing of the motion in which to submit any counter-affidavits, unless the trial court allows additional time. ORCP 64D.

The motion must be heard and decided by the court within 55 days after entry of the judgment. ORCP 64F. If the motion is not heard and decided within that time period, it is conclusively considered denied. ''Id. See also McCollum v. Kmart Corp,347 Or 707, 226 P3d 703 (2010) (letter opinion explaining grant of new trial entered within 55 days ineffective when order granting new trial entered after 55 days).

Practice Tips

Be aware of the 55-day time period for the trial court to reach a decision on the motion. It doesn’t matter if the judge is on vacation or something else prevents the motion from being heard within that time: if it is not heard by the 55th day after the judgment is entered, the judge loses the power to grant the motion.

For purposes of appeal, a timely filed motion for new trial delays the deadline for filing the Notice of Appeal. A late new trial motion has no effect on the Notice of Appeal timeline unless, probably, the trial court accepts the filing and rules on the merits within 55 days of the entry of judgment. Relying on that sounds risky. If the motion is not timely, it has no effect. If a motion for new trial has been timely filed, the Notice of Appeal must be filed within 30 days after the earlier of either 1) the date of entry of the order denying the motion, or 2) that 55th day on which the motion is deemed denied. In that case, the notice of appeal is due 30 days after the ruling denying the new trial motion. If the Notice of Appeal is filed before the trial court rules on the motion, the trial court loses jurisdiction to grant the motion. Johnstone and Johnstone, 152 Or App 801, 804, 955 P2d 762 (1998).

Note the time for the filing of the motion is triggered by the filing of the judgment, while the 55-day period for a decision on the motion commences when the judgment is entered. Filing and the entry of the judgment are two distinct events which may occur on different days.

In State v. Alvarez-Vega, 240 Or App 616, 251 P3d 199 (2011), the court suggested in dicta that if a case is remanded for resentencing, the time within which to seek a new trial is not reset, even though a new judgment will be entered after resentencing.

III. Grounds for a New Trial

The grounds for a motion for new trial under ORCP 64B are identical in both civil and criminal cases, and the case law suggests similar rules for each. Since the motion for a new trial is a statutory procedure, only those grounds set forth in the statute are properly raised in a motion for new trial.

The grounds under which a court may grant a new trial are broad. SeeMcCollum v. Kmart Corp,228 Or App 101, 207 P3d 1200 (2009) (discussing permissible grounds for motion)reversed on other grounds347 Or 707, 226 P3d 703 (2010).

A new trial motion does not preserve an earlier error without a contemporaneous objection, but developing the argument in the written new-trial motion may help to preserve the error. ''See, e.g., State v. Sundberg, 349 Or 608, 247 P3d 1213 (2011) (discussing rule). Accordingly, appellate decisions usually focus on the earlier ruling and underlying substantive law rather than the ruling on the new-trial motion; there is little authority relating directly to the substantive scope of new trial motions.

A. Irregularities in Proceedings or Abuse of Discretion—ORCP 64B(1)

A new trial may be granted when there has been an “[i]rregularity in the proceedings of the court, jury or adverse party, or any order of the court, or abuse of discretion, by which such party was prevented from having fair trial.”

Examples: Improperly empanelling an anonymous jury led to a new trial in State v. Sundberg, 349 Or 608, 247 P3d 1213 (2011). The court decided that, under the circumstances, the written new-trial motion helped to preserve the error for review, but the court did not otherwise discuss the right to a new trial as distinct from the right to a non-anonymous jury.

Communication between judge and jury off the record is a basis for a new trial if the communication relates to the issues at trial rather than “housekeeping.” State v. Vettrus, 142 Or App 359, 922 P2d 673 (1996) (remanding for hearing on the substance of the communication).

Incompetency of counsel is not an issue that may properly be raised in a motion for new trial. State v. McGhee, 31 Or 1089, 572 P2d 333 (1977). McGheerefers to an earlier case that apparently stands for the proposition that tactical decisions by counsel are not a basis for appeal; in other words, it is about the merits rather than the procedure. In the right case, (such as counsel’s admission of negligence), a new-trial motion based on counsel’s errors might succeed. Federal law discourages but does not bar raising ineffective assistance claims in new-trial motions. ''E.g. United States v. Birges, 723 F2d 666, 670 (9th Cir. 1984).

B. Misconduct of the Jury or Prevailing Party—ORCP 64B(2)

1. Jury Misconduct

Juror misconduct sufficient to obtain a new trial is rare. The evidentiary requirements are strict, and the misconduct must be severe.

To support a new trial, juror misconduct must rise to the level of contempt of court. Carson v. Brauer, 234 Or 333, 382 P2d 79 (1963). Carson suggests that jurors are held to a higher standard in criminal cases. If there is legally sufficient evidence to prove facts supporting a new trial, the grant of a new trial is discretionary for the trial court.'' Ertsgaard v. Beard,310 Or 486, 800 P2d 759 (1990).

Misconduct of a single juror could not materially affect defendant’s rights where verdict was 11–1. State v. Barker,26 Or App 297, 552 P2d 565 (1976). That doesn’t make sense, because a biased juror, if there was one, might be able to influence the votes of other jurors, but that’s still what the case says. If this comes up in your case, try to put on evidence about how the biased juror affected the other jurors.

Affidavits and testimony from jurors are not admissible to prove the juror’s own conduct during deliberations. Carson,234 Or 333''; Ertsgaard,310 Or 486; State v. Woodman,341 Or 105, 138 P3d 1 (2006).

Practice Tip

The rule excluding juror affidavits is nonsensical. Ertsgaard discusses the source of the rule, but it cites old common-law, legislative commentary, and secondary sources, but no actual current law besides case law. Case law cites some hoary old cases, but it seems to be an application of a common-law rule intended to prevent juror harassment. That’s a worthwhile goal, but so is avoiding illegal verdicts, and any potential witness is subject to a certain amount of harassment.

If a juror averred that he had voted to convict because people of the defendant’s ethnicity tend to be criminals, or that he had been bribed by the state, that would not be admissible. It seems likely that the defendant would be entitled to some procedure to attack the verdict, but with recent state supreme court decisions in Ertsgaardand Woodman, the general ban on juror affidavits is probably secure.

Ertsgaardsuggests that the ban is limited to prohibiting proof of a juror’s own conduct during deliberations; the court notes that some kinds of juror misconduct that might entitle a party to a new trial could only be proved by evidence from other jurors. That would permit a juror’s affidavit that the juror was bribed, but not that the juror voted to convict based on ethnicity. Also, if juror affidavits are useful for any purpose, than the stated purpose of the ban (avoiding juror harassment) is not advanced by the ban.

Examples: A comment by a deputy sheriff that the defendant had “done something like this before” made within earshot of two jurors warranted a reversal of the defendant’s conviction and a new trial. Dickson v. Sullivan, 849 F2d 403, 408 (9th Cir 1988). Dickson establishes a low bar for prejudice in this context.

In a burglary trial, a juror who had denied prior criminal charges but who had actually stabbed a burglar and had been convicted of assault in the first degree warranted the granting of a new trial. State v. Holcomb, 131 Or App 453, 886 P2d 14 (1994).

Prosecutorial misconduct in arguing, during closing argument, that defense counsel was a pimp and the expert witness a liar warranted grant of a new trial. State v. Lundbom, 96 Or App 458, 773 P2d 11 (1989). Lundbom was a new-trial motion, rather than a more appropriate mistrial motion; as in Sundberg the new-trial motion developed an argument made earlier.

Withholding of significant Brady evidence by the prosecution is a basis for a new trial; State v. Williams11 Or App 255, 500 P2d 722 (1972).

C. Accident or Surprise—ORCP 64B(3)

Accident or surprise that ordinary prudence could not have guarded against is grounds for a new trial. This basis comprises two conditions. First, the surprise can not be due to the lack of diligence of the moving party. Second, the party must immediately move for a continuance when the surprise occurs or the right to a new trial is waived. State v. Gardner, 33 Or 149, 153, 54 P 809 (1898); State v. Scott,12 Or App 79, 504 P2d 1053 (1973).

D. Newly Discovered Evidence—ORCP 64B(4)

In order to warrant the granting of a new trial, newly discovered evidence must meet the following requirements: 1) the evidence will probably change the result if a new trial is granted, 2) reasonable diligence would not have led to discovery of the evidence before or during trial, 3) the evidence cannot, with reasonable diligence, be used during trial, 4) it must be material, 5) it must not be merely cumulative, and 6) it must not be merely impeaching or contradicting of former evidence. State v. Arnold, 320 Or 111, 120–1, 879 P2d 1272 (1994).

Practice Tip

You must be diligent in trying to correct the problems resulting from accident, surprise, or newly discovered evidence. Ask for a continuance or whatever else you need to remedy the issue as soon as possible. Be specific about what you have learned, why you didn’t know it already, what you want to do in response, and why it’s important. Better that you ask and the judge turns you down than you don’t ask.

The test from Arnold does not appear in the text of ORCP 64B. In the right case, it might be worth attacking. For example, if the state’s case was based on a single witness and strong impeachment evidence came to light after trial, it would be worth seeking a new trial regardless of the prohibition on using impeachment evidence to seek a new trial.

E. Insufficiency of the Evidence—ORCP 64B(5)

A party may move for a new trial on the ground of “insufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict or other decision, or that it is against law.” A motion on this ground is addressed to the sound discretion of the court. State v. Gardner, 33 Or 149, 153, 54 P 809 (1898). This ground is subject to the controls of Article VII, section 3, of the Oregon Constitution, which provides that no fact tried by a jury shall be otherwise reexamined in any court of the state, unless the court can affirmatively say there is no evidence to support the verdict. State v. Moore, 194 Or 232, 241 P2d 454 (1952).

Practice Tip

If the evidence is insufficient, you should have moved for judgment of acquittal. After a guilty verdict, the court cannot grant a judgment of acquittal, only a new trial. State v. Metcalfe, 328 Or 309 974 P2d 1189 (1999). It is hard to imagine the court granting a new trial after denying judgment of acquittal. If the trial court erroneously denied a motion for judgment of acquittal, the defendant is entitled to acquittal. A new trial, even if the state finds more evidence, should not affect that entitlement, but no reported case to the author’s knowledge discusses that issue. The defendant in Metcalfethus might have been better off appealing the failure to grant a sua spontejudgment of acquittal, which grants more complete relief than a new trial motion. Both sound like long shots.

F. Error in Law—ORCP 64B(6)

A new trial may be granted on the ground that there was an error in law occurring at the trial and objected to or excepted to by the party making the application. To obtain a new trial on this ground, the error must have been excepted to at the time it occurred. State v. Ellis, 232 Or 70, 92–93, 374 P2d 461 (1962); Wegener v. Walter Kidde & Co., 73 Or App 22, 25, 697 P2d 981 (1985).

Practice Tips

A motion for a new trial will not substitute for making a timely objection, especially for grounds that are not based on unexpected evidence or events. Raise new-trial issues as soon as you are aware of them.

In State v. Sundberg, 349 Or 608, 247 P3d 1213 (2011), the court considered authority cited in the new-trial motion, although the issue initially came up with a less-thorough argument at the beginning of trial. A post-judgment motion for a new trial is too late to raise a wholly new issue for the first time. If the objection is timely made, it need not be raised again in a motion for a new trial in order to preserve the error for appellate review. Similarly, if you did not make a motion for judgment of acquittal, insufficiency of the evidence probably cannot be raised for the first time in a motion for a new trial.

The denial of a motion for a new trial made after entry of judgment is not reviewable on appeal, unless it is based on newly discovered evidence or juror misconduct. State v. Alvarez-Vega, 240 Or App 616, 251 P3d 199 (2011). Accordingly, the argument on appeal should be about the error that occurred at trial if there was one. However, ''see, e.g., Sundberg, id.; Lundbom,96 Or App 458, reviewing new-trial motions that were related to issues raised before the verdict was returned.

Motion in Arrest of Judgment

I. Introduction

A motion in arrest of judgment is a post-trial attack on a legally insufficient accusatory instrument. See ORS 136.500 defining a Motion in Arrest of Judgment as “an application on the part of the defendant that no judgment be rendered on a plea or verdict of guilty.”

The timing is identical to new-trial motions—within ten days of the judgment filing. ORS 136.535, ORS 136.400, ORCP 64F. The timing within which the court must rule is different, discussed below.

II. Statute

ORS 136.500 provides that the motion may be founded on either or both of the grounds specified in ORS 135.630(l) and (4), the statute specifying the grounds for demurrer, “and not otherwise.” Specifically, the motion may be founded when it appears upon the face of the accusatory instrument that 1) the grand jury which found the indictment had no legal authority to inquire into the crime charged because the same is not triable within the county, and 2) the facts stated do not constitute an offense. The issue need not be raised in a demurrer, but the wording of the indictment is construed strictly against the state as a demurrer but liberally when attacked as a motion in arrest of judgment. State v. Burns,213 Or App 38, 159 P3d 1208 (2007).

An argument that a statute under which the defendant is charged is unconstitutionally vague can be made for the first time in a motion for arrest of judgment.'' State v. McKenzie, 307 Or 554, 771 P2d 264 (1989). By raising the issue as a motion in arrest of judgment, the defendant retains the possibility of an acquittal (which cannot be appealed), and then can litigate the motion in arrest (which the state can appeal).

A claim that the statute is unconstitutionally overbroad is not subject to a motion in arrest of judgment but must be raised by way of demurrer or motion for judgment of acquittal. State v. Solomon, 133 Or App 184, 890 P2d 433 (1995). In the right case, Solomon is worth attacking; the court does not explain why a vague statute can be challenged post-trial but an overbroad one must be challenged pretrial. Solomonis hard to reconcile with McKenzie.

A motion in arrest of judgment may not be based upon the other four grounds for demurrer listed in ORS 135.630. State v. Carpenter, 29 Or App 879, 884, 565 P2d 768 (1977), nor on the statute of limitations. State v. Parker,119 Or App 105, 849 P2d 1157 (1993).

III. Procedure and Time Limits

A motion in arrest of judgment must be made within the same period of time allowed to file a motion for a new trial. If a motion in arrest of judgment is filed together with a motion for new trial, the motions may be made and heard as the court directs. ORS 136.500.

ORCP 64 controls the timing for new-trial motions. The motion must be filed within ten days after filing of judgment, or within such further time as the court may allow. ORCP 64F. However, the 55-day “deemed denied” period in ORCP 64F does not apply to motions in arrest of judgment. State v. Starr, 210 Or App 409, 150 P3d 1072 (2007). Accordingly, while the motion is pending, there is no final judgment and appeal is not possible. Id. See Appendix C.

IV. Effect of Allowance of Motion

A motion in arrest of judgment has the same effect as a dismissal. If the motion is allowed, the defendant is placed in the same situation in which he or she was before indictment. ORS 136.505. ''But see State v. Eberhardt, 225 Or App 275, 201 P3d 915 (2009) (rejecting argument that the trial court should have granted demurrer, holding that any error was harmless). The court can order the defendant held if trial evidence makes it likely that the state can come up with a proper accusatory instrument and convict the defendant of something. ORS 136.515.

If the motion is granted, the prior verdict is not a bar to a subsequent prosecution if it appears that the defendant can be convicted of something. ORS 136.515. If, however, the evidence “appears insufficient to charge the defendant with any offense, the arrest of judgment operates as an acquittal.” ORS 136.525. There are no reported cases construing this statute.

The state may appeal from an order arresting judgment. ORS 138.060(2); State v. McKenzie, 307 Or 554, 771 P2d 264 (1989).
