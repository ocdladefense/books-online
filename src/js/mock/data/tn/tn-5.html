Chapter 5. Admissibility of Electronic Evidence

By Adrienne Chin-Perez

I. Introduction

Like any other form of evidence, electronic evidence must meet certain requirements to be admissible at trial. Lorraine v. Markel Am. Ins. Co., 241 F.R.D. 534, 538 (D. Md. 2007). Electronic evidence presents a number of issues for trial counsel, paramount among them are hearsay and authentication. Given the evolving nature of electronic evidence, however, different types of electronic evidence have required different approaches. In particular, various new types of evidence have resulted in a range of approaches to authentication and hearsay questions. The following sections address how various courts analyze admissibility issues posed by electronic evidence.

II. E-mail

A. Authentication

Authentication is “the act of proving that something (as a document) is true or genuine, esp. so that it may be admitted as evidence[.]” Black’s Law Dictionary(9th ed. 2009). Under Oregon Evidence Code (OEC) § 901(1), “[t]he requirement of authentication or identification as a condition precedent to admissibility is satisfied by evidence sufficient to support a finding that the matter in question is what its proponent claims.” Given the ease of editing messages and fabricating e-mails, authentication of e-mail evidence is an important concern. However, most courts find that the bar for authentication is low. Some courts have found that the testimony of a lay witness with personal knowledge is sufficient to authenticate e-mails. See Shea v. Texas, 167 S.W.3d 98, 105 (Tex. Crim. App. 2005);United States v. Bansal, No. 05-193-2, 11, 2006 WL 2246203, at *13 (E.D. Pa. Aug. 1, 2006). Other courts have found that e-mail addresses containing a party’s name in it and the context of the e-mail are strong evidence of authentication.SeeUnited States v. Siddiqui, 235 F3d 1318, 1322 (11th Cit. 2000),cert. denied, 533 U.S. 940 (2001); United States v. Safavian, 435 F. Supp 2d 36, 41 (2006); Minnesota v. Bohlman, No. A05-207, 2006 WL 915765, at *7 (Minn. Ct. App. Apr. 11, 2006). Still other courts have found that if a party makes a plan via e-mail to meet and shows up, that is enough to authenticate. For example, if X makes a plan via e-mail to meet Y at the mall and X shows up at the mall, that can be enough to authenticate.SeeMassachusetts v. Amaral'', 941 N.E.2d 1143, 1147 (Mass. App. Ct. 2011).

E-mails may also be self-authenticating under OEC § 902 (g). Often times, e-mails sent from businesses will contain information that is specific to that business or the businesses will have special access to e-mails. For example, inSuperhighway Consulting, Inc. v. Techwave, Inc., the court found that the e-mail was self-authenticating because it was produced during discovery by the company that sent the e-mail. The court said that because the e-mails were produced by the purported sender, that was enough to authenticate them. 98 CV 5502, 1999 WL 1044870, at *2 (N.D. Ill. Nov. 16, 1999).

Indeed, the ease of fabricating e-mails is typically not an obstacle to authentication and does not warrant special consideration. Courts have come to this conclusion for two main reasons. First, courts agree that false claims of authorship are a realistic danger with e-mails, but the same danger exists with traditional letters. See Commonwealth v. Purdy, 945 N.E.2d 372, 377-78. Second, courts also agree that the reliability of the e-mails should be decided by the jury. Parties who object to e-mails for authenticity reasons can introduce evidence to call the authenticity of the e-mails into question. Therefore, it is the judge’s role to be the gatekeeper and the jury’s role to decide how much weight to assign the evidence. See United States v. Safavian, 435 F. Supp 2d 36, 41 (2006).

B. Hearsay

The rule against hearsay can be a substantial obstacle to the admission of e-mails because most e-mails have past e-mail messages embedded within them, thereby creating a hearsay within hearsay issue. Each message must therefore satisfy a hearsay exception to be admissible. Some e-mails may not be hearsay because they have been adopted as statements of a party-opponent. As discussed below, under OEC § 801(4)(b)(B), a statement is not hearsay if it is offered against a party and “the party has manifested the party’s adoption or belief in its truth.” Both hearsay exemptions and exceptions can be useful to admit e-mail evidence.

The most common hearsay exceptions used to admit are those commonly referred to as excited utterance, OEC § 803(2); then-existing mental, emotional, or physical condition, OEC § 803(3); recorded recollection, OEC § 803(5); and business records, OEC § 803(6). However, there is some debate as to whether all business-related e-mails are business records excepted from the rule against hearsay. Some courts find that business-related e-mails are not necessarily business records under OEC 803(6) because the proponent must show “some evidence of a business duty to make and regularly maintain records of this type.” United States v. Ferber, 966 F. Supp. 90, 98 (D. Mass. 1997)accord Rambus, Incl v. Infineon Techs. AG, 348 F. Supp 2d 698, 707 (E.D. Va. 2004). Some of these courts admit the e-mails under the present sense impression exception, but Oregon does not have a hearsay exception for present sense impressions. On the other hand, many courts have found that such e-mails are business records. See United States v. Stein, S1 05CRIM.0888 LAK, 2007 WL 3009650 (S.D.N.Y. Oct. 15, 2007); State v. Sherrills, 2008-Ohio-1950, 2008 WL 1822406, at *6 (Ohio Ct. App. Apr. 24, 2008);LeBlanc v. Nortel Networks Corp., CIV.A. 3:03-CV-65, 2006 WL 839180, at *5 (M.D. Ga. Mar. 30, 2006).

A hearsay exception will not be necessary if the out-of-court statement is statement by a party-opponent, OEC § 801(4)(b). If the declarant is a party-opponent, the statement is not considered hearsay. Another person’s statement can be adopted by a party-opponent under OEC § 801(4)(b)(B). If “the party has manifested the party’s adoption or belief in its truth,” then the statement has been adopted and is not defined as hearsay. Adoption most commonly occurs when the party-opponent answers a question. Typically, not only the answer comes in, but also the question. Therefore, an e-mail may be adopted even if the e-mail was not sent by the party-opponent.

E-mails that are forwarded may also be adopted. In [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=Sea-Land+Serv.,+Inc.+V.+Lozen+&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38&case=3217101368264661730&scilh=0 Sea-Land Serv., Inc. V. Lozen Int’l, LLC], the court held that a cover note was sufficient to constitute adoption of a forwarded e-mail and, therefore, the forwarded e-mail was not considered hearsay and was admissible. 285 F3d 808, 821 (9th Cir. 2002). If a party-opponent manifests “adoption or belief in [an e-mail’s] truth,” through an introductory statement to a forwarded e-mail, the forwarded e-mail will likely be deemed adopted and non-hearsay.

A party may adopt another’s statement not only through affirmative acknowledgment, but also through silence. The general standard is “whether the circumstances as a whole show that the lack of a denial is so unnatural as to support an inference that the undenied statement was true.” See United States v. Flecha, 539 F2d 874 (2d Cir. 1976). For example, inState v. Carlson, the defendant was charged with the unlawful possession of methamphetamine. When the officers asked about marks on the defendant’s arm, the defendant said that he was injured at work. The defendant’s wife then contradicted him and said they were from drugs. The defendant just “hung his head and shook his head back and forth.” The court held that the defendant’s response was too ambiguous and could not be seen as adoption of his wife’s statement. State v. Carlson, 311 Or 201, 214, 808 P2d 1002 (1991). Though the defendant’s actions inCarlson'' were too ambiguous to constitute adoption, Oregon courts have found adoption by silence. In Walls v. Clark, the court found that statements made by the plaintiff’s husband were adopted when the plaintiff said nothing. 252 Or 414, 417, 449 P2d 141 (1969). Adoption by silence may be more difficult to show with respect to e-mails but a lack of e-mail reply could be “so unnatural as to support an inference that the undenied statement was true.”

Finally, the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination may impact whether a statement may be deemed adopted by silence. Statements by the defendant in a criminal proceeding are inadmissible to prove guilt unless the statements were made before arrest or after a valid waiver of the defendant’s Fifth Amendment privilege. See generally Doyle v. Ohio, 426 U.S. 610 (1976); Jenkins v. Anderson, 447 US 231 (1980). Therefore, adoption and adoption by silence may not be possible, depending on when the statement was made and when the defendant purportedly adopted the statement.

C. Privilege

Evidentiary privilege rules apply to any type of evidence and e-mails are no exception. E-mails pose special considerations, however, with respect to the concepts of both waiver and reasonable expectation of privacy. If e-mails are transmitted over an insecure or monitored network, the sender and/or recipient’s privilege may be waived because the network user had no reasonable expectation of privacy. That means that if X, who is an employee of ABC company, sends an e-mail to his wife, Y, the e-mail could fall outside of privilege due to a lowered expectation of privacy. United States v. Etkin, 07-CR-913(KMK), 2008 WL 482281, at *4 (S.D.N.Y. Feb. 20, 2008). One court set forth a four-pronged waiver test that looks to whether the “. . .corporation maintain[s] a policy banning personal or other objectionable use, . . . [whether] the company monitor[s] the use of the employee’s computer or e-mail, . . . [whether] third parties have a right of access to the computer or e-mails, and . . .[whether] the corporation notif[ies] the employee, or was the employee aware, of the use and monitoring policies?” In re Asia Global Crossing, Ltd., 322 B.R. 247, 257 (Bankr. S.D.N.Y. 2005).

The attorney-client privilege also extends to e-mails, just as it extends to other forms of communication. The content of an e-mail is what determines whether an e-mail is a privileged attorney-client communication, not the fact that a communication takes the form of an e-mail. Barton v. Zimmer Inc., CIV.A. 1:06-CV-208, 2008 WL 80647, at *5 (N.D. Ind. Jan. 7, 2008).

Key Concepts for E-Mail

The ease of altering e-mails affects the weight of the evidence, not its authentication.

Be careful of hearsay within hearsay when trying to admit a string of e-mails (i.e., the e-mail response and previous e-mail messages in the chain).

It is possible to adopt e-mails by forwarding them or replying to them.

III. Authentication of Instant Messages

Under OEC § 901(1), “[t]he requirement of authentication or identification as a condition precedent to admissibility is satisfied by evidence sufficient to support a finding that the matter in question is what its proponent claims.” Courts vary on the threshold for authenticating instant messages (IMs). One court found that an IM transcript was authenticated when one party testified that the screen name in the transcript was his, that he wrote it, and that he printed the transcript from his computer. In re F.P., 2005 PA Super 220, 878 A.2d 91, 94 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2005). Another court found that IMs were authenticated when both parties testified what the appellant’s screen name was, that the IMs were used to plan a meeting, and that the appellant showed up to the meeting. United States v. Tank, 200 F3d 627, 630-31 (9th Cir. 2000). Some courts have even lower authentication requirements and only require testimony by a witness with personal knowledge of the creation of the chats. See Adams v. State, 2005 WY 94, 117 P3d 1210 (Wyo. 2005).

Courts have declined to authenticate certain IM transcripts. For example, in United States v. Jackson, the court found that chat transcripts were not authentic where the defendant claimed that parts of the conversation were missing after an FBI agent copied and pasted the chat into a word processing document. 488 F.Supp 2d 866, 871, 73 Fed.R.Evid. Serv. 959 (D. Neb. 2007). In a rare case, one court found that IMs were not authenticated because the accuracy of the evidence was called into question by a malfunction on the agent’s computer. Further, because there was no evidence that the malfunction of the agent’s computer was explained or corrected, the IMs could not be authenticated. People v. Johnson, 376 Ill. App. 3d 175, 314 Ill. Dec. 969, 875 N.E.2d 1256 (1st Dist. 2007).

Note: The way in which IMs are documented may affect what showing is required to authenticate them. For example, if IMs are printed directly from the program in which they were sent, less evidence of authentication might be required than when IMs are copied and pasted into a word processing document and then printed.

Key Concepts for Instant Messages

The specificity of the conversation can help authenticate the chat.

Similar to e-mail, making plans via IM and then following through with the plan can be enough to authenticate the IMs.

The format in which the evidence is presented can affect the showing needed to authenticate the IMs.

IV. Text Messages

A. Authentication

Under Oregon Evidence Code (OEC) § 901(1), “[t]he requirement of authentication or identification as a condition precedent to admissibility is satisfied by evidence sufficient to support a finding that the matter in question is what its proponent claims.” Authentication of text messages becomes more complicated when text messages are transcribed rather than printed out from the phone or provider’s records. One court held that the transcript of text messages was sufficiently authenticated by testimony from the transcribing agent and co-conspirators; even though the text messages had been deleted from a phone and from the provider’s records. United States v. Culberson, 05-80972, 2007 WL 1266131, at *2 (E.D. Mich. Apr. 27, 2007).

Aside from the complexities created by transcription, evidentiary standards similar to those governing e-mail typically govern text messages, as well. In Manuel v. State, the court explicitly stated that text messages could be authenticated by applying the same factors as applied to e-mails. 357 S.W.3d 66, 75 (Tex. App. 2011). The factors were:

(1) consistency with the e-mail address in another e-mail sent by the alleged author; (2) the author’s awareness, shown through the e-mail, of the details of the alleged author’s conduct; (3) the e-mail’s inclusion of similar requests that the alleged author had made by phone during the time period; and (4) the e-mail’s reference to the author by the alleged author’s nickname.

Manuel, 357 S.W.3d at 75. Courts have also treated cell phone numbers as similar to e-mail addresses where the phone numbers serve as circumstantial evidence tending to show who the sender of a message was. For example, if “joe_smith@gmail.com” sends an e-mail, the fact that the e-mail was sent by “joe_smith@gmail.com”can be used as circumstantial evidence to authenticate the e-mail as an e-mail from Joe Smith. Similarly, if Joe Smith’s phone number is 555-555-5555, and a text is sent from 555-555-5555, the phone number can be used as circumstantial evidence to authenticate the text message as a text message from Joe Smith. State v. Eleck, 23 A.3d 818, 821 (Conn. App. Ct. 2011); Commonwealth v. Koch, 2011 PA Super 201, 39 A.3d 996 (Pa. Super. Ct. 2011), reargument denied (Nov. 22, 2011), appeal granted, 44 A.3d 1147 (Pa. 2012). Testimony from a recipient of the text messages can aid in authenticating the text messages but the proponent must establish that the person testifying is actually the person who received the text messages. E.D.T. ex rel. Adamah v. Tayson, 09-CV-5477 (FB), 2010 WL 2265308 (E.D.N.Y. May 28, 2010); Sanders v. Mohtheshum, 10-CV-02897-KLM-DW, 2011 WL 6329866 (D. Colo. Dec. 19, 2011).

B. Hearsay

Text messages are out-of-court statements and, therefore, if offered for the truth of the matter asserted, must satisfy a hearsay exception. Each text message is treated as a statement, similar to any other out-of-court statements, so each text message must satisfy an exception. Flagg v. City of Detroit, 827 F. Supp. 2d 765, 775 (E.D. Mich. 2011).

A hearsay exception may not be necessary if the statement was made by a party-opponent, under OEC § 801(4)(b). If the declarant is a party-opponent, the statement is not considered hearsay. Some statements may be adopted by a party-opponent and become admissible as a party-opponent statement under OEC § 801(4)(b)(B). If “the party has manifested the party’s adoption [of the text message] or belief in its truth,” then adoption has occurred. Adoption occurs most commonly by answering a question. However, as with any other type of evidence, silence may be enough to adopt third-party text messages. No cases specifically address adoption of text messages (either explicitly or through silence), but under the general rule, a court must decide “whether the circumstances as a whole show that the lack of a denial is so unnatural as to support an inference that the undenied statement was true.” ''See United States v. Flecha, 539 F2d 874 (2d Cir. 1976). Most likely, courts that address the adoption of text messages will analyze text messages using the same framework used to determine whether an e-mail has been adopted by a party-opponent.

C. Best Evidence

Typically text messages fall under OEC § 1004(1) and, therefore, the best evidence rule does not preclude introduction evidence of a text message’s content other than the “original” text message itself. As long as the original text messages were not destroyed in bad faith, testimony about the text messages is permissible. State v. Espiritu, 176 P3d 885, 892-93 (Haw. Sup. Ct. 2008).

Key Concepts for Text Messages

A transcript of the chat may be admissible.

Authentication of a text message is similar to authentication of an e-mail.

The rule against hearsay applies to text messages just as it applies to more traditional forms of evidence.

V. Social Networking

A. Authentication

Authentication of material on social networking websites is governed by OEC § 901(1). In determining the admissibility of material from social networking websites, courts have typically evaluated the admissibility of individual items rather than making blanket determinations regarding the range of material that might be created or found on a social networking website. Put differently, social networking sites like Facebook and Google+ often have expansive functionalities. Users can not only share their interests, but also instant message with friends. A user might make an out-of-court statement by sharing a third party’s web content, or by commenting on another user’s posts. Accordingly, courts have applied different standards for authentication to different types of evidence from social networking sites.

1. Profiles The content of profiles varies depending on the social networking site, and the amount of information contained in a profile affects the requirements for authentication. Profiles on the most popular social networking websites, Facebook and MySpace, include pictures, posts from the user, posts from other users, interests, quotes, and other personal information. In contrast, in Griffin v. State, 19 A.3d 415, 415 (Md. 2011), the court found that the birth date of the alleged user and a photo of the alleged user was not sufficient to authenticate a print out of the alleged user’s MySpace profile.

Some courts focus on how distinctive and individualized the profile is. In People v. Goins, the defendant attempted to introduce the victim’s MySpace profile at trial. No. 289039, 2010 WL 199302, at *2 (Mich. Ct. App. Jan. 21, 2010). Although the court ultimately affirmed the exclusion of the MySpace profile under a different rule, the court found that the MySpace profile was authenticated. Goins, at *2. The court found that because the MySpace profile contained “details of the assault that fit within what a reasonable person would consider to be ‘distinctive content’ not generally known to anyone other than [the victim], [the] defendant, or someone in whom one or the other confided,” the page was authenticated. Goins, at *2. Similarly, in Tienda v. State, the court found that because the profile included the defendant’s name, nicknames, city, photographs, references to the crime, and current circumstances, the profile was authenticated. No. PD-0312-11, 2012 Tex. Crim. App. LEXIS 244, at *10 (Tex. Crim. App. Feb. 8, 2012).

Other courts require corroborating evidence if a profile is not sufficiently distinctive. Generally, courts require either testimony from someone that created the profile or observed the content being created, or from a computer expert witness. See Commonwealth v. Williams, 926 N.E.2d 1162, 1165 (Mass. 2010). Testimony that the witness merely viewed the profile page is insufficient; instead, a corroborating witness must have witnessed the profile being created. Experts can be useful because they can provide information “regarding how secure such a Web page is, who can access a My [S]pace web page, whether codes are needed to access, etc.” Williams, 926 N.E.2d at 1172. Courts tend to apply a more stringent standard for authenticating an entire profile from a social networking website than for authenticating e-mails or other individual pieces of content from a social networking website.

Be careful: Even if an entire profile is authenticated, the profile may still be inadmissible under OEC § 403 and OEC § 404. That is, the court may exclude evidence because it is unduly prejudicial or it is offered as character evidence. See United States v. Phaknikone,605 F3d 1099, 1101 (11th Cir. 2010) cert. denied, 131 S. Ct. 643, 178 L. Ed. 2d 486 (U.S. 2010) (State attempted to admit defendant’s MySpace page to prove modus operandi and identity, but court held that although authentication did not preclude admission, the page was inadmissible character evidence under OEC § 404 (2)).

2. Photographs Photographs from social network sites are not self-authenticating. That is, more than the image itself is needed to authenticate the evidence. See People v. Lenihan, 30 Misc. 3d 289, 911 N.Y.S.2d 588 (Sup. Ct. 2010). InLenihan, the defendant offered MySpace photographs as evidence that a witness was a known gang member and may have had a motive to fabricate his testimony. The court found that because the photograph could be easily edited and the defendant did not offer any evidence to authenticate the photograph besides the photograph itself, the photograph was not authenticated.Lenihan, 30 Misc. 3d at 294. Similarly, in People v. Beckley, the prosecution offered a photograph of the defendant from his MySpace page.People v. Beckley, 185 Cal. App. 4th 509, 514 (2010). The court found that a photograph needs to be authenticated by the testimony of someone who was present when the photograph was taken. If such a witness is not available, an expert witness or other authentication evidence must be offered.Beckley'', 185 Cal. App. 4th at 515.

3. Chats/Messages Chats and messages from social networking websites are also commonly offered at trial. Messages are often authenticated in the same manner as e-mails or traditional letters. See People v. Fielding, No. C062022, 2010 WL 2473344, at *4 (Cal. Ct. App. June 18, 2010), review denied (Sept. 1, 2010). For example, one court found that messages were properly authenticated when the victim testified that he sent messages, received replies, and he believed he was communicating with the defendant due to the content of the messages. Fielding, 2010 WL 2473344, at *5. As with e-mail authentication, the possibility that the messages could have been tampered with goes to the weight of the evidence and is a question for the jury. Just as with e-mail, the bar for authentication of social networking messages is comparatively low and authentication does not require testimony from an employee of the social networking website. Dockery v. Dockery, No. E2009-01059-COA-R3-CV, 2009 Tenn. App. LEXIS 717, at *16-17 (Tenn. Ct. App. Sept. 21, 2009).

Chats from social networking websites are often likened to instant messages through instant messenger clients (e.g., AOL Instant Messenger, Google Chat, Yahoo! Messenger, etc.). Some courts require that a chat contain distinctive content to be authenticated, while other courts do not require such distinctive content. Distinctive content can help authenticate because it is unique to the user—for example, nicknames, pictures, or code words. Typically, the content and distinctiveness of a chat is key to authentication. Lorraine v. Markel Am. Ins. Co., 241 F.R.D. 534, 556 (D. Md. 2007). Because chats are linked to a profile, the profile can be used to corroborate the distinctive elements of the chats. For example, in State v. Bell, the court found the following sufficient to authenticate MySpace chats: “(1) he [the victim] has knowledge of defendant’s e-mail address and MySpace user name, (2) the printouts appear to be accurate records of his electronic conversations with defendant, and (3) the communications contain code words known only to defendant and his alleged victims” 882 N.E.2d 502, 511 (Ohio Ct. Com. Pl. 2009), aff’d, No. CA 2008-05-044, 2009 WL 1395857 (Ohio Ct. App. May 18, 2009).

In contrast, some courts have not required distinctive content to authenticate social networking chats. For example, in New York v. Clevenstine, the prosecution sought to introduce chats between the victim and the defendant. 891 N.Y.S.2d 511, 514 (N.Y. App. Div. 2009). To authenticate the MySpace chats, the prosecutor offered testimony from the victim, the defendant’s wife, and a MySpace compliance officer, and the court found that this was sufficient. Clevenstine, 891 N.Y.S.2d at 514.

Note: Because the reliability of the evidence is decided by the jury, counsel seeking to counter social networking evidence should present evidence of the ease of creating forgeries of social networking websites. For example, http://www.fakebookstatus.com/ enables anyone to generate a webpage that looks almost identical to an actual Facebook status posting simply by entering a name, status, date, and time, and uploading a profile picture. The “fakebook” website also allows users to create comments on the fake statuses. Forging social networking information is thus no longer limited to those who obtain login information or are proficient in Photoshop. Though this type of evidence has no bearing on authentication, it is highly relevant to the question of the reliability of proffered social networking evidence.

B. Hearsay

Activity on a social networking site is generally considered an out-of-court statement and, when offered for truth of the matter asserted, must fall within a hearsay exception to be admissible. However, any statement made by a party-opponent is still nonhearsay under OEC § 801(4)(b)(A). Targonski v. City of Oak Ridge, 3:11-CV-269, 2012 WL 2930813, at *10 (E.D. Tenn. July 18, 2012); People v. Oyerinde, 298199, 2011 WL 5964613, at *10 (Mich. Ct. App. Nov. 29, 2011).

If a statement is hearsay, admission requires a hearsay exception. Because the Oregon Evidence Code does not recognize a hearsay exception for present sense impressions, the recorded recollection exception, OEC § 803(5), may pose the best hearsay exception to admit third party statuses and profiles. Though the entire profile may not be admissible under this exception, anything read into the record may be considered for the truth of the matter asserted. However, an adverse party can admit the entire record. The exception for statements of existing mental, emotional, or physical condition, OEC § 803(3), may also support admission of social networking information because statuses and wall posts often discuss current states of mind or future plans. Oregon has not adopted the Hillmon doctrine, however, so a declarant’s statement of intent cannot be introduced to prove the acts of a third party. State v. Clegg, 161 Or App 201, 210 (1999) aff’d but criticized, 332 Or 432, 31 P3d 408 (2001). For example, if Y posts a Facebook status asserting: “I’m going to the market with X,” the portion of the statement regarding X is not admissible, only the statement “I’m going to the market” is admissible. Courts have generally not addressed the application of specific hearsay exceptions to social networking website content, so social networking evidence may present many questions of first impression.

Note: New social networking websites may provide opportunities for party-opponents to adopt others’ statements pursuant to OEC § 801(4)(b)(B). For example, the Pinterest website, http://pinterest.com/, allows users to “pin” interests to their “pinboard” and share their “pinboard” with others. The website includes a comment feature. Many other websites are developing similar setups. Courts may soon have to decide whether particular items pinned to a person’s pinboard are out-of-court statements and whether, when a party-opponent pins an item to her pinboard, she is adopting the statement.

Relatedly, some forms of social networking allow users to communicate in ways that may or may not constitute “out-of-court statements.” For example, Foursquare, a location-based social networking site, posts your “check-ins” on your Foursquare profile and other social networking profiles like Facebook when you “check in” at a location. If a user checks in at a soccer game, a wall post displaying that information will appear on that user’s Facebook profile. Courts have not decided whether these check ins and the resulting displays of information are statements. The current standard provides that if communication was intended, the item or display is a statement. These check-ins may be communication and therefore may constitute statements. The issue becomes even more convoluted when a person checks in another user. The check-in will appear on the profile of the person who checked in and the profile of the person who was checked in. For example, if X checks in at the market and checks in Y, there will be a check-in entry on X’s and Y’s Facebook profiles. Assuming courts decide such “check-ins” of other users constitute statements, proponents will need to cite an applicable hearsay exception for the evidence to be admitted. The novel issues and developing state of the law together provide excellent opportunities for zealous advocacy by creative counsel.

Key Concepts for Social Networking

Focus precisely on what evidence you’re trying to present to jurors. The court is much likelier to admit individual photos or messages than an entire social networking profile.

The law governing social networking evidence has ample room for development because new social networking websites are created frequently.
