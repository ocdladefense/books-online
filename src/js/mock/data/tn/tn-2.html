I. Introduction

“You can get it if you really want You can get it if you really want You can get it if you really want But you must try, try and try Try and try, you’ll succeed at last.” —Jimmy Cliff

Few cases can be successfully tried without engaging in discovery far beyond what the state automatically provides under its statutory obligations, ORS 135.815, et seq. A thorough defense investigation is vital to getting all of the information you should—not only from the state, but from third parties. Pretrial discovery motions must be a routine part of trial practice, not only to obtain facts and evidence to prepare the defense, but to educate the prosecutor (and sometimes the court) about your entitlement to Brady material as well as more novel grounds for disclosure.

This chapter addresses those topic and many more that frequently confront defense counsel heading for trial, including the mechanics of obtaining records through pretrial subpoenas duces tecum and how to get quasi-privileged and privileged records through in-camera review. It features special sections on discovery of digital evidence and checklists of what to request when confronted with inculpatory forensic evidence, co-authored by experts in those fields. Is there a particular item you want disclosed but aren’t sure how to get it? This chapter contains an alphabetical checklist of specific types of items with supporting authorities for production, or suggestions for ways around current unfavorable case law. The chapter concludes with sections on reciprocal discovery obligations and sanctions for discovery violations.

Practice tips are offered throughout the chapter, and sample forms and motions are included in appendices. Check out the OCDLA Criminal Law Formbook, Chapter 8, for a compendium of additional discovery motions. See Appendix A-1 for the list of motions and materials in the Formbook. A working knowledge of Oregon’s discovery statutes and the Brady doctrine is presumed for use of this chapter. For a great primer (or refresher course) on the law of discovery in Oregon, see Criminal Law, Chapter 14, written by Marc Blackman and Kendra Matthews (Oregon State Bar CLE, 2005).

II. Pretrial Investigation

A. Finding Impeachment Evidence

While successful discovery practice means getting the state to give you what you want, only a labor-intensive defense investigation will achieve that goal. The basic nature of defense is to attack the credibility and reliability of the state’s witnesses and evidence, to raise reasonable doubt. That means most of what you want will not fall within the state’s statutory discovery obligations, which primarily require disclosure of information the state intends to use at trial. The Brady doctrine applies to impeachment evidence, but only when the information is shown to be within the possession or control of the prosecution. United States v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667 (1985). A thorough defense investigation will uncover impeachment evidence in the hands of state agents that the prosecutor does not know exists because he has no motivation to look for it, as well as impeachment materials in the hands of third parties. Without that information, you can’t draft specific discovery demands or prevail against a motion to quash a pretrial subpoena duces tecum.

In most cases, the state’s investigation stops once enough information is gathered to make an arrest—only to gear up again under the prosecutor’s direction shortly before trial. That can result in “last minute” and usually damaging disclosures, with little time for the defense team to regroup or respond. Conducting a thorough defense investigation can alert you to potential problems before the eve of trial, particularly regarding impeachment evidence that the state will try to use against your client or defense witnesses. And it is essential to provide you with the information needed to force the state to produce Brady materials and identify sources of records to obtain via subpoena or other means in advance of trial.

Some things that should be done at the outset of a thorough investigation to find impeachment evidence are as follows:

Visit the crime scene with your investigator as soon as possible, both in broad daylight and again under conditions approximating those at the time of the alleged crime, e.g., at night, raining, rush hour, etc. Seeing it for yourself will invariably raise questions about what’s missing from the police reports or provide insights into theories of defense. If you rely on police photos or videos, your knowledge will be constricted both by the officers’ selective perspective and a two-dimensional media view. At the very least, send your investigator to document the scene with video, photos, diagrams, etc., as soon as possible, and go there yourself well in advance of trial.

Conduct in-depth background checks on all state and potential defense witnesses (including your client). Look not just for criminal convictions and the sentences imposed but for restraining orders, domestic relation cases, civil actions, bankruptcies, etc. Those sources can lead to a wealth of impeachment evidence. Get relevant records from those court proceedings and then dig deeper—for example, through a public records request for police reports involved in a witness’s prior conviction. Background checks should include checking social media like Facebook and Myspace—and checking again periodically because even pages restricted as “private” can change status. Juvenile witnesses frequently lie about their age and personas to create a page on social websites.

To effectively cross-examine law enforcement witnesses, you need to know as much as possible about their policies and procedures related to the facts of your case, e.g., crime scene investigation, note retention and report writing, evidence collection, use of informants, etc. Many departments also have in-house training materials on specific topics such as interrogation techniques or gang activities. Most of these materials are available through a public records request. The Attorney General’s Public Records and Meetings Manual is the best resource for what you can get and how to get it, available at www.doj.state.or.us/pdf/public_records_and_meetings_manual.pdf (2011 ed.)(last accessed 8-9-12).

You have the right to inspect public records without having to pay for a copy of the records, ORS 192.430, although the agency may charge fees related to staff time to respond to your request.

Police officer training records are available for a nominal charge via an email request (see sample request with the email address in Appendix A). Visit the Department of Public Safety Standards and Training for more information. www.oregon.gov/DPSST/.

To the extent state witness background information is within the possession or control of the prosecution, it may be subject to statutory discovery obligations. ''See, e.g., State v. Johnson, 26 Or App 651, 554 P2d 624 (1976)(police reports); State v. Carsner, 31 Or App 1115, 575 P2d 339 (1977)(DMV records); State v. Warren, 304 Or 428, 746 P2d 711 (1987) (since confidential child protective service records are accessible to police under what is now codified at ORS 419B.035(1)(a), they are deemed to be in the possession of the district attorney).

Practice Tip: Get as much information as you can on your own, rather than trying to compel the state to produce it. That will allow you to complete your investigation without having tipped off the state to problems with its case. In addition, information available to a party through other means may prevent imposition of sanctions against the non-producing party. State v. Spada, 286 Or 305, 309-310, 549 P2d 815 (1979)(no sanctions imposed when defense could have obtained records through public records request or subpoena); see also State v. Andes, 104 Or App 719 (1990)(defendant’s statutory right to discovery does not include unlimited requests for information about cases of other individuals; state also is not required to compile information for defense where defense has access to info).

Alternatively, impeachment evidence within the prosecutor’s possession or control is subject to discovery under the Fourteenth Amendment of the United States Constitution. See the Discovery Checklist later in this chapter for specific types of impeachment materials. The Brady obligation applies to evidence that may not be known by or in the possession of the prosecutor. A “prosecutor has a duty to learn of any favorable evidence known to others acting on the government’s behalf in the case, including the police.” Kyles v. Whitley, 514 U.S. 419 (1995); Youngblood v. West Virginia, 547 U.S. 867 (2006). However, you will have to prod the prosecutor to do his duty. Furthermore, impeachment information must be disclosed under the Brady doctrine even if it has not been reduced to writing. State v. Fortune, 112 Or App 247,rev den, 313 Or 627 (1992)(victim’s unrecorded inconsistent statement to mother which prosecutor learned during trial should have been disclosed under Brady).

B. Interviewing Witnesses

Interviews will open up additional avenues of investigation. The prosecutor cannot be required to make witnesses available for pretrial interviews, even if those witnesses are in DHS care. State ex rel O’Leary v. Lowe, 307 Or 395, 404, 769 P2d 188 (1989). See also State ex rel Upham v. Bonebrake, 303 Or 361, 736 P2d 1020 (1987). However, the district attorney violates the discovery statutes if he advises witnesses it would be better not to talk with the defense. State v. York, 291 Or 535, 632 P2d 1261 (1981). And defense counsel does the same by instructing a witness not to speak to the prosecutor unless a defense representative is present. State v. Ben, 310 Or 309, 316, 798 P2d 650 (1990).

Formal Ethics Opinion No. 2005-131 explicitly recognizes the right of a party to contact an adverse expert witness in a criminal case, where the expert has been designated as a witness for trial. It is often possible to arrange pretrial interviews with OSP forensic personnel, who will be the most common type of expert witness offered by the state. However, because the defendant is not required to present any evidence, and may make the decision as to which if any witnesses to call after the state rests, a defense expert falls within the attorney-client privilege, OEC 503, until such time as the decision is made to call him to testify in a pretrial proceeding or at trial. See State v. Schmid, 80 Or App 545, 549, 723 P2d 334 (1986) (discussing timing of disclosure). Opinion No. 2005-131 recognizes “[t]he required disclosure of, and the opposing lawyer’s right to contact, a testifying witness does not, however, constitute a waiver of applicable privileges.”

C. Requests to Preserve Evidence

The duty to disclose evidence operates as a duty to preserve evidence once the government has first gathered it. E.g., United States v. Bryant, 439 F2d 642 (D.C. Cir. 1971); State v. Lance, 48 Or App 141 (1980); State v. Clements, 52 Or App 309 (1981). The duty to preserve evidence extends to information and items discoverable either as Brady material or mandated by Oregon’s statutory discovery provisions. If the state had a duty to disclose the evidence, it also had a duty to preserve that evidence so that it would be available for discovery purposes.Clements'', at 313.

In any case involving physical evidence that may be altered or consumed in forensic testing, consider making a formal request that the evidence be preserved. The difficulty is deciding early on whether that will ultimately help or hurt your case because a jury may be more skeptical of forensic test results that the defense could not independently confirm. The failure of law enforcement officers through mere negligence and not in bad faith to preserve evidence that might have been helpful to the defendant does not violate due process, Arizona v. Youngblood, 488 U.S. 51 (1988), but that doesn’t mean such conduct won’t raise reasonable doubt. However, with a timely request to preserve evidence, a due process violation occurs if material evidence possessed by police agencies or the prosecution is not preserved. State v. Clements, 52 Or App 309, 628 P2d 433 (1981); but seeIllinois v. Fisher, 540 U.S. 544 (2004). The defense will have the burden of proving that lost evidence would have been material or favorable to the defense on a motion to sanction the state for a discovery violation. State v. Ketchersid, 37 Or App 97, 586 P2d 119 (1978).

Practice Tip: In drug cases, homicides, assaults, etc., where you would expect evidence that may deteriorate or be destroyed to be gathered by the state, consider immediately filing and seeking an order granting motion to preserve evidence for independent defense testing and, if evidence is likely to be consumed or destroyed during state’s testing, for opportunity to have defense expert present during the state’s testing.

If you believe officer notes will be helpful, immediately send a letter notifying the prosecutor as well as the head of the involved law enforcement agency and individual officers that you consider the notes to be material evidence for the defense, that they will be obtained via subpoena for trial if not provided pretrial, and that this evidence should be preserved. See State v. Johnson, 26 Or App 651 (1976)(police officer’s field notes were paraphrased in a formal report, then destroyed). City police departments are required by OAR 166-200-0100(51) to preserve all officer notes for a minimum of two years, including notes made in digital media such as PalmPilots. Oregon Rule of Professional Conduct 3.4(a) forbids obstructing “another party’s access to evidence or unlawfully alter[ing], destroy[ing], or conceal[ing] a document or other material having potential evidentiary value.” The Ninth Circuit also has recognized that the “rough notes” of officers often provide fertile ground for impeachment of police witnesses; thus, these notes must be preserved because it is ultimately the court’s decision, not the prosecutor’s decision, as to whether the notes are discoverable. United States v. Harris, 543 F2d 1247, 1251–53 (9th Cir. 1976).

Practice Tip: Although Rule 3.4(a) does not apply to non-lawyers, make the same demand for tangible items in the custody of any third party, including the alleged victim. Evidentiary presumptions may come in to play if the witness destroys or alters the item you wanted preserved after getting notification. You should consider having your investigator either hand deliver such letters, for proof of service, or send the letter by both regular and certified mail.

D. Search Warrants Affidavits

Unless sealed by order of the court, all search warrants, supporting affidavits, and returns, are public records once the return is filed with the issuing court. There won’t be a case number—or at least not the same one as the case number assigned upon filing charges against a defendant. However, if you are retained before your client has been formally charged, you can get the search warrant affidavit without waiting for statutory discovery to start. You (or your investigator) simply need to ask the clerk of court for the search warrant files, which are generally kept by date of the return; know the address on the warrant in order to find the documents related to your client quickly.

E. Evidence/Property Records

You can learn a lot from studying Evidence/Property records, those dull-looking forms that list each item seized by law enforcement. You may find out other officers were involved in processing a crime scene beyond those who wrote reports. Often items are seized that are not mentioned or described in police reports; one of them may end up being important to your case. The time that items were seized can help fill a timeline when reports are vague about things such as how long the officers were inside your client’s house before they started to search and seize evidence. The location where an item was seized may be documented in greater (or with luck, conflicting) detail compared to the police reports.

Make a continuing discovery request for all Evidence/Property records showing chain of custody until time of trial and be sure the prosecutor complies. There will be documentation of items sent to the crime lab, with notations about what tests should be conducted. That will allow you to verify whether any forensic reports are missing from “automatic” discovery, as well as provide impeachment documentation if the investigating officer failed to submit items that should have been tested, or failed to request certain testing that should have been done. There should be Evidence/Property forms when officers seize tapes of your client’s jailhouse conversations or mail that will tell you when these items came into the state’s possession. That could be important to establish a discovery violation when these materials are dumped on you the afternoon before—or during—trial.

Practice Tip: If you have a high-profile case or otherwise suspect skullduggery, subpoena duces tecum the property control officer to bring a true and complete copy of all of the records to court the first day of trial, or if relevant to a pretrial evidentiary hearing. You should be entitled to the records if for no other reasons then to challenge chain of custody as well as to determine if there is any evidence you should subpoena for use in the defense case-in-chief; the prosecutor will look like he’s trying to hide the ball if he objects. If the court won’t let you have them, demand that the records be sealed for purposes of appeal. See also ORS 135.873(3) & (4).

F. Viewing Physical Evidence

You have the right to inspect all tangible objects seized as evidence. The discovery statute limits that right to tangible objects the district attorney intends to offer and any items obtained from or belonging to your client. ORS 135.815(1)(d). However, there is no lawful basis for law enforcement to retain an item in evidence—rather than destroy contraband or return an item to its owner—other than it may be offered as evidence at trial. (If it’s evidence the state doesn’t intend to offer, it may be evidence you will offer). You can also assert your right to inspect all evidence under Oregon Rule of Professional Conduct 3.8(d), discussed in more detail in the Pretrial Motions section of this chapter, below.

While you may rely on photographs of items or descriptions of items, there is no sure way to avoid surprise at a trial or a suppression hearing if you do not take the time to arrange and conduct an evidence view. Whenever there are one or more items of evidence that are important to your case, such as whether a knife is the type that constitutes a concealed weapon, whether an item was new or used, whether “miscellaneous documents from kitchen drawer” include anything incriminating or, more likely, exculpatory, it is foolhardy to not view the evidence before trial.

III. Pretrial Motions

The benefits of filing pretrial discovery motions are many, beyond the goal of obtaining information to prepare the defense:

Educate the prosecutor (and perhaps the judge) about your entitlement to Brady material (primarily what “Brady material” means); the prosecutor’s duty to learn about the existence of exculpatory information available from those assisting the prosecution; and that Brady applies to information that has not been reduced to writing. If you assume the prosecutor (or judge) knows all of these things, you are probably wrong.

Get the court to order deadlines for the prosecutor to produce information, which if not met will support your requests for sanctions or a continuance.

Give you an early opportunity to see how your opponent responds to oral arguments and interacts with court personnel, if that’s something you don’t know.

Gain insights into the prosecution’s investigation and theories based on the state’s responses to your motions; for example, you may learn what topics remain “under investigation” as well as what matters have not been investigated by law enforcement that should have been.

Make a solid record that you have made early and specific requests for discovery, so that “last minute” surprises from the prosecution that concern earlier requests can be effectively challenged as untimely and exclusion sought as a remedy.

Convey the message to the prosecutor that you are fighting hard for your client, and that taking this case to trial is going to be a ton of work for the state, which may produce a favorable plea offer.

Practice Tip: Be aware that the Oregon Judges Criminal Bench Book has a simplistic and restrictive section on Discovery. Available on-line at www.courts.oregon.gov/OJD/reference/criminalbenchbook.page (last accessed August 16, 2012). Don’t assume your judge has any in-depth knowledge of the law in this area, and seek oral argument to reiterate your position and bring copies of relevant case law, with sections highlighted, to hand the court and prosecutor.

In every case, file a Demand for Discovery (see Appendix B for sample). That makes your request a matter of record that the court must take judicial notice of if you later move to compel discovery and/or for sanctions. You cannot avoid the duty of reciprocal discovery by choosing to not request discovery from the state, ORS 135.805(1) & 135.835, but you may seek a protective order, ORS 135.873. You may not decline to disclose information simply because the state has not complied with its duty to disclose, without risking sanctions. See ORS 135.865.

A. Specific Requests for Discovery

Most courts require the parties to consult on discovery issues before filing any motions. The best practice is to put your requests in writing, whether by letter or email, for any items that the prosecution does not routinely produce. That will document your efforts to resolve the matter without court intervention, in a timely manner, as well as provide a list that can be checked off as items are provided.

Oftentimes prosecutors will give you requested items without demanding to know your authority requiring production, particularly if the item strikes them as something they’d like to know as well. So make your first request your “wish list” for items you know the DA could get if he wanted to, including requests for information from the alleged victim.

Always include language asking that the prosecutor notify you of any items he will not voluntarily provide so that you can have a discussion prior to seeking court intervention.

If the prosecution does not meet all of your specific requests conveyed in writing, it’s time to start drafting motions, with the following rules in mind.

With the exception of agencies that are “part of the prosecution” or deemed to be within the control of the prosecution, both statutory and constitutional discovery obligations are only applicable to opposing parties and their agents. See State v. Clements, 52 Or App 309, 315 (1981)(information in the possession of private security personnel may be deemed in possession of DA upon proper showing that they function like a police agency). Thus, ordinarily a complainant has no discovery obligations. State ex rel Beach v. Norblad, 308 Or 429, 781 P2d 349 (1989) (court could not order victim to provide access to her home once state released control of the crime scene).

Practice Tip: Don’t give up. Argue that the police had access to this property and therefore the prosecution had or is deemed to have had access, cf., State v. Warren, 304 Or 428 (1987), and that the crime scene produced material which the state intends to offer at trial via the officer’s testimony, expert testimony and usually photographs. Assert that the right to confrontation requires the defense to be given access to the scene itself to effectively question the accuracy of these witnesses’ observations and photographs. Argue that it is more efficient for the court to grant defense pretrial access to the scene in lieu of the more costly and disruptive jury view of the scene; or that, by the time of trial, the scene will have been altered.

Wwitnesses may not be compelled to provide pretrial statements to a party. State ex rel O’Leary v. Lowe, supra; see also State v. Hiatt, 303 Or 60 (1987)(court has no authority to compel psychiatric evaluation of victim/witness to determine competency outside of judicial proceeding; can hold competency hearing with expert present to observe witness and then render opinion, 303 Or at 67). However, evidentiary items in the possession of third parties can be sought by subpoena in advance of trial. See ORS 136.580(2)(discussed below).

B. Brady Motions and Beyond

Under the Due Process Clause of the 14th Amendment of the United States Constitution, the state must disclose all information which is favorable to the defense and material to guilt or punishment. ''E.g., State v. McDonnell, 313 Or 478, 500 (1992)(state has an affirmative obligation to make exculpatory evidence available to the defense). This materiality standard is normally not a heavy burden. Evidence is material as long as there is a strong indication that it will “play an important role in uncovering admissible evidence, aiding witness preparation, corroborating testimony, or assisting impeachment or rebuttal.” United States v. Lloyd, 992 F2d 348, 351 (D.C. Cir. 1993)(citations omitted).

The Oregon Court of Appeals long ago determined that Brady material is not limited to written material and is not limited to statements or witnesses the state intends to call. State v. Fleischman, 10 Or App 22, 32 (1972). The court more recently reaffirmed this rule in State v. Fortune, 112 Or App 247, rev den, 313 Or 627 (1992). In Fortune, during trial in a child rape case, the victim told her mother at a recess that two other men had also molested her. Multiple molestations “were potentially inconsistent with the medical evidence.” The prosecutor knew this but did not disclose the new statement to the defense because he thought it was irrelevant and inadmissible because of the rape shield law. The Court of Appeals affirmed the grant of a new trial because the unrecorded statement was exculpatory and should have been disclosed.

The Court of Appeals has also held that the state must afford the defense access to information or evidence in its possession that could be used to challenge the credibility of a key piece of the prosecution’s evidence, under the Brady doctrine. State v. Michener, 25 Or App 523 (1976)(defense entitled to retest breath test ampules in DUII prosecution).

You will invariably need to make a specific request before the state will produce impeachment information. If a letter informing the prosecutor that you believe particular records exist and must be disclosed under Brady does not get a favorable response, file a motion to compel discovery. (See Appendix C & D for sample Brady motions). Many of the specific items of information that the federal courts have recognized as Brady material are included in the Discovery Checklist section of this chapter. Compendiums of Brady case law are available for download on the Capital Defense Network, www.capdefnet.org, through the “Constitutional Issues: Cases on Point” link (reached through the Habeas Assistance and Training page, “Web Site Contents” link).

Practice Tip: Because the Due Process–based Brady doctrine has no counterpart in the Oregon Constitution, most of the case law on discovery of specific types of information comes from the federal district courts and circuit courts of appeal, which are non-binding on Oregon judges. Look to Oregon case law on the scope of cross-examination to cite to the trial court to back your requests, in addition to federal law. See, ''e.g., State v. Brown, 299 Or 143 (1985)(witness on probation, and had prior fights and quarrels with defendant); State v. Rodriguez, 115 Or App 281 (1992)(witness had been arrested, may harbor a hope for leniency); State v. Hubbard, 297 Or 789 (1984)(en banc)(defendant entitled to impeach officer by cross-examination of his knowledge of standard police department procedures; evidence is relevant to question of bias even if it has only mere tendency to prove bias or interest); State v. Gardner, 67 Or App 404 (1984)(uncharged crimes by witness if relevant to relationship to defendant or other witnesses). The basic argument is that if it is error or reversible error to deny the defendant the right to cross-examine a state’s witness on a particular topic, the impeachment value of such evidence is established as being “material” and therefore would fall within the Brady doctrine for pretrial discovery.

But don’t rely on Brady alone. The U.S. Supreme Court has recognized that a prosecutor’s disclosure duty “may arise more broadly under a prosecutor’s ethical or statutory obligations” than under Brady, citing ABA Model Rule of Professional Conduct 3.8(d)(2008). Cone v. Bell, 129 S Ct 1769, 1783 n.15 (2009). Oregon Rule of Professional Conduct 3.8(b) is identical to the Model Rule, and it requires a prosecutor to “make timely disclosure to the defense of all evidence or information known to the prosecutor that tends to negate the guilt of the accused, or mitigates the offense.” It goes on to require disclosure “in connection with the sentencing” “of all unprivileged mitigating information.”

This rule is broader than Brady in most respects, because it applies to “all evidence or information,” whereas Brady has a materiality requirement; it is not restricted to disclosure of admissible evidence; and must be timely produced so as to assist the defense in plea negotiations. It may not impose a duty on the prosecutor to learn of exculpatory evidence in possession of the state. See ABA Formal Opinion 09-454 (2009)(construing the Model Rule). However, once you inform the prosecutor the information probably exists and you want it, he may not take the position to remain deliberately ignorant. Id. Additionally, Oregon Rule of Professional Conduct 3.4(d) states that a lawyer shall not “knowingly make a frivolous discovery request or fail to make reasonably diligent effort to comply with a legally proper discovery request by an opposing party.” (Emphasis supplied.) For an in-depth discussion of this topic, see the Motion to Compel Discovery Under Rule 3.8(d) in Appendix E (NOTE: unavailable online; refer to hard copy/PDF or request from OCDLA).

Practice Tip: The rule requires disclosure of all mitigating information concerning the offense or punishment, but don’t expect a prosecutor to take a broad view of mitigation. Instead turn to death penalty jurisprudence, which has long dealt with the concept of mitigating evidence. Mitigating evidence includes many factors related to the crimes charged, such as evidence which could raise any doubt, as opposed to reasonable doubt, concerning whether the defendant committed the crime or his precise role in the offense. See Mak v. Blodgett, 970 F2d 614 (9th Cir. 1992). The relationship between the defendant and a co-defendant, particularly direct or circumstantial evidence of domination or undue influence by the co-defendant over the defendant, and the type of deal made by the co-defendant are all recognized areas of mitigation. Furthermore,any'' evidence that is inconsistent with the prosecution’s theory of the case is mitigating evidence. Chaney v. Brown, 730 F2d 1334 (10th Cir. 1984)(state withheld FBI reports that could have raised an inference that other suspects took part in the murder, which could mitigate punishment).

C. Motion for Early Inspection of Evidentiary Records

Records in the hands of third parties may be obtained pretrial if they meet the criteria of items likely to be offered as evidence. ORS 136.580(2). That statute authorizes the defense (or state) to move the court for authority to issue a subpoena duces tecum for the production of books, papers and other documents to the court prior to trial or the hearing at which they would be offered in evidence. It also authorizes the court to allow the parties to inspect and copy these records prior to trial. However, the statute is not a “discovery device” permitting early production of evidence to defense counsel or a court where no trial or evidentiary proceeding is scheduled. State v. Cartwright, 336 Or 408, 85 P3d 305 (2004).

The right to compel pretrial production of materials through subpoena extends only to items that are “material and favorable,” State v. Pelham,136 Or App 336, 344–45, 901 P2d 972 (1995), rev den, 323 Or 264, 916 P2d 312 (1996), or otherwise “demonstrably relevant” and with established “bearing” on the criminal case, State v. Cartwright,173 Or App 59, 66 n. 9, 20 P3d 223 (2001), rev’d on other grounds, 336 Or 408, 85 P3d 305 (2004). The “material and favorable” standard is analogous to the Brady standard, and need not be met unless the records are privileged or quasi-privileged. Cf., State v. West, 250 Or App 196, 203–04, 279 P2d 354 (2012)(holding defense not entitled to pretrial production of materials concerning the Intoxilyzer 8000 breath test machine when he did not know what the materials would show and sought them for the purpose of determining whether they were favorable).

Practice Tip: Talk to the prosecutor before you file a motion, explaining what you want and why you want it. If the prosecutor does not oppose you getting the records pretrial, i.e., if it is information he’s also interested in seeing, you may not have to establish its evidentiary value. Of course, the third party receiving the subpoena has standing to file a motion to quash.

Cartwright declared Oregon’s statute to be “essentially identical to the corollary federal subpoena provision of Rule 17(c) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure,” 173 Or App at 67. The only difference is that Oregon’s statute does not require consideration of whether production would be unreasonable or oppressive on the third party. Id., at n.10. Therefore, the court embraced federal law as providing guidance on when trial courts should exercise the discretion conferred by statute to allow early production of documentary evidence. Id., at 68-69.

You will probably be required to show: (1) that the documents sought are evidentiary and relevant; (2) that they are not otherwise procurable reasonably in advance of trial by exercise of due diligence; (3) that you cannot properly prepare for trial without pretrial production and inspection (plus unreasonable delay during trial if the records are first produced then); and (4) that their pretrial inspection is to identify “specific evidence of known value,” not “a general ‘fishing expedition’ to ascertain the existence of evidence.” Id., at 68 (citing United States v. Nixon, 418 U.S. 683, 699–700 (1974)). A sample memorandum of law in support of ORS 138.560(2) subpoenas is included in Appendix F.

If you are seeking records of prior statements of witnesses, as in Cartwright, be aware that federal statutory discovery law restricts disclosure of all witness statements until the witness has testified at trial or other evidentiary hearing. Therefore, reliance on federal law denying pretrial production of witness statements via Rule 17(c) should be questioned given Oregon’s statutory policy of pretrial disclosure of all relevant witness statements.

Most counties have a procedure, pursuant to ORS 136.580(2), for defense counsel to seek court permission to set a pretrial court date “for early production of material that, in the ordinary course and as a matter of right, will be available for evidentiary use at the proceeding to which they already have been subpoenaed.” Cartwrightat 415–416. The process for obtaining quasi-privileged records, such as medical, juvenile or child protective services records, is discussed in a separate section later in this chapter.

D. Motions in Limine to Obtain Discovery

Hearings on motions in limine can provide information and sometimes evidence not available through other means. Trial courts are generally unwilling to order the state to produce information within its possession that does not clearly fall under statutory discovery or the Brady doctrine. Two primary areas that “fall through the cracks” are materials relied on by expert witnesses and prior bad acts by your client or defense witnesses.

A motion challenging the reliability (or relevance) of an expert’s opinions may be the only way to get pretrial disclosure of facts or data underlying the opinions and, perhaps, the opportunity to cross-examine the expert pretrial. You should still file a discovery motion asserting a right to pretrial disclosure under the Oregon Evidence Code, or at least incorporate this in your memorandum of law supporting the motion in limine. OEC 705, “Disclosure of Facts or Data Underlying Expert Opinion,” provides:

An expert may testify in terms of opinion or inference and give reasons therefore without prior disclosure of the underlying facts or data, unless the court requires otherwise. The expert may in any event be required to disclose the underlying facts or data on cross-examination.

(Emphasis supplied.) The Legislative Commentary to this rule notes that the Legislature rejected a version that required prior disclosure in all cases. However, the Commentary goes on to note:

Without [prior disclosure] opposing counsel may not know when the proponent of an expert witness has failed to establish the basis for testimony, or when the testimony rests on an impermissible basis. . . . . The rule indicates that in appropriate situations the trial court can require advance disclosure of the underlying facts. . . . In short, prior disclosure should not be mandatory in all cases, nor should it be eliminated.

Practice Tip: If undermining the expert’s opinion is critical to your defense or for other strategic reasons, consider requesting an omnibus hearing and subpoena duces tecum the witness to bring all of his underlying facts, data, and notes to the hearing on your motion in limine. Make the discovery request in the motion and tell the court that requiring the DA to produce that information in advance of the hearing will save considerable time. Submit a proposed order for production of the materials by a specific date, and setting the motion in limine hearing, along with your motions.

The state is not required to tell you which of its witnesses will offer expert opinions, so assume that any police officer or other agencies’ personnel are likely to give opinion testimony. File a preemptive motion in limine against admission of any type of opinion you think such witnesses may state, given the facts of your case. In responding to the motion, the state will at least have to inform you whether or not that type of expert testimony will be elicited from any of its witnesses. See Appendix G for sample motions dealing with common types of “expert” witness testimony in child sex abuse cases and drug trafficking cases (NOTE: unavailable online; refer to hard copy/PDF or request from OCDLA).

A motion in limine to exclude prior bad acts by your client or defense witnesses should list all bad acts the state has disclosed as well as any other bad acts the state may offer at trial not yet disclosed. Incorporate a request for pretrial disclosure of prior bad act evidence based on the Oregon Evidence Code. Evidence of an alleged prior bad act offered under OEC 404(3) against the defendant is not admissible unless the prosecution can first establish by a preponderance of the evidence that the defendant committed the act. The issue is for the trial court under Rule 104(1). State v. Kim, 111 Or App 1, 5, rev den, 314 Or 176 (1992). Given the extreme dangers of prior bad act evidence, “it is important to ensure that the act did, in fact, occur and the accused party committed the act before the jury is allowed to consider it.” Rugemer v. Rhea, 153 Or App 400, 408-09 (1998); seealso United States v. Cook, 608 F2d 1175, 1186 (9th Cir. 1979 (en banc) (“trial by ambush” is counterproductive and “advance planning” for ruling on motion in limine to preclude evidence of prior conviction helps both parties and the court), cert. denied, 444 U.S. 1034.

E. Motions Required by Rules of Evidence

Proponents of certain hearsay statements concerning abuse must comply with OEC 803(18a)(b) which requires disclosure of the intention to offer the hearsay “and the particulars of the statement no later than 15 days before trial, except for good cause shown.” Simply providing discovery and notice of an intention to offer at trial hearsay statements contained in discovery is not sufficient notice. State v. Chase, 240 Or App 541, 248 P3d 432 (2011). At a minimum, adequate notice requires the state to identify the substance of the statement sought to be introduced, and the witness or the means by which the statement would be introduced. Id.at 546–47.

Notwithstanding compliance with this rule, Sixth Amendment objections may still bar admission of testimonial statements. Crawford v. Washington, 541 U.S. 36, 124 S Ct 1354 (2004); State v. Mack, 337 Or 586, 101 P3d 349 (2004).

OEC 803(28)(a), also known as the “residual” exception, provides for the admissibility of hearsay statements not covered by other hearsay rules having certain “guarantees of trustworthiness.” To the extent that anything remains of this exception after Crawford, supra, the proponent of such statements is required to disclose “the intention to offer the statement and the particulars of it…sufficiently in advance of the trial or hearing, or as soon as practicable after it becomes apparent that such statement is probative of the issues at hand, to provide the adverse party with a fair opportunity to prepare to meet it.”

IV. Discovery of Quasi-privileged or Privileged Records

This section discusses the procedure for obtaining non-public records. You will need to further research Oregon laws and any corresponding federal laws creating privacy rights for such records as well as statutory exemptions and constitutional grounds allowing disclosure. Some of the sample forms in the appendices referenced below will provide a head start on that research.

A. Records Deemed in the Prosecution’s Possession

Non-public or “quasi-privileged” as well as privileged records that are deemed in possession of the prosecution are subject to statutory discovery and the Brady doctrine. However, the court must serve as the “file inspector” to determine what records or portions of records are discoverable.

Reports or notes possessed by state agencies, such as the Department of Human Services, Services to Children and Families (hereinafter “DHS”) or the Medical Examiner’s Office, to which the district attorney or law enforcement has access or which are “part of the prosecution” are deemed to be in the possession and control of the state and are discoverable. State v. Warren, supra. Under ORS 135.873, a court may be required to conduct an in camera review of records subject to discovery to make appropriate judicial orders for the provision of certain documents, including those in the possession of child protective service agencies. State ex rel Carlile v. Lewis, 310 Or 541, 800 P2d 786 (1990); State v. Wattenbarger, 97 Or App 414, 776 P2d 1292 (1989).

Despite the apparent statutory obligation of the state to disclose records of this sort to the defense, it is often necessary for the defense to subpoena them for an in camera review. If records being sought include medical or mental health information, you may first be required to obtain a “qualified protective order” pursuant to Oregon Rule of Civil Procedure 55H (discussed further in the next subsection). And be mindful of the requirements and strictures of State v. Cartwright, supra, if you are seeking records not otherwise subject to statutory or constitutional discovery rights. See Appendix H for sample motion and protective order concerning Oregon Youth Authority files.

The judge is required to review even large, bulky, time-consuming files from DHS and cannot order the parties to undertake this task. State ex rel Carlile v. Lewis, supra; State v. Wattenbarger, supra. If the court refuses to review the records, you must move the court to seal the materials and include in the record for purposes of appeal. State v. Crenshaw, 307 Or 160 (1988). You will have to provide the court with a list of witnesses whose relevant statements (in the form of notes or reports) you are seeking and a description of what exculpatory evidence is believed to exist—e.g., topics for impeachment evidence—both for Warren motion purposes and also for appellate review of compliance with Warren. State v. Weaver, 139 Or App 207, 911 P2d 969 (1996). This also has the practical effect of telling the judge what he or she is looking for.

Practice Tip: Make your Warren motion early, in writing as a special discovery motion, to be assured of a full review of disclosed material substantially before trial. Otherwise State v. Hiatt, 303 Or 60, 733 P2d 1373 (1987), allows disclosure of the DHS records on the morning of trial where no motion to compel discovery was made. It is also an excellent idea to serve subpoena duces tecum on the local DHS record keeper to assure appearance of the records at the motion hearing. Also remind the court that under the Warren ruling the entire DHS record must be copied and appended to the court record for appellate purposes. State v. Crenshaw, 307 Or 160, 764 P2d 1372 (1988).

B. Records Possessed by Third Parties

Relevant medical and mental health records of witnesses are often in the possession of third-party treatment providers not affiliated with the prosecution. Medical records may be obtained by subpoena in criminal cases as provided in ORCP 55H and sent only to the court or clerk of court where the case is pending. ORS 136.447. All of the information you are seeking will involve “individually identifiable health information,” (IIHI) defined in part as information “which relates to the past, present or future physical or mental health or condition of an individual.” ORCP 55H(1)(a). However, if other laws, state or federal, further restrict or limit disclosure of the IIHI, then “the protected records shall not be disclosed in response to the subpoena” unless those laws have been complied with. 55(H)(2).

The rule further requires that IIHI can only be disclosed subject to a “Qualified Protective Order,” issued by the court, that restricts use and disclosure of the information for any purpose other than litigating the case, and requires return or destruction of the information (and all copies) at the end of the litigation. 55(H)(1)(b); or by an alternate method described below. Compliance with other state or federal laws that restrict or limit disclosure of the IIHI may mean a Qualified Protective Order is the only method to use. Additionally, if the prosecutor will not stipulate to the court issuing a Qualified Protective Order, see 55H(1)(b)(order issued “by stipulation or otherwise”), you may need to first try the alternate method that requires proof of notification to the person whose records you are seeking, and that the person either did not object or objections were resolved; if objections remain, those can be decided by the court in ruling on whether to issue the protective order. See Appendix K-1 for sample forms incorporating both methods (NOTE: unavailable online; refer to hard copy/PDF or request from OCDLA).

Practice Tip: Get your own client’s records, as well as any “friendly” witness’ or victim’s records, via a release of information; you often need to obtain the form direct from the treatment provider, rather than use a general HIPPA-compliant release that you may have on hand. This type of pretrial subpoena duces tecum appears authorized only by ORS 136.580(2), so unless your county follows a different practice, be sure to comply with that statute by obtaining permission from the court to issue the subpoena.

Service of the subpoena on a medical records custodian must include service of either (1) a Qualified Protective Order or (2) affidavit or declaration documenting you conducted a notification process to the person whose records you are seeking. Further explanation of these requirements is found at 55(H)(2)(a). Appendix I contains a sample Motion for Protective Order and Production of Records; Appendix J contains a sample Protective Order; Appendix K contains a sample affidavit to be served with the subpoena in lieu of a protective order.

Unlike the physician-patient privilege, the psychotherapist-patient privilege does generally apply in criminal cases. A primary exception is carved out by ORS 419B.040(1) which abrogates the privilege in cases of child abuse. SeeState ex rel. Juvenile Dept. of Multnomah County v. Spencer, 198 Or App 599, 605–606, 108 P3d 1189 (2005). Other exceptions noted in the OEC may apply under the facts of your case. If no statutory exception to or waiver of the privilege exists, you will likely have to make a strong showing that the evidence you seek is necessary to impeach a key prosecution witness and assert a constitutional ground such as confrontation or compulsory process. (See sample Response in Opposition to Motion to Quash in Appendix L.) However, unless you are seeking the alleged victim’s records, the state would have no standing to assert the privilege, but the treatment provider could assert it on behalf of the witness whose records are sought.

Records of drug and alcohol treatment may be obtained by following the procedures under 42 USC §290ee and 42 CFR Ch.1, sec. 2.61, et seq. A court order must be obtained in advance of seeking these records, for good cause shown. Seealso State v. Batchelor, 282 Or App 47, 50, 578 P2d 409 (1978)(discussing limited admissibility of drug abuse evidence).

A criminal defendant’s access to witnesses’ education records is restricted by statute, ORS 336.187, and the federal Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, 20 USC §1232g; 34 CFR Part 99, such that you may need to assert constitutional grounds unless you can make an argument that access is allowed by ORS 336.187. If you are seeking records from private educational institutions that do not receive federal funds, you may want to research further to see if these laws apply.

Practice Tip: Even when seeking quasi-privileged or privileged records for which the law does not require a protective order, offering to limit the use and disclosure of the requested information to the pending case, to not provide copies of the records to your client, and to destroy the records at the end of the litigation (which could include time for filing post-conviction relief), may be helpful in convincing the court to grant you access.

V. Discovery of Digital and Forensic Evidence

You will probably need expert assistance to interpret digital or forensic evidence, but with the information provided in this section you will know how to make specific requests for the materials your expert will want to examine before they are hired. That will allow you earlier access to materials that may prove exculpatory, at least for impeachment purposes, and time to study the materials before having a discussion about the case with your expert so you can better frame the issues you want your expert to address. A great resource that provides a detailed summary of forensic evidence collection and testing—and that could be used to impeach police officers who process evidence incorrectly—is the OSP Forensic Services Division Physical Evidence Manual, available on-line at http://www.oregon.gov/OSP/FORENSICS/docs/PhysEvidenceManual12_13_11.pdf?ga=t (last accessed August 24, 2012). A. Digital Discovery

(Co-authored by Richard Price, Litigation Support Specialist, based in Eugene, Oregon)

Whenever digital evidence is significant to your case—or when hidden additional evidence (“metadata”) from the digital media might be—obtain an exact digital copy of hard drives, memory devices, photographic, audio or video evidence in the original file format rather than a translation. That is the only way to ensure you get the metadata that can be accessed to show the date and time the file was created and, in some cases GPS coordinates, on the device. With many police in-car video systems, metadata includes speed of the patrol car, vehicle number, and light bar/siren/brakes/door activation information. Translations of these videos into easy-play formats often have none of the metadata, or they may have a subset.

Digital discovery of complete computers, hard drives, flash memory devices and mobile phones that have been seized as evidence will also contain significant metadata and other peripheral information that is often important to learn.

If you aren’t willing to put in the time and effort to get original digital discovery instead of a translation that is easy to access on your computer, you will be relying on the police to determine the edited version you receive. You should insist on an exact duplicate of the original data, then seek your own translation of the file format if needed.

Digital Photographs

Photo files produced by digital cameras, including those in mobile phones, contain metadata in the form of Exchangeable Image File Format (EXIF) data. There is no industry standard, but most EXIF data will have data similar to that shown in Figure 1. Only an exact copy of the .jpg or .tif photo file will have this metadata as well as the date and time that the file was saved. Digital photos from some mobile devices, such as cell phones, may include GPS data. Although the date and time in EXIF data can be disputed based upon how they are set in the camera or phone, the time delta between photographs and audio/video recordings from the same device can also aid in producing a timeline.

Digital Audio

Digital audio has metadata; e.g., a music MP3 file can have the artist, album, genre and other information in the metadata that appears on the playing device. Date and time of the recording may also be available. However, audio CDs do not have metadata, so a 911 call or witness interviews provided in discovery on an audio CD will have no metadata.

Digital Video

If you haven’t yet encountered videos from the state that won’t play, are poor quality, or are claimed not to have recorded anything due to equipment malfunction, you will. Fully addressing such issues is beyond the scope of this article, but a basic understanding of digital video will help you draft the best discovery requests when confronted by video problems.

Unlike the analog video days where there were two video standards—VHS and BETA—the digital video world has hundreds of proprietary and incompatible video file formats. The basic problem is file size, and this affects quality and resolution. The raw digital video equivalent to an old VHS tape would require 16 DVDs to play one hour of video. This is impractical, so the video is compressed, much like when you compress a large word processing document into a .zip file.

Competition has produced proprietary compression solutions. Police departments across Oregon have purchased in-car video systems from different vendors, and the original output formats are different for each manufacturer. Commercial surveillance camera systems have the same formula: [different vendors]=[different video formats].

All of these systems have one thing in common—they have the means to translate the original video into an output video file in a more “standardized” format. This standardized format might be Windows .wmv, or .mp4, an industry standard that will play on Windows and Apple computers. These files are not the same quality as the original, and most probably would not contain the original metadata.

Sometimes the state will provide the video on a disc along with the proprietary “player” software that, once installed on your computer, will allow you to play the copy of the original video file format. This is the best choice for your discovery request—give us a digital copy of the original video file and player software—but it can also cause the most problems once you get it because the player software may be incompatible with your computer’s operating system and you will have few options for technical support. If the software is older, it may only play on older operating systems, like Windows 2000 or XP or Vista. Virtually none of this software will function on an Apple computer operating system. It may be necessary to employ a video expert in order to access this discovery, but that is the only way to ensure you will be getting all of the data rather than just some of it.

If the prosecutor replies that no player software is available, then ask for the manufacturer and model of the recording equipment. Approach the manufacturer directly for a temporary “player” license for the software so it can be downloaded from their web site. There may be a small fee for this license, although in general there is no fee for defense attorneys. Once the license and software are obtained, renew your request for an exact copy of the video, explaining that you now have the necessary software to view it. This process can be grueling; it may be necessary for the manufacturer to obtain permission from the police department in order to provide player software, and you will have to be vigilant in keeping things moving. The end result, however, can be remarkable—you will see all of the features of the recording, all of the metadata, and you will see it in the best quality possible.

If you fail to obtain original video or can’t access it, demand to see it played back for you and your investigator using the agency’s hardware and proprietary software so that you can take notes of metadata that may not appear on a translated copy. There may be multiple audio tracks (the cop’s microphone and the in-car microphone), meaning you will have to play it twice to hear each of the two different audio tracks. You will never know if some detail is in the original and missing from a reproduction unless you have seen the original. Consider the importance of an exact recorded GPS location that you never knew existed.

Practice Tip: If possible, take a video camera and a tripod and make a video of their video screen while it is playing the video (do this twice to get both audio tracks). This technique works for both police video and surveillance video. A high quality digital video camera on a tripod can produce a good quality reproduction of the original and may be useful if metadata like GPS coordinates only appear on the original. Be very quiet in the room when making these recordings since any talking during playback can easily reduce the recorded audio from the playback. This on-site viewing should not be in lieu of obtaining whatever is the best translation video available for discovery. But viewing the original video at the police department will let you know if anything is missing once you get the translation.

If for some reason you elect not to obtain the original video file, request a translated video to a full-quality standard DVD format. DVD format is not the same as burning proprietary (and perhaps unplayable) video files onto a DVD. DVD format will play on any DVD player and doesn’t require a computer or any translation software, and it will also play in most modern computers. OSP can do this from some if not all of their in-car video systems.

If you cannot obtain original video, full-quality DVD format, or uncompressed .AVI format, then you may have to take what you can get. The quality will likely be lower than the original, and you may get no metadata. In those cases it may be essential that you and/or your investigator watch the original played on the police system.

All video discovery requests should demand that all audio tracks be provided, and with separate video files as necessary, since playing two audio tracks at the same time would be jibberish. Standard computer video file formats will generally only have one playable audio track. In addition, all video discovery requests should specify that you want all metadata from the original video, including latitude and longitude locations, date and time stamps, light bar/siren/brakes/door information, etc.

If you want video from a commercial source outside of government discovery, like a convenience store, send your investigator there very soon. Because of storage limitations, many of these systems overwrite the recordings after only a few days.

Digital Mobile Phone Data

Many police departments have hardware devices that can extract everything from a mobile phone including contact lists, phone logs, SMS messages (text messages), audio and video recordings, photographs and web browsing history. The best known such device is UFED®by Cellebrite. The UFED® device can produce reports in several proprietary and standard formats, including Adobe PDF.

The state will usually provide paper or PDF discovery that has a multipage printout of the phone extraction. You should obtain an exact copy of the original UFED® report and all of the original photo files, audio files, and video files in addition to a paper or PDF report. Cellebrite makes a proprietary report reader available, at no cost upon request, so you can view and print out your own reports from the original UFED® extraction report. Contact Cellebrite via their website, http://www.cellebrite.com, or by telephone at their New Jersey office: (201) 848-8552. You will not get all of the metadata in mobile phone extractions unless you get an exact digital copy of the original report and exact digital copies of the photo, audio, and video files.

Digital Computer Hard Drives and Memory Devices

Investigators use forensic software to create exact mirror images of memory devices such as computer hard drives, thumb drives, DVDs, SD cards, etc. A computer hard drive image is unique in that the forensic software can permit “booting” the computer and seeing/operating the computer just as it was when it was imaged. This can be useful since browsing history, file downloads, and other metadata is maintained precisely as it was. All of the folders and files are intact. In addition, many deleted files and much deleted history can be recovered and viewed.

If child porn is discovered on an image, the prosecution will refuse to supply an exact copy of the memory device in discovery. Instead, you or your investigator will be required to view the contents at a forensic office on their computer, or you may be permitted to have your own forensic expert view the mirrored memory device on his or her forensic computer at the government forensic facility under their supervision. The government may discover redacted portions of materials found on the memory device. Child porn cases are unique regarding digital discovery, so the rest of this subsection will apply mainly to all other cases.

If the state has seized memory devices, you have two optional paths for your discovery request. You can 1) request exact copies of the images, or 2) request all of the extracted files from the images with the folder structure maintained. The state may not be willing to do the file extraction work for you, and so you may be limited to the first option. In either case you may be required to supply one or more clean hard drives or other memory devices to the state to obtain your copy of the images.

If you obtain exact copies of the memory device images, you will need a forensic expert who has the specialized software capable of extracting and searching the files within the proprietary images. The images are usually in Encase or Forensic Tool Kit (FTK) format. Your forensic expert will also be able to boot complete computer images and operate the computer just as it existed at the time it was imaged. All of the computer files, e.g., Word, Excel, and Acrobat (PDF) documents, will be in their original state and as saved, with the date and time of last saved. These files will also contain the original metadata.

This type of metadata is available from most documents saved by Word, Excel, Acrobat, and other computer programs. If the state provides you with PDF files that have been created from original documents, all of the metadata is lost.

B. Forensic Discovery

(Co-authored by forensic experts Kenn Meneely, Willamette Valley Forensics, and Donald Schuessler, International Forensic Experts, both based in Eugene)

Expect the state to provide only the crime lab’s formal report in making “automatic” discovery. That report will not include issues the analyst may have encountered during the testing (i.e., contamination, errors in testing, expired materials). For example, in the case of urine toxicology, each analyst has discretion to report compounds that were detected. This may result in many compounds the analyst noted being excluded from the final report. As the state’s scientist may testify to compounds that were not in the formal report, you should always obtain the lab’s notes to properly prepare for trial. Because the formal report will not have incorporated all of the analyst’s “notes,” the notes (including drawings, diagrams and photographs) are discoverable under Oregon statutory discovery law.

Prior to trial it is always advisable to contact the state’s scientist to clarify any document concerns and determine the scope of forensic examination—and possibly his testimony. During this contact, your scientist should also be present. At times the District Attorney’s Office will want to be present.

If you are concerned about a scientist’s technical performance, the quality assurance (QA) manager, located at the Oregon State Police Forensic Division General Headquarters in Salem, is the person to contact. Should you require the scientist’s proficiency test results, request the QA manager’s protocol for proficiency testing review and documentation.

During a crime scene investigation where the transfer and packaging of evidence takes place, the original position or pattern of bloodstains can be altered. It is essential when examining the evidence (e.g., bloodstained clothing) that the scientist have access to the original scene photographs which show the item’s location and condition prior to collection. If the state’s expert failed to do this, you should have grounds for impeachment.

The Oregon State Police Forensic Laboratory may assist other agencies in death investigations with crime scene reconstruction and generate a formal report stating the location and description of articles. In cases involving bullet trajectories, the forensic scientist may place a probe through walls or other areas to determine the origin of the shot(s). The report may or may not include the analyst’s conclusion as to this origin. In the case of bloodstain pattern interpretation, the analyst’s report will only describe the location and general form of the stain (e.g., pooled blood, dripped blood). Conclusions as to the origin (position) of the individual casting the blood or the reconstruction of the events based upon bloodstain analysis may or may not be included in the report, but the expert may testify to those opinions at trial. Again, you would be entitled to a copy of all “notes” because the formal report does not fully incorporate this information.

Currently, the Oregon State Police Forensic Division performs toxicological testing on urine for controlled substances and some medications, and on blood for alcohol. The laboratory provides toxicology examinations only on blood for death investigation cases. If toxicology must be performed on blood in other types of cases, or on hair, the lab must send the sample to a private facility such as National Medical Services.

Most of the forensic labs will only provide discovery documents after payment to the laboratory has been received. The general process is:

1. Request discovery documents through the District Attorney’s Office.

2. Your request should be very specific as to the records required; otherwise the lab will only provide you the general packet of information. 3. The District Attorney’s Office will advise the forensic laboratory of the request. 4. The forensic lab will advise the District Attorney’s Office, or your office directly, as to the cost of the photocopies and/or CD that will contain the records you requested. (Note: the lab will charge for labor, cost per page, and the CD.) 5. Once the forensic lab has received funds, the documents will be copied and sent to your office. 6. This process may take some time, so make your discovery request early in the case. 7. The Medical Examiner’s Office protocol for obtaining reports can be found on that website. However, the Oregon State Police Forensic Division performs the analytical examinations, so the laboratory report and notes pertaining to the autopsy must be requested through the Oregon State Police Forensic Division. In every case, consult your expert for additional information they want in discovery; make supplemental requests as needed. A Discovery Request Template for Toxicology Issues in cases that involve forensic toxicology, e.g., DUII, sexual assaults and death investigation, authored by Kenn Meneely, is included in Appendix M.

Latent prints can be evidence in a variety of types of criminal cases. In Oregon, virtually every police agency will process physical evidence for latent prints. While latent print development processes are fairly standardized, the specific process used is highly dependent upon the type of evidence being examined for prints.

The subsequent comparison/identification of latent prints is conducted by significantly fewer police agencies. Several of these agencies are but are not limited to the Oregon State Police, Eugene Police Department, Washington County Sheriff’s Department and Portland Police Bureau. While individual departmental procedures may vary, a standardized latent print comparison methodology exists and is utilized worldwide. It is known as ACE-V Methodology:

A (Analysis). Review all information visible in a print to determine if it is of sufficient quality for comparison. One of three conclusions is possible: (1) the print is of no value; (2) the print is suitable for elimination purposes; or (3) the print is of identification value.

C (Comparison). View the latent side-by-side with available standards and look for similarities and dissimilarities between characteristics of the known and latent print.

E (Evaluation). Review the information gathered during A and C and reach one of three conclusions: (1) Identification (the two impressions originated from the same source); (2) Exclusion (the two impressions did not originate from the same source); or (3) inconclusive (Insufficient information exists between the latent and the standard to reach a determination).

V (Verification). All identifications are verified by another qualified examiner, completing the Analysis, Comparison, and Evaluation methodology phases.

Written documentation should exist for each individual step of the ACE-V methodology. For example, documentation of the Analysis phase should include the reasoning for determining the comparison quality of each latent prints examined in the case. Documentation of the Comparison phase may include a written description and photograph of a unique characteristic found in the latent print and searched through the defendant’s fingerprint standards or photographs with marked individual characteristics found in both. Written documentation of the Evaluation phase is commonly a formal report citing one of the three possible conclusions of a latent print comparative examination. Verification phase documentation normally is the written word “Verified” along with a second examiner’s initials or signature attesting to the identification of a latent print to a defendant’s fingerprint or palm print standard.

For discovery, a complete copy of the laboratory or investigative case file and documents covering the crime scene investigation, latent print processing, ACE-V and associated AFIS records are necessary for review by your expert. See Latent Print Discovery Template authored by Donald Schuessler in Appendix N for specific language to use in making the request.

Your expert needs the same quality images that were used by the crime lab to make the latent print identification. Images taken of latent prints are taken normally close up, high resolution and saved in .tiff format. These images along with the defendant’s fingerprint and palm print standards should be copied to DVD or CD for discovery. Low resolution photocopies or .pdf files of hard copy images of latent prints or the defendant’s known fingerprint standards are not suitable for comparison.

VII. Reciprocal Discovery

The defendant’s privilege against self-incrimination is reflected in the statutory exemption for discovery from the defense of a defendant’s statements. ORS 135.825(1). But that right does not generally exempt the defense from other discovery obligations. Williams v. Florida, 399 U.S. 78 (1970). A lawyer’s ethical obligation to preserve privileged “information relating to the representation of the client,” under the Oregon Rule of Professional Conduct 1.6, may also affect what is voluntarily disclosed in discovery. However, not even a defendant’s right to compulsory process under the state and federal constitutions creates an absolute bar to exclusion of defense evidence for deliberate violations of a discovery rule, even if it was the fault of the attorney and not the defendant. Taylor v. Illinois, 484 U.S. 400 (1988).

A. Interviews of Witnesses

If your investigator takes notes or otherwise records an interview, whether of a prospective state or defense witness, those materials may fall within statutory reciprocal discovery under ORS 135.835. Notes of defense witness interviews—provided you intend to call the witness at trial—are subject to reciprocal discovery unless your investigator prepares a report that is disclosed. See State v. Moss, 147 Or App 658, 663 (1997)(defense violated discovery statute by failing to disclose notes concerning testimony of rebuttal witness). However, only “relevant” statements must be disclosed, ORS 135.835(1). Thus, if your investigator makes notes of things the witness said that are not relevant to the subject mater of the testimony you intend to elicit from the witness, the statute does not appear to mandate disclosure of those portions of the notes or require irrelevant statements to be included in a formal report that is provided in lieu of notes.

Fragmentary notes are generally not discoverable if they have been used to prepare a report that is provided in discovery. State v. Bray, 31 Or App 47, 569 P2d 688 (1977); State v. Armstrong, 71 Or App 467, 692 P2d 699 (1984).

B. Defense Experts

An expert retained by the defense to examine the defendant or to consult on other evidentiary matters is a “representative of the lawyer,” under OEC 503(1)(e), and his statements and reports are not subject to disclosure to the state unless and until defense counsel decides to call the expert as a witness at trial. State v. Jenkins, 190 Or App 542, 79 P3d 347 (2003); State v. Corgain, 63 Or App 26, 663 P2d 773 (1983). Providing a copy of your expert’s report to the prosecution, without the defendant’s express consent, does not waive the attorney-client privilege. State v. McGrew, 46 Or App 123, 127, 610 P2d 1245 (1980).

However, an expert originally retained by the defense, even though a “representative of the lawyer” as to privileged communications from the lawyer and defendant, may be called as a witness by the state to testify “about segregated information or opinions that the expert has formed without regard to any confidential information.” State v. Riddle, 330 Or 471, 8 P3d 980 (2000); State v. Bockorny, 125 Or App 479, 866 P2d 1230 (1993).

An expert witness’s detailed list of questions and answers, prepared to assist the defendant with the presentation of the expert’s testimony, is subject to disclosure in discovery. State v. Cunningham, 197 Or App 264, 105 P3d 929 (2005).

See additional discussion about restrictions on the state’s ability to interview your expert, under “Interviewing Witnesses” in the Pretrial Investigation section of this chapter.

C. Impeachment Witnesses and Evidence

The discovery statutes have no exemption for production of impeachment witness identity and statements, or evidence such as exhibits which counsel plans to introduce at trial. State v. Burdge, 295 Or 1, 664 P2d 1076 (1983). This includes any memorialization of statements obtained from prosecution witnesses.

In State v. Young, 94 Or App 683, 689, 767 P2d 90 (1989), defense counsel was found to have committed a discovery violation for failure to disclose a tape recorded statement of a state’s complaining witness that counsel intended to use only for impeachment purposes. The appeals court made the sweeping observation that where “defense counsel, even though not certain, can ‘reasonably predict’ that she will use certain exhibits to impeach a state’s witness, she must give timely discovery to the prosecutor.”

Some prosecutors and trial courts have read this language to require defense disclosure of statements of state’s witnesses, obtained by defense investigators, even where defense counsel’s only purpose is to use the statements for purposes of cross-examination as prior statements of a witness under OEC 613. That rule of evidence, as well as a plain reading of the discovery statutes which require disclosure of statements of witnesses whom the defense intends to call, ORS 135.835, would not require disclosure of such statements. Nonetheless, the attorney who intentionally fails to disclose such statements should be prepared to meet an argument that he or she has committed a discovery violation, or a motion by the state to compel production of your investigator’s notes. See Memorandum of Law Concerning Defendant’s Discovery Obligations, addressing this issue, in Appendix Q.

The legislative history of the discovery statutes shows the drafters intended disclosure of witness statements taken by the prosecuting or defense attorney. Presumably the attorney-client privilege would protect the defense attorney’s notes of the defendant’s statements, OEC Rule 503. Other objections to the DA seeking our notes of other witness statements could be tied to attorney-client privilege, i.e., the questions you asked were based on confidential communications from the client, so that disclosing the notes would tend to reveal client confidences (like playing “Jeopardy”), and constitutional right to prepare and present a defense. Of course, the DA can’t get the notes unless it’s of a witness the defense intends to call at trial. 135.835(1).

D. Additional Disclosure Requirements

Notice and other disclosure requirements are also imposed on the defense by the alibi statute, ORS 135.455, the statutes on mental state evidence and defenses, ORS 161.309, and the statutes governing use of a witness who has been subject to hypnosis. ORS 136.675.

If a defendant accused of a sex offense intends to introduce evidence of a complainant’s past sexual behavior, such evidence is only admissible subject to a written motion to offer the evidence filed not later than 15 days before trial. OEC 412. The defendant’s motion “shall be accompanied by a written offer of proof,” served on the prosecutor.

VIII. Standards and Procedures for Resolving Discovery Disputes

The court is authorized to conduct a hearing on the regulation or denial of disclosures in camera, provided a party requests it and good cause is shown; a record shall be made of such proceedings. ORS 135.873(2) & (3). The court should not be hesitant to fully examine all contested matters and to seek further input from the defense if it has any doubts regarding disclosure. The court should allow ex-parte written submissions, to be filed under seal by the defense in support of requests, when necessary to protect the attorney-client privilege. Unlike the deference afforded the state’s decisions of whether to prosecute an individual or what charges to seek, there is little, if any, reason for the court to defer to prosecutorial discretion in the area of pretrial discovery. Seealso State v. Kull, 298 Or 38, 44 (1984)(court “should exercise supervisory authority to ensure that discovery proceeds properly, that the parties’ rights are not adversely affected, and that the trial is not unreasonably delayed.”).

The defense is under no duty to establish that requested material is beneficial or exculpatory under Brady as a predicate to asking the court to conduct the in-camera review authorized by ORS 135.873 when making a statutory-based discovery request. State v. Warren,supra, 304 Or at 433 (court is required to act as a “file inspector”).

For requests outside the scope of statutory discovery, the defense is required to make some showing, to the extent reasonable under the circumstances of the particular case, to support at least a belief and contention in good faith that the evidence is favorable and material to guilt or innocence. In deciding whether to act as a “file inspector” for Brady materials, the court should be guided by a balancing test: “The greater the burden of production would be, the greater should be the showing of possible materiality and favorableness by the moving party.” State v. ex rel. Johnson v, Schwartz, 26 Or App 279, 283–84 (1976).

The defense is not required to make the showing by affidavit; reviewing courts have looked to the motion, any affidavits filed, and the representations of counsel on the record. ''See, e.g., State v. Koennecke, 274 Or 169 (1976):

The request of the defendant, in the form of a motion, should state with as much particularity as possible the evidence that the defendant contends is favorable to him. If a difference of opinion exists between the defendant and the prosecution as to whether the evidence is favorable to the defendant, the difference should be resolved by the trial court by an in camera inspection of the evidence.

274 Or at 178. In the situation where the defense wants to conduct scientific tests with the evidence sought, and neither the state nor defense can know in advance the outcome of the tests so the court has nothing to inspect in camera, the defense must “make at least some showing, to the extent reasonable under the circumstances of the particular case, to support at least a belief and contention in good faith that the evidence demanded is ‘favorable’ to the defendant and ‘material’ to his guilt or innocence.” 274 Or at 179.

Koennecke involved a pretrial ruling in a prosecution for attempted murder of a police officer. The defense moved to inspect and test fire officers’ guns, under Brady, claiming “necessity” versus alleging a factual basis to support a claim that information gained thereby would be either favorable or material. The Oregon Supreme Court found this did not meet the Brady test. The court went on to note, “Brady is not authority for what defendant here seeks to do, that is, obtain evidence of unknown import to test and determine whether it helps or hurts his case.” 274 Or at 181. The court went on to explain:

Defendant’s right to discovery under Brady does not depend upon the question of defendant’s ‘necessity’ or ‘need’ for evidence withheld by the state, but upon the question whether such evidence is ‘favorable to the accused’ and ‘material to his guilt or innocence. . . . It follows that the state cannot justify the withholding of evidence by contending that defendant has no ‘need’ for such evidence or because it has furnished defendant with other evidence.

274 Or at 181-182 (citations omitted). The court addressed briefly the holdings of other jurisdictions that trial courts have inherent power to require the state to produce evidence for inspection by defendants in criminal cases if not otherwise required by statute or constitutional law. While noting Oregon had not adopted such a rule, the court also noted the question was not before it for decision in this case. 274 Or at 182–183. Cf. State v. Hartfield, 290 Or 583, 592 (1981)(requiring disclosure of grand jury testimony of witness, once witness has testified for the state, “in the furtherance of justice”).

When the court reviews in camera, a sealed record must be made of all materials reviewed by the court for purposes of appeal. ORS 135.873(3). ''See also State v. Crenshaw, 307 Or 160 (1988)(proper to have trial court seal materials and include in record when court refuses to conduct in camera review).

IX. Sanctions for Discovery Violations

Discovery obligations arise in “all criminal prosecutions in which the charging instrument has been brought in a court of record.” ORS 135.805(1). Disclosures by both sides are to be made “as soon as practicable.” ORS 135.845(1). Although the statutes envision mandatory, reciprocal and automatic disclosures, the trial court is granted authority to “supervise the exercise of discovery to the extent necessary to insure that it proceeds properly and expeditiously.” ORS 135.845(1). A party may also seek a protective order, under ORS 135.873, restricting, deferring or denying disclosures. And to the extent that a court is “apprised of any breach of the duty” to provide discovery, it may order a “party to permit inspection of the material, or grant a continuance, or refuse to permit the witness to testify, or refuse to receive in evidence the material not disclosed or enter such other order as it considers appropriate.” ORS 135.865.

Untimely discovery can draw sanctions as well as failing to disclose altogether. See State v. Dillard, 100 Or App 645 (1990)(reversing trial court which found the party “could have or should have” found the material earlier; there is no violation until the party “finds” the material and does not “promptly notify the other party.”); State v. Harshman, 61 Or App 711 (1983)(finding violation when state first gave notice of evidence concerning other bad acts by defendant on morning of trial, despite state’s argument that defendant was aware of these acts, because notice not “prompt” or “as soon as practicable”); State v. Clarke, 106 Or App 204 (1991)(defense provided witness’ name but not address; court finds discovery violation because defendant knew where witness lived and could have gone there to determine address).

A. Sanctions Against the State

Sanctions the courts may impose for discovery violations by the state include dismissal, exclusion of evidence, required stipulations, and continuances.

Dismissal is a permissible sanction for aggravated discovery violations by the state, especially in cases where there has been a “history of noncompliance with court rules and court directives” by the state’s attorney. No prejudice to the defense is required. State v. Dyson, 292 Or 26, 636 P2d 961 (1981). However, in State v. Fortune, 112 Or App 247, 828 P2d 483 (1992), a first trial resulted in a hung jury. In a second trial the defendant was convicted of rape in the first degree. Before sentencing, the defense discovered that the victim had recanted her story. This exculpatory evidence was never disclosed to the defense during the trial, even though it was known to the prosecutor. The defendant moved for a new trial, which was granted. Prior to that trial, new counsel moved to dismiss with prejudice based on the discovery violation. The Court of Appeals held that bad faith by the state was required for dismissal with prejudice.

If the state refuses to disclose an informant’s identity after the court has so ordered, dismissal is appropriate. State v. Martinez, 97 Or App 170, 776 P2d 3 (1989).

However, it may be error for a trial court to impose the sanction of dismissal unless it has exhausted the availability of less severe sanctions. State v. Vasquez-Hernandez, 159 Or App 64, 977 P2d 400 (1999).

The court may exclude state’s evidence not disclosed in a timely fashion even without a showing of prejudice to the defendant. State v. Dyson, supra.

Where the state lost a videotape of a defendant doing field sobriety tests, the court allowed the defendant to choose whether to suppress “some, all or none” of the field sobriety test evidence. State v. Zinsli, 156 Or App 245, 966 P2d 1200 (1998).

Where the state has negligently destroyed evidence, or allowed for its destruction or loss, the court may require the state to enter into stipulations regarding that evidence that is favorable to the defendant. In State v. Lance, 48 Or App 141, 616 P2d 546 (1980), the state was required to stipulate that brakes were not working at the time of collision when the state negligently destroyed a brake cylinder that was in its possession.

A continuance is an appropriate remedy for the state’s failure to disclose evidence. In State v. Kersey, 54 Or App 944, 636 P2d 1009 (1981), a 13-day continuance was granted to the defense for the state’s failure to disclose tape-recorded statements of witnesses prior to trial.

Practice Tip: Make a diligent good-faith effort to resolve all discovery disputes by consultation with the prosecutor, and document your efforts in letters which can be attached as exhibits. In the right case, consider requesting the sanction that the district attorney pay your attorney fees and costs in compelling production. See Oregon Rule of Civil Procedure 46A(4).

B. Sanctions Against the Defendant

Reciprocal discovery violations by the defense can result in sanctions including exclusion of evidence and continuance.

Exclusion of a defense witness’s testimony or of other defense evidence is not absolutely barred by the Sixth Amendment and its compulsory process clause. Taylor v. Illinois,484 US 400, 108 S Ct 646, 98 L Ed2d 798 (1988). Nor is it absolutely barred by Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution. State v. Mai, 294 Or 269, 656 P2d 315 (1982). However, before a court may exclude defense evidence because of a discovery violation, the court must find that the prosecution is prejudiced by the defendant’s failure to comply with the reciprocal discovery statutes, and must also determine that no sanction short of preclusion effectively will avoid the prejudice which the defendant’s lack of compliance created. State v. Mai, supra.

If the trial court is considering exclusion of defense evidence as a sanction for a discovery violation, it is imperative that the defense not only object to the imposition of some sanction, but also object to the particular sanction of exclusion of evidence. Failure to do so will result in the issue not being preserved for appeal. State v. Wyatt, 331 Or 335, 15 P3d 22 (2000).

Generally, where the court finds exclusion of evidence to be too great a sanction against the criminal defendant, the less onerous alternative remains a continuance to allow compliance with the discovery statutes. State v. Clarke, 106 Or App 204, 808 P2d 92 (1991); State v. Ben, 310 Or 309, 798 P2d 650 (1990); State v. Fain, 132 Or App 488, 888 P2d 1052 (1995).

Practice Tip

Make a clear offer of proof why:

1. It wasn’t reasonably predictable you would use the evidence.

2. There is no prejudice to the state.

3. The evidence is exculpatory or particularly material to the defense.

4. A lesser sanction accomplishes the aim of the statute.

E. PRETRIAL DEFENSE SUBPOENAS DUCES TECUM

Effective November 4, 1993, the Legislature added the following provision to ORS 136.580:

(2)Upon the motion of the state or the defendant, the court may direct that the books, papers or documents described in the subpoena be produced before the court prior to the trial or prior to the time when the books, papers or documents are to be offered in evidence and may, upon production, permit the books, papers or documents to be inspected and copied by the state or the defendant and the state's or the defendant's attorneys.

This statute is modeled after Rule 17(c) of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, which provides, in relevant part:

(c)For Production of Documentary Evidence and of Objects. . . . .The court may direct that books, papers, documents or other objects designated in the subpoena be produced before the court at a time prior to the trial or prior to the time when they are to be offered in evidence and may upon their production permit the books, papers, documents or objects or portions thereof to be inspected by the parties and their attorneys.

The Court of Appeals, in State v. Cartwright, 173 Or App 59, 67-69 (2001), reversed on other grounds, 336 Or 408 (2004), held that this statute should be interpreted the same as the federal courts have interpreted Rule 17(c). The Oregon Supreme Court in Cartwright, 336 Or 408 at 414-415, explained that the statute was designed to allow production of evidentiary materials in advance of the hearing at which a party intended to offer the evidence. Furthermore, the defense has no right to issue such subpoenas without the Court’s permission, and early production is left to the Court’s discretion. Id. Cartwright holds that the defense is entitled to subpoena for trial documentary materials in the possession of third parties, even over a claim of privilege, see 336 Or at 418-419; it therefore suggests that a Court could order the same production in advance of trial under this statute, provided the defense could show the privilege would be defeated once the witness claiming the privilege testified at trial.

The scope of a federal Rule 17(c) subpoena duces tecum is historically broad, encompassing materials not otherwise discoverable from the government pursuant to the federal discovery Rule 16. It provides the defense with an opportunity to obtain evidentiary material by subpoena. The material subpoenaed need not actually be used in evidence, but the court may issue a subpoena upon a party's good faith effort to obtain evidence. Bowman Dairy Co. v. United States, 341 US 214, 219-220 (1951); Cf., Cartwright, 336 Or at 415 (Oregon’s statute “provides a specific mechanism for obtaining access to the subpoenaed material before the court proceeding at which the material will or may be admitted as evidence.”)(emphasis supplied).

[R]ule 17's chief innovation was to expedite the trial by providing a time and place before trial for the inspection of the subpoenaed materials. However, the plain words of the Rule are not to be ignored. They must be given their ordinary meaning to carry out the purpose of establishing a more liberal policy for the production, inspection and use of materials at the trial.

Bowman Dairy Co. v. United States, 341 US at 220-21.

Subpoenas under Rule 17(c) may be served upon third parties in possession of documents relevant to the defense.United States v. Nixon, 418 US 683, 702 (1974). The federal courts have required the movant to demonstrate a sufficient likelihood that the materials will be relevant and admissible to an issue before the court, and that the subpoena be specific enough so that the person subpoenaed or other party having standing can lodge any objections to relevancy or admissibility. Id.at 700. The showing of relevancy required by the movant is to state facts which permit a rational inference that at least part of the items sought relate to the offenses charged. 418 US at 700.

Turning to ORS 136.580(2), it is clear the statute operates independently of the Oregon Discovery statutes, ORS 135.805-135.873. Thus, the mere fact that the defense may be unable to show it is entitled to the materials under the discovery statutes or the constitutional rules for the discovery of evidence material to guilt or punishment, does not prevent the defense from seeking a subpoena duces tecum in advance of trial. Indeed, the primary use of ORS 136.580(2) is to obtain materials outside the scope of statutory or constitutional discovery rights, because the materials are in the possession of third-parties rather than in the State’s possession.

If a defendant could use the compulsory process guaranteed him under the state and federal constitutions to inspect the materials at trial, there is little sense in reading ORS 136.580(2) to be more restrictive simply because it allows inspection in advance of trial to promote judicial efficiency. See Article 1, Section 11, Oregon Constitution and Amendments Six and Fourteen, United States Constitution; see also, Dennis v. US, 384 US 855, 873 (1966)("it is especially important that the defense, the judge and the jury should have the assurance that the doors that may lead to truth have been unlocked. In our adversary system for determining guilt or innocence, it is rarely justifiable for the prosecution to have exclusive access to a storehouse of relevant fact.").

II. DEFENDANT HAS A CONSTITUTIONAL RIGHT TO DISCOVERY OF THE REQUESTED MATERIALS

A. Brady Places a Duty on the District Attorney to Disclose Exculpatory Evidence.

Statements made by witnesses to state agencies, such as Oregon Youth Authority, and information contained in the state agency files of witnesses that potentially exculpate a criminal defendant are discoverable because “suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to an accused upon request violates due process where the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment.” Brady v. Maryland, 373 US 83, 87, 83 SCt 1194 (1963). Oregon Youth Authority is a state agency under ORS 420A.010. Withholding evidence from a defendant that would clearly “exculpate him or reduce the penalty” is not consistent with American “standards of justice,” even if withheld innocently. Id. at 87-88. Withholding such information violates the federal and state constitutions. A prosecutor’s personal responsibility for disclosing exculpatory evidence under Brady cannot be avoided by claiming a lack of control over other executive agencies. US v. Jennings, 960 F.2d 1488, 1490 (1992).

B. The Confidentiality Limitations are not Absolute:

Confrontation rights under the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments of the United States Constitution and Article I, §11 of the Oregon Constitution can override confidentiality interests in otherwise privileged documents. Brady, 373 U.S. 83, 83 S.Ct. 1194 (1963); Pennsylvania v. Ritchie, 480 U.S.39, 107 S.Ct. 989 (1987); State v. Koennecke, 274 Or. 169, 545 P.2d 127 (1976). The Oregon Supreme Court has struck a balance between the competing interests of confidentiality and disclosure, by providing that such materials be disclosed to a defendant through in camera inspection by the trial judge. State v. Warren, 304 Or. 428, 746 P.2d 711 (1987); State v. Graville, 304 Or 424, 746 P.2d 715 (1987).

Having established that Mr. Day’s request meets the facial requirements under ORS 135.815, though ORS 419A.255 preserves the confidentiality of certain juvenile records, that confidentiality is not absolute; certain exceptions exist. See ORS 419A.255(1) (“The record***shall be open to inspection by the child or youth, parent, guardian, court appointed special advocate, surrogate,” etc). Indeed, the statute provides that “no information appearing in the record of the [juvenile] case***may be disclosed***without the consent of the court. ORS 419A.255(3) (emphasis added). Clearly, then, the statute contemplates potential situations in which the documents could be disclosed. 

The interest in confidentiality must be weighed against the constitutional and statutory rights to compulsory process in a criminal trial. The court must balance the competing interests of confidentiality and disclosure. ORS 135.873 allows a court to conduct in camera inspection of materials; that inspection is “designed to accommodate the needs of discovery and the needs of confidentiality.” State v. Heisler, 106 Or. App. 7, 9-10, 806 P.2d 1154 (1991) (citations omitted) See also Warren, 304 Or 428 (CSD files, though otherwise discoverable, subject to in camera inspection because straight disclosure to defense would be improper considering statutory

confidentiality concerns). Because the requested files may have statutorily protected confidentiality interests, in camera inspection is the proper filter prior to enforcement of the discovery requirements.

III. ISSUANCE OF A SUBPOENA IS THE PROPER TOOL TO COMPEL DISCOVERY.

A. Cartwright and Nixon Do Not Govern This Case:

ORS 136.555 et seq governs the issuance of subpoenas to compel attendance of witnesses, and “books, papers or documents” when necessary. See ORS 136.580. As the Attorney General correctly notes, a subpoena duces tecum is a means for production of evidence, not informational discovery. Motion to Quash at Pg 3; citing State v. Cartwright, 173 Or. App. 59, 68, 20 P.3d 223 (2001). Mr. Day has a statutory right to discover the requested evidence. Because it has not been provided to him, he issued a subpoena to compel “production of [the] evidence.” With this, the inquiry should end. Mr. Day has a statutory right to the evidence. Mr. Day issued a subpoena to compel production of the evidence.

In such, the Attorney General’s contention that Cartwright governs the issue in this case is in error. In that case defendant was accused of sexually harassing co-workers at SCAA. After the initial accusations were made against the defendant, and in anticipation of civil litigation, SCAA initiated an internal investigation which included recorded interviews with the alleged victims. Id. at 60-61. Defendant sought, through a subpoena, to compel production of the tapes. The defendant hoped that the interviews would reveal prior inconsistent statements by the victims, which could then be used to impeach their testimony at trial. Id. 61. The trial court denied defendant access to the tapes.

In upholding the trial court’s decision, the Court of Appeals answered three questions. First, the Court held that the defendant did not have a statutory right to discovery of the materials because, as is implicit in the discovery statute quoted above, “a criminal defendant has no statutory right to informational discovery of materials in the possession of nonparties...” Id. at 66. ORS 135.805 et seq. requires disclosure of materials and information “within the possession or control of the district attorney.” Therefore, as an initial matter, the defendant was not statutorily entitled to the tapes.

Second, the Court discussed whether defendant has a “statutory right to obtain the [tapes] pursuant to the applicable criminal subpoena procedures.” Id. In other words, the Court questioned whether the subpoena power is the proper means for obtaining “informational” discovery. The defendant in Cartwright misused the subpoena power because he failed to affirmatively show the four Nixon factors adopted by the court in that case. In particular, the Court emphasized the weakness of Cartwright’s argument under two of the Nixon factors: 1) pretrial disclosure is premature when, as in Cartwright, the content of the evidence is unknown and the value of the evidence is the potential impeachment of a witness’s prior inconsistent statements; and 2) the facts clearly supported the trial court’s finding that Cartwright’s demand for the tapes was a “fishing expedition.” See Id.; Cartwright, 173 Or. App. at 69-70, 71-72.

Third, after holding that Cartwright failed to show any statutory right to the tapes, the Court considered whether the defendant had a constitutional right to discovery under the Oregon and Federal compulsory process clauses. Cartwright, 173 Or. App. at 72; See also Or Const, Art I, §11; US Const, Amend VI. In this final part of the Court’s analysis it was held that:

For a defendant to compel the production of privileged or protected materials, the defendant must clear two hurdles. First, the defendant must demonstrate that the testimony or other information sought is not only favorable to him, but is material to the issues in the case. If the defendant so demonstrates, then the defendant must identify an interest in the evidence that outweighs the legitimate interest reflected in the privilege or evidentiary limitation that the defendant seeks to overcome.

Cartwright, 173 Or. App. at 73. In clearing the first “hurdle,” the Court recognized that impeachment evidence “as well as directly exculpatory evidence” is “material and favorable” and included in the defendant’s constitutional rights. Id. at 75. However, this does not include all impeachment evidence. Impeachment evidence is only material and favorable if, when used effectively, it “may ‘make the difference between conviction and acquittal,’” or if there is a “reasonable probability that its use at trial would change the result of the proceeding.” Id. citing U.S. v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667, 676, 682, 105 S.Ct. 3375 (1985). In Cartwright, defendant anticipated, but wasn’t certain, that the tapes would have impeachment value of showing the victims’ prior inconsistent statements. Further, even if defendant knew the tapes would provide such ammunition, the value to him could only be determined at trial, not pre-trial, because the actual value of the impeachment testimony necessarily would be based on the victims’ actual testimony. Id. at75-76. Thus, Cartwright’s constitutional argument failed as well.

This case is factually distinguishable in many aspects because, unlike in Cartwright, the defendant in this case has a statutory right to the evidence. In Cartwright, once the Court established that Cartwright did not have a statutory right to discover the tapes, the Court then moved on to discuss why he therefore misused the subpoena power to obtain the tapes. Cartwright, in essence, tried to use a subpoena to obtain tapes he did not otherwise have a right to obtain. Under those circumstances, the Court concluded, the four Nixon factors must first be met. Id. at 68-69. The subpoena power is not a tool for discovery of evidence. Id. The Nixon factors ensure that when a defendant requests something he/she is not entitled to, that he/she does so with proper justification. Hypothetically, of course, Cartwright may have been able to subpoena production of the tapes even though he did not have a statutory discovery right to them. In order to see if, indeed, he could properly do so, the Court looked to the Nixon factors. Cartwright did not meet them.

The discussion in Cartwright concerning applicability of the Nixon factors and the proper use of the subpoena power follows the Court’s framing of the second issue in that case: does Cartwright have a “statutory right to obtain [the tapes] pursuant to the applicable criminal subpoena procedures.” At 66. This is not a question of the defendant’s right to discovery; rather it is a question that asks, can a defendant obtain something by means of a subpoena? In Cartwright, firstly, the defendant did not have a statutory right to discover the tapes. Secondly, but separately, he could not use the subpoena power to obtain them because he did not meet the four Nixon factors. In sum, Mr. Day submits that this is not a matter governed by Cartwright or the Nixon factors because he is otherwise entitled to the documents under the Oregon discovery statute, and as discussed briefly below, under the U.S. and Oregon Constitutions. Therefore, issuance of a subpoena to compel production of the requested evidence was proper in this case.

B. Assuming, Arguendo, that Nixon Applies, Mr. Day’s Subpoena Meets the Four Factors:

Mr. Day’s subpoena to compel production of the records does satisfy the four Nixon factors. First, the documents are evidentiary and relevant. As discussed above, the documents contain impeachment evidence of a key state witness that goes to showing the reliability and credibility of his testimony. In Cartwright the defendant sought impeachment evidence that would have potentially been used to impeach prior inconsistent statements of the witness. Cartwright, 173 Or. App. at 69-70. This was only potentially valuable to Cartwright because his ability to contradict the prior statement clearly depended on how and to what the witness actually testified to at trial. Id. In such, the request was premature and not relevant at pre-trial. Here, however, the impeachment value is known. The documents are therefore evidentiary and relevant.

Second, the documents are not “otherwise procurable reasonably in advance of trial by exercise of due diligence.” Id. at 68. Mr. Day has filed other motions seeking these documents and the District Attorney and Attorney General offices have not complied. Third, Mr. Day cannot “properly prepare for trial without pretrial production and inspection and that failure to produce the documents for inspection” may delay the trial. See Id. at 68. The requested evidence is critical to Mr. Day’s defense, and without the information contained in the files, the defense will not be able to fairly and completely prepare for trial.

Finally, this request is not a general “fishing expedition to ascertain the existence of evidence, rather than to produce specific evidence of known value.” Id. at 68. As explained above, unlike in Cartwright, Mr. Day knows the value of this evidence to his case. Mr. Day knows the files exist. Clearly, the defense can never know completely what is contained in the requested files as the defense has clearly never seen the files. Cartwright was hoping that inspection of the documents might reveal information, which he might have been able to use should the witness testify as he hoped. Simply stated, Mr. Day’s initial intention is not to hopefully find impeachment evidence of prior inconsistent statements; his intention is to inspect documents of known value to his defense, and to use them to impeach witnesses against him at trial. In sum, though Cartwright does not control this case, nor should the court consider the Nixon factors in making its ruling, in any event, Mr. Day’s subpoena does meet those four requirements.

C. Cartwright Also Provides a Separate Constitutional Right to Discovery of the Documents.

The Cartwright Court recognized, distinct from the statutory discovery right and the independent subpoena power, a constitutional right to “compel the production of evidence.” Id. at 72. As a separate matter, Mr. Day has a constitutional right to discovery. Since the discovery was not provided, Mr. Day compelled production of the evidence through the subpoena power.In it’s analysis of Cartwright’s constitutional right to compel evidence, it was held that:

For a defendant to compel the production of privileged or protected materials, the defendant must clear two hurdles. First, the defendant must demonstrate that the testimony or other information sought is not only favorable to him, but is material to the issues in the case. If the defendant so demonstrates, then the defendant must identify an interest in the evidence that outweighs the legitimate interest reflected in the privilege or evidentiary limitation that the defendant seeks to overcome. Cartwright, 173 Or. App. at 73.

In clearing the first “hurdle,” the Court recognized that impeachment evidence “as well as directly exculpatory evidence” is “material and favorable” and included in the defendant’s constitutional rights. Id. at 75. However, this does not include all impeachment evidence. Impeachment evidence is only material and favorable if, when used effectively, it “may ‘make the difference between conviction and acquittal,’” or if there is a “reasonable probability that its use at trial would change the result of the proceeding.” Id. citing U.S. v. Bagley, 473 U.S. 667, 676, 682, 105 S.Ct. 3375 (1985). In Cartwright, defendant anticipated, but wasn’t certain, that the tapes would have impeachment value of showing the victims’ prior inconsistent statements. Further, even if defendant knew the tapes would provide such ammunition, the value to him could only be determined at trial, not pre-trial. Id. at 75-76. Thus, Cartwright’s constitutional argument failed. As explained above, this case is distinguishable in that respect.

IV. THE COURT MAY INSPECT THE RECORDS IN CAMERA:

Having established that Mr. Day has a statutory and constitutional right to discovery of the requested documents, the State’s alternative request of the court to view the material in camera is proper. Though ORS 419A.255 preserves the confidentiality of certain juvenile records, that interest in confidentiality must be weighed against the constitutional and statutory rights to compulsory process in a criminal trial. As mentioned above, the court must balance the competing interests of confidentiality and disclosure. ORS 135.873 allows a court to conduct in camera inspection of materials; that inspection is “designed to accommodate the needs of discovery and the needs of confidentiality. State v. Heisler, 106 Or. App. 7, 9-10, 806 P.2d 1154 (1991) (citations omitted). Because the evidence in question has known impeachment value of great importance, and may, in fact, contain other exculpatory or otherwise discoverable information, in camera review is precisely the tool this court may use to determine whether the records must be disclosed.

Additionally, the Oregon Administrative Rules require OYA to submit reports for in camera review when there are issues of confidentiality. OAR 416-110-0030 states:

(b) Whenever any OYA staff member is served with a subpoena or any other legal process which might involve the release of confidential information, the staff will bring to the attention of the presiding judge the state and federal statute and regulations relating to confidentiality of the records in question and will ask the court's guidance in testifying. The staff will then answer questions at the direction of the judge.

This rule requires OYA to comply with subpoenas and other legal requests for information.

Mr. Day therefore requests that the trial judge order the prosecution to provide the documents listed above to the defense, and turn over to Mr. Day all statutorily mandated discovery, all exculpatory material, and all other materials reasonably likely to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence, or inspect the requested records in camera.

Arguments

  1. SCOPE OF THE PRIVILEGE

The psychotherapist-patient privilege recognized by the Supreme Court as a rule of evidence in Jaffee v. Redmond, 518 U.S. 1(1996), protects only “confidential communications” made by the patient to his therapist in the course of diagnosis and treatment: “The question we address is whether statements the officer[patient] made to her therapist during the counseling sessions are protected from compelled disclosure in a federal civil action brought by the family of the deceased.” 518 U.S. at 3. This rule does not prohibit disclosure of all information that may be contained in a therapist’s file, but only “confidential communications” by the patient. Jaffeedoes not hold that a psychiatric diagnosis of a mental disease or disorder, or related medical condition such as insomnia or stress, is privileged, nor does it make privileged that the psychiatrist prescribed certain medications or other treatment as a result of the diagnosis; nor does it hold that the length of time a person received psychiatric services, or the number of counseling sessions, is privileged.2

Jaffeealso does not hold that the psychotherapist-patient privilege is absolute and has no exceptions. To the contrary, the Supreme Court observed:

A rule that authorizes the recognition of new privileges on a case-by-case basis makes it appropriate to define the details of new privileges in a like manner. Because this is the first case in which we have recognized a psychotherapist privilege, it is neither necessary nor feasible to delineate its full contours in a way that would "govern all conceivable future questions in this area.” 518 U.S. at 18 (citation omitted).

The Court went on to note:

Although it would be premature to speculate about most future developments in the federal psychotherapist privilege, we do not doubt that there are situations in which the privilege must give way, for example, if a serious threat of harm to the patient or to others can be averted only by means of a disclosure by the therapist. Id. at n.19.

  1. APPLICATION OF THE PRIVILEGE IN A CRIMINAL CASE

Jaffee does not discuss how the evidentiary privilege is to be applied when a criminal defendant's constitutional rights are implicated. At least one noted commentator has concluded that an exception to Jaffee will be established when necessary to vindicate a defendant's constitutional rights to a fair trial. 3 Weinstein's Federal Evidence, §504.07[9], 504-24 (2nd ed. 2000). The Ninth Circuit has not addressed this issue; however, several district courts have addressed the application of Jaffee in criminal proceedings.

In United States v. Alperin, 128 F. Supp. 2d 1251, 1255 (N.D. California 2001), the Court determined that an in camera review of mental health records sought by a Rule 17(c) subpoena was required to determine what records should be disclosed, given that the patient was a key Government witness and the defense argued the witness’ mental state was relevant both to the defense theory of the case, as well as to the credibility of the witness. The witness in Alperinwas under the care of a psychiatrist for depression, much like the situation in the case at bar. While recognizing that Jaffee rejected the use of a balancing test to determine when the privilege should give way in civil proceedings, Alperinreasoned that case law supported a balancing test in criminal proceedings. See 128 F. Supp. 2d at 1252-1255.

In United States v. Hansen, 955 F. Supp. 1225, 1226 (D. Montana, 1997), the Court granted defendant’s Rule 17(c) subpoena for psychiatric records of the deceased victim after engaging in a balancing test:

In Jaffee, the Court found that the important public and private interests underlying the privilege outweighed the "modest" evidentiary benefit that would likely result from denial of the privilege. Jaffee, 116 S. Ct. at 1929. Here, in contrast, the likely evidentiary benefit is great: The defendant is charged with homicide and faces a possible loss of liberty. The mental and emotional condition of the deceased is a central element of her claim of self-defense. The holder of the privilege has little private interest in preventing disclosure, because he is dead. The public does have an interest in preventing disclosure, since persons in need of therapy may be less likely to seek help if they fear their most personal thoughts will be revealed, even after their death. See Jaffee, 116 S. Ct. at 1928-29. However, I find that the defendant's need for the privileged material outweighs this interest. 955 F. Supp. At 1226.

In United States v. Haworth,168 F.R.D. 660, 661 (D.New. Mexico 1996), the court conducted in camera review of mental health records of a prosecution witness obtained under a Rule 17(c) subpoena, and concluded: “Defendants' confrontation rights permit them to cross-examine Maximino Baca fully regarding his treatment by Dr. Landau. They will not, however, have the benefit of Dr. Landau's treatment records.” One commentator has read this decision to stand for the proposition that the mental health treatment provider could likewise be subject to cross-examination by the defense regarding the witness’ diagnosis and treatment. See SYMPOSIUM: THE UNIFORM RULES OF EVIDENCE (1999): The Mental Health Provider Privilege in the Wake of Jaffe v. Redmond, 54 Okla. L. Rev. 591, 605 (2001). Assuming the witness-patient’s testimony about his mental condition is material to his ability to have accurately observed and recalled the events in question, and that testimony is suspect, the defense may have the right to present extrinsic evidence, such as the therapist’s testimony—if not his records—to contradict the witness. See generally, United States v. Castillo, 181 F3d 1129, 1132-34 (9th Cir. 1999).

In United States v. Doyle, 1 F. Supp.2d 1187 (D. Or. 1998), this Court quashed two subpoenas for mental health records issued by the defendant to the victim's psychologist and licensed clinical social worker, holding that Jaffee precluded disclosure of the records. The Court held the defense’s assertion of the Sixth Amendment compulsory process right was inadequate to support rummaging through the victim’s mental health files, and also declined a request to conduct an in camera review, finding that act would likewise breach the privilege. Id. at 1190-91. However, Doyle arose in the context of a disputed sentencing enhancement, not in the context of a criminal trial. Furthermore, Doyleconcerned mental health records generated after the fact, as a result of the defendant’s criminal conduct against the victim, not the mental health status of a witness at the time the witness perceived the events in question, or at the time of the witness’ trial testimony.

  1. THE ISSUE OF WAIVER

Mr. Snitch has voluntarily disclosed information concerning his diagnosis and treatment by Doctor __to his employer, and to at least one state agency. See Exhibits 1, 2, & 3. It is likely that Mr. Snitch has agreed to release these records to a U.S. Probation officer in connection with his presentence report, and may have agreed to release this information to Pretrial Services, given the conditions of his release. The Court must consider the issue of waiver in deciding the scope of the privilege which remains for Mr. Snitch. If Mr. Snitch is willing to waive the privilege to obtain benefits from his employer, the state, and the federal government, he should not be allowed to claim the privilege when the same information may be used to discredit his testimony at trial.

  1. THE NEED FOR IN CAMERA REVIEW

The defense concedes that some of the subpoenaed records should reflect statements by Mr. Snitch to his psychiatrist that are privileged under Jaffee. The defense also concedes that it has no good faith reason to believe that Mr. Snitch’s otherwise privileged statements regarding his divorce and resulting feelings to Dr. __ should be disclosed in order to preserve Mr. Unlucky Sixth Amendment rights to confrontation or Fifth Amendment due process right to a fair trial. That said, the defense contends the privilege does not apply to the content of all of the records, because the scope of the privilege is limited, even if, arguendo, the privilege is “absolute” and not subject to balancing in criminal cases. Furthermore, Mr. Snitch has waived the privilege, at least as to Dr.__’s diagnosis and course of treatment.3 To determine what records of those subpoenaed fall within the privilege and those as to which the privilege has not been waived, the Court must conduct an in camera review. Compare, United States v. Amlani, 169 F3d 1189, 1196 (9th Cir. 1999)(trusting that district court will conduct an in camera review of all attorney-client privileged documents so that only those documents or portions of documents to which the privilege had been waived would be disclosed).
