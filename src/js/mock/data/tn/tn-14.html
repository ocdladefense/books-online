Chapter 14. Defense Witnesses

By Bob Moon, Brook Reinhard

I. Introduction

The purpose of the Trial Notebookis to be able to find legal authority quickly as issues arise during trial. This chapter focuses on defense witnesses. (Note: “defenses” is covered in Chapter 3). I intend to discuss the foundational requirements for various types of affirmative defense evidence, along with the types of evidence deemed admissible by Oregon courts. Most of these citations are to “the bible”: Laird Kirkpatrick’s Oregon Evidence, 5th edition, which is a book that sits on every trial judge’s desk (and should be at your counsel table, too).

II. Pretrial

You have worked hard on this case. You have received all of the discovery, reviewed it with your client and have hired an investigator. (You chose not to interview the witnesses yourself because you did not want to become a witness at trial.) Your investigator has located both fact and character witnesses. You have had thorough discussions with an expert you think will help you at trial (see the next chapter). You’re feeling good – you know that pretrial preparation is the key to winning cases. You have developed a theme for trial.

A. Defense Obligation to Provide Discovery

Now that you have carefully crafted your case, the last thing you want is a judge to rule that your witnesses cannot testify because you failed to provide the state with discovery (see Chapter 2 for further information on discovery).

ORS 135.835 states, “Except as otherwise provided in ORS 135.855 and 135.873, the defense shall disclose to the district attorney the following material and information within the possession or control of the defense:

(1) The names and addresses of persons, including the defendant, whom the defense intends to call as witnesses at the trial, together with relevant written or recorded statements or memoranda of any oral Statements of such persons other than the defendant.

(2) Any reports or statements of experts, made in connection with the particular case, including the results of physical or mental examinations and of scientific tests, experiments or comparisons that the defense intends to offer in evidence at the trial.

(3) Any books, papers, documents, photographs or tangible objects that the defense intends to offer into evidence at the trial.

ORS 135.865 states, “[u]pon being apprised of any breach of the duty imposed by the provisions of ORS 135.805 to 135.873 and 135.970, the court may order the violating party to permit inspection of the material, or grant a continuance, or refuse to permit a witness to testify, or refuse to receive into evidence the material not disclosed, or enter such other order as it considers appropriate.”

The court has the authority in the face of a discovery violation to preclude that testimony of a witness. However, to do so the court must find that: 1) actual prejudice to the other party and 2) no other sanction, short of exclusion, would remedy that prejudice. State v. Ben, 310 Or 309 (1990); State v. Lindquist, 141 Or App 84 (1996); State v. Johanesen, 110 Or App 348 (1991).

The courts have been paying more attention lately to issue preservation, so if you find yourself on the wrong end of a discovery violation and the judge is considering sanctions, make sure to ask for less severe sanctions, and for the trial court to make findings. See State v. Wyatt, 331 Or 335 (2000) (defendant failed to preserve his argument that exclusion of the defense witness was too severe of a sanction).See also State v. Cunningham, 197 Or App 264 (2005). In Cunningham, the defense witness had notes that were not turned over. The trial court excluded the witness testimony as a remedy, and the defendant failed to seek a less severe remedy, or to ask the judge for a recess so the prosecutor could review the notes. The Court of Appeals therefore upheld the exclusion of the witness testimony.

Prosecutors tell me that defense lawyers rarely provide discovery. I am amazed when I hear this. The last thing I want is for a judge to order sanctions because I haven’t complied with the discovery requirement. More importantly, however, providing discovery lets the prosecution know that you have worked hard on the case and they are in trouble. Many of my cases have been dismissed or we get tremendous plea offers after providing the state with discovery.

Once you have complied with this discovery requirement, the prosecutor may attempt to contact your witnesses. In State v. York, 291 Or 535 (1981), the Oregon Supreme Court held that the state may not order or advise a witness not to speak to the defense. It is not improper, however, to advise witnesses of the right to refuse to submit to an interview by the defense. The same rules apply to the defense. Defense witnesses can be advised by you of the right to refuse to submit to a prosecutor or police officer interview. When contacted, your witness might say, “I would rather say what I have to say in court under oath” or “I would be glad to discuss this case with you, but I would like to have the defendant’s lawyer present.” However, you cannot advise your witness not to talk to the prosecutor.

B. Subpoenas

You just received a trial date, so it is time to issue subpoenas. You know to issue these subpoenas as soon as possible after receiving the trial date because if a subpoena is not served, and you are asking for a continuance, you know the judge’s first question will be, “Counselor, when did you issue the subpoena?”

I tend to subpoena everyone who has the slightest possibility of helping me on the case (excluding my experts). Do not assume that because a witness is on the state’s witness list that the person has been subpoenaed and will show up at trial. If you might want the person there, subpoena them. Never assume a “friendly” witness will appear voluntarily. Tell them that it is simply your practice to subpoena everybody. (I once had a defendant’s mother talk me out of issuing a subpoena by assuring me that she would certainly be there in court and then she didn’t show).

1. Out-of-State Subpoenas For out-of-state subpoenas, follow the procedures in ORS 136.623 et. seq. and give yourself a lot of lead time. The procedures in both Oregon and the foreign jurisdiction vary from county to county and state to state. In general, however, you will need to file a motion for a certificate for the out-of-state witness with the judge in your Oregon criminal case. Attached to that motion should be your affidavit explaining why the out-of-state witness is material to your defense. If the state opposes your motion or if there is otherwise a hearing, you will ask the Oregon judge to find that the witness is “material” in your case. If the judge agrees, the judge will sign a Certificate of Out-of-State Witness.

A proceeding must then be initiated in the county in which the witness resides. This proceeding would be an order to show cause why the witness should not be compelled to attend your Oregon trial. The foreign judge would set a hearing date in the local court and issue a summons commanding the witness to appear there. Again, consult the local court to learn their procedures. At this hearing, you will likely need someone to represent your Oregon client. Sometimes either the district attorney’s office in the out-of-state county or the public defenders’ office there will help. Sometimes you have to hire private counsel for this hearing.

At this hearing, the judge typically reconsiders your position that this witness is “material” in the Oregon proceeding. The judge will also make sure that commanding the attendance of this out-of-state witness at your Oregon trial is not unduly burdensome. The judge wants to be assured that the out-of-state witness’s food, lodging, and travel expenses will all be paid. Though you may have out-of-state counsel representing your client, you should try to appear by phone at this hearing to answer any of the judge’s questions. Oftentimes the court expects a check for an amount covering all of these expenses to be in the file at the time of this hearing. If the judge finds that the witness is material to your Oregon trial and that the check you provided will cover the witness’s expenses, the judge will issue an order commanding the appearance of that witness at your Oregon trial. The judge will also serve that witness with a summons commanding the appearance. The judge will also give the check for expenses to the witness.

Again, procedures differ from county to county. Sometimes we’ve found that the court has no procedures since no one has ever tried to secure the attendance of the out-of-state witness there. This procedure can take up to two months.

See the Appendix for forms on subpoenaing out-of-state witnesses.

2. Police Officer Subpoenas If you are subpoenaing a police officer, review ORS 136.595(2), which reads:

(2)(a) Every law enforcement agency shall designate an individual or individuals upon whom service of subpoena may be made. At least one of the designated individuals shall be available during normal business hours. In the absence of the designated individuals, service of subpoena pursuant to paragraph (b) of this subsection may be made upon the officer in charge of the law enforcement agency.

(b) If a peace officer’s attendance at trial is required as a result of employment as a peace officer, a subpoena may be served on such officer by delivering a copy personally to the officer or to one of the individuals designated by the agency that employs the officer. A subpoena may be served by delivery to one of the individuals designated by the agency that employs the officer only if the subpoena is delivered at least 10 days before the date the officer’s attendance is required, the officer is currently employed as a peace officer by the agency, and the officer is present within the state at the time of service.

(c) When a subpoena has been served as provided in paragraph (b) of this subsection, the law enforcement agency shall make a good faith effort to actually notify the officer whose attendance is sought of the date, time and location of the court appearance. If the officer cannot be notified, the law enforcement agency shall contact the court and a continuance may be granted to allow the officer to be personally served.

(d) As used in this subsection, “law enforcement agency” means the Oregon State Police, a county sheriff’s department or a municipal police department.

I do office service if it can be done outside of 10 days before trial. I read the statute to compel the attendance of a police officer if the officer is personally served at any time. I’ve found, however, that many police officers believe that they do not need to comply with any subpoena unless it is served at their office outside of 10 days before trial. So if you serve an officer personally within the 10 days of trial you may get an uproar. I’ve seen motions to quash filed by the district attorney or even the attorney general’s office and I once had a DA ask the judge for sanctions against me (for serving an officer with a subpoena during a trial) and to have me pay attorney’s fees. (This request was denied and the officer was ordered to testify.)

3. Medical Records If you want to subpoena medical records, make sure you comply with the HIPAA Privacy Regulations 45 CFR § 164.512.

In general, in a criminal case there are two main ways to obtain medical records that are covered by HIPAA. You can either get a signed consent or subpoena the documents after you have obtained a qualified protective order.

a. Consent

The easiest and safest way to receive protected health information is pursuant to a signed individual authorization. An individual authorization to disclose medical records must meet the specific HIPAA requirements of 45 CFR § 164.508 and any applicable state requirements. The HIPAA regulations specify the terms of the authorization. All authorizations must include:

A specific and meaningful description of the information to be used and/or disclosed;

The name or other specific identification of the person(s) or entity authorized to make the requested use and/or disclosure;

The name or specific identification of the person(s) or entity to whom the use and/or disclosure will be made;

A description of each purpose of the requested use or disclosure (“at the request of the individual” is a sufficient description of the purpose);

An expiration date, or an event that will trigger expiration of the authorization;

A statement of the individual’s right to revoke the authorization in writing, including exceptions to such written revocation, and an explanation of how the individual can exercise his or her revocation rights;

A statement that the information to be used and/or disclosed may be subject to redisclosure by the recipient of the information, in which case it is no longer subject to the privacy standards;

The individual’s signature and date; and

If the signature belongs to an individual’s legal and/or authorized representative, a description of that person’s authority to act on behalf of the individual.

b.Subpoena/Qualified Protective Order

A covered entity may disclose protected health information in the course of any judicial proceeding in response to a HIPAA compliant subpoena. Under the HIPAA regulations, the covered entity must be given “satisfactory assurances” from the party seeking the information that “reasonable efforts” have been made to secure a qualified protective order.

A qualified protective order is an order from a court that prohibits the parties from using or disclosing the protected health information for any purpose other than the litigation or proceeding, and requires the return or destruction of the information (including all copies) at the end of the litigation or proceeding.

HIPAA requirements for a subpoena are set forth in ORCP 55. There must be documentation demonstrating compliance with HIPAA.

4. Drug and Alcohol Records Like medical records, the two ways for defense counsel to obtain drug and alcohol records are through consent or through a subpoena that has been issued in compliance with 42 CFR § 290dd-2. (A great book to guide you through these regulations is Confidentiality and Communication: A Guide to the Federal Drug and Alcohol Confidentiality Law and HIPAA, Legal Action Center; et al. eds., 2006.)

a. Consent

Like medical records, the fastest and easiest way to get drug and alcohol records is to have the person whose records you want sign a consent form. Under both 42 CFR § 2 and HIPAA, a proper consent form must be in writing and contain each of the following items:

The name or general designation of the program making the disclosure;

The name of the individual or organization that will receive the disclosure;

The name of the patient who is the subject of the disclosure;

The purpose or need for the disclosure;

A description of how much and what kind of information will be disclosed;

The patient’s right to revoke the consent in writing, and the exceptions to the right to revoke or, if the exceptions are included in the programs notice, a reference to the notice;

The program’s ability to condition treatment, payment, enrollment or eligibility of benefits on the patient agreeing to sign the consent, by stating either that the program may not condition these services on the patient signing the consent, or the consequences of the patient refusing to sign the consent;

The date, event or condition upon which the consent expires if not previously revoked;

The signature of the patient (and/or authorized person); and

The date on which the consent is signed.

b. Subpoena

To issue a valid subpoena under the federal regulations you must file a motion in your Oregon criminal case for an “authorizing order” from the court. In the motion, you must be careful not to disclose the name of the person whose drug and alcohol records are sought. You should ask the court for an in camerahearing to protect the confidentiality of the person whose records are being sought. The drug and alcohol treatment program which holds the records must be served with notice of this hearing and given an opportunity to make an oral or written statement to the court. 42 CFR §§ 2.64(a),(b) 265(a),(b), 2.66(a). The easiest way to comply with this rule is to serve the motion for the authorizing order on the individual whose records are sought and the drug and alcohol treatment program and give them notice of the hearing. This issue often arises when the state seeks to obtain drug and alcohol records in the course of a termination of parental rights case. A copy of the State’s form motion and order is in the Appendix.

At the hearing, the court must determine there is “good cause” to enable the court to issue an “authorizing order” allowing you to subpoena the drug and alcohol records. A court can find “good cause” only if it determines that the public interest and the need for disclosure outweigh any adverse effect that disclosure will have on the patient, the doctor-patient relationship, and the effectiveness of the program’s services. If the information sought is available elsewhere, the court should not issue the order. 42 CFR § 2.64(d). The judge may examine the records in camerabefore making a decision. 42 CFR § 2.64(c).

There are also general limits under 42 CFR Part 2 on the scope of disclosure that a court may authorize, even when “good cause” is present. Disclosure must be limited to the information as essential to fulfill the purpose of the order, and it must be restricted to those persons who need the information for that purpose. The court should also take any other steps that are necessary to protect the patient’s confidentiality, including sealing court records from public scrutiny. 42 CFR § 2.64(e).

A court may not authorize disclosure of “confidential communications” by a patient to the program unless the disclosure (a) is necessary to protect against a threat to life or of serious bodily injury, or (b) is necessary to investigate or prosecute an extremely serious crime, or (c) it is in connection with a proceeding in which the patient has already presented evidence concerning confidential communications. In all other situations, not even a court can order disclosure of confidential communications. 42 CFR § 2.63.

After the court has considered the above issues and signed an authorizing order, you can then issue your subpoena to the drug and alcohol treatment program or the patient.

5. Objections to Defense Subpoenas – Generally If anyone files a motion to quash the subpoena, always remember that in a criminal case your client has the right to compulsory process under the [#amendmentvi Sixth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution] and Article 1, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution. Also remember that these constitutional rights “trump” statutory obligations. State v. Keenan, 307 Or 515 (1989).

Now that you have had all of your subpoenas served, you know to have the proofs of service filed with the court. You do this because you know that if you have served a subpoena and the witness doesn’t show up and you ask for a continuance, the first thing the judge is going to ask is, “Counselor, have you served a subpoena?” and you want to be able to answer, “Yes, your Honor, and the proof of service is in the file.”

C. Jail Witnesses

Clothing. “Incarcerated witnesses and defendants appearing for trial must be dressed in neat, clean civilian clothing, unless otherwise ordered by the court.” UTCR 3.020.

Shackles. A trial judge has the discretion to order shackling of the defendant only if there is substantial evidence of an immediate and serious risk of dangerous or disruptive behavior or escape. State v. Glick, 73 Or App 79, 697 P2d 1002 (1985). The judge must make findings to support shackling. It is not sufficient to simply rely on what the jail deputy or the prosecutor says. See State v. Merrell, 170 Or App 400 (2000),accord, State v. Bird, 59 Or App 74, 77 (1982).

''See also State ex rel Juvenile Dept. of Multnomah County v. Millican, 138 Or App 142, 906 P2d 857 (1995). Same considerations apply to shackling of a juvenile in court trial. The Court of Appeals concluded that because the trial court received no evidence that child posed an immediate and serious risk of dangerous or disruptive behavior and made no findings to that effect, the denial of child’s motion to remove the leg chains was error. The court found the error to be harmless, however.

See also State v. Bates, 203 Or App 245 (2005). Although the court cites with approval the principles noted inState ex rel Juvenile Department of Multnomah County v. MillicanandState v. Glick, supra, the court held that if there was any error in requiring a defendant to wear a non-visible “stun belt,” any such error was harmless under the facts of this particular case.Bates'' provides the most recent thorough discussion of the prejudice involved in requiring defendants to be shackled during trial.

Practice Tip

Make sure the judge conducts an inquiry outside the jury’s presence before your client is seen by the jury in restraints. The record must show a substantial justification for the restraint; if not, it is a basis for reversal on appeal.

If your client is seen in shackles by the jury and there is no justification for the restraint, move for a mistrial.

This analysis also applies to other jail witnesses, although there are no Oregon appellate cases directly on point.

D. Preparing Defense Witnesses

You are now within a few days of trial, and you have set time aside to prepare your witnesses. Many of the witnesses have never testified and are nervous. Witness preparation is very important. You will talk to them about what to wear (comfortable clothes “dressed up” enough to be respectful for the jury and not out of character for the what the person is – accountants should wear suits, not blue jeans, and factory workers should wear clean comfortable clothes, not a suit). They will want to know what they will be testifying about and what to expect on cross-examination.

I find this to be one of the most important functions of a criminal defense lawyer. I know how precious time can be before trial, but I don’t make the mistake of overlooking or not spending enough time on this critical pretrial function. I start off with witnesses by explaining the “rules of testifying.” After going over the rules, I give them a form to take home with them. The form reads:

==

THINGS TO REMEMBER WHEN TESTIFYING

==

1. LISTEN CAREFULLY TO THE QUESTION. Be sure you understand the question completely before you give an answer. Make sure you take enough time to be sure that you understand the question before answering. If you do not understand the question, ask the lawyer to repeat it.

2. MAKE SURE THE LAWYER HAS FINISHED ASKING THE QUESTION BEFORE YOU ANSWER. You may be able to anticipate the lawyer’s question and you may be anxious to give the answer. When this happens, two people are talking at the same time. It is very difficult for a court reporter to take down what people are saying when two people are talking at the same time. For that reason, it is a good practice to take a breath after the lawyer asks a question before you answer.

3. TELL THE TRUTH. When testifying, honesty is the only policy. Not only is it a crime to lie when testifying, it is often very obvious to the judge or jury when a person is lying. If you are caught in a lie it is likely that no one will believe anything you said. If you are asked under oath whether you talked to me about your testimony, tell the truthby saying that you talked to me about your testimony and I told you that you had to tell the truth.

4. DON’T GUESS. Remember that “I don’t know” is an answer. If you don’t know the answer to something, say you don’t know.

5. DON’T VOLUNTEER INFORMATION. Fully answer the question that was asked of you but then stop. Don’t volunteer information that was not part of the lawyer’s question.

6. WHEN YOU’RE BEING CROSS-EXAMINED, DON’T ARGUE WITH THE PROSECUTOR. Lawyers and witnesses should be courteous with each other in court. If the prosecutor becomes angry or makes you think that he/she doesn’t believe your testimony, remember to listen to the question, take a deep breath and be courteous.

7. NEVER GET ANGRY OR LOSE YOUR TEMPER. If the DA is trying to prove that you lose your temper easily there is no better way to prove it than to have your lose your temper on the stand. In any event, judges and juries have a hard time believing someone who has lost their temper.

8. TALK LOUD ENOUGH. The reason you are testifying is because you have important information in this case. Make sure the person in the back of the courtroom can hear you. Try to look at either the judge or the jury when you are testifying. They are trying to judge your credibility and you are telling the truth, so look them in the eye when you discuss the important facts you know about this case.

9. NEVER SAY “NEVER” OR “ALWAYS.” These are common exaggerations that all of us use that are usually not correct.

10. ASK TO REVIEW RECORDS, IF NECESSARY. If the other attorney is talking to you about a written record or is reading from a written record, ask to look at it to refresh your memory.

If you follow these rules, you will do fine when testifying.

-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When I’m done explaining the rules to the witness, I actually practice their testimony with them. I don’t simply go over the areas I expect the witness to testify to. I have the witness raise their right hand in my office, swear to tell the truth, and I then go over their testimony question by question.

I have found this to be a very important process. Sometimes I learn new things about the case that I didn’t know before. I try to help the witness become more effective telling their story to the jury. I believe in the old adage that says, “It is not what you say, but the way that you say it.” If your client shot someone in self-defense and they are explaining to a jury how terrified they were at the moment they pulled the trigger, they better not deliver this testimony deadpan.

Never tell a witness what to say. You can, however, improve on their delivery of the truth to the jury.

Finally, anticipate areas of cross-examination, then get stern, pretend to be the prosecutor, and ask the witness good cross-examination questions and see how they handle themselves.

III. Trial Issues

It is now the morning of trial. You are nervous but ready to go. Then, you find out one or more of the witnesses you subpoenaed has not shown up for trial.

A. Defense Witness Not Showing Up for Trial

You will have to make a tactical decision. Do you want to proceed to trial without the witness? If not, ask for a postponement under ORS 136.070. The absence of a material witness is normally basis for the postponement of the trial. A record must be made showing that the witness could be produced with a postponement and that the witness would testify to a fact material to the defense. State v. Otten,234 Or 219 (1963); State v. Niswender,79Or App 539 (1986). Remind the judge of the right to compulsory process under Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution and the [#amendmentvi Sixth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution]. Again, expect the judge to ask, “Is the witness under subpoena?” and hopefully you will answer, “The proof of service is in the file.”

If you have a hostile witness (you subpoena the person who really committed the murder) and they don’t show up at trial, ask the judge to take judicial notice of the proof of service under OEC 201. You can then argue to the jury that the witness had a motive for ignoring this subpoena. Also remember that some prior statements may be admissible if the witness is unavailable. See OEC 804.

B. Defense Witnesses

The state has put on their case. You have moved for judgment of acquittal and the motion has been denied. You have carefully considered whether the state’s case is so weak at this point that you are better off not calling any defense witnesses. You were mindful of the warnings of many trial judges who’ve seen defense lawyers, by offering defense evidence, “snap defeat out of the jaws of victory.” After carefully thinking it all through, you have decided to call defense witnesses.

1. Foundational requirements or limitations a. Corroboration or fact witnesses

“I saw defendant at the bar and he did not exhibit the signs of intoxication.”

A corroboration or fact witness need only offer relevant evidence under OEC 401. Under that rule, relevant evidence means “evidence having any tendency to make existence of any fact that is of consequence to the determination of the action more probable or less probable than it would be without the evidence.”

b. Character Witnesses

i. Truthfulness of Defense Witnesses

“I am aware of (the witness’) reputation in the community for truthfulness. He has a reputation for being truthful. In my opinion, he is truthful.”

OEC 608(1) reads:

The credibility of a witness may be attacked or supported by evidence in the form of opinion or reputation, but:

1. The evidence may refer only to character for truthfulness or untruthfulness; and

2. Evidence of truthful character is admissible only after the character of the witness for truthfulness has been attacked by opinion or reputation evidence or otherwise.

A character witness, whether testifying in the form of reputation or opinion, will not be allowed to testify until a foundation has been laid showing that the witness has sufficient acquaintance with the reputation of the person in the relevant community or sufficient personal contact with the individual to have formed a personal opinion. The contact must have been sufficiently recent so that there will be a current basis for the testimony. State v Caffee, 116 Or App 23, 27 (1992); State v Maxwell, 172 Or App 142, 150-56 (2001).

ii. Character Trait of Defendant

“Defendant has the character trait of being sexually appropriate among young girls.”

OEC 404(2)(a) states: “Evidence of a person’s character is not admissible for the purpose of proving that the person acted in conformity therewith on a particular occasion, except:

[Character of accused.] Evidence of a pertinent trait of character offered by an accused, or by the prosecution to rebut the same...”

Under Rule 404(2)(a), every criminal defendant may offer evidence of a pertinent trait of character as a defense to the charge. What constitutes a pertinent trait depends upon the crime charged. A reputation for honesty would be irrelevant to the crime of violence, and a reputation for nonviolence would be irrelevant to the crime of larceny.

This evidence must be in the form of reputation or opinion.

Examples of character trait evidence:

“Law-abiding,” United States v. Hewitt, 634 F2d 277 (5th Cir. 1981). But note, in a narcotics prosecution, evidence of a defendant’s reputation for “truth and veracity” was held to be properly excluded as not a pertinent trait. United States v. Jackson, 588 F2d 1046 (5th Cir. 1979).

“Sexual propriety” in sex abuse cases, State v. Enakiev, 175 Or App 589 (2001).

“Truthfulness and honesty,” United States v. Lechoco, 542 F2d 84 (D.C. Cir. 1976).

Defendants who chose to offer evidence of a pertinent trait of their character pursuant to this rule take a certain element of risk. By doing so, they open the door to rebuttal character evidence that the prosecutor would not otherwise be able to offer.

If criminal defendants offer what is in essence character evidence by their own testimony, the state is entitled to rebut any false characterization.

Examples of a defendant “opening the door”:

Defendant portraying himself as a “good father” in a sodomy case “opened the door” to purchasing marijuana at the park with his daughter present. State v.Guritz, 134 Or App 262, 266 (1995).

Defendant’s testimony that he had “changed a great deal” and how he now despised thieves and drug users “opened the door” for a more recent conviction for resisting arrest and fourth-degree assault. State v. Peoples, 146 Or App 565, 568–70 (1997).

iii. Character of Victim

OEC 404(2)(a) states: “Evidence of a person’s character is not admissible for the purpose of proving that the person acted in conformity therewith on a particular occasion, except:

(b) [Character of victim.] Evidence of a pertinent trait of character of the victim offered by an accused, or by the prosecution to rebut the same…”

Any evidence of the victim’s character must be in the form of opinion or reputation. State v. Whitney-Biggs, 147 Or App 509, 527 (1997) (evidence of assault of character of victim is admissible on issue of self-defense).

This is not a well-developed area of law – the defense was unable to get in evidence of a victim’s reckless, erratic or dangerous behavior in State v. Lotches, 331 Or 455 (2000), and the fact that the witness had never seen the victim become physically violent was not admitted in State v. Hicks, 133 Ariz 64 (1982), and the defense could not offer evidence of a person’s “reputation” for giving property as collateral for a debt and claiming it was stolen in State v. Marshall, 312 Or 367 (1991). In each of these cases, the courts found that the “character trait” evidence of the victim really amounted to a defense effort to offer prohibited specific act evidence about the victim. In State v. Ryel, the defendant tried to introduce evidence of the pertinent character trait of the victim and his associates for violence, but the court never directly addressed the issue because defendant framed his offer of proof as evidence admissible under 404 generally instead of specifically 404(2)(a).Ryel, 182 Or App 423 (2002) (Armstrong, J,dissenting).

iv. Bad Act Evidence

OEC 404(3) prohibits the use of evidence of other crimes, wrongs or acts to prove character in order to show that a person acted in conformity with that character. Rule 404(3) does allow, however, the use of such evidence for other purposes, such as to prove motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or accident.

I’m sure that defense bar is painfully aware of this rule as prosecutors try their best to offer “bad act” evidence concerning defendants. However, Rule 404(3) evidence can also be offered by defendant. Also, keep in mind that the bad act is not even required to be criminal in order to be admissible. State v. Hardman, 196 Or App 522 at 531 n. 6 (2004).

In US v. Cohen, 888 F2d 770, 776 (11th Cir. 1989), the court held that it was error to exclude prior “bad act” evidence offered by a defendant where the defendant claimed that a third person carried out the fraudulent scheme. Defendant offered evidence that a third person had previously engaged in similar fraud. The appellate court noted that “the standard for admission is relaxed when evidence is offered by defendant.” ''See also State v. Saavedra, 103 NM 282 (1985), where a defendant argued that the actual perpetrator of the armed robbery and felony murder for which he was charged was a third person. Defendant offered evidence that the third person had been convicted of committing eight robberies under circumstances highly similar to the crime charged within the same two-month period.

The key to admitting this type of evidence seems to be how similar it is to the crime charged. If it is dissimilar, it may not be admissible. SeeState v. Bockorny, 125 Or App 479, 487 (1993); State v. Cole, 66 Or App 203 (1983).

C. Offers of Proof

Hopefully, your defense evidence will come rolling in without any problems. However, if the prosecutor objects and the judge sustains an objection, you should make an offer of proof (See Chapter 11, “Making a Record and Offers of Proof”). Keep in mind that if the offer of proof contains both admissible and inadmissible evidence, the entire offer of proof can be rejected. ''See State v. Howard, 49 Or App 391 (1980).

D. Witness Invoking Privilege Against Self-Incrimination

The attorney must first seek a determination by the court, outside the jury’s presence, as to whether the witness will claim the privilege and is entitled to do so. If not, the prosecutor can argue to the jury the defense’s failure to call the witness. State v. Froats, 47 Or App 819, 615 P2d 1078 (1980).

It is improper to call a witness to the stand knowing that the only testimony the witness will give is the invocation of privilege against self-incrimination. Howard, infra. This is a basis for a mistrial.

Where the witness has been granted immunity by the state and still refuses to testify, the defense has a right to put that witness on the stand in front of the jury. Because the refusal to testify in such a case is not privileged, the jury is entitled to draw inferences from the refusal to testify. State v. Bishop, 49 Or App 1023, 621 P2d 1196 (1980).

E. The Constitutional Right to Present Defense

If the judge rules that part of the defense you intend to offer is inadmissible, remind the judge that you have a constitutional right to present a defense.

IV. Conclusion

The trial is over. The jury has come back with a not guilty verdict. Do not poll the jury! Preparation has been the key to your victory. Congratulations.
