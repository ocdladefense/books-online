Chapter 13. Motion for Judgment of Acquittal

By Rankin Johnson, IV

I. Introduction

The motion for judgment of acquittal tests whether the prosecution’s evidence is legally sufficient to prove the elements of the offense. A true judgment of acquittal, whether granted at trial or on appeal or subsequent review, is final and prevents a retrial.

II. Primary Authority

A. Statutory

ORS 136.445 provides: “In any criminal action the defendant may, after close of the state’s evidence, or of all the evidence, move the court for a judgment of acquittal. The court shall grant the motion if the evidence introduced is such as would not support a verdict against the defendant. The acquittal shall be a bar to another prosecution for the same offense.”

B. Constitutional

There is a constitutional right to acquittal if the state’s evidence is insufficient. Oregon Constitution, Article I, section 2; U.S. Constitution, Amendments V and XIV; State v. Harris, 288 Or 703, 609 P2d 798 (1980); Wright v. West, 505 US 277, 112 S Ct 2482, 120 L Ed 2d 225 (1992). Failure to assert a federal right may preclude federal review in a habeas corpus petition.

III. The Standard

A criminal defendant is entitled to judgment of acquittal if “after viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the prosecution, any rational trier of fact could not have found the essential elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.” State v. King, 307 Or 332, 339, 768 P2d 391 (1989) (quoting Jackson v. Virginia, 443 U.S. 307, 319 (1979)). The “light most favorable” standard is generous to the state. See, e.g., State v. Cervantes, 319 Or 121, 873 P2d 316 (1994) (inferring venue in Coos County from, inter alia, the involvement of the Coos County Victim’s Coordinator and the location in the city of Coos Bay). State v. Hennagir,246 Or App 456, 266 P3d 128 (2011) (discussing permissible inferences).

A true motion for judgment of acquittal relates solely to the sufficiency of the state’s proof, i.e., whether the evidence is sufficient to prove the elements of the offense. See, e.g., State v. Lovins, 177 Or App 534, 537, 33 P3d 1060 (2001) (so stating). It is not a basis to attack the indictment or the statute. For example,State v. Hankins, 342 Or 258, 151 P3d 149 (2007). It may, however, focus on a legal question rather than a factual one. For example, State v. Kurtz, 233 Or App 573, 228 P3d 583 (2010) (judgment of acquittal should have been granted because a tribal officer is not a police officer for purposes of attempt to elude statute).See also State v. Depeche, 242 Or App 147, 252 P3d 861 (2011) (where facts are not in dispute, motion for judgment of acquittal is reviewed as a question of law)

IV. The Procedure

A. Move Early and Often

The motion is typically made at the end of the state’s case and again at the end of all the evidence. See “Repairing the Error,” below. Beyond those statutory points, motions have been submitted after the state’s opening statement, State v. Keaton, 15 Or App 477, 516 P2d 490 (1973), and after the case is submitted to the jury, State v. Brawley, 4 Or App 229, 476 P2d 942 (1970) (trial court had no duty to rule). The trial court lacks the authority to grant a post-verdict motion for judgment of acquittal. State v. Metcalfe, 328 Or 309, 974 P2d 1189 (1999). Metcalfe considers constitutional double-jeopardy issues but not the constitutional right to a judgment of acquittal, and could be attacked in the right case.

It is customary to make the motion outside the presence of the jury, but the defense might benefit from the jury hearing counsel’s argument about why the evidence is inadequate.

B. Be Specific

Be specific about why the state’s evidence is not sufficient. ''See State v. Schoen, 229 Or App 427, 211 P3d 948 (2009), rev’d on other grounds348 Or 207, 228 P3d 1207 (2010) (discussing preservation for motions for judgment of acquittal). “[A] general motion for judgment of acquittal without specifying any theory on which the state’s proof was legally insufficient…preserves no ground for challenge on appeal.” State v. Schodrow, 187 Or App 224, 231 n 5, 66 P3d 547 (2003).

A challenge to the sufficiency of the evidence made during closing argument in a bench trial might permit appellate review of that issue. State v. McCants/Walker, 231 Or App 570, 576, 220 P3d 436 (2009), ''rev’d on other grounds sub nom State v. Baker-Krofft, 348 Or 655, 239 P3d 226 (2010). But it would be foolish to risk it; counsel should move for judgment of acquittal in a bench trial just as in a jury trial.

C. Insist on a Ruling

Oregon courts must rule on the motion before the jury’s verdict; once the jury returns a verdict, the court cannot grant a judgment of acquittal. Metcalfe, 328 Or 309.

A ruling that the state has not carried its burden of proof is dispositive; the state cannot appeal, and a subsequent prosecution is prevented by double-jeopardy principles. See “VI., Effect of a Judgment of Acquittal,” below.

By contrast, a ruling that the accusatory instrument is insufficient or varies too widely from the proof permits a subsequent prosecution. Id. Counsel should not raise these issues mid-trial as a motion for judgment of acquittal. The court cannot properly rule on the sufficiency of the accusatory instrument at that time and they are not preserved for appeal. Hankins, 342 Or 258.

D. Repairing the Error

A trial court has discretion to grant the state’s motion to reopen the case. ORCP 58B; State v. Agee, 223 Or App 729, 196 P3d 1060 (2008). The court’s discretion might be more limited, and is more likely to be exercised in your favor, if the defense has offered evidence before the motion is made. So, in the right case, it would be worth not making the motion at the close of the state’s case, putting on evidence (preferably completely unrelated to the gap in the state’s case) and then making the motion. Id. (discussing factors to guide the exercise of discretion). Agee cites Creasey v. Hogan, 292 Or 154, 169, 637 P2d 114 (1981), for the proposition that the court should not reopen evidence once the jury starts deliberating, but a trial court might find the motion untimely once the jury is out. ''See State v. Brust, 158 Or App 455, 974 P2d 734 (1999) (suggesting that the trial court has discretion to rule on an MJOA at that time, and reviewing on appeal after the trial court rules on the merits.) The court cannot grant an MJOA post-verdict. Metcalfe, 328 Or 309.

Evidence produced during the defense case will be considered by the trial court on a renewed motion at the end of the case, and may cure any error in the mid-trial ruling. State v. Nix, 7 Or App 383, 491 P2d 635 (1971); State v. Stothoff, 99 Or App 197, 781 P2d 412 (1989). Defense counsel should object to this procedure on due process and equal protection grounds; the defendant should not be in a worse position than an otherwise identical defendant for whom the trial court rules correctly and grants the motion made at the close of the state’s case. ''See State v. Moore/Coen, 349 Or 371, 245 P3d 101 (2010) (where defendant takes the stand to refute improperly-admitted evidence, that testimony may not be offered by the state on retrial).

Practice Tip

A court’s oral ruling on a motion for judgment of acquittal is not final, and the court can reverse itself if there has been no material change in the record or the party’s positions. For example, State v. Sperry, 149 Or App 690, 945 P2d 546 (1997). So once the trial court has made a ruling, change the record or your position on something as quickly as possible.

There are no reported decisions relating to an erroneous denial of a motion for judgment of acquittal in a district court followed by a de novo appeal to a circuit court, but it presents a similar problem. If the state corrected the error in the circuit court, the defendant would be penalized for the district judge’s error because a correct ruling would preclude a retrial. In the right case, counsel should argue that a de novo circuit court trial is a lesser remedy than the acquittal to which the defendant is entitled.

V. Applying the Standard

A. Elements

Whether a fact is an element, a defense or affirmative defense, or something else is not always clear, especially in light of Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 US 466, 120 S Ct 2348, 147 L Ed 2d 435 (2000), and its progeny. See State v. Tamler, 19 Or 528, 25 P 71 (1890) (cited with approval in State v. Vasquez-Rubio, 323 Or 275, 917 P2d 494 (1996)), explaining how to distinguish elements and offenses). ''See alsoState v. Kirkland, 241 Or App 40, 249 P3d 554(2011) (rejecting defense argument that statutory provision establishing prima facie evidence of intent created additional elements). Counsel should argue that a disputed fact is an element, or in the alternative an ordinary defense, as to which the state bears the burden of proof. MJOAs relating to ordinary defenses are discussed below.

If the indictment unambiguously alleges more than required, such as alleging that the defendant acted “knowingly” when the statute only requires recklessness, the state must prove the allegations of the indictment. State v. Lane, 341 Or 433, 440, 144 P3d 927 (2006). For purposes of this rule, ambiguous allegations will be construed against the defendant. State v. Reigard, 243 Or App 442, 259 P3d 966 (2011).

B. Intent

Where the crime requires intent beyond the intent to do the act, such as the intent to commit a crime inside in the case of burglary, the state’s evidence is less likely to be sufficient. See State v. Chatelain, 347 Or 278, 220 P3d 41 (2009) (applying rule).But see State v. McAtee, 245 Or App 233, 261 P3d 1284 (2011) (finding intent to defraud based largely on the defendant’s possession of a stolen credit card).

A diminished-capacity defense under ORS 161.300 serves to negate intent. If the evidence of diminished capacity were so strong that no rational juror could find intent beyond a reasonable doubt in light of the evidence, defendant is entitled to judgment of acquittal. ''See State v. Stockett, 278 Or 637, 643–644, 565 P2d 739 (1977) (discussing the state’s burden of proof when evidence of mental illness tends to negate intent). But appellate decisions relating to MJOA rulings tend to focus on the absence of evidence. Courts rarely discuss when evidence is so strong that no rational juror could disbelieve it.

C. Venue

The state may fail to prove venue inadvertently by forgetting to ask the necessary questions. Counsel should be alert for that issue, but the trial court may permit the state to reopen, and reviewing courts will be generous with the inferences that may be drawn to prove venue. For example,'' State v. Cervantes, 319 Or 121, 873 P2d 316 (1994).

The state may also be unable to prove venue because the nature of the evidence complicates or precludes proving any location. See State v. Ritchie, 228 Or App 412, 423, 208 P3d 981 (2009),aff’d in part, rev’d in part on other groundsState v. Ritchie, 349 Or 572, 248 P3d 405 (2011) (state failed to prove venue of downloaded child pornography on a laptop computer).

D. Variance

In addition to the statutory elements of an offense, the defendant is entitled to fair warning of the state’s theory of the facts. Fair warning must be sufficient to enable the defendant to present a defense. State v. Smith, 182 Or 497, 500–01, 188 P2d 998 (1948); Russell v. United States, 369 US 749, 82 S Ct 1038, 8 L Ed 2d 240 (1962).

If the state’s evidence is sufficient to prove the defendant’s guilt of a different offense than the charged offense, there is a variance between the pleading and the proof. If the variance is “material,” the defendant is entitled to acquittal. ''See State v. Duvall, 187 Or App 316, 67 P3d 424 (2003) (discussing rule, citing cases). If the variance is not material, i.e., if the defendant is not prejudiced, there is no remedy for the variance. State v. Woodward, 187 Or App 233, 66 P3d 556 (2003). If the defendant is convicted notwithstanding a material variance, the remedy is a new trial. State v. Alben, 139 Or App 236, 911 P2d 1239 (1996). As discussed under “Repairing the Error,” above, it is anomalous for a defendant who wins on appeal to be in a worse position than a defendant who wins at trial, and that issue would be worth raising in the right case.

If the state seeks to amend the indictment in response to the defendant’s argument about variance, the defendant should object. See State v. Wimber, 315 Or 103, 113–114, 843 P2d 424 (1992) (regarding what amendments are proper).Wimber'' discusses, but does not resolve, the proper remedy following an objection to a material variance.

A ruling on a defense motion for acquittal by reason of a variance may not bar a retrial. ORS 131.535; 136.490; State v. Freeman, 127 Or App 640, 873 P2d 1107 (1994) (trial court dismissed with prejudice accusatory instrument alleging wrong date; Court of Appeals held dismissal was error). But, if the vague terms of the indictment threaten the defendant with a double-jeopardy violation because there are multiple acts as to which jeopardy might attach, defendant should argue that a retrial is barred. See State v. Roper, 34 Or App 273, 277, 578 P2d 479 (1978),reversed on other grounds'', 286 Or 621 (1979) (noting that a variance might be prejudicial if it exposed the defendant to a possible double-jeopardy violation). If the accusatory instrument alleges one theory, but the proof is another theory so closely related that the two would have to be joined for trial, retrial may be barred by statutory double jeopardy under ORS 131.535.

Practice Tip

Be prepared to explain how the variance prejudiced you. If your understanding of the charges was affected by the police reports or other discovery, explain how and offer them as exhibits or provide other evidence of their contents. ''See State v. Shadley/Spencer/Rowe, 16 Or App 113, 517 P2d 324 (1973) (discovery may provide specificity as to vague terms in the indictment).

E. Corroboration

Counsel should watch for situations where corroboration is necessary: ORS 136.425 (confession); ORS 136.440 (accomplice testimony). A confession can be corroborated by contemporaneous admissions. State v. Manzella, 306 Or 303, 759 P2d 1078 (1988). The state does not need to corroborate facts relating to the degree, but it does need to corroborate facts distinguishing separately named and classified crimes. State v. Chatelaine, 347 Or 278, 220 P3d 41 (2009).

F. Lesser-included Offenses

A motion for judgment of acquittal can be used to reduce a count to a lesser-included charge if the evidence is sufficient as to the lesser but not to the greater. See Chatelaine, 347 Or 278 (applying rule). The jury’s tendency to compromise might prejudice the defendant even if the jury ultimately convicts only on the lesser charge. SeeState v. Christener, 71 NJ 55, 362 A2d 1153 (1976), ''modified by State v. Wilder, 193 NJ 398, 939 A2d 781 (2008) (so stating, listing authorities).

A partial acquittal judgment (as to one count or reducing to a lesser-included charge) should be promptly announced to the jury. This action may aid the defense by implying the weakness of the state’s case in general, and foreclose premature (though forbidden) deliberation and compromise in the jury room. Counsel should consider whether disclosing the court’s reasoning to the jury would benefit the defendant.

G. Defenses

Once raised, an ordinary (non-affirmative) defense such as self-defense or entrapment must be disproved by the state beyond a reasonable doubt. ORS 161.055(1). “‘Raised by the defendant’ means either notice in writing before commencement of trial or affirmative evidence by a defense witness in the defendant’s case in chief.” ORS 161.055(3). Thus, if a defense has been “raised” by pretrial notice and the state fails to present evidence sufficient to support a rational determination that it has been disproved beyond a reasonable doubt, a motion for judgment of acquittal should be made specifying the failure of proof at the end of the state’s case and renewed at the close of all the evidence. See State v. Pranger, 110 Or App 237, 822 P2d 714 (1991),rev den ''313 Or 75, 828 P2d 458 (1992) (considering whether the trial court should have granted judgment of acquittal based on an ordinary defense).

Even an affirmative defense, as to which the defendant bears the burden of proof, could be so strong as to require a defense verdict as a matter of law. State v. Darnall, 47 Or App 161, 614 P2d 120 (1980).

Despite the obscure language of ORS 161.305, partial responsibility is not an affirmative defense; it provides, redundantly, for the admission of evidence relating to intent. The state bears the burden of proving intent. State v. Stockett, 278 Or. 637, 643–644, 565 P2d 739 (1977); Mullaney v. Wilbur, 421 US 684, 95 S Ct 1881, 44 L Ed 2d 508 (1975). The defense bears the burden of proof as to mental disease or defect. ORS 161.305.

H. Sentencing Factors

A motion for judgment of acquittal is appropriate for any element as to which the defendant is entitled to a jury trial. The Court of Appeals has reviewed such a motion relating to commercial drug offense factors, State v. Lupercio-Quezada, 224 Or App 515, 198 P3d 973 (2008), and an upward departure, State v. Skaggs, 239 Or App 13, 244 P3d 380 (2010).

I. Constitutional Issues

Whether a statute is unconstitutionally overbroad can be raised as a motion for judgment of acquittal. State v. Ryan, 350 Or 670, 261 P3d 1189 (2011) (rejecting on the merits defense argument that his stalking conviction violated his free speech rights under Article I, section 8, of the Oregon Constitution).

VI. Effect of Judgment of Acquittal

As discussed above, a ruling that the state has not carried its burden is final. It cannot be appealed and it precludes subsequent prosecution. The scope of the protection provided by a judgment of acquittal will depend on the language of the accusatory instrument, by the substantive law providing that accusatory instruments are read broadly and, possibly, by the discovery, which can have the effect of clarifying the indictment.

Practice Tip

If the accusatory instrument does not clearly capture all conduct for which the defendant might be prosecuted, the defendant might benefit from clarity (strengthening a double-jeopardy argument in the event of a future prosecution) or from obscurity (providing double-jeopardy arguments if other events form the basis for a future prosecution.) Counsel should decide whether to provide police reports or other evidence attached to a pleading, to call witnesses, or otherwise offer evidence about the likely scope of the accusatory instrument. ''See State v. Shadley/Spencer/Rowe, 16 Or App 113, 517 P2d 324 (1973) (discovery may provide specificity as to vague terms in the indictment).

A ruling that the state has not carried its burden of proof is dispositive; the state cannot appeal, and a subsequent prosecution is prevented by double-jeopardy principles, both by statute, ORS 136.445, and under the Oregon and federal constitutions. For example,State v. Wolfs, 312 Or 646, 649, 826 P2d 623 (1992); United States v. Scott, 437 US 82, 93, 98–99, 98 S Ct 2187, 57 L Ed2d 65 (1978). But see “Repairing the Error,” above.

A nominal judgment of acquittal does not provide double-jeopardy protection if the motion is granted on the basis of a variance between the pleading and the proof, State v. Freeman, 127 Or App 640, 873 P2d 1107 (1994), or a defect in the indictment, State v. Wolfs, 312 Or 646, 653, 826 P2d 623 (1992). Because a true judgment of acquittal provides greater protection than a nominal judgment of acquittal granted on a basis other than the sufficiency of the state’s case, trial counsel should consider moving for a judgment of acquittal before attacking the statute or accusatory instrument through, e.g., a motion in arrest of judgment.

VII. Conclusion

There are two important things to remember about MJOAs. First, a favorable ruling on an MJOA is as good as an acquittal after trial. It precludes any further prosecution, and the state cannot appeal. (However, in response to your motion, and prior to a ruling, the court has the discretion to allow the state to reopen.)

Second, a true MJOA is based on the state’s failure to offer sufficient evidence to prove an element. There are several reported cases involving issues that looked like MJOAs, but were actually something else, such as demurrers or motions in arrest of judgment. Favorable rulings on those motions do not necessarily result in double jeopardy protection or preclude subsequent prosecution. Counsel should be aware of the difference.
