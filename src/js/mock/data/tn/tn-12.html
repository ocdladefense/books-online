Chapter 12. Mistrial

By Pete Castleberry and Emily Elison

A mistrial is a judicial termination of a trial before a verdict is reached, granted on the motion of either party or on the judge’s own motion. Except under certain limited circumstances discussed below, jeopardy is annulled when a mistrial is granted, and the case may be retried. A mistrial is a significant event for a defendant who may ultimately be convicted. For many clients, it may be the next best thing to an acquittal.

Because the most common result of a successful motion for a mistrial is reprosecution, there are tactical considerations in determining whether to make the motion. Even if an event gives rise to a meritorious motion for a mistrial, a client who is in custody may wish to have the case resolved, or the case may be going well for the defense due to a lack of preparation by the state or some other problem. Moreover, the delay may benefit the state by providing an opportunity to strengthen its case in light of your opening statements, cross examinations, and other evidence. Counsel should include the client in the discussion of whether to make the motion or not. Because of the need to make a timely objection and motion, the better practice is to make the objection and motion, then ask the court for an opportunity to discuss the options with the client before the court rules on the motion. The motion can be withdrawn if the client decides not to seek a mistrial.

Often, your real hope is that the court will deny the motion and inject error into the record. If the motion for a mistrial is denied, the denial provides the basis for appellate review and potential reversal of a client’s conviction.

I. The Motion Itself: Procedure and Preservation

Whether to grant a mistrial is a determination committed to the sound discretion of the trial court because the trial judge is in the best position “to assess and to rectify the potential prejudice to the defendant.” State v. Garner, 234 Or App 486, 228 P3d 710 (2010) (citations omitted). “In ruling on a motion for a mistrial, a trial court must decide whether to grant the motion, to cure the effect of inappropriate conduct or testimony by giving a proper instruction instead, or to do nothing at all.” State v. Evans, 211 Or App 162, 166, 154 P3d 166 (2007), aff’d, 344 Or 358, 182 P3d 175 (2008).

A. Objection and Motion; Timing

If an event occurs in the trial that you believe is unduly prejudicial to your client’s cause, you should first object, stating the specific grounds for the objection, and then move for a mistrial. The objection raises the issue and preserves the error for appeal. The motion addresses the remedy that you are proposing to the court. A failure to object may waive the issue. State v. Bowen, 340 Or 487, 135 P3d 272 (2006). The contemporaneous objection rule applies, so the objection and motion should follow the event as closely as possible. State v. Walton, 311 Or 223, 248, 809 P2d 81 (1991) (“To preserve error, a motion for a mistrial must be timely. It is timely if it is made when the allegedly objectionable statements were made”).

Defense counsel generally must move for a mistrial immediately after the jury is exposed to the prejudicial evidence or commentary. Requiring an immediate mistrial motion allows the court to take prompt curative action if the court believes it is warranted. State v. Veatch, 223 Or App 444, 196 P3d 45 (2008). Thus, the best practice requires defense counsel to object to the prejudicial evidence or commentary and then immediately move for a mistrial outside the presence of the jury. However, the rule requiring an immediate mistrial motion is not absolute.

In Veatch, the defendant moved for a mistrial after a police officer testified about a phone call the defendant made to his attorney regarding taking a breath test. Pretrial rulings excluded evidence of the defendant’s phone calls to his attorney. After the police officer mentioned the phone call, defense counsel stated, “Objection. I have a matter for the court.” The judge sustained the objection but, instead of excusing the jury and hearing defense counsel’s motion, immediately attempted to give a curative instruction. After the police officer finished testifying, the state rested, and the jury was excused, defense counsel moved for a mistrial. The trial court denied defendant’s motions for mistrial.

The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that when defense counsel stated, “I have a matter for the court,” the court understood that he intended to move for a mistrial. “Where it is clear that the trial court understands that the defendant intends to seek a mistrial, and it responds by giving a curative instruction, the need for an immediate mistrial motion is obviated.” Veatch, 223 Or App at 453–54. Although it is always best to make an immediate objection and motion for mistrial, under Veatch, counsel can take extra measures to preserve arguments for appeal by explicitly informing the court that she intends to move for a mistrial if certain pretrial evidentiary rulings are violated.

Other cases reviewing the timeliness of mistrial motions include:

State v. Cunningham, 197 Or App 264, 275 n 7, 105 P3d 929 (2005), “We assume, for the sake of argument, that the mistrial motion was timely despite the passage of a day, given that the court adjourned immediately after excluding [the witness’s] testimony and denying defendant’s oral mistrial motion and that the written motion was filed the following morning.”

State v. Dream, 202 Or App 245, 248, 121 P3d 699 (2005), vacated for reconsideration on other grounds, 345 Or 315, 195 P3d 62 (2008), where the defendant did not argue until after voir dire that the trial court needed to make findings of a security risk to justify the use of restraints during voir dire, his motion for mistrial was too late.

State v. Hilton, 187 Or App 666, 69 P3d 779 (2003), finding a motion untimely when, by the time defendant moved for mistrial based on witness testimony, the direct examination and cross-examination had finished, the court and counsel had discussed two unrelated matters, and the court had called a recess.

State v. Cheney, 171 Or App 401, 16 P3d 1164 (2000), defendant did not timely object to the prosecutor’s “vouching” when counsel did not object immediately, but waited until after the end of the rebuttal closing—ten transcript pages later—to move for a mistrial.

State v. Larson, 325 Or 15, 933 P2d 958 (1997), finding a motion timely when the gap between the disputed comment and the defendant’s motion encompassed less than two pages of the trial transcript, there was no significant lapse of time, no additional testimony, no recess, and no discussion of another issue.

State v. Williams, 322 Or 620, 912 P2d 364 (1996), finding a motion untimely when defense counsel timely objected the prosecutor’s rebuttal argument but did not move for a mistrial until after the jury had been instructed.

B. Curative Instructions

From the court’s perspective, the favored and less drastic remedy after a prejudicial event is to strike it from the record and instruct the jury in a way that “cures” the harm. Therefore, to be successful in seeking a mistrial, you must be prepared to demonstrate why a curative instruction will not work. The better practice is to object to any curative instruction that the court may wish to give on the basis that the instruction will merely highlight the issue to the jury and further impress it upon their memory. Fiswick v. United States, 329 U.S. 211 (1946). Failure to object to the curative instruction is not necessarily a waiver of the motion for mistrial. State v. Johnson,55 Or App 98, 101 n.1, 637 P2d 211 (1981); ''but cf. State v. Evans, 211 Or App 162, 154 P3d 166 (2007) (“[I]n evaluating whether the trial court abused its discretion in denying defendant’s mistrial motion, we take into account that, to the extent that the trial court did not deliver a curative instruction, it was because defendant elected not to request one”).

A violation of a constitutional right may create a “presumably harmful effect” that a curative instruction cannot ameliorate. In contrast, reviewing courts have often held that a violation of an admissibility principle can be cured by instruction in lieu of granting a mistrial. See, e.g., State v. Bowen, 340 Or 487, 135 P3d 272 (2006) (comparing the effect of a prosecutor’s comment on defendant’s silence with the effect of a prosecutor’s question regarding inadmissible prior convictions).

After you have made a record including these steps, you can participate in fashioning a curative instruction without waiving the issue. You can augment the record by noting that you do not waiver from your position that a curative instruction will not work, but if the court insists on denying the motion and giving a curative instruction, you believe it should be in the form you feel best addresses the problem.

If the trial court gives your suggested curative instruction, you do run the risk that an appellate court will believe that your instruction adequately cured the problem. If the court gives a different instruction, then it is possible that an appellate court will find the trial court should have given your instruction. Accordingly, if the reviewing court believes the trial court’s instruction was inadequate, it can reverse the conviction because the court refused yours, orit can agree that no curative instruction could have cured the problem and reverse the conviction on that basis.

In some cases, the prejudice to the defendant created by an incident is so severe that a curative instruction is insufficient, and a mistrial is the only legally acceptable alternative. See, e.g., State v. Jones, 279 Or 55, 566 P2d 867 (1977) (prosecutor in defendant’s trial for rape repeatedly suggested to the jury that the defendant had committed multiple rapes before, although the prosecutor knew that there was no proof of any prior rape convictions); State v. White, 303 Or 333, 736 P2d 552 (1987) (prosecutor remarked during opening statements that the defendant refused to testify at a co-defendant’s trial); State v. Johnson, 199 Or App 305, 111 P3d 784 (2005) (admission of non-testifying co-defendant’s redacted confession required a mistrial despite a limiting instruction, because the redacted confession plainly implicated the defendant).

C. Complete and Protect the Record

State the grounds for the motion clearly, citing applicable statutes, state and federal constitutional provisions, and relevant case law. If there are physical reactions or events that have occurred that may not show in a recording or be part of the court reporter’s notes, it is important to take the time to describe those features of the problem into the record while making your motion. As the trial court listens to you describe the event into the record, she is also aware that an appellate court may someday read your same words. This increases your chance of being successful in gaining the mistrial if you do it well. Finally, the better practice is to renew the motion at the close of the state’s case and the close of all evidence. No Oregon cases require this, but other jurisdictions do.

II. Possible Grounds for the Motion

Keep in mind that the state has no appeal from an order granting a mistrial, and so the cases that reach the appellate court are a review of a denial of the motion. Thus there is no easy way to catalogue successful trial-level motions. However, even cases where the reviewing court affirmed the trial court’s denial of the motion help illustrate when the motion is appropriate. ORS 131.525 provides a partial list of potential grounds for mistrial. This statute outlines the circumstances when a subsequent prosecution is not barred after “the previous prosecution was properly terminated. . . .” Under ORS 131.525(1)(b), a trial court properly terminates a prosecution under all of the following circumstances:

(A) It is physically impossible to proceed with the trial in conformity with law; or

(B) There is a legal defect in the proceeding that would make any judgment entered upon a verdict reversible as a matter of law; or

(C) Prejudicial conduct, in or outside the courtroom, makes it impossible to proceed with the trial without injustice to either the defendant or the state; or

(D) The jury is unable to agree upon a verdict; or

(E) False statements of a juror on voir dire prevent a fair trial.

Under each of these circumstances, the statute operates to “annul” the jeopardy that attached when the court empanelled the jury. ''See State v. O’Donnel, 192 Or App 234, 85 P3d 323 (2004); State v. Bannister, 118 Or App 252, 846 P2d 1189 (1993).

Therefore, a mistrial proper under ORS 131.525(1)(b) does not bar subsequent prosecution. Events that trigger subsections (C) and (E) may also constitute violations of the state and federal constitutions, such as the Article I, section 11, protection against self-incrimination or the Confrontation Clause of the Sixth Amendment. Thus when moving for a mistrial, counsel should refer directly to ORS 131.525(1)(b), in addition to other applicable statutes and constitutional provisions.

A. Prosecutorial Misconduct

One consideration in a mistrial motion based on prosecutorial misconduct is whether the misconduct was deliberate or inadvertent. For example, bothState v. Jones, 279 Or 55, 566 P2d 867 (1977), and State v. White, 303 Or 333, 736 P2d 552 (1987), involved deliberate prosecutorial misconduct, which produced “presumably harmful effect[s]” that required more than a simple instruction to ignore the evidence. InJones, the prosecutor in defendant’s trial for rape repeatedly suggested to the jury that the defendant had committed multiple rapes before, although the prosecutor knew that there was no proof of any prior rape convictions. InWhite'', the prosecutor “deliberately chose to offend the rules” by specifically drawing to the jury’s attention that the defendant had been called to testify in an earlier trial but had refused. In those circumstances, the court held that a curative instruction was insufficient and a mistrial was required.

In contrast, a prosecutor’s non-deliberate conduct may not produce the sort of prejudice that cannot be cured by an appropriate instruction. See, e.g., State v. Bowen, 340 Or 487, 135 P3d 272 (2006); State v. Voits, 186 Or App 643, 64 P3d 1156 (2003).Nonetheless, timely objection is particularly important, as “it is not clear whether an Oregon judge is ever required to grant a mistrial sua spontebased on improper argument.” State v. Cheney, 171 Or App 401, 16 P3d 1164 (2000). See Cheney for a detailed discussion of sua sponte mistrials after prosecutorial misconduct.

The timing of prosecutorial misconduct is also relevant. “[O]pening statements pose less danger to a defendant’s right to a fair trial than when inadmissible evidence is placed before the jury during trial because ‘the jury will ordinarily be able to limit its consideration to the evidence introduced during the trial.’” State v. Davis, 345 Or 551, 201 P3d 185 (2008) (quotingFrazier v. Cupp, 394 U.S. 731 (1969)). Similarly, while prosecutorial misconduct in closing is serious, misconduct during rebuttal is particularly disfavored because the defendant has no opportunity to respond.See Mathis v. United States, 513 A.2d 1344, 1349 (D.C. Cir. 1986).

Note that even if a prosecutor’s conduct was “improper, tasteless, or inappropriate,” a trial court does not abuse its discretion in denying a motion for mistrial unless the conduct denied defendant a fair trial. State v. Smith,310 Or 1, 24, 791 P2d 836 (1990); ''cf. State v. Atkinson,28 Or App 909, 562 P2d 978 (1977) (the purpose of a mistrial is not to punish counsel).

[For more on prosecutorial misconduct, see the section in Chapter 16 titled, “The Ethical and Legal Problems of Prosecutorial Vouching.”]

1. Commenting on Defendant’s Silence Because a prosecutor’s comment on defendant’s failure to testify is always improper, “[i]t follows that, had the trial court chosen to grant defendant’s motion for mistrial, that choice would have been a permissible one.” State v. Larson, 325 Or 15, 933 P2d 958 (1997). When a prosecutor makes an improper reference to a defendant’s exercise of the right to remain silent, “the trial court has a duty to allow a timely motion for a mistrial or to cure the effect of the improper comment by giving a proper instruction.” State v. Alvord, 118 Or App 111, 846 P2d 432 (1993).

a. Mistrial properly denied

Trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying motion for mistrial after prosecutor stated, “[Defendant] can always get up and testify himself if he wants to establish alibi.” The jury was not likely to have drawn prejudicial inferences because the prosecutor made only one reference to defendant’s ability to testify, the comment was directed to the judge (although the jury was present), and the prosecutor made the comment in frustration, in the context of objecting to defendant’s repeated attempts to introduce hearsay. State v. Larson, 325 Or 15,933 P2d 958 (1997). See also State v. Galloway, 202 Or App 613, 123 P3d 352 (2005), vacated on other grounds, 345 Or 315 (2008) (Mistrial was properly denied after prosecutor’s closing argument emphasized that “in this trial you folks haven’t heard a single thing about alibi.” The argument was not a comment on defendant’s silence, but instead alerted the jury that the defense had not presented any evidence regarding an alibi).

Trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying motion for mistrial after the prosecutor commented on the defendant’s silence despite opportunities to explain to the police what had occurred. This comment appeared to have been made inadvertently, and the focus of the prosecutor’s argument, both immediately before and after the objectionable comments, was to attack a defense alibi witness’ credibility, and thus the comment was not likely to have prejudiced the defendant. State v. Farrar, 309 Or 132, 786 P2d 161 (1990).

Trial court properly denied mistrial after prosecutor argued in closing that “it is clear that [defendant] murdered [the victim] . . . even without [defendant] telling us so.” Court found the prosecutor’s comment to have been inadvertent and the risk of a prejudicial inference slight. The defendant never requested a curative instruction, and the trial judge’s decision not to give a curative instruction was proper because it would highlight that defendant did not testify. State v. Madison, 93 Or App 182, 760 P2d 1384 (1988).

b. Mistrial improperly denied

Mistrial was required after the prosecutor explicitly questioned defendant about why she had not told officer certain information after her arrest and suggested to the jury that a reasonable person would have spoken to the officer. This conduct impermissibly and prejudicially commented on defendant’s invocation of her rights. State v. Ragland, 210 Or App 182, 149 P3d 1254 (2006); see ''also State v. Mullenburg, 112 Or App 518, 829 P2d 98 (1992) (trial court improperly denied mistrial motion after the prosecutor commented on defendant’s silence when the arresting officer interviewed the defendant).

Mistrial improperly denied after prosecutor, inadvertently or not, sent a clear message to the jury that defendant intended to retain an attorney and refused to talk. Although defendant failed to request a curative instruction, the presumption of harm was not overcome. State v. Alvord, 118 Or App 111, 846 P2d 432 (1993).

Trial court improperly denied mistrial after prosecutor, in opening statements, deliberately referenced defendant’s refusal to testify at a co-defendant’s trial. The curative instruction could not remedy the “presumably harmful effect” of such prosecutorial misconduct. State v. White, 303 Or 333, 736 P2d 552 (1987).

Trial court abused its discretion in failing to give a curative instruction after the prosecutor argued directly to the jury that the defendant’s failure to contradict or deny evidence of forgery was significant. Although the reference was inadvertent, it “could not have gone unnoticed by a jury which had waited in vain through two days of trial for the defendant to take the stand so they could hear what he had to say about the case.”'' State v. Wederski, 230 Or 57, 368 P2d 393 (1967).

2. Commenting on Presumption of Innocence Trial court erroneously denied a motion for mistrial based on prosecutor’s comments, during closing arguments, on the presumption of innocence. Prior to the motion for mistrial, the court overruled multiple defense objections to the prosecutor’s statements to the jury that the presumption of innocence disappeared at various stages of the proceedings. Although decisions on motions for mistrial are reviewed for abuse of discretion, the underlying error here was one of law in failing to recognize that the prosecutor’s argument was improper. “Because the effect of those errors was to deny defendant a fair trial, the court’s decision to deny defendant’s motion for a mistrial did not fall within the range of legally correct choices and did not produce a permissible, legally correct outcome.” State v. Worth, 231 Or App 69, 218 P3d 166 (2009).

3. Commenting on Character of Defendant, Witness or Counsel When prosecutor implied to jury that defense counsel had knowledge of facts outside of the evidence presented—the validity of Intoxilyzer results—the curative instruction was sufficient and denial of motion for mistrial proper. State v. Schneider, 201 Or App 546, 120 P3d 16 (2005).

If the prosecutor had been making gestures indicating disbelief, such as head-shaking, scoffing and laughing, toward one of the jurors during defense witness’s testimony, that conduct was not so prejudicial that the trial court’s decision not to grant a mistrial denied defendant a fair trial. State v. Lotches, 331 Or 455, 17 P3d 1045 (2000).

Trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying motion for a mistrial after it ordered the prosecutor to refrain from referring to the defendant as an “animal” and gave a curative instruction. State v. Wilson, 69 Or App 569, 687 P2d 800 (1984).

Mistrial improperly denied due to the extensiveness and highly prejudicial potential of the cross-examination, and because the curative instruction was not given until the witness’s answers “had almost a full day in which to settle in the jurors’ minds.” State v. Swensen,40 Or App 465, 595 P2d 518 (1979) (involving cross-examination on defendant’s character for belligerence).

The trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying motion for mistrial after prosecutor referred to defendant as a “one-man patrolling army” during opening statements in an assault trial. State v. Barker,26 Or App 297, 552 P2d 565 (1976).

4. Other Bad Acts/Defendant’s Prior Record All “bad acts” evidence should be viewed through the framework set out in State v. Johns, 301 Or 535, 725 P2d 312 (1986). Counsel should be familiar with the case and its progeny. See, e.g., State v. Deloretto, 221 Or App 309, 189 P3d 1243 (2008) (appending cases where evidence of uncharged, allegedly sexual acts was deemed admissible). Additionally, counsel should review Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence(2002), Article IV, p. 33–46, and Article VI, p. 54–77, for a more comprehensive discussion.

Trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying a mistrial motion after the prosecutor elicited testimony that the defendant, on trial for murder, had a prior conviction for manslaughter. The trial court immediately gave a curative instruction to the jury to disregard any convictions that were not within the permissible fifteen-year period. State v. Bowen, 340 Or 487, 135 P3d 272 (2006).

A curative instruction was sufficient to remedy prejudice resulting from prosecutor’s passing reference, during opening statements, to the defendant’s pretrial incarceration. State v. Voits, 186 Or App 643, 64 P3d 1156 (2003).

Mistrial properly denied after a detailed cross-examination of defense character witness on circumstances in defendant’s prior rape conviction. The state presented overwhelming evidence of guilt, and the court gave a curative instruction. State v. Kendrick, 31 Or App 1195, 572 P2d 354 (1977).

Trial court improperly denied mistrial afterthe prosecutor’s impeachment of a state witness with her prior statement that she “had heard he had done it so many times before that he would go to the pen” in rape trial. State v. Jones,279 Or 55, 566 P2d 867 (1977).

Trial court improperly denied mistrial motion after the prosecutor mentioned past convictions beyond the scope of defendant’s admission and not based on evidence in the record. A curative instruction was insufficient to limit prejudice, as the case against the defendant was circumstantial, and the impact of the prosecutor’s remarks was not likely to be minimal. State v. Treit,29 Or App 461, 564 P2d 708 (1977).

5. Retaliation to Defense Tactics Trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying mistrial motion after the prosecutor asked a state witness about the defendant’s ability to procure specific evidence, which the defense had faulted the police for not gathering. Defendant argued that the prosecutor’s question impermissibly shifted to defendant the burden of presenting evidence of his own innocence. The court reasoned that the prosecutor was entitled to rebut defendant’s implication that the state had failed to gather potentially exculpatory evidence, and the prosecutor’s question directly rebutted that implication. State v. Henderson, 242 Or App 357 (2011) (''relying on State v. Lincoln, 250 Or 426, 443 P2d 178 (1968)).

Trial court properly denied mistrial motion when defense counsel sought to create the impression that defendant would have given the police exculpatory information if given the opportunity. Thus, defense counsel put the state in the position of having to challenge the implication, and the prosecutor was permitted to challenge that impression by pointing out that she did have an opportunity and did not take it. State v. Clark, 233 Or App 553, 226 P3d 120 (2010).

Mistrial motion based on the prosecutor’s commenting on the defendant’s invocation of the right to remain silent was properly denied. Although comment by a prosecutor on a defendant’s silence ordinarily is improper, the prosecutor has the right “to reply to argument made by opposing counsel, and, in doing so, statements may be made which otherwise would be improper.” State v. Guritz, 134 Or App 262, 894 P2d 1235 (1995).

Mistrial improperly denied after prosecutor responded to defense counsel’s reference to one-sided grand-jury process by stating, “90 percent plead guilty. . .70, 80 or 90 percent are convicted.” State v. Flores, 31 Or App 187, 570 P2d 94 (1977).

6. Polygraph/Opinion on Credibility See Part II(B)(2), infra.

7. Misstatement of Law Although a decision to grant or deny a mistrial is reviewed for abuse of discretion, “the court does not have discretion to deny a mistrial when a prosecutor’s comment is a misstatement of the law, and that misstatement has the effect of denying the defendant a fair trial unless the court gives a sufficiently clear curative instruction.” State v. Bilsborrow, 230 Or App 413, 215 P3d 914 (2009) (internal citations omitted).

Trial court should have either given a curative instruction or granted the mistrial motion after prosecutor misstated the law and confused the jury. State v. Dyer, 16 Or App 247, 418 P2d 184 (1974).

8. General Misconduct When prosecutor referred in closing argument to domestic violence statistics and several local crimes of domestic violence that were not committed by the defendant, a new trial was required because the comments were likely to incite or inflame the jury to decide the case on an improper basis. State v. Bolt, 108 Or App 746, 817 P2d 1322 (1991).

Trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying mistrial motion after the prosecutor called the victim’s sister as a witness, who refused to answer all the state’s questions and testified that she was scared of the defendant. The witness only decided to refuse once she was on the stand, and the defendant did not object to the question or the response. State v. Carr, 91 Or App 673, 756 P2d 1263 (1988).

Motion for mistrial improperly denied after prosecutor’s opening statement suggested to the jury that because a co-defendant had been found guilty in a separate trial, defendant should also be found guilty. Even though no curative instruction was requested and the statement was inadvertent, “[a]dvocating an improper basis for a conviction cannot be anything but prejudicial.” State v. Grenawalt, 86 Or App 96, 738 P2d 232 (1987).

At trial for first-degree burglary, the defendant testified that he never entered the relevant building. In closing, the prosecutor referred to the defendant’s request for jury instructions on the included offenses of second-degree burglary and criminal trespass and then commented: “The defendant took the stand and under oath he swore that he was not in this building. You will notice that this element of entering and remaining is common to all three crimes and I’d like to hear [defense counsel] explain.” Despite a curative instruction, the prosecutor’s comment was improper and prejudicial, and the trial court improperly denied the motion for a mistrial. State v. Johnson,55 Or App 98, 637 P2d 211 (1981).

B. Witness Misconduct

Generally, the same conduct discussed above will provide the basis for a motion for mistrial when engaged in by a witness.

1. Other Bad Acts Mistrial motion properly denied after an officer-witness inadvertently made a non-specific mention of another case against defendant, and the defendant refused a curative instruction. State v. Jordan, 79 Or App 682, 719 P2d 1327 (1986).

Mistrial motion properly denied after witness in a murder trial referenced defendant’s unrelated threat to kill a police officer. The trial court struck the testimony and gave a curative instruction. State v. Charles, 54 Or App 272, 634 P2d 814 (1981).

Trial court did not abuse its discretion in denying mistrial motion after a rape/sodomy victim suffered an epileptic seizure while testifying. The court had the jury removed and gave a curative instruction. State v. Hill, 49 Or App 297, 619 P2d 671 (1980).

2. Commenting on Polygraph Trial court properly denied motion for a mistrial when a witness’s reference to a polygraph was inadvertent and ambiguous about whether the results were favorable or disfavorable to the defendant, and the court gave a curative instruction. State v. Terry, 333 Or 163, 37 P3d 157 (2001).

Mistrial motion properly denied when a witness’s reference to a polygraph was isolated, made in passing, did not disclose the results, and the state never argued that the test impacted the witness’s credibility or any other issue in the case. State v. Farrar, 309 Or 132, 786 P2d 161 (1990).

Trial court properly denied motion for a mistrial after a witness’s brief reference to a polygraph, because “reference to the word ‘polygraph,’ without more, was so indefinite as to render any prejudicial effect speculative at best.” State v. Eby, 296 Or 63, 673 P2d 522 (1983).

3. Opinion on Credibility State v. Middleton,294 Or 427, 657 P2d 1215 (1983); State v. Milbrandt,305 Or 621, 756 P2d 620 (1988); State v. Isom, 306 Or 587, 761 P2d 524 (1988); State v. McQuisten, 97 Or App 517, 776 P2d 1304 (1989) (no witness may testify to an opinion on whether he believes a witness is telling the truth).

C. Judge/Courthouse Staff Conduct

A trial court’s untimely administration of the jury oath does not necessarily require a mistrial where there is no showing of prejudice to the defendant. However, if the failure to swear the jury in a timely manner “results in unfair prejudice or affects a substantial right of a party, the trial court is without discretion to deny a motion for mistrial.” State v. Barone, 329 Or 210, 986 P2d 5 (1999).

When defendant remained restrained with handcuffs and a stomach chain during trial, without substantial justification, the trial judge had no discretion to deny the motion for a mistrial. State v. Glick, 73 Or App 79, 697 P2d 1002 (1985); but cf. State v. Merrell, 170 Or App 400, 12 P3d 556 (2000) (finding trial court properly denied motion for a mistrial after concluding that the defendant posed an immediate, serious risk of dangerous or disruptive behavior and a serious risk of escaping the courtroom).

After the trial judge repeatedly questioned defense witnesses, defendant objected to “the type of questioning but also to the suggestions and insinuations as we interpret them that the Court has made. . . .” After the court refused to give a curative instruction immediately, defendant moved for a mistrial. Although it was plain error for the court to ask the questions at issue, the court deemed it harmless, and the denial of the mistrial motion was proper.'' State v. Mains, 295 Or 640, 669 P2d 1112 (1983).

Trial court properly denied motion for a mistrial after finding the defendant in contempt in the presence of the jury. Defense counsel did not ask to excuse the jury even though the defendant’s repeated refusal to answer and the trial court’s warning foreshadowed defendant’s being held in contempt. The trial judge allowed the defendant to explain his reluctance to answer, did not berate the defendant, and gave a curative instruction regarding the contempt.'' State v. Middleton, 46 Or App 381, 611 P2d 698 (1980).

Motion for a mistrial improperly denied after the trial judge stated in the jury’s presence that if the defendant “[knew] the English language, answer the questions in order to avoid a lot of rigmarole here. You have been nine years in this country and you are working elsewhere and if [sic] you know the language.” There was a reasonable probability that the jury interpreted this as personal disapproval of the defendant by the judge, and “small misstatements become big errors when uttered by the court.”'' State v. Medina, 39 Or App 467, 592 P2d 1042 (1979).

D. Jury Conduct

Appellate courts will defer to trial court determinations of the extent of misconduct or prejudice to a defendant and assessments of juror credibility. See, e.g., State v. Moore, 324 Or 396, 927 P2d 1073 (1996) (deferring to the trial court when, after communications between a juror and her son, the court immediately questioned the juror and satisfied itself that no misconduct had occurred. When the issue arose again, the court emphasized that it had found the juror credible and saw no need to question the juror further).

No abuse of discretion to deny motion for mistrial after a prospective juror explained that she had an outstanding stalking order against the defendant. The court excused her for cause but declined to grant a mistrial, although the court offered to give a curative instruction, which defense counsel ultimately did not request. The prospective juror’s comment was not so inherently prejudicial that the trial court had no choice but to grant a mistrial. State v. Evans, 344 Or 358, 182 P3d 175 (2008).

Mistrial properly denied after one juror, during the trial, repeatedly and regularly discussed defendant’s case with her son, an inmate housed in a cell near defendant. Immediately after learning of the communications, the trial judge questioned the juror and satisfied itself that no misconduct had occurred. State v. Moore, 324 Or 396, 927 P2d 1073 (1996).

No abuse of discretion to deny motion for mistrial after an alternate juror commented about evidence in the case to other jurors and to court staff. Additionally, after viewing the autopsy photographs of the victim, the alternate commented to the court bailiff, out of the hearing of the rest of the jury, that “nobody deserves that kind of treatment.” The trial court assessed whether the comments could have caused any possible prejudice to defendant and concluded that no harm had been done. State v. Pratt, 316 Or 561, 853 P2d 827 (1993).

No abuse of discretion to deny motion for mistrial when the record supported the trial court’s finding, after the questioning of a juror who had been contacted by the mother of a victim, “that [the] juror . . . was not contaminated by the contact and could still serve as an impartial juror.” Victim’s mother had “thanked” juror when they encountered each other in the bathroom. State v. Rogers, 313 Or 356, 836 P2d 1308 (1992).

Mistrial improperly denied after three jurors attended a drug-education seminar where the prosecutor and one of the state’s witnesses, a deputy sheriff, spoke. “This was tantamount to an impermissible ex parte contact between the prosecution and the jury.” Additionally, one of the jurors brought to the jury room a booklet and a book she had been given at the seminar. “The cumulative effect was impermissibly prejudicial by any reasonable standard.” State v. Mapel, 54 Or App 795, 636 P2d 445 (1981).

E. Cumulative Error

If more than one event occurs, include them all in the next motion.

F. Other Possible Grounds

The precipitating event can occur at any time from the jury’s trip from the assembly room to the courtroom, voir dire, opening statements, evidence, closing arguments, jury deliberation, and taking the verdict.

Examples include events such as the death or illness of the prosecutor, defense counsel, the judge, or a juror when there is no alternate but a mistrial. See, e.g.,State v. Vega, 206 Or App 668, 139 P3d 260 (2006),vacated on other grounds'', 345 Or 316, 195 P3d 64 (2008) (actual prejudice must be shown).

Other triggering events include court staff taking exhibits that were not admitted into evidence to the jury room during deliberations; jurors who know of the case or know the defendant and make improper remarks during jury selection; on-lookers in the courtroom or hall making comments the jury hears; and jurors undertaking their own investigation of the scene or the law. The list is endless.

Denying defendant’s motion for a mistrial was beyond the court’s discretion after a non-testifying co-defendant’s redacted confession was admitted, in violation of Bruton. The redacted confession plainly implicated the defendant and emphasized the defendant’s existence. The redactions, even after a precautionary instruction, were insufficient to avoid a Confrontation Clause violation. State v. Johnson, 199 Or App 305, 111 P3d 784 (2005).

III. Reprosecution After A Mistrial

A mistrial implicates a defendant’s weighty interest in having criminal charges resolved at the first proceeding, an animating principle of double jeopardy jurisprudence. The double jeopardy protection in Article I, Section 12, of the Oregon Constitution, “is, in principle, the same as that embodied in the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution.” State v. Kimsey, 182 Or App 193, 47 P3d 916, 922 (2002) (citing State v. Kennedy, 295 Or 260, 272–76, 666 P2d 1316 (1983)). In Arizona v. Washington, 434 U.S. 497, 503–05 (1978), the Supreme Court explained that:

[e]ven if the first trial is not completed, a second prosecution may be grossly unfair. It increases the financial and emotional burden on the accused, prolongs the period in which he is stigmatized by an unresolved accusation of wrongdoing, and may even enhance the risk that an innocent defendant may be convicted.

Therefore, as a general rule, “the prosecutor is entitled to one, and only one, opportunity to require an accused to stand trial.” Id.

The general double jeopardy rule is codified at ORS 131.525(1). Two broadly interpreted exceptions to the general rule—consent of the defendant and “manifest necessity”—are invoked far more often than the general rule itself. Under the current state of the law, and despite the general rule, reprosecution after a mistrial is only barred in a narrowly construed category of cases.

The basic rules can be summarized as follows:If the court declares a mistrial over the defendant’s objection, either sua sponteor on the prosecutor’s motion, a subsequent prosecution is barred unless (1) the “manifest necessity” standard was satisfied, and (2) the trial was properly terminated under ORS 153.534.State v. Cole, 286 Or 411, 595 P2d 466 (1979); State v. Embry, 19 Or App 934, 530 P2d 99 (1974).

If the defendant moves for a mistrial or fails to object to one, subject to one exception, the defendant is deemed to have consented to the mistrial and is subject to retrial. State v. Garner, 234 Or App 486, 228 P3d 710 (2010). The one exception arises where official misconduct prompts the defendant’s successful mistrial motion. The misconduct must be so prejudicial to the defendant that it cannot be cured by means short of a mistrial, and the official knows that the conduct is improper and prejudicial and either intends or is indifferent to the resulting mistrial. Id.

A. Pleading Former Jeopardy

The procedural mechanism for raising double jeopardy issues is a motion to dismiss the accusatory instrument pursuant to ORS 135.470(1), which provides that “[t]he court shall dismiss the accusatory instrument if, upon motion of the defendant, it appears, as a matter of law, that a former prosecution bars the prosecution for the offense charged.” See'' State v. McClendon, 204 Or App 459, 131 P3d 765 (2006) (motion to dismiss on former jeopardy grounds untimely when made for the first time at trial) and State v. Toste, 196 Or App 11, 100 P3d 738 (2004) (mere citation to state constitutional double jeopardy provision in motion to dismiss on former jeopardy grounds insufficient to preserve the issue for appeal) for discussions of timeliness and preservation in pleading former jeopardy.

There is no interlocutory appeal from a trial court’s denial of a defendant’s motion to dismiss on former jeopardy grounds. State v. Salzmann, 119 Or App 217, 850 P2d 1122 (1992). However, a petitioner’s claim that double jeopardy will arise as a result of trial court’s denial of his motion to dismiss constitutes sufficient injury to justify a request for mandamus relief. State ex rel. Wark v. Freerksen, 84 Or App 90, 733 P2d 100 (1987).

B. Mistrials Over Defendant’s Objection

“Although both the Oregon and the United States Constitutions purport to prohibit placing a criminal defendant in jeopardy twice for the same offense, it is well settled that neither bars a retrial if the state shows that there was a ‘manifest necessity’ for terminating the defendant’s first trial before the verdict.” Freerksen, 84 Or App 90, 733 P2d 100 (1987). Similarly, ORS 131.525(1)(b) provides that a previous prosecution does not bar subsequent prosecution if the trial court finds that terminating the prosecution is necessary under certain compelling circumstances.

Therefore, a mistrial over the defendant’s objection will not bar subsequent prosecution if two standards are met: the constitutionally based standard of “manifest necessity” and the statutory requirements for “necessity” under ORS 135.525(1)(b). Generally, “both the statutory and constitutional standards are satisfied if the trial judge properly concludes within the bounds of sound judicial discretion that the ends of justice would not be served by continuing the prosecution.” Id. However, the statute provides a slightly more specific test for necessity than does the case law articulating the constitutional standard of “manifest necessity.”'' State v. Cole, 286 Or 411, 595 P2d 466 (1979).

1. “Manifest Necessity” After a mistrial is declared without the defendant’s consent, retrial will not be barred if the state can show that there was a “manifest necessity” for the termination of the trial before verdict. State v. McFerron, 52 Or App 325, 330, 628 P2d 440 (1981) (citing Arizona v. Washington,434 U.S. 497 (1978); Illinois v. Summerville, 410 U.S. 458, 93 (1975)).

Manifest necessity is one of those nebulous constitutional concepts that “abjures the application of any mechanical formula by which to judge the property of declaring a mistrial in the varying and often unique situations arising during the course of a criminal trial.” Illinois v. Somerville, 410 U.S. 58 (1973). However, the case law provides some minimal guidance.

Manifest necessity exists when continuing the trial would defeat “the ends of public justice.” United States v. Perez, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat) 579 (1824). The classic example of manifest necessity is the hopelessly deadlocked jury. “The words ‘manifest necessity’ appropriately characterize the magnitude of the prosecutor’s burden.” Arizona v. Washington, 434 U.S. 497 (1978).

When determining whether manifest necessity for a mistrial existed at the time it was declared, courts give considerable weight to whether the trial judge gave sufficient consideration to alternatives to a mistrial, such as curative instructions. In United States v. Jorn,400 U.S. 470 (1971), the Supreme Court declined to find manifest necessity when the trial court granted a mistrial to allow government witnesses to consult with lawyers about the possibility of self-incrimination before testifying. The Court emphasized that the trial judge failed to consider viable alternatives.

In Arizona, the Court stopped short of requiring trial judges to make explicit findings with respect to the viability of potential alternatives to a mistrial but stressed that the record must reflect consideration of those alternatives. Thus, Jornand Washingtonfairly stand for the proposition that where the record does not reflect any judicial consideration of alternatives to a mistrial, the manifest necessity standard is not satisfied and a subsequent prosecution is barred under the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

In Illinois v. Somerville, 410 U.S. 58 (1973), the Supreme Court held that manifest necessity is satisfied if the record shows that an impartial verdict cannot be reached, or where an obvious procedural error makes reversal on appeal a virtual certainty. In Somerville, the trial court had granted a mistrial on the prosecutor’s motion after the prosecutor discovered the indictment was fatally defective. Contrast Somerville with Downum v. United States, 372 U.S. 734 (1963) (declining to find manifest necessity where key prosecution witness failed to appear and prosecutor knew in advance of trial that the witness had not been found and subpoenaed). Consistent with Downum, the Arizona Court held that when determining the existence of manifest necessity, “the strictest scrutiny is appropriate when the basis for the mistrial is the unavailability of critical prosecution evidence.” 434 U.S. 497 (1978).

2. ORS 135.525(1)(b): Statutory Necessity ORS 135.525 was originally drafted in an effort to make somewhat more specific the test of “manifest necessity,” as set forth in United States v. Perez, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat) 579 (1824), by stating five general reasons that justify termination, including (1) physical necessity, (2) legal necessity, (3) prejudicial conduct, (4) a hung jury, and (5) false statements of a juror on voir dire. State v. Cole, 286 Or 411, 595 P2d 466 (1979). The constitutional standard of “manifest necessity” and the statutory standard concerning impossibility to proceed without justice require, at least, that a trial not be terminated if any reasonable alternative action is possible under the facts of each case. State v. Embry, 19 Or App 934, 530 P2d 99 (1974) (finding a lack of necessity where the prejudicial effect of certain witness testimony could have been eliminated by a curative instruction and thus barring reprosecution on double jeopardy grounds). “A trial judge, then, is not to foreclose the defendant’s option to go to the jury unless he reasonably concludes that the ends of public justice cannot be served by a continuation of the proceedings.” Id.

If a mistrial is justified under ORS 135.525(1)(b), the court must still determine whether the federal constitutional standard of manifest necessity is satisfied. Id. Cases prior to Cole—specificallyState v. Misten, 26 Or App 681, 554 P2d 584 (1976), andEmbry—held that the statutory standards under ORS 135.525 and manifest necessity were equivalent standards, butCole, if not expressly overruling that proposition, calls into doubt that portion of their holdings. Thispotentially'' leaves the door open for counsel to argue that although the statutory necessity is satisfied, manifest necessity has not been satisfied, and thus double jeopardy bars further prosecution.

3. Retrial Permitted Courts have found the requisite necessity and permitted retrial in the following situations:

Defense counsel (our own Mr. John Henry Hingson, III) declared in open court that “[T]he prosecution is hiding all the facts from the jury . . . and I’m trying to bring out the truth and nothing but the truth.”'' State ex rel. Wark v. Freerksen, 84 Or App 90, 733 P2d 100 (1987).

A juror failed on voir dire to respond to a question about his ability to be impartial, yet later stated to court and counsel that he could not impartially consider the case, and the juror was dismissed because of “false statement” within the meaning of ORS 135.525. State v. McFerron, 52 Or App 325, 628 P2d 440 (1981).

The trial judge became severely ill on the first day of trial and checked into a hospital. State v. Cole, 286 Or 411, 595 P2d 466 (1979).

Unavailability of state witnesses due to extraordinary circumstance justified mistrial on basis of physical impossibility of proceeding. State v. Misten, 26 Or App 681, 554 P2d 584 (1976).

After the jury was sworn, the judge discovered that the defendant had not entered a plea to the indictment. Lovato v. New Mexico, 242 US 199 (1916).

The court discovered that a juror had served on the grand jury that issued the indictment. Thompson v. United States, 155 US 271 (1894).

C. Mistrials on Defendant’s Motion

A defendant can waive his protection against double jeopardy. State v. Kimsey, 182 Or App 193, 47 P3d 916, (2002). Thus, when a defendant moves for a mistrial or otherwise consents to have the trial terminated, a subsequent trial on the same charges ordinarily is not barred. Id. (citing Oregon v. Kennedy, 456 U.S. 667, 672–73 (1982)). When a defendant consents to a mistrial, “the ‘manifest necessity’ standard has no place in the application of the Double Jeopardy Clause.” Id.at 672.

An exception to the rule allowing re-prosecution after a defendant’s consent to a mistrial arises from certain egregious forms of prosecutorial or judicial misconduct. Compare, e.g., State v. Rathbun,287 Or 421, 600 P2d 392 (1979) (re-prosecution barred on double jeopardy grounds following mistrial resulting from a hung jury, where misconduct by a bailiff was assumed to be a factor in hanging the jury) with State v. Williams, 48 Or App 319, 617 P2d 629 (1980) (bailiff’s inadvertent action in leaving jury room door open so that jurors saw defendant in handcuffs did not bar retrial).

The state and federal analyses differ when determining whether such misconduct bars re-prosecution.'' Kimsey, supra (citing State v. Kennedy, 295 Or 260, 666 P2d 1316 (1983)) (concluding that the objective to be served by the two clauses is the same, even if the analyses adopted by Oregon and federal courts differ).

1. State Analysis under Article I, Section 12, of the Oregon Constitution In Kennedy, the Oregon Supreme Court set forth the test for determining when prosecutorial misconduct is sufficiently egregious to bar retrying a criminal defendant under Article I, section 12. As articulated by the Court, retrial is barred when (1) the misconduct is so prejudicial that it cannot be cured by means short of mistrial; (2) the prosecutor knew that the conduct was improper and prejudicial; and (3) the prosecutor either intended or was indifferent to the resulting mistrial or reversal. State v. Garner, 234 Or App 486, 228 P3d 710, 723 (2010) (construing Kennedy).

“Negligent error, ‘gross’ or otherwise, is not enough,” rather, “the standard requires some conscious choice of prejudicial action before the [double jeopardy] guarantee bars correction of the error by a new trial.” Kimsey, supra (internal citations and quotations omitted). Thus, prejudice sufficient to warrant a mistrial is not enough, in and of itself, to trigger former jeopardy.Id.

2. Federal Analysis under the Fifth Amendment The Fifth Amendment test for determining whether official misconduct will bar a subsequent trial is more demanding than the test under Article I, section 12. Kimsey at 923. Like the test under Article I, section 12, the Fifth Amendment test “focuses on the official’s mental state in engaging in the misconduct.” Id. However, “neither the official’s knowledge that the conduct is improper and prejudicial nor indifference to the resulting mistrial will suffice.” Id. “Rather, the official must actually intend the mistrial in the sense that the conduct in question is “intended to ‘goad’ the defendant into moving for a mistrial.” Id.(citing Oregon v. Kennedy, 456 US at 676).

D. Guidelines for Litigating a Motion to Dismiss Based on Official Misconduct

In opposing a defendant’s plea of former jeopardy based on official misconduct leading to a mistrial, the prosecutor may attempt to testify as to his intent or indifference with respect to the mistrial. The OCDLA amicus curiae brief in Kennedy cited Collins v. State, 640 SW 2d 288, 291 (Tex. Crim. App. 1982), and questioned whether the prosecutor, the trial judge, or others may be called as a witness on the issue and questioned while also testifying as to intent. Oppose permitting the prosecutor to testify in the immediacy of the situation and consider these guidelines:

1. Request a continuance. 2. Move for and subpoena the entire file of the prosecutor, including work product. It is necessary to determine if the prosecutor was aware in advance of the possible prejudice caused by the conduct. Such evidence may be in the file. See State v. Gallup, 108 Or App 508, 816 P2d 669 (1991) (district attorney’s file was not work product and was discoverable where file contained notes of interviews with witnesses and file contained no opinions, theories, or conclusions). 3. Move for disclosure of other mistrials occasioned by the offending prosecutor under the Brady v. Maryland, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), doctrine. 4. Move for and/or subpoena any information in the District Attorney’s office regarding any other case in which this prosecutor had a mistrial declared. 5. Urge a pattern of apparently negligent acts of the prosecutor to compel a conclusion of intent to cause a mistrial. United States v. Banks, 383 F.Supp. 389, 393 (BSD 1974); State v. Seydell, 252 Or 160, 446 P2d 678 (1968). 6. At the hearing, introduce evidence of prior mistrials to prove intent. Include findings of intent to cause a mistrial in other cases, if available. McCuller v. Gaudry, 59 Or App 13, 650 P2d 148 (1982). 7. Call other attorneys who appeared in any of the prior mistrial cases and attempt to qualify them as experts to testify that the prosecutor’s conduct was “consistent with” what a prosecutor intending to cause a mistrial “acts like.” State v. LeBrun, 37 Or App 411, 587 P2d 1044 (1978). 8. Call the prosecutor as a witness to determine knowledge of the facts prior to the error. Further, move to disqualify the prosecutor from arguing any aspect of the motion. Doneen v. Craven, 204 Or 512, 284 P2d 758 (1955) (probate case stating that an attorney-witness shall leave the trial to another attorney).

E. Mistrial Resulting from a Hung Jury

A mistrial is appropriate and does not bar retrial when the jury is unable to reach a verdict and the jury’s inability is not the result of prosecutorial or judicial misconduct. State v. Bannister, 118 Or App 252, 846 P2d 1189 (1993); ORS 131.525(1)(b)(D).

ORS 131.525(1)(b)(D) applies, even in the absence of an explicit finding, where (1) the record demonstrates that the jury was unable to reach a verdict and further deliberations would have been unavailing, (2) the trial court discharged the jury after confirming that inability to reach a verdict, and (3) the record does not disclose any other plausible reason for the court to have discharged the jury at that time.”'' State v. O’Donnell, 192 Or App 234, 85 P3d 323 (2004).

If a motion for a mistrial with prejudice is not made at the time of the hung jury, then double jeopardy claims are waived. State v. McClendon, 204 Or App 459, 131 P3d 765 (2006).

IV. Tactical Decisions and Strategy

Whether to move for a mistrial is a tactical decision. Counsel must consider whether the client faces a new trial with a less favorable jury should the court grant the motion. Thus, included in the decision-making calculus is the likelihood that re-prosecution will be barred under the rules of former jeopardy. See Part III, supra. If the court grants the motion, the client may face re-prosecution and a new trial unless the Kennedy factors are satisfied.

Moreover, should the state reprosecute, it will have time to strengthen its case in light of the original trial. If trial is going well and the jury seems favorable, a motion for a mistrial may not be in the client’s best interests. If you are considering waiving a potentially meritorious motion for a mistrial, the best practice is to make an objection to the offending evidence or commentary and confer with the client about the consequences of a successful motion—in most cases, a new trial.

Leyva-Grave-De-Peralta v. Blackletter, 232 Or App 441, 223 P3d 411 (2009), a post-conviction ineffective assistance case, illustrates an example of tactical decision-making by trial counsel. There, a jury could not reach a verdict on the charged offense. Under ORS 136.460, when a jury hangs on the charged offense, the state and the defendant may stipulate that the jury may consider any lesser-included offense. However, when the jury failed to reach a verdict on the original charge, the judge simply instructed the jury to deliberate on a lesser-included offense and the jury convicted. In his petition for post-conviction relief, the petitioner alleged that trial counsel performed deficiently when he failed to object to the improper order of deliberation and move for a mistrial. The state obtained an affidavit from trial counsel in which he explained that his acquiescence in the improper procedure was a tactical choice:

We had a favorable jury. When the jurors were unable to reach a verdict on the Assault in the First Degree, the best option for petitioner was to let them deliberate on the [lesser-included offense]. If I had requested a mistrial, it would have opened the door to a less favorable jury convicting petitioner of Assault I at the second trial.

The post-conviction court found ineffective assistance, but the Court of Appeals reversed on procedural grounds without reviewing the merits of the claim. ''See also Monahan v. Belleque, 234 Or App 93, 227 P3d 777 (2010) (A post-conviction petitioner alleged that trial counsel was inadequate for failing to move for a mistrial when a juror knew a man who was with the petitioner during the shooting for which he was convicted. The articulated test for prejudice for failing to move for a mistrial requires petitioner to show that “had trial counsel moved for a mistrial, that motion would have had a tendency to cause the court to either grant the motion or to give a curative instruction that would have had a tendency to affect the prosecution in the underlying criminal case”)

V. Mistrial Checklist

1. Object.

2. Decide if a mistrial is the best tactical decision for this case.

3. Make the motion.

4. Argue that prejudice cannot be cured by an instruction to the jury or any other remedy.

5. If the motion is denied, take an active role in fashioning the curative instruction.

6. Look for cumulative prejudice.

7. Renew the motion at the close of the state’s case and the evidence.

Trial counsel’s objections to the trial judge’s sua sponte declaration of a mistrial, or the prosecutor’s request for a mistrial should be grounded in an explanation of why the standards are not met. Strategically, it may be in the client’s best interest simply to preserve the objection without any lengthy persuasive argument why the standards are not met. If a mistrial is declared over the defendant’s objection, and a reviewing court determines that manifest necessity did not exist or that the trial was not properly terminated under ORS 135.525(1)(b), then a subsequent prosecution is barred.
