Chapter 8. Jury Selection

By Janet Lee Hoffman and Sara F. Werboff

I. Foundation of the Right to a Jury Trial in State Court

A. Constitutional Sources

In all criminal cases, the accused has the right to a trial by an impartial jury. Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution; Sixth Amendment of the United States Constitution. The guarantee of trial by an “impartial jury” means “trial by a jury that is not biased in favor of or against either party, but is influenced in making its decision only by evidence produced at trial and legal standards provided by the trial court.” State v. Amini, 331 Or 384, 391, 15 P3d 541, 545 (2000).

B. State Constitutional Right to Jury Trial Is Broader than Federal Right

The Oregon appellate courts have held that the right to a jury trial extends to some violations when that violation retains substantial penal characteristics. Brown v. Multnomah County Dist. Ct., 280 Or 95, 109-111, 570 P2d 52 (1977) (jury trial for DUII violation); State v. Fuller, 252 Or App 391, 287 P3d 1263 (2012) (jury trial for theft charged as a violation).

The court considers the following factors in deciding whether the accused has a right to a jury trial:

the type of offense

the nature of the prescribed penalty

the collateral consequences associated with conviction

the significance of the conviction to the community, and

and the pretrial practices associated with an arrest and detention for the offense.

State v. Fuller, 252 Or App 391, 394, 287 P3d 1263, 1265 (2012).

C. Statutory Sources

ORS 136.001 provides that “The defendant and the state in all criminal prosecutions have the right to a public trial by an impartial jury.”

NB: In State v. Baker, 328 Or 355, 976 P2d 1132 (1999), the Oregon Supreme Court held that the 1997 amendment to the statute, granting the state the right to demand a jury trial, was unconstitutional under Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution if the defendant waives jury trial and the court accepts that waiver.

D. Waiver of Right to Jury Trial

ORS 136.001(2) expressly requires a written waiver of a jury trial. Written waivers are also required by Article I, section 11.

''See State v. Anderson, 208 Or App 335, 144 P3d 1024 (2006) (written waiver required); State v. Barber,343 Or 525, 173 P3d 827 (2007) (lack of a written waiver of jury trial is plain error on the face of the record requiring reversal).

Waiver of jury for the guilt phase of a trial will act as a waiver of jury for sentencing purposes even if not specified in the waiver. State v. Curry, 209 Or App 31, 146 P3d 348 (2006).

If defendant waives a jury trial and the court accepts that waiver, the state cannot demand a jury trial. State v. Baker, 328 Or 355, 976 P2d 1132 (1999).

II. Composition of Jury

A. Constitutional Minimum

The United States Supreme Court held in Ballew v. Georgia, 435 US 223, 98 S Ct 1029 (1978), that juries must be comprised of at least six jurors. Juries consisting of fewer jurors would substantially deprive an accused of the right to a jury trial guaranteed by the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution.

Similarly, Article VII (amended), section 9, of the Oregon Constitution precludes the enactment of any statutory provision authorizing less than six jurors.

B. Oregon Circuit Court

For all felony cases, the jury shall be comprised of 12 jurors, unless both parties consent to be tried by fewer jurors. ORS 136.210(1).

The defendant’s waiver of 12 jurors cannot be implied from a silent record or a failure to object to continuing the trial. ORS 136.001. State v. Lutz,306 Or 499, 760 P2d 249 (1988) (defendant must consent to trial by fewer than 12 jurors on the record).

If the defendant is accused solely of misdemeanor charges, then the jury shall consist of six persons. ORS 136.210(2).

C. Justice Courts

Jury procedure in Justice Court is governed by ORS 54.010 et seq.The jury in Justice Court is comprised of six jurors.

III. Juror Qualifications

A. Eligibility to Serve as a Juror

ORS 10.030(3)(a) provides that:

Any person is eligible to act as a grand juror, or as a juror in a criminal trial, unless the person:

(A) Is not a citizen of the United States;

(B) Does not live in the county in which summoned

for jury service;

(C) Is less than 18 years of age;

(D) Has had rights and privileges withdrawn and not restored under ORS 137.281;

(E) Has been convicted of a felony or served a felony sentence within the 15 years immediately preceding the date the person is required to report for jury service; or

(F) Has been convicted of a misdemeanor involving violence or dishonesty, or has served a misdemeanor sentence based on a misdemeanor involving violence or dishonesty, within the five years immediately preceding the date the person is required to report for jury service.

Recent prior service on a jury also excludes a person from service. A person is ineligible to act as a juror in any circuit court in Oregon within 24 months after being discharged from jury service in a federal court in this state or a circuit court of this state unless that person’s service is required because of a need for additional jurors.   ORS 10.030(5).

B. Juror Eligibility Cannot Be Limited on the Basis of Discrimination Against a Cognizable Group

Except as otherwise provided by statute, “the opportunity for jury service may not be denied or limited on the basis of race, religion, sex, sexual orientation, national origin, age, income, occupation or any other factor that discriminates against a cognizable group in this state.” ORS 10.030(1).  

Further, a person cannot be excluded from service on a jury on the basis of blindness, hearing or speech impairment, or physical disability alone. ORS 10.030(4).  

IV. Excusal of Jurors by the Court or Clerk

A. Power of Judge or Clerk to Excuse a Juror for Undue Hardship

ORS 10.050 permits the judge or clerk of court to excuse a person from acting as a juror on a showing of “undue hardship or extreme inconvenience to the person, the person’s family, the person’s employer or the public served by the person.”  

In making this determination, the court “shall carefully consider and weigh both the public need for juries which are representative of the full community and the individual circumstances offered as a justification for excuse from jury service.”

B. Power of Judge or Clerk to Grant Exemptions from Jury Service

A person may request and be granted an exemption if that person is 70 years of age or older, ORS 10.050(3), a women who is breastfeeding a child, ORS 10.050(4), or if the person is the sole caregiver for a child or other dependent and is unable to afford or make arrangements from the care of the dependent, ORS 10.050(5).  

C. Power of Judge to Excuse a Juror on the Court’s Own Motion

The court may also, on its own motion, excuse a juror “whose presence on the jury would substantially impair the progress of the action of trial or prejudice the parties thereto.”   ORS 10.050(2).   This provision specifically applies to jurors who are blind, hearing or speech impaired, or physically disabled, and are qualified under ORS 10.030(4).

NB: The trial court may also exercise its power under ORS 10.050(2) to exclude a non-English-speaking juror. State v. Haugen, 349 Or 174, 183, 243 P3d 31 (2010) (trial court may “respond to the potential difficulties of seating a non-English-speaking juror by excusing that juror”).

V. Challenges to the Venire Panel

A. Procedure for Challenging Venire Panel

A motion under ORS 136.005 is the exclusive means by which the District Attorney or defense counsel may challenge a jury panel.

Challenges to the venire panel mustbe made before voir dire. ORS 136.005(2).

B. Grounds for Motion under ORS 136.005

“The district attorney or the defendant in a criminal action may challenge the jury panel on the ground that there has been a material departure from the requirements of the law governing selection of jurors by filing a motion with the court supported by an affidavit alleging facts that, if true, constitute a material departure from the requirements of the law governing the selection of jurors. The party making the motion shall serve the motion and supporting affidavit on the other party, the trial court administrator and the State Court Administrator.””

ORS 136.005(1).

A “material departure” is a “‘deviation’ of ‘great consequence’ from the constitutional or statutory procedures that govern the selection of juries.”   State v. Rogers, 334 Or 633 (2002).   Counsel need only allege facts that, if true, would amount to a material departure from the requirements of law.   Id.

If the court determines that there has been a material departure, it may stay the proceedings pending the selection of a jury panel in conformity with the law or “grant such other relief as may be appropriate.   ORS 136.005(3).  

Practice Tip: If the defendant contemplates making a challenge to the venire panel, counsel should subpoena the source lists, master lists, term lists, and individual voir dire lists. See State ex rel. Click v. Brownhill, 331 Or 500, 508 (2000) (defendants allowed to subpoena jury information necessary to support a challenge to the venire panel). ORS 10.205et seq. details the record preservation requirements concerning the jury selection process. All of these records are subject to a subpoena.Brownhill'', 331 Or at 508.

C. The Fair Cross Section Requirement

A jury venire must represent a fair cross section of the community. Drawing a jury from a non-representative cross section of the community implicates both the right to an impartial jury, see Taylor v. Louisiana, 419 US 522 (1975) (claim grounded in Sixth Amendment) and State v. Rogers, 334 Or 633, 490 (2002) (claim grounded in Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution), and equal protection concerns,see Castaneda v. Partida, 430 US 482, 494 (1977) (claim grounded in Equal Protection Clause of Fourteenth Amendment).

The “fair cross section” requirement is a component of both the state and federal constitutional rights to an impartial jury. State v. Rogers, 334 Or 633, 490 (2002); Taylor v. Louisiana, 419 US 522 (1975) (“the presence of a fair cross section of the community on venires…from which the petit juries are drawn is essential to the fulfillment of the Sixth Amendment’s guarantee of an impartial jury).

NB: In State v. Rogers, the Oregon Supreme Court said that the Article I, section 11, analysis for a fair cross section claim is the same as that applied under the Sixth Amendment. 334 Or 633, 490 n. 3 (2002).

The fair cross section requirement applies only to the pool from which the petit jury is drawn; there is no requirement that the final jury “reflect the composition of the community at large.” Lockhart v. McCree, 476 US 162, 173 (1986).

Fair Cross Section Challenge: Right to Impartial Jury To make a prima facie case that the venire selection procedure violates the fair cross section requirement under the Sixth Amendment and Article I, section 11, the defendant must demonstrate the following facts:

the alleged exclusion affects a “distinctive group”

the representation of the group in venire is unreasonable in proportion to their number in the community, and

the underrepresentation results from “systematic exclusion.”

Duren v. Missouri, 439 US 357, 364 (1979).

Once the defendant has made this prima facie showing, the state can only save its selection system if it can demonstrate that “a significant state interest [is] manifestly and primarily advanced,” by the challenged aspect of the selection system. Duren v. Missouri, 349 US 357, 367 (1979).

NB: The defendant does not have to show discriminatory intent, only that the exclusion is “systematic.” A systematic exclusion can be shown by the impact of the rule, instead of from the face of the discriminatory rule.

The defendant does not need to be a member of the excluded group in order to bring a fair cross section claim.

There is some debate over what constitutes a “distinctive group” – the Supreme Court has not precisely defined the term and so far has stated only that it applies to “race, gender, [and] ethnic background.” Lockhart v. McCree, 476 US 162, 175 (1986).

Fair Cross Section Challenge: Equal Protection To make a prima facie case that the jury venire violates the Equal Protection Clause, the defendant must:

Establish that the excluded group is one that is a “‘recognizable, distinct class,’” singled out for different treatment under the law, as written or applied.” State v. Haugen, 349 Or 174, 184-85, 243 P3d 31 (2010)] (quoting State v Rogers, 334 Or 633, 642 n 8, 55 P3d 488 (2002)).

Show the degree of under representation by comparing the proportion of the group in the total population to the proportion called to serve as jurors, over a significant period of time. Id.

Demonstrate that the state’s selection procedure is susceptible of abuse or is not racially neutral in support of the presumption of discrimination raised by the statistical showing. Id.

Show that the exclusion of a cognizable group is “purposeful.” This in turn requires the defendant to “establish a clear pattern that emerges from the effect of the state’s action, inexplicable on ground other than race, even when the governing legislation appears neutral on its face.” Id.

It is more difficult to establish a violation of the Equal Protection Clause because the defendant must show “purposeful” discrimination, whereas under the impartial jury rationale, the defendant need only show that there is a discriminatory impact resulting from the state’s system of jury panel selection.

D. Anonymous Juries

Although empaneling an anonymous jury is an uncommon and disfavored procedure, counsel should challenge this procedure if it is used.

An anonymous jury panel occurs when identifying information of the jurors is withheld from either the defendant or defendant’s counsel or if jurors are instructed to withhold information. State v. Rogers, 352 Or 510, 540, 288 P3d 544 (2012). An anonymous jury panel risks offending a defendant’s right to an impartial jury in two ways: first, it hinders the defendant’s ability to conduct voir dire and select impartial jurors, and second, it is an external factor that compromises the jury’s ability to remain impartial because it undermines the defendant’s presumption of innocence by creating an inference that the defendant is dangerous. State v. Sundberg, 349 Or 608, 620, 247 P3d 1213 (2011).

Oregon has adopted the federal standard for empanelling an anonymous jury, but it appears that the procedure is far more uncommon in state court. Under that standard, the trial court has the authority to order an anonymous jury selection only if the trial court concludes there is a strong reason to believe the jury needs protection and the court takes reasonable precautions to minimize any prejudicial effects on the defendant and to ensure his fundamental rights are protected. Sundberg, 349 Or at 621 (citing U.S. v. Paccione,949 F2d 1183, 1192 (2d Cir.1991), cert. den. 505 U.S. 1220, 112 S.Ct. 3029, 120 L.Ed.2d 900 (1992)).

1. A trial court has the authority to protect jurors from the risk of physical harm, intimidation, or harassment, whether by parties, the press, or the public, by withholding juror names and other identifying information; but a determination that such a risk exists must be made on the facts of each case and not on the basis of a generalized desire to protect the anonymity of all jurors in all cases in the interests of juror privacy. State v. Sundberg, 349 Or 608, 247 P3d 1213 (2011).

2. If the trial court does make sufficient findings that an anonymous jury procedure is required, it must then take steps to minimize the prejudice to the defendant. Such measures may include “permitting extensive voir dire of each juror regarding potentially relevant matters and providing a ‘plausible and nonprejudicial’ explanation to jurors for not disclosing their identities. Id. at 623.

The two Oregon Supreme Court cases that have addressed the use of anonymous juries concluded that the use of the procedure was reversible error. Particularly after Rogers, it appears that in Oregon anonymous juries are highly disfavored. It is possible that any remedial measure will be inadequate in light of the very great risk of prejudice faced by the defendant.

Practice Tip:The use of an anonymous jury most directly implicates the defendant’s right to conduct voir dire, however, it really is a problem with the venire and should be raised prior to the commencement of voir dire. If the trial court improperly uses an anonymous jury procedure, counsel should move to have the entire venire panel dismissed and a new panel called.

VI. Voir Dire

A. Purpose and Approach to Voir Dire

Voir dire serves the right to trial by a fair and impartial jury. Lambert v. Sisters of St. Joseph, 277 Or 223, 230, 560 P2d 262 (1977). An impartial juror is one which favors neither side and whose mind will allow a decision based upon the evidence and the law. Lambert, 277 Or at 230.

Voir dire is to be conducted so as to determine juror impartiality. Thus, any inquiry into whether a juror would be willing to have a person who is in the questioner’s frame of mind sit on the jury has been held improper as being an examination designed to identify partiality to one side or another. State v. Wakefield, 111 Or 615, 619-620 (1924).

Defendant’s due process right to a fair trial requires an adequate voir dire to identify unqualified jurors. Morgan v. Illinois, 504 US 719, 112 S Ct 2222, 119 L Ed 2d 492 (1992). See also Swain v. Alabama, 380 US 202 (1965); Rosales-Lopez v. United States, 451 US 182 (1981); United States v. Baldwin, 607 F2d 1295 (9th Cir 1979).

The principal purpose of voir dire is to discover whether a potential juror harbors opinions and biases that would justify an excusal for cause. ORCP 57D; State v. Barone, 328 Or 68, 969 P2d 1013 (1998); State v. Nefstad, 309 Or 523, 526, 789 P2d 1326 (1990); State v. Montez, 309 Or 564, 789 P2d 1352 (1990); State v. Barnett, 251 Or 234, 445 P2d 124 (1968); McElwain v. Kabatoff, 275 Or 393, 551 P2d 105 (1976).

A juror’s probability of bias, as determined by the court, is the test of that juror’s disqualification. Lambert, 277 Or at 230; and Lane County v. Walker, 30 Or App 715, 721 (1977). The qualification of a juror is a question for the trial court, whose decision will not be disturbed on appeal absent an abuse of discretion. State v. Olberman, 33 Or 556, 558 (1899); State v. Brumfield, 104 Or 506, 526-529 (1922).

Another purpose of voir dire it to elicit sufficient information to ascertain whether to challenge a juror peremptorily. State v. Miller, 10 Or App 636, 501 P2d 326 (1972);Jones v. Imperial Garages, Inc., 174 Or 49, 57-58, 145 P2d 469 (1944);State v. Lauth, 46 Or 342, 80 P 660 (1905). Under the United States Constitution, a defendant possesses a Sixth Amendment right to intelligently exercise peremptory challenges. Nebraska Press Association v. Stuart, 427 US 539, 600-02, 96 S Ct 2791 (1976).

Practice Tip:Counsel is entitled to question jurors on all issues that are relevant to actual issues in the case. If the court senses that you are using voir dire for an impermissible purpose, the court may cut you off, and the opportunity to explore areas that are relevant and necessary to the intelligent exercise of challenges maybe be lost.

However, defense counsel, through the use of questions that directly relate to a juror’s actual bias under the facts presented by the specific case, has as an opportunity to introduce the jury to the claims and defenses of the case. Jury selection must be considered an integral part of establishing the defense theory of the case. In preparing jury selection, defense counsel should consider the theory of the case and how it requires particular questions to be posed during voir dire.

B. Manner of Conducting Voir Dire

1. Trial Court’s Discretion to Conduct Voir Dire Before voir dire, counsel should prepare a memo for the trial court to alert the court to the issues that will be explored during voir dire.

Practice Tip:The pretrial memo should include potential issues of bias. Because counsel is entitled to question on relevant issues including bias, ''see State v. Barnett, 251 Or 234, 238, 445 P2d 124 (1968), this prepares the court for the fact that you will prod jurors on certain issues that are important to your case. Counsel does not have to accept a yes or no answer from a juror and is entitled to prod jurors to more fully disclose personal information if it is relevant to the issues in the case. A pre-voir dire memo helps to establish that relevancy.

The trial court has discretion to direct the method and manner of voir dire. State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 860 P2d 860 (1993); State v. Loke, 131 Or App 751 (1994). Although this discretionary power of the court is broadly construed, the court cannot prohibit a defendant from seeking relevant information. State v. Barnett, 251 Or 234, 238, 445 P2d 124 (1968) (counsel entitled to question on relevant issues); State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 860 P2d 860 (1993) (40-minute limitation on voir dire is not adequate to provide counsel with an “opportunity to ascertain the existence of grounds of challenges for cause or to obtain information to make intelligent decisions on peremptory challenges”), State v. Loke, 131 Or App 751, 886 P2d 507 (1994) (limiting voir dire to 32 minutes and denying request for additional time was reversible error).

The trial court also cannot constrain voir dire to the extent that counsel is not permitted to examine the prospective jurors who actually serve on the jury. State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 551, 860 P2d 860 (1993).

2. Individual or Group Voir Dire The general practice in Oregon is to voir dire the panel. But certain circumstances might call for an individual voir dire. ''See State v. Rogers, 313 Or 356, 836 P2d 1308 (1992) (allowing individual voir dire). For example, if you are trying a sex abuse case it is important to know whether any of the venire persons have experienced sexual abuse. Obviously, this is a very sensitive topic that many potential jurors would rather not disclose to a group of strangers. If the court allows limited individual voir dire, and if you know that you will be questioning a juror about a sensitive matter, ask that juror whether he or she would be more comfortable answering questions outside of the presence of others. In that way, you can accommodate both the court’s need to expedite voir dire and that juror’s needs for privacy.

Practice Tip:Jury questionnaires are generally a good idea, especially in cases that involve sensitive issues such as race or history of abuse. The potential jurors may be more willing to disclose that information in a questionnaire than out loud in front of other jurors. Equipped with answers on a jury questionnaire, you will know which jurors may need to be questioned on those topics; you can then alert the court to the need for individual voir dire on selected issues in advance.

Make sure to alert the court of your intent to use juror questionnaires well in advance—it takes time and organization by court staff. Jurors have to come in early to fill them out or the case is delayed for several hours while the questionnaires are filled out and copied.

3. Types of Voir Dire Questions Use voir dire to disclose the bad evidence in your case. Find out from the juror how this evidence will affect him or her and if a single factor will influence him or her in making his or her decision. An example of a question involving a single factor would be, “Would this single factor make you vote guilty regardless of any other information?” This will assist in cause challenges.

Practice Tip:The type of questions that you use in voir dire have different purposes. Use hypothetical questions to avoid the prohibition against asking jurors to commit to specific facts of your case. ''See State v. Montez, 309 Or 564, 584, 789 P2d 1352 (1990) (question asking juror to comment on how he would react to specific evidence “patently improper”).

• Use open-ended questions in order to learn all of the information you can about a juror.

• Use close-ended questions to obtain commitments from jurors or to establish for cause challenges.

Open-ended questions designed to understand jurors’ history and bias will generally not be objected to. Questions that also educate as to case theory will generally not be objected to. Questions that educate as to the case theory are proper if designed to elicit information that can be used in for-cause challenges or obtain sufficient education to intelligently exercise a peremptory challenge. Be careful to always accurately restate a juror’s opinion using their own words if you are going to enter the world of cause challenges. Do not misinterpret or misstate what a juror is saying or else the other jurors will consider you unfair or tricky.

C. Scope of Voir Dire

Trial court has discretion to determine the scope of voir dire. This is consistent with the court’s broad powers in regulating the presentation and interrogation of witnesses at trial under OEC 611(1). The aim of an impartial jury, however, serves to guarantee voir dire in areas relevant to a juror’s qualification, including case-specific issues and individual predispositions or biases. State v. Barnett, 251 Or 234, 238, 445 P2d 124 (1968), and State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 551-52, 860 P2d 860 (1993). Federal due process guarantees similarly serve to limit curtailment of voir dire. Ham v. South Carolina, 409 US 524, 527, 93 S Ct 848(1973).

Counsel is entitled to inquire into prejudicial matters:

A litigant is entitled to inquire into any interest or matter that may affect a juror’s verdict because the right to an impartial jury means an impartial jury with relation to the actual issues being litigated. Leishman v. Taylor, 199 Or 546, 556-557, 263 P2d 605 (1953).

Despite the right to inquire into potentially prejudicial matters, the line between exploring jurors’ prejudices in view of case specific issues and introducing prejudice into the case is a fine one. Leishman, 199 Or at 556-557; and Johnson v. Hansen, 237 Or 1, 4-5, 389 P2d 330 (1964).

Counsel must be prepared to show the need for making a particular inquiry relevant in the context of the case at hand. Johnson, 237 Or at 5. Time limits that preclude relevant inquiry may be unconstitutional. State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 551, 860 P2d 860 (1993).

D. Specific Areas of Permissible Inquiry

1. Racial Bias – The particular facts of a case may constitutionally require inquiry of jurors on racial prejudices. In Ham v. South Carolina, 409 US 524 (1972), such inquiry was critical to insuring the “essential demands of fairness” where the defendant, a black man, was accused of killing a white police officer. 409 US at 526-527. See also State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 552 (1993). 2. Organizational Affiliations – Voir dire on jurors’ attitudes toward an organization is allowed where the defendant is a member of and active in that organization. State v. Dixon, 5 Or App 113, 122-123, 481 P2d 629 (1971). 3. Religious Beliefs – Inquiry into jurors’ religious beliefs is proper if relevant to case-specific issues. State v. Barnett, 251 Or 234, 238, 445 P2d 124 (1968). In Barnett, the defendant, in an abortion prosecution, was entitled to examine jurors regarding their religious beliefs because of the “widely accepted belief” that certain religious faiths have varying feelings regarding abortion than do others. 251 Or at 236. 4. Child as Alleged Victim – A defendant may appropriately question jurors on their ability to disregard the fact that a child was the alleged victim. State v. Elam, 37 Or App 365, 372, 587 P2d 491 (1978). 5. Jurors’ Occupations – Inquiry into some details concerning a juror’s occupation is justified where that occupation or its duties may supply a basis for questioning the juror’s impartiality. a. A juror’s occupation as a police officer may, but will not necessarily, indicate bias. Parks v. Cupp, 5 Or App 51, 53, 481 P2d 372 (1971).

b. Government employee jurors were properly questioned regarding a government policy, specifically the “Loyalty Order,” where that policy could reasonably affect their ability to judge a case of willful refusal to produce documents to Congress. Morford v. United States, 339 US 258, 70 S Ct 586 (1950). The trial court’s refusal to allow inquiry into this area was an abuse of discretion, requiring reversal.

6. Attitude Towards Insanity Defense - Litigants have a clear right to voir dire on jurors’ attitudes about insanity defenses if insanity is in issue. State v. Wallace, 170 Or 60, 109, 131 P2d 222 (1942). 7. Attitude Towards the Death Penalty – In capital proceedings, the parties are entitled to inquire into prospective jurors’ attitudes towards the death penalty. State v. Barone, 328 Or 68, 969 P2d 1013 (1998); State v. Nefstad, 309 Or 523, 526, 789 P2d 1326 (1990); State v. Montez, 309 Or 564, 789 P2d 1352 (1990). 8. Concept of Self-Defense – A juror’s attitude towards the concept of self-defense is within the proper scope of voir dire where that legal concept is relevant to the case in issue. Everly v. State, 395 NE2d 254, 255 (Ind. 1979). Note that OEC 306, concerning the court instructing the jury on burdens of persuasion, does not preclude counsel’s discussion of burdens during voir dire. See Legislative Commentary to OEC 306. 9. Mental Concepts of Deliberateness and Premeditation – Counsel was allowed to question jurors closely, through use of hypothetical questions, concerning deliberateness and premeditation in a murder prosecution. State v. Humphrey, 63 Or 540, 547-548, 128 P 824 (1912). 10. Jurors’ Opinions on State Policy – Inquiry into jurors’ opinions on state policies is justified where the policies are an issue in the case. Highway Commission v. Hewitt, 229 Or 582, 591, 368 P2d 346 (1962). 11. Defendant’s Prior Conviction – A defendant’s prior criminal conviction is a proper matter for voir dire because of the possibility of prejudice as a result of that conviction. State v. Ziebert, 34 Or App 497, 501-502, 579 P2d 275 (1978). 12. Previous Litigation Experience – Inquiry into juror’s previous experience as a party, witness or juror is proper to determine juror’s state of mind. State v. Miller, 10 Or App 636, 639, 501 P2d 326 (1972); State v. Nagel, 185 Or 486, 506, 202 P2d 640 (1949). 13. Jurors’ Exposure to the Case – Inquiry into prior exposure to the case, either through direct knowledge, relationships or publicity, assists in determining jurors’ states of mind toward the case and potential bias. State v. Rogers, 313 Or 356, 836 P2d 1308 (1992); State v. McDonald, 231 Or 24, 37-38, 361 P2d 1001 (1961); State v. Humphrey, 63 Or 540, 547-548, 128 P 824 (1912). This inquiry relates to the question of inflammatory pretrial publicity, but also focuses upon a juror having a fixed attitude versus an attitude allowing the juror to disregard previously formed opinions. Humphrey, 63 Or at 548. 14. Jurors’ Attitudes Towards Types of Witnesses – Voir dire on jurors’ attitudes towards types of witnesses, where those attitudes indicate some bias or prejudice would appear necessary to achieving an impartial jury. See, however, State v. Hoffman, 85 Or 276, 279-280, 166 P 765 (1917), in which the court held improper voir dire on a juror’s opinion of the credibility of informers as witnesses because the court saw it as an effort to have the juror disclose what class of witnesses would or would not be credible. The court apparently failed to see the connection between the issue of credibility of classes of witnesses and bias or prejudice for or against a defendant.

E. Remedies if Jurors Are Exposed to Prejudicial Comments During Voir Dire

Injection of prejudice into the proceeding during voir dire, as opposed to exploration of jurors’ attitudes of potentially prejudicial or inflammatory matters, may provide grounds for a mistrial. State v. Flores, 31 Or App 187, 190-191, 570 P2d 94 (1977). In evaluating the propriety of granting a mistrial, the court looks to whether strong likelihood of prejudice to the defendant resulted. Flores, 31 Or App at 187.

In Flores, for example, the court found that the prosecutor’s remarks that “probably over 90 percent” of persons indicted by grand juries plead guilty and that of the remaining 10 percent mostly are convicted, were clearly improper. The court held that defendant’s motion for a mistrial should have been granted notwithstanding the state’s argument that the comments were in response to misleading comments by defense counsel.

The context in which the improper comments were made will be examined. State v. Wampler, 30 Or App 931, 935, 569 P2d 46 (1978). In Wampler, the Court of Appeals upheld the trial court’s denial of defendant’s motion for mistrial based upon the prosecutor’s statements where the court found that those comments, although inappropriate, were prompted by defense counsel’s improper statements. 30 Or App at 935. Compare Flores, 31 Or App 187, 190-191, 570 P2d 94 (1977).

A mere exception to a prosecutor’s improper comments during voir dire isinsufficient to form the basis of an appeal. State v. Morris, 83 Or 429,435-436, 163 P 567 (1917).

Consider, also, the prejudice to defendant resulting from a juror’s false statement on voir dire. Discovery of such prejudicial misconduct may serve as a basis for a motion for mistrial or motion for new trial. State v. Lauth, 46 Or 342, 346-352, 80 P 660 (1905).

Jurors’ Exposure to Extraneous Information After Voir Dire – Although the general rule is to preclude counsel’s inquiry of jurors once the jury has been sworn, this rule may find exception where jurors are exposed to extraneous and potentially prejudicial information or experiences during the trial. State v. Miller, 10 Or App 636, 639 (1972). In Miller, the Court of Appeals held that defendant’s motion for mistrial should have been granted where jurors served on another case during a recess in the defendant’s case, citing the strong possibility that service on the other case might create bias.

VII. Challenges for Cause

A. Statutory Challenges under ORS 136.210

ORS 136.210 cross-references Oregon Rule of Civil Procedure 57 D(1)(a), D(1)(b), and D(1)(g) as grounds for challenging a juror for cause.

1. A juror can be challenged for cause if that juror does not meet the requirements for juror eligibility described in ORS 10.030. ORCP 57 D(1)(a). 2. A juror can be challenged if there is a physical or mental defect which satisfies the court that the challenged person is incapable of performing the duties of a juror without prejudice to the substantial rights of the challenging party. ORCP 57 D(1)(b). 3. A juror can be excluded for actual bias. ORCP 57 D(1)(g). a. Actual Bias “is the existence of a state of mind on the part of a juror that satisfies the court, in the exercise of sound discretion, that the juror cannot try the issue impartially and without prejudice to the substantial rights of the party challenging the juror.”

b. Actual bias may be in reference to:

i. the action

ii. either party to the action

iii. the sex of the party, the party’s attorney, a victim or a witness

iv. a racial or ethnic group that the party, the party’s attorney, a victim or a witness is member of or perceived to be a member of.

c. If it appears that the challenged juror has formed or expressed an opinion upon the merits of the case based on what that juror has heard or read, such an opinion is not automatically sufficient to sustain a challenge to that juror. The court must be satisfied, from all the circumstances, that the juror cannot disregard such opinion and try the case impartially.

B. Challenging a Juror for Actual Bias

Actual bias exists when the prospective juror’s viewpoint would prevent or substantially impair the performance of the duties of a juror if the person is selected. State v. Wagner,305 Or 115, 175, 752 P2d 1136 (1988), vacated 492 US (1989), on remand309 Or 5, 786 P2d 93 (1990); State v. Dalessio, 228 Or App 531, 208 P3d 1021 (2009). State v. Nefstad,309 Or 523, 526, 789 P2d 1326 (1990).

When an actual bias challenge is raised, the court must determine whether the juror is capable of trying the case fairly and impartially upon the evidence adduced in court. State v. Moen, 309 Or 45, 93, 786 P2d 111 (1990); State v. Brumfield, 104 Or 506, 529, 209 P 120 (1922); “The trial court in exercising discretion must find from all the facts that the juror will be impartial and fair and not be consciously or unconsciously biased. The test of a juror’s disqualification is the probability of bias or prejudice as determined by the court.” State v. Montez, 309 Or 564, 575, 789 P2d 1352 (1990); Lambert v. Sisters of St. Joseph, 277 Or 223, 230, 560 P2d 262 (1977); State v. Fanus, 336 Or 63, 83, 79 P3d 847 (2003).

Practice Tip:In order to establish the basis for a “cause challenge” during voir direquestioning, the attorney must delicately and inoffensively lead the juror to say he or she cannot decide the case fairly. Preliminary questioning that determines the juror’s commitment and desire to be on the jury may be a helpful avenue to pursue in establishing the foundation for the jurors to downplay areas of bias. A juror who really wants to be on the jury or who has a hidden bias will usually be difficult to remove for cause.

1. Rehabilitation The trial court or opposing counsel may try to rehabilitate the witness. Counsel must politely and firmly get the juror to commit to his or her earlier statements indicating his or her feelings of bias or lack of ability to be fair.

The purpose of rehabilitative questioning is for investigation and not for the purpose of persuasion, nor to elicit pro forma answers to leading questions. Lane County v. Walker, 30 Or App 715, 568 P2d 676 (1977).

The initial answers of the juror should be given greater weight than rehabilitative answers. Lambert v. Sisters of St. Joseph, 277 Or 223, 231, 560 P2d 262 (1977).

2. Cause Challenges on Appeal On appeal, the standard of review of trial court’s denial of a challenge for cause to a juror is deferential, but not unbounded. State v. Carter, 205 Or App 460, 134 P3d 1078 (2006).

In State v. Carter, 205 Or App 460, 134 P3d 1078 (2006), the court held that denial of rape defendant’s challenge for cause to juror was an abuse of the trial court’s discretion where a prospective juror stated he believed the defendant was guilty and that he did not think it was fair to put himself on the jury, and in response to questioning from prosecutor and court, he merely said that he “probably” could follow the law and, “I guess I could try” to follow the law.

In State v. Dalessio, 228 Or App 531, 208 P3d 1021 (2009), thedefendant was granted a new trial after a seated juror in a murder case came forward following the first day of trial and explicitly stated that he had been making some judgments that he thought he really should not be making and implicitly disclaimed his earlier voir dire statement that he could be fair and impartial. Not excusing this juror was held to be an abuse of the trial court’s discretion.

C. Challenges for Implied Bias under ORS 136.220

The court will excuse a juror for cause if any of the following causes are shown:

1. Consanguinity or affinity within the fourth degree to the person alleged to be injured by the charged offense, the defendant or the complainant. 2. The prospective juror stands in the relation of guardian and ward, attorney and client, physician and patient, master and servant, debtor and creditor, principal and agent, or landlord and tenant with the defendant, the person alleged to be injured by the charged offense, or the complainant. 3. The prospective juror is a member of the family, a partner in a business with, or in the employment of the defendant, the person alleged to be injured by the charged offense, or the complainant, or is a surety in the action or otherwise for the defendant. But state employment alone does not disqualify a juror from service because the complainant must be a person and the state is not a person. State v. Rogers, 313 Or 356, 369-72 (1992)].

4. The prospective juror served on the grand jury that returned the indictment. 5. The prospective juror served on a jury formerly sworn in the same action and whose verdict was set aside or was discharged without a verdict after the cause was submitted to it. 6. The prospective juror served on a jury in a civil action, suit, or proceeding brought against the defendant for substantially the same act charged as an offense. 7. The prospective juror served on a jury in a criminal action upon substantially the same facts, transaction, or criminal episode.

VIII. Peremptory Challenges

A peremptory challenge is a challenge to a juror that is not based on cause. Peremptory challenges can be used when the trial court denies a challenge for cause. Peremptory challenges may also be used against a juror that counsel has a hunch will be unfavorable but not sufficient evidence of bias to bring a cause challenge. For example, a defense lawyer may have determined individuals fitting within certain demographics would be negative jurors in his or her client’s case. Peremptory challenges allow counsel to act on stereotypes, with one significant caveat: counsel cannot exercise peremptory challenges solely on the basis of the juror’s membership in a cognizable group.

A. Number of Challenges in Circuit Court

1. If the accused faces the death penalty or life imprisonment, both parties have 12 peremptory challenges. ORS 136.230(1). 2. If the trial is otherwise before a jury of more than 12 jurors, each side has six peremptory challenges. ORS 136.230(1). 3. If the trial is before a jury of six, each side has three peremptory challenges. ORS 136.230(1).

B. Manner of Taking Peremptory Challenges

Under ORS 136.230(2), peremptory challenges are taken two at a time, starting with the defendant, by secret ballot.

The parties may stipulate to taking peremptory challenges orally.

In a co-defendant case, each defendant is entitled to the same number of challenges as he or she would have had if tried separately. But the state is entitled to the total number of challenges that the defendants could have exercised.

After each challenge, a new juror is called and questioned in order to pass or be excused for cause. In the panel method of jury selection, some judges expect you to question every potential juror on the panel (not just the 12 in the box). Then the next juror in line automatically replaces the challenged juror.

Once a party indicates satisfaction with the panel by not challenging, then that party is precluded from challenging any of the jurors already seated.

Practice Tip: The state can continue to exercise challenges even if the defense states they are satisfied. The defense can only challenge new jurors that replace ones struck by the defense. In some circumstances, it may be wise to indicate satisfaction with the panel if counsel has only a single peremptory challenge remaining and if counsel has not questioned the next possible juror. Counsel then would have the right to use their remaining challenge to any new juror that is called followed a prosecution challenge and avoid potential surprises. However, there is a major caveat to this strategy because indicating satisfaction with the panel acts as a waiver of prior cause challenges denied by the court.

C. Challenging the Discriminatory Use of Peremptory Challenges

In the seminal case of Batson v. Kentucky, and its progeny, the United States Supreme Court held that the exercise of peremptory challenges on the basis of race, ethnicity, or sex violates the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. Batson v. Kentucky,476 US 79, 106 S Ct 1712, 90 L Ed2d 69 (1986), Georgia v. McCollum,505 US 42, 112 S Ct 2348, 120 L Ed2d 33 (1992), and Miller-El v. Cockrell, 537 US 322, 123 S Ct 1029 (2003); State v. Longo, 341 Or 580, 148 P3d 892 (2006). Batson involved the prosecutor’s use of peremptory challenges against black jurors in a case involving a black defendant.

Although Batsoninvolved peremptory challenges exercised by the prosecutor on the basis of race, its holdingis not limited solely to race. The Batson rule could apply to any cognizable group. See J.E.B. v. Alabama, 511 US 127, 114 S Ct 1419 (1994) (extendingBatsonto gender-based exercise of peremptories). Further, theBatsonrule is not limited to the prosecutor’s exercise of peremptory challenges, it applies to defense counsel as well.See Georgia v. McCollum'', 505 US 42, 112 S Ct 2348 (1992) (defense counsel cannot make race-based peremptory challenges); United States v. De Gross, 960 F2d 1433, (9th Cir. 1992) (en banc) (defense counsel prohibited from exercising peremptory challenges on the basis of gender).

In another significant expansion of the Batsonrule, the United States Supreme Court held in Powers v. Ohio, 499 US 400, 111 S Ct 1364, 113 L Ed2d 411 (1991), that the defendant does not necessarily need to be a member of the cognizable group that is being excluded through peremptory challenges because the Equal Protection Clause also protects the public’s right to serve on a jury.

There is a three-step process for making a constitutional challenge under the Batson framework. See State v. Henderson, 315 Or 1, 843 P2d 859 (1992) (applying the Batson framework in state court).

1. The challenger must establish a prima facie case of purposeful discrimination of cognizable group. The challenger may do so by showing that defendant is a member of a cognizable group and that the prosecutor has exercised a peremptory challenge to remove a member of defendant’s cognizable group; however, a defendant may also raise a Batsonchallenge even when he or she does not belong to the particular cognizable group. Powers v. Ohio, 499 US 400 (1991).

2. If a prima facie case of purposeful discrimination is established, the burden shifts to the prosecutor to articulate a “neutral explanation” for the peremptory challenge. Explanation must be “related to the particular case to be tried, and must be clear and reasonably specific.” Henderson, 315 Or at 4. A prosecutor’s explanation that a peremptory challenge is race-neutral is a question of law. State v. Ruiz-Martinez, 173 Or App 202 (2001). The explanation does not have to be persuasive, however, the explanation must be reasonably specific and case-related. Id.

3. Court must determine as a matter of fact whether the defendant has carried the burden of establishing purposeful discrimination. Purkett v. Elem, 514 US 765, 1155 S Ct 1769, 131 L Ed 2d 834,rehearing denied, 515 US 1170 (1995). The court does not have to accept the prosecutor’s “race-neutral” explanation if that explanation is clearly pretextual or belied by the record. Kesser v. Cambra, 465 F3d 351 (9thCir 2006) (en banc).For example, when the government had been exercising its peremptories against members of a cognizable group that share the same “race neutral” characteristics as other jurors, the court found the prosecutor’s explanation pretextual. See, e.g., Green v. LaMarque, 532 F3d 1028 (9thCir 2008) (prosecutor’s race-neutral explanation for striking all six African-Americans on jury panel were pretexts as they also applied to unchallenged white jurors); Reynoso v. Hall, 395 F. Appx 344 (9thCir 2010) (unpublished) (striking two Hispanic jurors with similar characteristics to white jurors violated Batson). Courts also have found the government’s explanations to be pretextual when the cited reasons for exclusion would actually have favored the state, Ali v. Hickman, 584 F3d 1174 (9thCir 2009).

This rule also is codified in ORCP 57 D(4), made applicable in criminal proceedings through ORS 136.230(4). That provision describes the proper manner of raising a challenge to the discriminatory use of peremptories.

4. Courts shall presume that a peremptory challenge is not discriminatory, but the presumption may be rebutted in the following way: a. If a party believes that the adverse party is exercising a peremptory challenge on a basis prohibited under paragraph (a) of this subsection, the party may object to the exercise of the challenge. The objection must be made before the court excuses the juror. The objection must be made outside of the presence of potential jurors. The party making the objection has the burden of establishing a prima facie case that the adverse party challenged the potential juror on the basis of race, ethnicity or sex.

b. If the court finds that the party making the objection has established a prima facie case that the adverse party challenged a prospective juror on the basis of race, ethnicity or sex, the burden shifts to the adverse party to show that the peremptory challenge was not exercised on the basis of race, ethnicity or sex. If the adverse party fails to meet the burden of justification as to the questioned challenge, the presumption that the challenge does not violate paragraph (a) of this subsection is rebutted.

c. If the court finds that the adverse party challenged a prospective juror on the basis of race, ethnicity or sex, the court shall disallow the peremptory challenge.

IX. Preserving the Record for Appeal of Jury Selection Issues

Jury selection issues must be carefully and thoroughly preserved in order to be reviewable on appeal. Jury selection issues often carry a particular risk of being waived if the defendant does not continue to assert his or her objections to a particular juror.

A. Preserving Challenges for Cause

A challenge for cause is not preserved if the accused fails to exercise all of his or her peremptory challenges. Failure to use peremptory challenges to remove an objectionable juror after denial of a challenge for cause provides no error for review''. State v. Griffin, 19 Or App 822, 825, 529 P2d 399 (1974); and [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=Holmgren+v.+Westport+Towboat+Co.,+260+Or+445,+490+P2d+739+%281971%29&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=16669167090704569930&scilh=0 Holmgren v. Westport Towboat Co.][http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=Holmgren+v.+Westport+Towboat+Co.,+260+Or+445,+490+P2d+739+%281971%29&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=16669167090704569930&scilh=0 , 260 Or 445, 447, 490 P2d 739 (1971)]; Lambert v. Sisters of St. Joseph, 277 Or 223, 231, 560 P2d 262 (1977).

When a good challenge for cause is denied, it is not error when it appears that the challenger was not compelled to accept that juror. State v. Megorden, 49 Or 259, 88 P 306 (1907).

To make the record, defendant must challenge any objectionable juror for cause after exhausting the peremptory challenges. State v. Pettit, 66 Or App 575, 675 P2d 183,rev den297 Or 227 (1984).

A challenge for implied bias is waived unless submitted before the jury is sworn. Holmgren v. Westport Tow Boat Company, 260 Or 445, 490 P2d 739 (1971).

Objection to a juror on the ground of incompetency is waived by failing to challenge at the proper time. State v. McDonald, 8 Or 113 (1879). Objections to a juror must be made during voir dire. State v. Simonsen, 329 Or 288 (1999).

Voir dire must be reported to preserve the record. State v. Valasco,23 Or App 532, 543 P2d 37 (1975).

To preserve a cause challenge for appeal, do the following:

Make sure the court reporter is recording the voir dire, including all bench conferences.

The juror should be asked a sufficient number of questions to extract an unequivocal “cause challenge.”

Move to challenge the juror.

State clearly the reasons for the challenge into the record, including the applicable case law or statutory provisions.

Get a ruling from the court on the cause challenge.

Use a peremptory challenge on each of the “cause” jurors.

Exhaust all peremptory challenges.

Preferably out of the presence of the jury, request an additional peremptory strike for each “cause” juror.

State for the record that by overruling the challenge for cause the court has required counsel to take an objectionable juror.

State into the record that if granted an additional peremptory strike it would have been used against juror # ___, who is seated on the jury panel.

Make sure the appellate record includes:

– Master jury panel list. – Defense peremptory strikes. – List of strikes by the prosecutor.

B. Preserving Challenges to the Discriminatory Exercise of Peremptories

Identify the “cognizable group” and state for the record, outside of the presence of the jury and before the commencement of voir dire:

The name of the cognizable group

The name and juror numbers of the juror or jurors who belong to that group.

Upon conclusion of voir dire and before the exercise of peremptory challenges, again state into the record the aggregate number, names, and juror numbers of the members of the cognizable group.

After jury is seated but before they are sworn, make a record similar to the following:

That the state has exercised its peremptory strikes in such a way as to exclude from the jury members of a cognizable group.

That the state has exercised its peremptory challenges on the specific (names and juror numbers) members of a cognizable group.

That none of the named jurors were subject to a challenge for cause.

That the responses to questions posed during voir dire as well as their demeanors demonstrate that each juror is qualified, would have been a fair and impartial juror, would fairly weigh and evaluate the evidence, would render a verdict based solely on the evidence, and would impose no greater burden on the prosecution than that required by law.

That counsel objects to the composition of the jury on the ground that the prosecution has used peremptory strikes to exclude panel members from the jury on account of their inclusion in a cognizable group in violation of defendant’s right to equal rights and privileges under the Oregon Constitution, equal protection and due process under the United States Constitution, and the public’s right to serve under each constitution.

That counsel demands that the prosecution offer, on the record, its justification for striking each individual juror who was a member of the cognizable group.

X. Conclusion

It is crucial that you are prepared for voir dire because jury selection can make or break your case. In addition to knowing the issues in your case, it is important not to be afraid of asking questions that elicit harmful information or attitudes from jurors. Trial counsel does not want to be unaware of these beliefs and risk having those beliefs shared behind the closed doors of the jury room.

Counsel also should not be persuaded to leave someone on the jury because they like the juror or the juror really wants to sit on the case. At times, jurors who share the same values as trial counsel or would be a great person to talk to at a party may not be favorable to the defendant’s case. It is important to separate the idea of a good juror from a person who counsel would enjoy spending time with. On the other hand it is very important to be able to communicate with a juror. If a juror does not understand or seems hostile to counsel’s way of thinking or analyzing issues that will potentially impact how persuasive counsel will be with that one juror during trial.

Although jury selection has many potential pitfalls, the right to a trial by a jury of one’s peers is among our most cherished constitutional rights and worth the effort.
