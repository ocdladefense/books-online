Chapter 9. Effective Opening Statements

By Steve Sherlag

I. Introduction

While in Oregon state courts voir dire is the first chance defense counsel has to educate the jury about the main defense themes and theories in an interactive format, the defendant’s opening statement is the defense’s opportunity to set forth case themes in a persuasive narrative format, i.e., through a good story.

However, a persuasive narrative format has limitations. Although there is an expectation that the statement be persuasive, pervasive arguments are likely to be met with sustained objections and an impatient judge. Therefore, well-prepared counsel must carefully craft the opening statement’s three parts (introduction, body and conclusion) to carefully and persuasively set forth the defense theories and the facts that support those theories.

Approach an opening statement with the following concepts in mind:

1. Primacy. The first thing you say in opening is very important, maybe the most important thing you’ll say the whole trial. Primacy means that jurors tend to remember what was said first, thus what is said first is veryimportant. Spend half of your prep time dedicated to your opening statement on the very first thing you say. Make it simple and punchy, and bring it to the heart—not necessarily the intellectual core—of your case. One sentence—that’s all—giving the theme, the thrust of your defense.

2. Recency. Your conclusion is almost as important as the first thing you say, but your introduction will write your conclusion. Recency means that jurors tend to remember what was said last, so make it good and memorable.

3. Rhetoric in opening. A little more rhetoric, or argument, is tolerated at the very beginning and very end of your opening statement, especially if you are summarizing your theory, or the main defense thrust. But for the most part plain rhetoric is objectionable so it should be avoided. But that does not mean that the story, told in opening, cannot be effective and persuasive. In fact, it better be.

4. Don’t make promises you cannot keep. Be wary of overcommitting to specific facts, evidence, or witnesses, and whether or not your client will testify.

5. Storytelling. Tell a good story in narrative, chronological form. A damn good story is persuasive, maybe more persuasive than anything else you can do.

6. Passion. You are the voice of your client! Let the jury know it, let them feel it.

II. The Law of Opening Statements

The statute describing the purpose of an opening statement could hardly be more bland. ORCP 58B(3) provides in pertinent part:

The plaintiff shall concisely state plaintiff’s case and the issues to be tried; the defendant then, in like manner, shall state defendant’s case based upon any defense or counterclaim or both.

Similarly, the Oregon Supreme Court opined in State v. Reynolds,164 Or 446, 453, 100 P2d 593 (1940), “[t]he purpose served by an opening statement is to acquaint the jurors with the issues, the contemplated testimony and the relationship of the latter to the issues. The making of one is subject to the discretion of the trial judge.” See also United States v. Dinitz,424 U.S. 600, 612 (1976) (Burger, C.J., concurring) (“An opening statement has a narrow purpose and scope. It is to state what evidence will be presented, to make it easier for the jurors to understand what is to follow, and to relate parts of the evidence and testimony to the whole; it is not an occasion for argument”). Opening statement should not refer to matters that are not to be presented as evidence. United States v. Taren-Palma,997 F2d 525, 532 (9th Cir. 1993), cert. denied, 511 U.S. 1071 (1994). The scope and extent of an opening statement is within the trial court’s control. United States v. Hershenow,680 F2d 847, 857 (1st Cir. 1982). The trial judge can exclude irrelevant facts and stop argument if it occurs. United States v. Zielie,734 F2d 1447, 1455 (11th Cir. 1984), cert. denied, 469 U.S. 1189 (1985), and cert. denied, 469 U.S. 1216 (1985). The trial judge must “actively supervise the trial and, if necessary, reiterate instructions in the wake of prejudicial events.” United States v. Tootick,952 F2d 1078, 1085 (9th Cir. 1991).

III. Objectionable Opening Statements

A. “Argument”

The most common objection in opening is that counsel is “arguing” the case. The objection derives from the bland statutory and historical understanding of what an opening statement is.

Nonetheless, don’t have any doubt that you are arguing your case, absolutely so. YOU ARE ARGUING YOUR CASE IN OPENING. Repeat after me: YOU ARE ARGUING YOUR CASE IN OPENING. In fact, the whole trial long YOU ARE ARGUING YOUR CASE. Every minute, everything you’re doing. It’s just that at each stage you have different constraints, different formats. In opening, you argue it by setting forth the good facts, telling them like you were actually there. Tell a damn good story—it’s a lot more credible than anything else you can more transparently “argue.” In fact, when it comes down to it, you should be thankful for this format. It lets you build credibility with the jury so you don’t just look like another biased lawyer. Tell a damn good story—it won’t “technically” be argument and you’ll be credible. What could help your client more than being a credible advocate?

The line between an opening statement and an opening argument may seem determined by little more than how willing you are to risk a sustained objection or two. Some lawyers are loathe to object to opposing counsel’s opening in light of the fact that the most common judicial response is a rote explanation to the jury, “The statements of counsel are not evidence. Continue.”

Some lawyers are unwilling to do things that are objectionable because they think it will hurt their credibility with the jury, or may risk disposing the judge against them. It’s up to you in your own personal litigation style, considered on a case-by-case basis, to make the judgment call.

Practice Tip

Preempting the “Argument” Objection: “The evidence will show…”

Be aware that even very experienced judges sometimes have a hard time with really great unobjectionable opening statements, largely because a really great story can be so persuasive the judge may start thinking they are argumentative even when they are not. That’s why opening statements can be the toughest part of a defense case. Be proactive and occasionally sprinkle your opening with, “The evidence will show….” Merely saying “occasionally” may preempt the “argument” objection and give you enough to convince the judge: “I’m just telling the jury what the evidence will show, that’s the purpose of opening statements.” Surprisingly simple and effective.

Objections in opening can backfire, punctuating opposing counsel’s opening statement. However, the judge is more likely to sustain the objection when counsel argues incessantly, bores the judge or jury, or uses particularly descriptive or flowery verbiage (“Officer SoAndSo is a big fat liar” is objectionable versus “The evidence will demonstrate that you cannot trust Officer SoAndSo because…” and supply applicable facts).

Objections are also more likely to be sustained the more aggressive or passionate counsel is. With a more even tone, they are more tolerable.

Know your judge since different judges will have varying levels of tolerance for argument pursuant in the court’s exercise of discretion administering opening statements. See State v. Ragan, 123 Or 521, 262 P 954 (1928); State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336, 337, 479 P2d 240 (1971). Therefore, research your particular judge’s outlook with other counsel if you are not familiar with that judge. Keep in mind that it is possible to argue a little more in your introduction and conclusion. It also never hurts to say, “The evidence will show…” every now and then, irrespective of what you say next. Telling the jury what “the evidence will show” can never be found to be objectionable, even if you are arguing.

Judges will also vary greatly in their permissiveness in regard to discussions of the law in opening statements. If you have not previously tried a case before a particular judge, you must talk to those who have to discover how far you can go without objection. Anything other than a passing discussion of the law should be avoided in opening irrespective of the judge’s outlook.

Practice Tip

The Officious Intermeddling Judge

There will be times where you’ve drafted a great opening, gotten rid of all the flowery rhetoric and boiled it down to a short intro, a great story sprinkled with “the evidence will show,” and a short closing, yet the judge cannot sit on his or her hands. Sometimes this happens because the thrust of your case is one the judge doesn’t like, often because you are alleging corruption, inappropriate or even, gasp, criminal behavior on behalf of the cops. What to do?

You have a fundamental due process right to explain your defense theory in opening and the facts that support it. You’ve got a couple of options:

1) Just move on – sometimes you’ve got to accept the lumps and do your best.

2) You could try a slightly more risky response, a brief speaking objection: “Judge, I’m just tying up a couple aspects of the defense, if you can give me some leeway here….” Risky, but if you really are just tying a few things together and the judge knows you well, this sometimes works.

3) You can get assertive. Sometimes you have no choice but to look the judge in the eye coolly, calmly, and completely earnestly and ask for a bench conference or a conference outside the presence of the jury. If the judge denies the conference, look the judge coolly, calmly, and respectfullyin the eye and say, “Thank you, your Honor, I’ll take Mr. Defendant’s exception, and reserve further argument for the next break.” To have any chance at preserving the error, explain the problem at the n break, but because the standard on appeal is against you here, really what you’re doing is letting the judge and jury know that you take your job seriously. Sometimes this is enough to get the judge off your back for the rest of opening and, maybe, off your back in trial. Judges don’t like to look bad, and sometimes having a judge shut you down when you are respectfully and earnestly doing your job sends a couple of good messages to the jury: They will see that you care about your client—so much so that you are willing to take flak from the judge. Second, they sometimes get it that the judge isn’t always right and can get in the way of us doing our job. But you’ve got to do it with outward respect, even if in your heart you don’t have it.

B. Personal Opinions or “Vouching”

What the lawyer thinks is irrelevant, at least legally. Neither your personal opinions nor the prosecutor’s have any place in the case. You should never, at any point of the trial, say, “I think” or “I believe”—that’s inappropriate vouching. Still, your faith in your client’s innocence will be obvious and should not explicitly state that it is your personal belief. (“I believe Mr. Client is innocent” is objectionable; stating “Mr. Client is innocent” is not).

Prosecutorial Vouching. Prosecutors may resort to “vouching,” or attempt to use their personal appraisal of the value of a case or the credibility of a witness to sway the jury’s judgment. While vouching occurs in closing argument far more than opening statements, stay alert and object immediately. SeeState v. Parker,235 Or 366, 376–78, 384 P2d 986 (1963) (discussion of vouching). There is a vast collection of examples of vouching behavior found in both state and federal case law. An excellent compendium is found in Chapter 15 under the section, “The Ethical and Legal Problems of Prosecutorial Vouching.”

C. Comment on Defendant’s Silence

It is improper to comment on the defendant’s silence in opening statement. See State v. Alvord, 118 Or App 111, 115, 846 P2d 432 (1993) (comment on silence is presumptively prejudicial); State v. White,303 Or 333, 343, 736 P2d 552 (1987) (after improper comment on silence, trial court has a duty to allow a timely motion for a mistrial or to cure the effect of the improper comment by giving a proper instruction); State v. Grenawalt,86 Or App 96, 738 P2d 232,revden,304 Or 405 (1987); State v. Alvord, supra.

Proactively protect the Fifth Amendment. If your client has wisely invoked the right to silence, move in limine to prevent the prosecutor from referring to your client’s invocation, be it an explicit oral invocation or implicit by your client’s failure to respond to a direct question. It never hurts to put the prosecutor on notice and make them more cautious. It also will further your later motion for mistrial and dismissal if the prosecutor cannot contain him- or herself.

D. References to Inadmissible Evidence

Counsel is restricted in opening to describing evidence that in good faith they have a reasonable expectation will be offered and admitted. See State v. MacLaren, 115 Or 505, 507, 237 P 969 (1925) (“opening statement should always be confined to reference to competent evidence”). Where information conveyed to the jury is clearly inadmissible and prejudicial, even inadvertent statements can justify a mistrial. Closer calls regarding admissibility make a mistrial less likely, especially if opposing counsel could have anticipated issues relative to the evidence and failed to act in limine. Move in limine to disabuse the prosecutor of any good faith or reasonable expectations about the availability or admissibility of any potentially objectionable evidence.

Courts frequently forgive references to inadmissible evidence and will try to cure them through instructions. See State v. Frazier,245 Or 4, 418 P2d 841 (1966) (comment on expected testimony of co-defendant); State v. Williams,49 Or App 893, 621 P2d 621 (1980) (reference to defendant’s exercise of his constitutional right to counsel did not entitle defendant to mistrial); State v. Barker,26 Or App 297, 552 P2d 565 (1976) (violation of court’s pretrial order prohibiting introduction of evidence concerning other bad acts by defendant); State v. White, 119 Or App 424, 850 P2d 1158 (1993) (statement that defendant was a heavy drinker does not require mistrial); but see State v. Herrera, 236 Or 1, 5, 386 P2d 448 (1963) (“inflammatory nature of the prosecutor’s remarks in the case at bar outweighed any reasonable excuse for including them in the opening statement”; defendant’s character was not on trial). The “good faith” of the prosecutor or lack of prejudice to the defendant are the usual reasons for denying the motion for mistrial. Be prepared to object later if the issue reoccurs. See State v. Wolfs, 119 Or App 262, 267–68, 850 P2d 1139 (1993) (subsequent failure to object to admission of evidence of prior convictions waives appeal of failure to grant mistrial regarding prosecutor’s opening statement referencing prior convictions).

In State v. Davis, 345 Or 551, 201 P3d 185 (2008), the court considered the prejudicial impact of a prosecutor’s opening statement which included a summary of testimony of a witness that the prosecutor had more than just an inkling would not appear at trial. The opening statement summary included the reading of a note penned by Davis, accused of aggravated murder, which implicated him in the offense. Just after opening statement, the prosecutor let the court know of problems related to procuring the witness, in spite of having served him with a lawful subpoena; the defense made no motion at that time, but moved for a mistrial at the conclusion of the evidence when the witness failed to appear. The prosecutor detailed his efforts and affirmed under oath his recitation. Trial counsel did not challenge the good faith of the prosecutor’s statements, yet when appellate counsel did, the state supreme court did not consider the issue as it was not preserved. Id. at 587 n 20. The trial court offered a curative instruction, which defense counsel declined so as to not “ring the bell.” The trial court denied the motion for mistrial and the Oregon Supreme Court affirmed since:

It may be that some remarks included in an opening or closing statement could be so prejudicial that a finding of error, or even constitutional error, would be unavoidable. But here we have no more than an objective summary of evidence which the prosecutor reasonably expected to produce. . . . Certainly not every variance between the advance description and the actual presentation constitutes reversible error, when a proper limiting instruction has been given.

Id. at 587, citing Frazier v. Cupp, 394 U.S. 731, 736, 89 S Ct 1420, 22 L Ed 2d 684 (1969).

E. How to Make Your Objections

1. Timely An objection must be made at the time of the offending statement. See Carlson v. Lyman Slack Motors, 49 Or App 63, 618 P2d 1314 (1980) (objection and motion for mistrial at end of opening statement was untimely); State v. Cooper, 130 Or App 209, 880 P2d 514, 515 (1994) (counsel’s failure to timely object to comment on silence waives issue on appeal; to preserve error, a motion for a mistrial must be timely).

2. Timely Move for Curative Instruction and Mistrial When doing so, argue outside the presence of the jury. If appropriate, argue as to the language of the curative instruction and, in the alternative, for a mistrial. There is a high standard on appeal for the trial court’s denial of a motion for mistrial. See State v. McCartney,65 Or App 766, 768, 672 P2d 1210 (1983) (reviewed for an abuse of discretion), rev den296 Or 638, 678 P2d 739 (1984); State v. Grenawalt, 86 Or App 96, 98, 738 P2d 232, rev den.304 Or 405, 745 P2d 1225 (1987) (looking to the particular facts of this case, the decisive issue is whether defendant’s ability to receive a fair trial was impaired). Since the standard is so high, don’t expect the motion to be granted except in extraordinary and unusual situations:

Even if we find the prosecutor’s remarks to be improper, tasteless, or inappropriate, we will not find an abuse of discretion in the trial court’s denial of the motion for a mistrial unless the effect of the prosecutor’s remarks is to deny a defendant a fair trial.” 

State v. Smith,310 Or 1, 24, 791 P2d 836 (1990). 

Practice Tip

Moving for a mistrial

Bottom line, even if you don’t expect the motion to be granted, object, move for a mistrial. If you don’t, the issue won’t be preserved on appeal. SeeState v. Davis,supra.

Moreover, objecting to the prosecutor’s improper actions can be helpful in other ways, such as creating an argument for cumulative prosecutor misconduct, putting the prosecutor on notice that you’re not going to put up with any shenanigans, and, hopefully, dissuading the prosecutor from future misconduct. Finally, you may be able to get the trial judge on your side, to lean on the prosecutor and scrutinize them such that, in the event of future misconduct, you just may get your mistrial and be able to move for dismissal under State v. Kennedy, 295 Or 260, 666 P2d 1316 (1983). When you’ve caught the prosecutor doing something improper once, judges tend to get less patient the second or third time. Even if you don’t get your mistrial, it seldom hurts to have the judge jump down the prosecutor’s throat once or twice, either in front of or outside the presence of the jury. Prosecutors tend to get less flack from judges, so when they do it tends to affect them more. If you do get a mistrial, argue after the jury is discharged that jeopardy attached and your client cannot be retried under Kennedy (reckless indifference to resulting mistrial prohibits retrial).

3. Object If the Issue Returns A failure to later object on a related evidentiary issue may deem to waive earlier objectionable statements in opening. See State v. Wolfs, 119 Or App 262, 267-68, 850 P2d 1139 (1993) (subsequent failure to object to admission of evidence of prior convictions waives appeal of failure to grant mistrial regarding prosecutor’s opening statement referencing prior convictions). 

IV. Crafting a Persuasive Opening Statement

A. Developing Your Defense Theory and Case Theme or “Case Story”

The well-crafted opening statement makes a good first impression. First impressions count everywhere else in life, including before a jury. Once a juror’s mind is made up, it can be difficult to bring them around. So take the opening as an opportunity to set the stage in the case and pull the jury over to your side. For example, there is a significant difference in the following opening statement topic introductions:

“This case is about self-defense.”

“Bang! As the shot whizzed past his ear, John Client knew immediately that he had but one choice – to fight back or die.”

The former, while a classic, simple, and easily understood beginning, tells the jury nothing about the emotion of the case. The latter needs only a few facts to fill in some of the details in the body of the opening statement. But its strength is that it immediately informs the jury of both the emotion and crux of the case. It also places the jury at the specific moment in time when your client, conflicted, was forced to make the choice that the government contends is criminal and you contend was not. It immediately appeals to the jury’s sense of right and wrong and crystallizes what is important. It makes the jury want to hear more. Most of all, it avoids specific reference to the law and goes to the heart of the case.

B. Make Your Introduction and Conclusion Clear, Concise, and Simple

Draft your introduction as if you were going to give it to a middle school class. Consider how marketers or Hollywood “sells”a story:

Primacy and Recency.Lawyer-speak for “a jury remembers what it hears first and last, and not too much in between.” There is a tremendous amount of lore and trial consultant literature regarding trial strategy and what “works” with jurors. Some lore and literature place tremendous responsibility on the opening statement, suggesting that sixty percent or more of jurors make up their minds by the end of opening. Irrespective of these “studies,” it is clear that an opening will shape the way the jury hears, ignores, distorts, or rationalizes the evidence they hear and remember. It tells them what to listen for and what the issues are in the case.

Use simple language. You are not in law school. You are not talking to college professors, lawyers, or judges. Your audience is a group of people of varying backgrounds and varying degrees. Drive yourself to use short sentences, short words, and simple themes. Remember K-I-S-S.

Look the jurors in the eye.No notes, at least not for your introduction and conclusion. Use that opportunity to connect with jurors as people. Pick out friendly faces, then move to others who might be less open or friendly.

Smile, where appropriate. Appropriate levity opens minds and can be used at many stages of trial. Opening is one.

Consider a pithy quote,especially from a well-known author or commentator if it cuts to the point. Samuel Clemens and Benjamin Franklin are fountains of witty commentary.

C. Keep to the Same Rules in the Body of the Opening Statement

The body of your opening should use the classic narrative form—tell a good story in chronological order. The jury will give you their rapt attention for the introduction and two or three minutes. After that, you have to earn their attention by being the voice of your client, the voice of reason, and by telling damn good story. It need not be a great story. A good, earnest story is fine. You will not need to argue your case because the facts, the events that led to your client’s involvement, will argue your case for you. In addition to the tips above:

Drop legalese, except to the extent absolutely necessary to explain your defense. If you must use legalese, keep it to an absolute minimum.

Use and reuse key words and phrases to remind the jury of your theme. Think of the way that Fox News covers an event. Fox News isn’t successful because it has the most educated, most well-read, or most impressively credentialed reporters. Fox News is successful because its employees understand how to effectively communicate, and they do it over, and over, and over, and…

Enlist jurors to your sidewith the emotion of your case. Absolutely do not talk down to jurors. You want them thinking about your client and being on your side.

Direct the story to the head and heart. Jurors tend to find facts and resolve inconsistencies the way their heart wants them to, not their head.

Personalize your client. you will do this through telling your client’s story, using pictures, and by looking at and smiling at your client. Don’t forget that the person sitting next to you is a live body, with desires, goals, and a place in our community. Use the defendant as a demonstrative prop—the jury is going to look at your client anyway. I like to look at my client in opening at least once, and, where appropriate, more frequently. When you do, and you’re connected with your client, the jury will see the relationship. If there isn’t a good relationship, fake it. This is very important. They’ll see it in your eyes, your posture, the way you tilt your head towards your client.

Like your client. If it looks like you don’t like your client, whom you’ve spent a ton of time with, why should they do something so incredible as render a not guilty verdict in the face of a stressed-out complaining witness, an earnest and seemingly likeable DDA, and all those damn facts that point to the notion that your client is as guilty as hell?

Use good storytelling skills. What are good storytelling skills? We all know people who can light up the room, turn a good phrase, crack the room up, brin smiles to even a Scrooge or Crime Victims United charter member. You don’t need those types of skills to be a great storyteller. Great storytellers:

• Humanize their story (and hear our client and his/her story)

• Speak at a moderate pace

• Aren’t afraid of rhetorical pauses and silence (subtle but highly effective rhetorical punctuation marks)

• Speak in phrases with very brief pauses (giving the audience time to digest what was said and imagine or visualize what happened)

• Take the listeners to the place and time of the story itself

• Have comprehensive knowledge of the facts, or acknowledge the unknown (increasing credibility)

• Hit key facts

• Tap into the story’s emotional core

• Use and appropriately repeat key phrases (the pitch, the tag line)

• Work hard to be and remain credible

Avoid bad storytelling Habits.Bad storytellers:

  • Talk down to their audience, or fail to understand their audience
  • Talk too fast for their audience to process what is being said
  • Use jingoistic phrases (“Where’s the beef?”)
  • Assume their audience knows as much about the story as they do
  • Don’t know the key facts, or misstate the facts
  • Talk too long
  • Bore their audience
  • Act like they know it all
  • Don’t reveal their vulnerabilities

D. Advantage for the Defense: Addressing the Jury Second

Although many defense lawyers bemoan that they have to wait for the prosecutor to present the state’s opening, there are several decided benefits of going second. First, if the prosecutor is boring, the jury will appreciate hearing an entertaining story from you. Second, if the prosecutor doesn’t know the state’s case and makes material mistakes, your credibility goes up in the eyes of the jury before the evidence even begins. Third, you get the final word framing the evidence before the government starts its case. More than taking the wind of the state’s case, you can thoroughly confuse a prosecutor with a persuasive story – especially when the state starts off with boring direct testimony. Jurors will sometimes get antsy if the state doesn’t get on with the evidence that matters – hopefully, what the defense lawyer talked about in opening.

There are many theories as to whether or not you should respond, or even acknowledge, the government’s opening statement. This depends on the circumstances of your given case, because a well-crafted opening statement should anticipate the state’s case. Moreover, there are dangers in straying from your prepared remarks. First, by merely acknowledging the government’s statement you might convey that you are worried about your presentation and the strength of your case. By responding, you may give the government’s remarks more credibility in the eyes of the jury. Second, when rebutting, you are more likely to fall into objectionable argument which may draw the ire of the court. Third, when rebutting you may unintentionally overstate your case or set forth evidence which is still at issue, damaging your credibility. Think twice about responding. There’s time for that later -- in communicating with the jury in cross-examination, direct testimony, and in closing argument. In closing you can always remind the jury what the prosecutor said in opening and how the evidence did not bear it out.

E. Building Expectations

An opening statement, well-crafted or not, will continue what you began in voir dire – you will build expectations with the jury. These expectations go well beyond the description of the expected evidence. Your opening statement will affect your rapport and credibility with the jury throughout the trial.

F. The Danger of Overstating Your Case

Generally, you are better off remaining vague, general, and quick about details unless you’ve got some really great facts that the jury has to hear immediately. Trial testimony will often surprise you, even with a good police and defense investigation. If you make promises, you may pay a price if they are unfulfilled. If there are things which are unknown, tell the jury that—it brings them into the discovery process and can be exciting for them to see it unfold before them. Most jurors have no idea that we don’t have the right to a deposition or mandate a witness speak with us before trial.

If the jury expects your client to testify, you may have to explain your change in tactics later. However, if you tell the jury the client will testify and client does not, the prosecutor cannot argue the inference of silence in closing. See State v. Halford,101 Or App 660, 792 P2d 467 (1990) (presumptively prejudicial, denial of timely instruction, requires reversal, and perhaps dismissal with prejudice under State v. Kennedy,295 Or 260, 666 P2d 1316 (1983)).

G. Reserving Opening Statement

The common understanding is that reserving an opening statement is considered risky. Because of the common view, it is not often done. Yet it can be quite effective in the right situations, such as when you do not wish to warn the prosecution about certain evidence (or tactics), or in which you have serious uncertainties about the evidence.

Don’t be caught unawares, however, because the court is not required to allow a defendant to reserve closing. See State v. Ragan, 123 Or 521, 262 P 954 (1928) (administration regarding opening statement is subject to the discretion of the court); see also State v. Seeger, 4 Or App 336, 337, 479 P2d 240 (1971). While the court will usually grant the motion to reserve, if denied, be prepared to open your case. Consider apprising the court of your reservation outside the presence of the jury so as not to catch the court by surprise, or being caught by surprise if the court denies your motion to reserve. Moving outside the presence of the jury also avoids the possibility that the DA will inappropriately object to the reservation in the presence of the jury. See, e.g., State v. Seeger (abuse of discretion not to grant mistrial re prosecutor’s speaking objection to defendant reserving opening: “I object to that kind of a tactic, allowing the defendant to make up a story while the case is going on. And I think the rules should be played in this game”). If you’ve reserved opening, make sure at the appropriate time the court allows you to make your opening, and does not treat the reservation as a waiver. It never hurts to kindly remind the court, before the jury returns from a break, that you’ve reserved opening and will be giving it when the jury returns. Judges appreciate it when counsel gives them a heads-up.

H. Use of Exhibits in Opening

There is little reason not to use your anticipated exhibits in opening as long as you are sure that you have the witnesses prepared to lay the foundation for admission and relevance. And there are lots of good reasons to use good exhibits because it helps the jury have a road map to your case and be familiar with what they are going to see. Remember the concepts of Primacy and Recency. The exhibits should be carefully chosen, however, not argumentative, and they don’t have to include every picture you have from every angle.

Consider showing your opening exhibits to the prosecutor before opening. However, it is prudent and frequently required to show them to the prosecutor and sometimes even the court prior to giving your opening. Don’t expect opposing counsel to do much with them or argue much over your use, especially if you advise the court during motionsin liminethat opposing counsel has had the opportunity to review them. Opposing counsel’s silence will likely be viewed as acquiescence to their use.

I. To PowerPoint or Not?

There are certainly times when going to trial with a skinny single file, a yellow legal pad, and couple of pens sends the right message of purity of message and “underdog” to a jury.

There are lots of reasons not to PowerPoint, such as fear of tech failure and not wanting to appear as a slick defense lawyer, i.e., risking compromising credibility.

But there are lots of reasons to PowerPoint. Jurors nowadays set their expectations by slickly produced TV shows (not necessarily CSI but the evening news), so anywhere but the simplest cases there is no reason not to PowerPoint (or Prezio or any other presentation software) and many good reasons to do so. First, it will keep your presentation organized and will drive your opening—so much so that you might be able to skip the yellow pad. Second, once you learn a few basic maneuvers, PowerPoint becomes instinctive and easy to use. Third, and most importantly, PowerPoint is an effective tool, helping convey your message cleanly and persuasively. Once you get past the anxiety caused by fears of technology failure, you’ll start relying on PowerPoint to keep your presentations snappy and will find jurors even more engaged. Invest in a wireless mouse and things will get surprisingly simple.

J. Jump in the Ring and Ad Lib, Rough Outline, or Write Out the Whole Enchilada?

Every case is different, and every case calls for its own techniques and process. Every lawyer is different in terms of their skill set and rhetorical experiences. Certainly there is no harm in outlines and writing out the whole opening, even if you’re going to jump into the ring with no notes, no outline and no PowerPoint. What writing out the whole opening does for you is force you to create a narrative structure, and sometimes discard other less effective structures, in pursuit of your best work. Longer trials with lots of complicated facts might call for longer opening statements, thus writing it out helps pare out the necessary from that which is not.

So, you’ve gone through all of the work to write out your opening, pare it down to what is necessary, add plenty of great facts which tell the story of the case and appeal to the heart, and now you want to readit to the jury. Really? Read it? Is that effective?

The answer is yes, it can be, as long as you remember that it’s a script. You can deviate, you can stop and tell little stories. You can ad lib. You can cut in the middle. Just don’t be boring. Use techniques that actors and TV news people use when they readtheir scripts. Make it lively. Look up from the page. Use eye contact. Read a phrase, then look at the jury and deliver it.

Just remember that if you spend all of your client’s opening statement looking at the page, the jury will be looking at the inside of their eyelids before long. If you write it out, be organized and type it, and consider putting it in its own three-ring binder in large type so it’s easy to read. I find it very effective in complicated cases to write the opening out in dedicated phrases – even considering the meter, placing a return after every phrase thus scripting short pauses, longer pauses, and even PowerPoint slide changes.

V. Dangers in Opening Statements

A. Judicial Admission in Opening

While there is considerable lore of the rare case where a judgment of acquittal was granted after opening, this is mostly lore. However, the court does have the ability to consider what is said as a judicial admission. See State v. Adams, 29 Or App 827, 565 P2d 394 (1977) (defense counsel’s admission in opening cured lack of evidence in government’s case); but see State v. Keaton, 15 Or App 477, 482, 516 P2d 490 (1973) (prosecutor’s failure to describe evidence supporting charge is not fatal); quoting Lane v. Portland Ry., L. & P. Co., 58 Or 364, 114 P 940 (1911) (“The opening statement is . . . not intended to take the place of a complaint or other pleading, and how full it shall be . . . is left to the attorney’s discretion”). 

B. Opening the Door to Uncharged Misconduct

Much more common than an admission is the considerable risk to a defendant when counsel creates new issues in opening and brings into issue formerly inadmissible and highly prejudicial rebuttal evidence. See State v. Louis,296 Or 57, 62, 672 P2d 708 (1983) (in trial for indecent exposure, defendant claimed in opening statement that he did not know that his neighbors could see him exposed through his home’s window; defendant’s statement opened the door to evidence of prior contacts where police told the defendant of neighbors’ complaints of his state of undress to prove defendant’s knowledge).

C. Being Boring and Speaking Too Long

Neither is excusable to the jury. They are prepared to hear evidence and long, droning opening statements will not endear you or your client. Better “short, sweet, and on to the evidence.” Watch a few crime dramas. Learn from how much an actor can convey in a one- or two-minute opening statement. Just as Samuel Clemens apologized that he would have written a shorter letter if he had had more time, spend more time crafting a shorter, tighter, opening statement.
