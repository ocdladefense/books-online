Chapter 1. Uniform Trial Court Rules

By DeAnna Horne, Amanda Alvarez

I. Introduction

I’m not one who has ever been big on rules. As a matter of fact, I tend to feel rather resentful of them. That said, I believe the [http://courts.oregon.gov/OJD/programs/utcr/utcrrules.page? Uniform Trial Court Rules] (hereinafter “UTCR”) offer some interesting opportunities for the trial lawyer, if you don’t get too caught up in them. Remember: The rules are just tools that should be used along with all the other ones already in your trial kit. It’s important to view these rules creatively and to bring them out when they suit the best interest of your client.

This is not an attempt to analyze each and every rule promulgated by the UTCR committee and the Chief Justice. This chapter will alert you to the rules that you can use while preparing for a trial and that will aid your client’s cause. This chapter is, of course, no substitute for reading and knowing the UTCR yourself.

First, the entire text of the Oregon Uniform Trial Court Rules is available online for free at http://courts.oregon.gov/OJD/programs/utcr/utcrrules.page. They can be downloaded in PDF format. Additionally, the Supplemental Local Rules (hereinafter “SLR”) for each county are also available on-line through the Judicial Department at http://www.ojd.state.or.us/Web/OJDPublications.nsf/SLR?OpenView&count=1000

II. UTCR Chapter 1, General Provisions

Chapter 1 of the UTCR covers the scope of the rules, the administrative details, sanctions, definitions, time calculations, and guidelines for enacting SLRs. There are some interesting tidbits in the General Provisions, especially in the provisions that deal with conflicts between the UTCR and the SLR.

A. Supplemental Local Rules (SLR)

UTCR 1.030(2) provides that all inconsistent SLR or portions of SLR are simply superseded by the UTCR. Additionally, the UTCR does not require that any notice be given to the offending jurisdiction. Therefore, if your local rules are inconsistent with the UTCR, you may simply ignore them.

However, “when justice requires, a judge may order that an action or proceeding pending…be governed by the previous SLR or practice of the court.” UTCR 1.030(2). If the local rules suit your client, use those. If the local rules don’t suit your client, find an SLR inconsistent with the UTCR so that the local rules are simply superseded.

The promulgation, review, and enforceability of supplemental local rules are covered by UTCR 1.050. The SLR may be promulgated and enforced only for the purpose of giving full effect to the UTCR and for the “prompt and orderly dispatch of the business of the court.” UTCR 1.050(1)(a). Any SLR adopted by the trial courts may not:

duplicate or conflict with the constitutions, statutes, ORCP, UTCR, Chief Justice Orders, Supreme Court Orders, disciplinary rules for lawyers, judicial canons, or ORAP. A court may not adopt SLR that establish internal operating procedures of the court or trial court administrator that do not create requirements or have potential consequences for parties or attorneys.

UTCR 1.050(1)(b). Proposed local rules must be reviewed and approved by the Chief Justice. The rules will only be reviewed once a year, between September and December. Approved changes will become effective on February first of the following year. Remember, the trial courts may not change the local rules without this review. UTCR 1.050(2)(a),(b) and (c).

Any local practice not outlined in a court’s SLR and reviewed by the Chief Justice cannot be enforced. Additionally, the trial court may not impose a sanction for failing to follow a local practice unless the attorney has been given an opportunity to “cure the violation” through compliance. UTCR 1.050(3). Arguably, this rule is in conflict with the portion of UTCR 1.050(1)(b) which states that “a court must incorporate into its SLR any local practice, procedure, form, or other requirement (‘local practice’) with which the court expects or requires parties or attorneys to comply.” UTCR 1.050(1)(b). If “a court must,” than unwritten local practices should be considered unenforceable.

B. UTCR Escape Clause

Under UTCR 1.100, relief from application of either the UTCR or the SLR may be given by a judge on “good cause shown,” if it is necessary to avoid “hardship or injustice.” Always keep this in mind, as this is the UTCR’s escape clause. If the rules will cause “hardship or injustice,” move the court to make a “good cause” finding and allow you relief from the requirement.

C. Definitions

UTCR 1.110 is the definition section for the UTCR. Nothing in it is really odd or complicated.

D. Time Computation

Any time period described by the UTCR is governed by Rule 10 of the Oregon Rules of Civil Procedure (hereinafter “ORCP”). Rule 10 reads:

In computing any period of time prescribed or allowed by these rules, by the local rules of any court or by order of court, the day of the act, event, or default from which the designated period of time begins to run shall not be included. The last day of the period so computed shall be included, unless it is a Saturday or a legal holiday, including Sunday, in which event the period runs until the end of the next day which is not a Saturday or a legal holiday. If the period so computed relates to serving a public officer or filing a document at a public office, and if the last day falls on a day when that particular office is closed before the end of or for all of the normal work day, the last day shall be excluded in computing the period of time within which service is to be made or the document is to be filed, in which event the period runs until the close of office hours on the next day the office is open for business. When the period of time prescribed or allowed (without regard to section C of this rule) is less than 7 days, intermediate Saturdays and legal holidays, including Sundays, shall be excluded in the computation. As used in this rule, “legal holiday” means legal holiday as defined in ORS 187.010 and 187.020. This section does not apply to any time limitation governed by ORS 174.120.

UTCR 1.130.

E. UTCR Remedy Provision

Chapter 1 includes a sanctions provision for failure to follow the UTCR. This is the UTCR “remedy” provision. Remember this provision as you look at the rest of the UTCR and its timelines for discovery and motion practice in criminal cases. Under this provision, the court may strike a pleading or document that is filed in violation of the rules in either form or time frame. UTCR 1.090(1). Also, for “willful and prejudicial resistance or refusal to comply with the UTCR or SLR the court, on its own motion or that of a party after opportunity for a hearing, may” assess reasonable costs and attorneys’ fees against the offending party. UTCR 1.090(2).

III. Chapter 3, Decorum in Proceedings

Chapter 3 covers rules for witnesses, attorneys and judges ranging from proper apparel to the controversial rule barring communication with jurors. It also provides instruction to attorneys about speaking to clients and witnesses and explaining the procedures and proper behavior in the courtroom.

A. Behavior in Court

UTCR 3.010 instructs “all persons attending the court must be dressed so as not to detract from the dignity of court.” This rule is extended to members of the public and allows for their removal from court for failure to comply. UTCR 3.010(1). It is unlikely that you would succeed in excluding state’s representatives or victims from the courtroom using this rule, but in the right case it might be worth a try. Lastly, the rule requires that incarcerated witnesses and defendants appearing for trial wear civilian attire.

Litigants and their attorneys must address adult witnesses, jurors, or opposing parties by their last names. UTCR 3.030. Except in voir dire, jurors cannot be addressed individually at all. Id. As trial attorneys, we must have a conversation with our clients and our witnesses to explain to them the formalities of the court and to encourage their cooperation. UTCR 3.040.

There are several more rules governing behavior in the courtroom of note: UTCR 3.060 requires a criminal defendant to stand during arraignment, plea and sentence; UTCR 3.080 states that the “swearing of witnesses shall be conducted as a serious ceremony and not as a mere formality”; UTCR 3.090 bars undue recognition of or familiarity with “any person in the courtroom” by the judge; and UTCR 3.0150 dictates that upon the reading of a verdict, all persons must remain seated until the jurors have left the room and no one shall react to the jury verdict “in a manner which disrupts the dignity of the courtroom.”

B. Communication with Jurors

Except as necessary during trial, and provided under subsection (2), UTCR 3.120 bars parties, witnesses and court employees from communicating with jurors. Seriously. They mean it.

This rule is rather unfortunate. In other states, such as California, attorneys and jurors are encouraged to speak to one another after trial. Jurors get to ask questions of the attorneys and attorneys get feedback from jurors. In no way should a juror be compelled to speak to an attorney, but once the case is over, the juror really should be given the opportunity to ask questions and offer feedback.

Some judges across the state have been “debriefing” jurors and allowing them to engage in a conversation with the attorneys after discharge. This may be outside of the rules, but I haven’t heard of anyone actually being sanctioned for participating in these sessions.

C. Rules Related to Media Coverage

Also covered in Chapter 3 of the UTCR are rules relating to media, or “public access” coverage, of court events. UTCR 3.180. “Upon request or on the court’s own motion, after notice to all parties, public access shall be allowed in any courtroom” except as provided under the rest of the rule. UTCR 3.180(1). The media is not allowed to cover proceedings in chambers, notes or conversations intended to be private, voir dire or recesses, or engage any juror anywhere during the course of the trial. Additionally, the media cannot cover juvenile, paternity, adoption, custody, mental commitment, restraining order, or stalking order proceedings. At a victim’s request, they cannot cover sex offense proceedings either. UTCR 3.180(2)(a–g).

The judge may deny access to the media only if findings of fact are made on the record outlining “substantial reasons for the denial.” UTCR 3.180(3). If there is a “reasonable likelihood” that the media would interfere with the right to a fair trial, affect the presentation of evidence, or affect the outcome of a trial, the judge may restrict media access to the courtroom. UTCR 3.180(3)(a). Additionally, if the media will place a large financial burden on the court, the judge may restrict access. UTCR 3.180(3)(b).

Should you decide that the presence of the media is not in the best interest of your client, you have the options presented by UTCR 3.180(3). Additionally, the judge can prohibit media coverage of a particular witness’s testimony if the court finds on the record that the media presence would “endanger the welfare of the witness or materially hamper the witness’ testimony.” UTCR 3.180(4).

IV. Chapter 4, Proceedings in Criminal Cases

The UTCR devotes an entire three-and-a-half pages to the proceedings in criminal cases. This chapter covers timelines for filing motions, guidelines for arguing them, procedures for transporting clients and witnesses, communications with witnesses and victims, motions to suppress, and the proper way to dismiss charges after diversion, among other things.

A. Time for Filing Pretrial Motions

UTCR 4.010 sets the time period for filing certain motions at “not less than 21 days before trial or within 7 days after the arraignment, whichever is later, unless a different time is permitted by the court for good cause shown.” UTCR 4.010.

The motions covered by this provision are motions brought under ORS 135.037 and ORS 135.805 through ORS 135.873. These are the statutes that cover omnibus hearings (ORS 135.037) and the discovery statutes (ORS 135.805 through ORS 135.873).

The Oregon Court of Appeals addressed the question of exactly when a trial begins in State v. Iverson,185 Or App 9 (2002),rev den335 Or 655 (2003). The question in this case was whether or not the state had complied with the 15-day notice under OEC 803 18(b). The state argued that a trial begins when the jury is sworn. Iverson, at 12. The court disagreed. Using UTCR 4.010, as well as other statutes and rules providing timelines, the court held that the “trial date [is] the date on which proceedings begin, not an imprecise future date on which the jury is sworn.” Iverson, at 14.

However, remember the escape clause under UTCR 1.100. If the requirement that discovery motions and motions in omnibus hearings must be filed 21 days before trial is going to prejudice your client, you may be able to seek relief under that rule.

B. Communications with Victims and Witnesses

Article I, section 42, of the Oregon Constitution, the “victims’ rights” section, allows victims to:

refuse an interview, deposition, or other discovery request by the criminal defendant or other person acting on behalf of the criminal defendant, provided, however, that nothing in this paragraph shall restrict any other constitutional right of the defendant to discovery against the state.

Or Con Art. 1 § 42(c). This rule requires the district attorney to act as the attorney for the victim and perpetuates the illusion that district attorneys have clients other than the state.

Additionally, Article I, section 43, of the Oregon Constitution discusses a victim’s right to be protected from the defendant. Section 1(b) indicates that a victim has the right to “have decisions by the court regarding the pre-trial release of a criminal defendant based upon the reasonable protection of the victim and the public…” Section 4(a)(b) demonstrates that, at the victim’s request, the prosecuting attorney assert and enforce a right guaranteed to the victim under this section.

In 2008, the primary UTCR dealing with victims’ rights was amended. While (now repealed) UTCR 4.040 required some action by the prosecuting attorney on behalf of a victim or witness (like reporting to the court whether the victim has received notice of a rescheduled trial date), these requirements were made stricter and more formal with the creation of UTCR 4.100.

UTCR 4.100 requires that the prosecuting attorney “must file a notification of compliance as provided in ORS 147.510, in substantially the form set out in Form 4.100.1a or 4.100.1b in the UTCR Appendix of Forms.”

ORS 147.510, in part, requires that: the prosecuting attorney inform the court whether the victim is present; inform the court whether the victim requested advance notice of any critical stage, and if so, whether the victim was in fact notified and provided with victim’s rights; certify that the charging instrument includes the name of each victim known to the prosecuting attorney and that the prosecuting attorney attempt to identify any victim not so included; and indicate to the court whether or not the victim intends to assert a right granted to the victim by section 42 or 43 of Article I of the Oregon Constitution.

The Oregon Supreme Court recently dealt with a violation of victim’s rights and the implications it can have on the criminal defendant. In State v. Barrett, the court ruled that a trial judge is required to grant a victim’s request to vacate a sentence where the judge has found that the victim’s constitutional right to be informed in advance of sentencing was violated. 350 Or 390 (2011). Clearly, even if it is the state that violates the victim’s rights, it can still mean trouble for your client.

C. Oral Argument on Motions

The court is required to hear oral arguments in criminal cases if oral argument has been requested in the caption of the motion or response. UTCR 4.050(1). However, the court is not required to hear argument on motions to postpone trial. Id. In order to request a motion, the attorney must estimate time for oral argument and request official court reporting services. Id.

If oral argument is not specifically requested, the court does not have to hold argument and can merely rule on the pleadings. Don’t forget to request oral argument!

Should you need to request telephonic argument, make sure you are familiar with UTCR 4.050(2), which sets out lengthy rules for oral argument by telecommunication.

D. Motion to Suppress

Rule 4.060 sets out guidelines for motions to suppress and responses to motions to suppress. These guidelines include the amount of specificity required in motions to suppress, time requirements, and the requirements for affidavits based on “information and belief.”

All motions to suppress “must make specific reference to any constitutional provision, statute, rule, case, or other authority upon which it is based.” UTCR 4.060(1)(a). Additionally, the rule requires that the motion be accompanied by a “brief” that is sufficient to notify the court and the opposing party of the “arguments and authorities relied upon.” UTCR 4.060(1)(b).

Under the UTCR, a response to a motion to suppress is not required. UTCR 4.060(4). However, if the district attorney’s office does write a response, they only have seven days to file it. UTCR 4.060(2)(a). The response is subject to the same rules of specificity required for the original motion. UTCR 4.060(2)(b and c).

E. Other Rules for Criminal Proceedings

Chapter 4 of the UTCR lays out rules for defendants to appear in court via “simultaneous electronic transmission” and other rules for electronic filing of violation complaints and criminal citations. UTCR 4.080 and 4.090. For further information on simultaneous electronic transmission, you should refer to ORS 131.045, ORS 135.030 (arraignments), ORS 135.355–360 (plea), ORS 137.0404 (sentencing), and ORS 137.545 (probation violations).

V. Chapter 6, Trials

Chapter 6 of the UTCR governs both criminal and civil trials, and many of the rules have limited application in a criminal practice. However, some of these rules offer a real opportunity for creative arguments.

A. Postponement of Trial; Scheduling Conflicts

Requests to postpone a trial must be by motion. UTCR 6.030(1). Section 2 of this rule lays out the information that must be in a motion to postpone. The attorney must have informed the client, and the motion needs to contain the date currently scheduled for trial, the reason for the postponement, the date of each previous postponement, and whether any of the parties object. UTCR 6.030(2)(a–e).

The rules address motions to postpone when the motion is based on a conflict in another court. UTCR 6.030(3). Specifically, there is a provision for resolving scheduling conflicts. Any attorney who is scheduled to be in two courts at once is entitled to a resolution of the conflict. In fact, the rules require the presiding judges of the affected courts to work it out on the motion of “the affected party in both courts.” UTCR 6.040(1). Remember this rule, and don’t let the courts bully you into appearing in Klamath County and Washington County on the same day! The rules lay out the factors that must be considered in determining which case will be postponed. UTCR 6.040(2)(a–g). In order to make a decision, the presiding judges must consult with one another. If they cannot come to an agreement, the Chief Justice will make a “summary resolution.” UTCR 6.040(3).

B. Proposed Jury Instructions and Verdict Forms

Requested jury instructions must be in writing and delivered to the court and the opposing party. UTCR 6.060(1). Additionally, the court must be provided with an original and one copy of both the requested instructions and the requested verdict form. UTCR 6.060(2).

Uniform instructions may be requested simply by referring to the instruction number and its title. UTCR 6.060(3). If the uniform instruction requires a “fill in the blank,” make sure you have provided the information. UTCR 6.060(3). The rule does not require you to type and print the requested instruction with the information filled in, but it is a good idea to provide the judge with a completed instruction. A turn of phrase can be very important.

UTCR 6.060(4) is a list of requirements for preparing requested jury instructions. The court must consider requested instructions and inform the parties which instructions it intends to give before argument. UTCR 6.060(5). A proposed verdict form must be submitted at the commencement of trial. UTCR 6.060(6).

C. Hazardous Substances, Controlled Substances, Weapons, and Dangerous Instruments in the Courtroom

The UTCR committee and the Chief Justice have found it necessary to promulgate rules that dictate how these substances are handled in the courtroom. These rules may very well be prejudicial to defendants in many situations, but they also provide an opportunity to keep evidence out if the district attorney does not abide by the rules.

A “hazardous substance” is defined in UTCR 6.140(2) as:

any substance listed or hereafter added to the Department of Transportation Hazardous Substances List and the Oregon State Police List of Chemicals and Precursors for Methamphetamine Production and any other hazardous substance designated by SLR.

If a party intends to offer into evidence anything fitting this description, he must file a motion with the court no fewer than 28 days prior to the hearing or trial seeking court order regulating the handling, use and disposition of the substance. UTCR 6.140(1). However, UTCR 6.140(4) provides that “failure to file a timely motion under subsection(1) of this rule may be grounds for excluding any hazardous substance from the courthouse.” It is this provision that you should draw to the judge’s attention when the prosecuting attorney fails to abide by the rules.

Practice Tip

If you are successful in arguing that the state may not present a “hazardous material” as evidence, make sure that you also object to any discussion of the material as “just too dangerous to bring here for you to see.”

Additionally, the court may order a jury view or photograph of the substance in lieu of its transportation to the court room. Moreover, the court has the discretion to appoint a custodian, a disposition expert, or a medical expert. UTCR 6.140(3)(a–d). Lastly, the court may also limit the amount of material to be transported, dictate the type of container in which it is stored, order the disposition of the material, or order other safeguards. UTCR 6.140(3)(e–i).

UTCR 6.150 addresses “weapons and dangerous instruments.” All “firearms, BB guns, and pellet guns intended to be offered into evidence must be unloaded and either rendered inoperable or have a trigger guard installed.” UTCR 6.150(1)(a). If the mechanical operation of a weapon is an evidentiary issue, the UTCR committee intended that there be exceptions granted to this rule. See 1990 Commentary to UTCR 6.150. All guns and ammunition must be kept separate at all times. UTCR 6.150(1)(b).

Knives, scissors and other sharp objects must be in puncture-proof containers or provided with protective sheaths. They must be “rendered harmless.” UTCR 6.150(1)(c). Again, in the right case, you may be able to use this rule creatively to keep evidence out of the jury’s hands.

Section 2 of UTCR 6.150 addresses other “hazardous materials” such as hypodermic needles, chemicals, pharmaceuticals and biological substances. These items must be in containers that provide for safe handling and viewing. UTCR 6.150(2)(a & b).

Only a small, representative sample of controlled substances may be brought into the courtroom. UTCR 6.160(1). This rule is more focused on preventing theft than for the safety of the jury or the court. Drugs must be sealed in a see-through bag and may not be opened except upon court order. Id.Additionally, the controlled substance must be kept in the court’s evidence locker or in the possession of court staff or a law enforcement agency. UTCR 6.160(2).

“Jurors must be advised if any controlled, hazardous, or infections substances or chemicals…present a danger.” UTCR 6.170. Jurors must be further advised on how to safely handle these substances. Id. Arguably, giving these warnings constitutes a comment on the evidence. Therefore, in the right case, you may be able to use the prejudicial nature of these warnings to keep the evidence at issue out.

D. Other Provisions

  • The parties must notify the court immediately if the case will be dismissed or if the defendant will make a change of plea. UTCR 6.020(1).
  • UTCR 6.080 explains how to mark exhibits and requires that exhibits be marked unless not available at the commencement of trial, not reasonably anticipated to be used, or solely to be used for impeachment purposes.
  • Except for good cause shown, UTCR 6.100 prohibits more than one attorney for each party examining a witness or presenting argument on an issue.

VI. Chapter 7, Case Management and Calendaring

Chapter 7 of the Trial Court Rules addresses pleas, negotiations, discovery and trial dates in criminal cases. It also addresses designating “complex cases,” the use of foreign language interpreters, and providing accommodations for those with disabilities.

A. Pleas, Negotiations, Discovery, and Trial Dates in Criminal Cases

At arraignment, the court may either accept a not guilty plea and set a trial date, or it may set a date for a later entry of a plea. UTCR 7.010(1). Plea agreements, negotiations, discovery and investigation in criminal cases must be completed well in advance of trial. UTCR 7.010(2). For defendants in custody, these tasks must be concluded by a deadline set by the court no earlier than 21 days after arraignment, but no later than 21 days before trial. UTCR 7.010(2)(a). For defendants out of custody, that timeline is no earlier than 35 days after arraignment, but not later than 35 days before trial. UTCR 7.010(2)(b).

According to UTCR 7.010(3), the timeline imposed by section 2 requires trial counsel to report a number of things about trial within the same time frame: whether a jury trial is requested, the probable length of trial, the need for pretrial hearings, and “any other matter affecting the case.” UTCR 7.010(3)(a–d).

These deadlines can be impractical in a criminal case. As such, always remember that UTCR 1.100 offers an “escape clause” from all of these rules. If your client will be harmed by these deadlines, and the court is insisting on holding you to them, you may make a “good cause” argument under the escape clause. As a matter of fact, the commentary to the rules from both 1988 and 1990 encourage that the court give relief from the timelines.

Additionally, the 1990 commentary to this rule states that UTCR 4.010 should be read in conjunction with UTCR 7.010. Section 4.010 addresses timelines for filing motions. The commentary suggests that “parties may request that the court decide any legal issue, including motions to suppress, before plea negotiations are concluded.” 1990 Commentary to UTCR 7.010. The commentary does note that the court is not required to allow the request.

B. Designating Complex Cases

Any party to a case may apply to the presiding judge to have the case designated as a “complex case.” UTCR 7.030(1). UTCR 7.030(2) suggest that the criteria for determining what is a complex case includes: “the number of parties involved, the complexity of the legal issues, the expected extent and difficulty of discovery, and the anticipated length of trial.” A complex case has the advantage of being assigned to a particular judge who shall have “full or partial responsibility for the case.” UTCR 7.030(3).

A complex case is specifically given relief from the timelines imposed on civil cases. Nothing suggests that a complex case is immune from the timelines imposed on criminal cases. UTCR 7.030(4).

C. Americans with Disabilities Act Accommodation

The court will provide special accommodation for those requiring it. In order to do so, it must be notified “as soon as possible” but no later than four judicial days in advance of the proceeding. UTCR 7.060(1).

Notice to the court must contain the name of the person needing accommodation, the case number, charges, the nature of the proceeding, the person’s status in the proceeding, the time, date, and estimated length of the proceeding, the type of disability that needs accommodation, and the type of accommodation needed. UTCR 7.060(2)(a–h).

D. Foreign Language Interpreters

The court will always provide interpreters for those needing it. In order to obtain an interpreter, notify the court exactly how you would if you notifying the court of a need for an ADA accommodation. UTCR 7.070(2)(a–g).

Additionally, the interpreter may request a list of specialized terminology that may be used during the proceeding. UTCR 7.080. Even though the interpreter is provided by the court, the list is confidential and is not discoverable by the state. Id.

VII. Conclusion

The Uniform Trial Court Rules are packed with opportunity. Remember that if the rule prejudices your client, the court is required to provide relief from the rule upon “good cause shown.” Additionally, remember that the rules are promulgated only by the judicial branch. They have not been approved by the legislature and signed by the governor. As such, any statute or constitutional provision will trump the UTCR.

Use the rules to your client’s advantage. If nothing else, have fun with them.
