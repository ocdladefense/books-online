I. Rules for Introduction

A. Uniform Trial Court Rules (UTCRs)

Marking Exhibits – UTCR 6.080 provides that “before the commencement of the trial” the parties must mark the plaintiff’s exhibits consecutively from 1 through 99 and the defendant’s exhibits from 101 through 199. Parties may use their own labels, or labels may be requested from the court.

In addition, “parties shall submit to the court at the time of trial a list of premarked exhibits,” except that exhibits “not reasonably anticipated to be used,” impeachment exhibits, and exhibits not available at the trial’s start need not be premarked.

Hazardous Evidence – UTCRs 6.140, 6.150, 6.160, 6.170 and 6.180 govern the use of weapons, controlled substances and hazardous materials in the courtroom. Under UTCR 6.140, a party intending to offer a hazardous substance into evidence must file a motion at least 28 days before trial or hearing seeking a court order regulating the substance’s handling, use and disposition. Under UTCR 6.150, all firearms must be unloaded and “either rendered inoperable or have a trigger guard installed,” guns and ammunition must be kept separate, knives must be sheathed or otherwise protected, needles must be covered or sealed in a transparent bag and “chemicals, pharmaceutical, and biological substances” must be in an unbreakable transparent tube. (Under UTCR 1.100, exception to these requirements may be had “for good cause shown.” An example of good cause would be when compliance with the UTCRs affects a mechanical operation and mechanical operation is an issue. See 1990 Commentary to UTCRs.)

Drugs as Evidence – Under UTCR 6.160, unless otherwise cleared by the court only a “representative sample” of a controlled substance may be brought into the courtroom, heat sealed in a transparent container. Jurors shall be advised whether any substances to be handled in the jury room are dangerous and shall be instructed on proper handling. UTCR 6.170. Pretrial stipulations can be used to prevent introduction of exhibits especially prejudicial to the defense.

B. Discovery Statutes

ORS 135.805–873 regulate pretrial discovery “in all criminal prosecutions in which the charging instrument has been brought in a court of record.” ORS 135.805(1). In addition to requiring reciprocal discovery of witnesses and their recorded statements (see ORS 135.815(1)(2) and 135.835(1)), Oregon’s discovery laws require the prosecution to disclose to the defense “any reports or statements of experts…including results of physical or mental examinations and scientific tests, experiments or comparisons” which the prosecutor intends to offer at trial, and “any books, papers, documents, photographs or tangible objects” intended to be offered at trial. ORS 135.815(3)(4).

The defense must disclose the same items to the prosecution. ORS 135.835(2)(3).

The rules of discovery apply to exhibits offered for impeachment purposes. State v. Young, 94 Or App 683, 767 P2d 90 (1989).

C. Materials in Possession of a Third Party

Asubpoena duces tecum directed to a third party cannot be used as a discovery device to command the pretrial production of evidence to be used at trial without express permission of the court. However, a subpoena duces tecum can be used to command a third party to bring evidence to court for use at trial or for in camera inspection. State v. Cartwright,336 Or 408, 416, 85 P3d 305 (2004). If the court reviews the materials or documents and then refuses access, the proponent must have the evidence marked as an exhibit and made part of the record to preserve this issue for appeal. Wulff v. Sprouse-Reitz Co., Inc.,262 Or 293, 311–12, 498 P2d 766 (1972); State v. Harvey,203 Or App 343, 125 P3d 792 (2005).

ORCP 55 requires that a subpoena duces tecummust be served 14 days prior to production of the evidence. Also note ORCP 55H has special requirements for individually identifiable health information: to wit HIPAA. Counsel should review that subsection prior to attempting to obtain health care records. See Appendix A.

D. Jury View

A jury view may be ordered under ORS 10.100 (formerly ORS 17.230) at the discretion of a court to assist the jury to understand the evidence. Baumbach v. Poole, 266 Or 154, 511 P2d 1219 (1973). A trial court is authorized by ORS 10.100 to determine whether a view of the scene of the crime should be granted to assist a jury in its deliberations; a trial court was within its discretion to deny a criminal defendant a jury view where the defendant made no showing of any need for or even the desirability of a jury view and the court made the view contingent on defendant’s assumption of the cost of the view, which the defendant refused to pay. State v. Henry, 24 Or App 535, 546 P2d 769 (1976).

E. Constitutional Requirements

Pretrial disclosure of exhibits the prosecution intends to offer is in some cases constitutionally required. Under the U.S. Constitution, production is required by the Sixth Amendment’s rights to be confronted with witnesses and to have compulsory process, and by the Fifth Amendment’s guarantee that no person shall be deprived of liberty without due process of law. SeeArizona v. Youngblood, 488 US 51, 109 S Ct 333, 102 LEd2d 281 (1988); United States v. Nixon, 418 US 683, 94 S Ct 3090, 41 LEd2d 1039 (l974); Brady v. Maryland, 373 US 83, 83 S Ct 1194, 10 LEd2d 215 (1963). Oregon courts have yet to consider and decide whether our state constitution requires any greater disclosure than that required by the United States Constitution. State ex rel Juvenile Dept. v. Huskey, 130 Or App 419, 882 P2d 1127 (1994).

F. Preservation of Evidence by Government

The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment “guarantees for a criminal defendant access to evidence in the state’s possession that “is material either to guilt or to punishment….” Brady v. Maryland, supra, 373 US 83 (1963). To support a claim of denial of due process on the ground that constitutionally material evidence was lost, the defendant must make some showing that either the state acted in bad faith in failing to preserve the evidence or that the evidence sought to be discovered will be favorable. State v. Hendershott, 131 Or App 531, 535, 887 P2d351 (1994), rev den320 Or 587, 890 P2d 993 (1995) (citing Arizona v. Youngblood, supra, 488 US 51 (1988)); State ex rel Juv. Dept. v. Huskey, supra, 130 Or App 419 (1994), rev den320 Or 567, 889 P2d 1299 (1995). Where the state has not acted in bad faith, the defendant must show that the claim of favorableness is genuine, not speculation, State v. Hockings, 29 Or App 139, 143, 562 P2d 587 (1977), rev den279 Or 301 (1977), cert den 434 US 1049, 98 S Ct 899, 54 L Ed 2d 802 (1978), and that comparable evidence cannot be obtained “by other reasonably available means.” California v. Trombetta, 467 US 479, 489, 104 S Ct 2528, 81 L Ed2d 413 (1984).

OEC 1004(1) precludes the admission of testimony describing the contents of a “lost” or “destroyed” original recording if the party offering that testimony “lost” or “destroyed” the original. OEC 1004(1); State v. Nelsen, 219 Or App 443, 450 (2008).

Practice Tip

If the government loses or destroys physical evidence, consider seeking the Less Satisfactory Evidence instruction, UCrJI 1030.

G. No Alteration After Introduction

Once an exhibit is introduced, it may not be altered without the court’s permission or control because “[o]ne of the constitutional rights of an accused person is to meet his witnesses face to face and be tried upon the evidence as it is produced in court, and not upon evidence after it has been mutilated and manipulated by hostile parties….” State v. Fitzgerald, 111 Or 455, 466, 227 P 306 (1924).

II. Rules for Admission

A. Physical Evidence Admissible—ORS 41.660

Whenever an object, cognizable by the senses, has such a relation to the fact in dispute as to afford a reasonable grounds of belief respecting it, or to make an item in the sum of the evidence, the object may be exhibited to the jury, or its existence, situation and character may be proved by witnesses. The exhibition of the object to the jury shall be regulated by the sound discretion of the court.

B. Relevance—OEC 402 and 403

The admissibility of physical evidence is limited by OEC 402 and 403. OEC 402 provides that only relevant evidence is admissible. Under OEC 403:

Although relevant, evidence may be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury, or by considerations of undue delay or needless presentation of cumulative evidence.

C. Photographs of Victims and Defendants

In criminal homicide trials, ORS 41.415 specifically provides that “a photograph of the victim while alive shall be admissible evidence when offered by the district attorney to show the general appearance and condition of the victim while alive.”

The court has decided ORS 41.415’s directive precludes balancing under OEC 403. State v. Rose, 313 Or 19, 28, 828 P2d 1006 (1992). See also State v. Nefstad, 309 Or 523, 588–61, 789 P2d 1326 (1990). Arguments under OEC 402 and 403 should continue to be made in cases in which ORS 41.415 does not apply (i.e., in non-murder cases and in murder cases where the photograph offered is not of the victim while alive demonstrating general appearance and condition pre-death). But see State v. Rose, supra (defendant failed to object on grounds of relevance to admission of videotape of victim’s body at crime scene and hospital, and trial court’s conclusion that evidence’s probative value was not substantially outweighed by its potential for unfair prejudice was upheld on appeal); State v. Nefstad, supra (photos of victim alive and dead, offered for comparison purposes, relevant to prove victim killed intentionally); State v. Eby, 63 Or App 35, 39, 663 P2d 778, rev’d on other grounds,296 P2d 63 (1983) (over OEC 403 objection court upheld admission of photos of the victim’s corpse where relevant “to show such matters as how and where the victim died, the location of the wound on the victim’s body and the bullet’s trajectory”).

Other appellate cases show creative use of photos by the government. In State v. Lerch, 63 Or App 707, 666 P2d 840 (1983), the court admitted a photo of defendant naked from the waist up to show he had sufficient strength to strangle the victim.

Neither the United States Constitution nor the Oregon Constitution requires a search warrant or its equivalent before the state may take pictures of or inspect defendant’s torso because, once defendant became a prisoner, he enjoyed few rights regarding his privacy. See Hudson v. Palmer, 468 U.S. 517, 526, 104 S Ct 3194, 82 L Ed2d 393 (1984) (prisoner does not have subjective expectation of privacy in prison cell); Bell v. Wolfish, 441 U.S. 520, 558, 99 S Ct 1861, 60 L Ed2d 447 (1979) (visual cavity search of prisoner does not violate Fourth Amendment); Sterling v. Cupp, 290 Or 611, 620, 625 P2d 123 (1981) (“Those sentenced to prison forfeit many rights that accompany freedom”). Once defendant was imprisoned, he lacked the right to privacy that he enjoyed when he was not in prison. Among the rights that he forfeited was the right to keep his personal appearance – including any distinguishing marks such as tattoos – from being known to the state. The state thus reasonably could compel defendant to remove his shirt so that he could be photographed. The state’s directive that defendant remove his shirt so that police could photograph his tattoos therefore was not a violation of Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution or the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution. The police report itself is inadmissible under OEC 803(8)(b). However, OEC 803(5) permits an officer who testifies in a criminal trial to read relevant parts of his report into the record when he has insufficient present recollection to testify fully and accurately. State v. Scally,92 Or App 149, 151 (1988). Practitioners should consider a confrontation challenge to this conduct if it occurs at trial.

E. Other Written Witness Statements May be Admissible for Impeachment—ORS 40.460/OEC 803(5)

An exception to the general rule of hearsay exclusion is “a memorandum or record concerning a matter about which a witness once had knowledge but now has insufficient recollection” to be able to testify fully and accurately. If the memorandum is shown to have been made or adopted by the witness when the matter was fresh in the witness’ memory and to be accurate, the memorandum may be read into evidence “but may not itself be received as an exhibit unless offered by an adverse party” (emphasis added). Kirkpatrick explains that the purpose of the rule is to prevent writings received under this exception from receiving greater emphasis in the jury room than testimony given on the witness stand. Oregon Evidence, 744–746 (5th ed. 2007).

F. Authenticating Evidence—OEC Article IX (Rules 901–902)

Authentication and identification are aspects of relevancy under Rule 402 and are dependent “upon fulfillment of a condition of fact” under Rule 104. See Legislative Commentary to OEC 901.

Some examples of authentication and identification that satisfy minimum foundational requirements are set out in OEC 901 and include:

1. Handwriting

(a) Testimony by a witness with knowledge that a matter is what it is claimed to be; or

(b) Nonexpert opinion as to the genuineness of handwriting, based upon familiarity not acquired for purposes of the litigation; or

(c) Comparison by the trier of fact or by expert witnesses with specimens which have been authenticated. State v. Cahill, 208 Or 538, 293 P2d 169 (1956).

2. Voice

(a) Identification of a voice, whether heard firsthand or through mechanical or electronic transmission or recording, by opinion based upon hearing the voice at any time under circumstances connecting it with the alleged speaker. State v. Miller,6 Or App 366, 487 P2d 1387 (1971).

(b) Telephone conversations, by evidence that a call was made to the number assigned at the time by the telephone company to a particular person or business, if:

(A) In the case of a person, circumstances, including self-identification, show the person answering to be the one called; or

(B) In the case of a business, the call was made to a place of business and the conversation related to business reasonably transacted over the telephone.

3. Photos

In general, any method of authentication or identification that provides evidence “sufficient to support a finding that the matter in question is what its proponent claims” will satisfy Rule 901. A photograph need not be authenticated by the photographer; rather, a witness with knowledge may testify to a depiction’s accuracy. A writing need not be authenticated by the writer; a witness with demonstrable knowledge may authenticate. Maps, drawings and diagrams, if they are within the limitations of OEC 403, need not be drawn to scale.

Absolute certainty by a witness making an identification is not necessarily required. See United States v. Johnson, 637 F2d 1224, 1247–48 (9th Circuit 1980).

4. Chain of Custody

Exhibits unique and particularly distinctive usually need no chain of custody shown; however, if an item is not easily recognizable or distinguishable from other similar items, proof of the chain of custody may be required. See Oregon Evidence, 667. Chain of custody problems generally affect physical evidence’s weight rather than admissibility. Id. But see Fox v. Olsen, 87 Or App 173, 741 P2d 924,rev den,(1987) (appellate court affirmed trial court exclusion of blood test results where there was no affidavit or testimony from person who transferred blood to a tray from which laboratory results read), and White v. Keller, 188 Or 378, 388, 215 P2d 986 (1950) (exclusion of car parts purchased several months after accident: “It is elementary that before a material object may be admitted in evidence, it must be shown that there has been no substantial change in condition since the time in issue”). Chain of custody issues are within the discretion of the court, so the criminal defense attorney should be prepared to demonstrate chain of custody.

5. Self-Authenticating Exhibits

Under Rule 902, some exhibits are self-authenticating. These include: domestic public documents under seal, officially signed domestic public documents that are certified, officially certified foreign public documents, certified copies of public records, official publications, newspapers and periodicals, trade inscriptions, acknowledged documents, commercial paper, documents presumed authenticated under law, and documents bearing the seal of a tribal government. This rule, like 901, addresses only admissibility. The ultimate decision on authenticity is for the trier of fact.

G. Documents—OEC Article 10 (Rules 1002–1005)

The broad rule of OEC 1002 is “to prove the content of a writing, recording or photograph, the original writing, recording or photograph is required, except as otherwise provided.” If a photograph, for example, is offered for independent probative value (e.g., identity), the original is required unless an exception applies. Rule 1003 provides a “duplicate is admissible to the same extent as an original” unless a “genuine question” is raised as to the original’s authenticity, or “in the circumstances it would be unfair to admit the duplicate.” Rule 1003 is a significant qualification of Rule 1002: in most cases an original writing, recording or photograph is not required. Kirkpatrick suggests one instance of unfairness in admitting a duplicate may be when the original has been lost or destroyed, making it impossible to verify the accuracy of the duplicate. See Oregon Evidence, 933–934.

Rule 1004 is a further limitation on the requirement of originals. When an original has been lost or destroyed, is not obtainable, is in the opposing party’s possession or concerns a collateral matter, other evidence of the contents of a writing, recording or photograph will suffice. Under Rule 1005, the contents of an official record or document may be proved by certified copy (under Rule 902(4)), or may be testified to be correct by a witness who has compared it with the original.

H. Charts—OEC 1006

Rule 1006 allows a party to summarize voluminous materials into charts and PowerPoint presentations as long as originals are produced in discovery.

I. Live Testimony from Criminalists

ORS 475.235 provides that in all prosecutions for controlled substances a certified copy of the signed analytical report shall be prima facie evidence of the results, and if the defendant wants to challenge it, she can subpoena the criminalist to trial.

However, requiring the defendant to subpoena the criminalist who prepared the laboratory report introduced by the state violates defendant’s right to confront witnesses. State v. Birchfield, 342 Or 624, 157 P3d 216 (2007).

If the lab report is received over the defendant’s objection without a criminalist to lay the foundation, it is reversible error. State v. Willis, 348 Or 566, 571, 238 P3d 714 (2010).''See also Melendez-Diaz v. Massachusetts, 557 US, 129 S Ct 2527, 174 L Ed 2d 314 (2009).

K. When the Trial Is Over

UTCR6.120 Governs DISPOSITION OF EXHIBITS

Requires counsel to take custody of their own exhibits at the end of the trial (though some courts have the practice of keeping exhibits in criminal cases).

Requires counsel to return the exhibits to the trial court upon the court’s request if a notice of appeal is filed.

III. Practical Tips for Handling and Admitting

A. Prepare a Master Exhibit List

In advance of trial, prepare a master list of all exhibits you intend to offer at trial. Include check-offs for exhibits having been identified, offered and received for both the prosecution and the defense. This will assure that, for appellate purposes, all of your admissible exhibits actually make it into the record. It will also help you track the evidence in the case and enable you to object when the prosecution begins relying on evidence from excluded exhibits. A sample exhibit list may be found at the end of this chapter.

Prepare a blank exhibit list and keep your own record of every exhibit the state offers by number and description.

B. Prepare Exhibits Before Trial

Before trial is underway, mark your exhibits in the order you intend to offer them. Exhibits of several pages in length should be securely fastened together and marked to indicate a single unit. Maps should bear directions.

Review foundational questions with the witnesses through whom you intend to offer your exhibits before the trial begins. Laying a foundation at trial with an ill-prepared witness can be frustrating at best and potentially fatal on appeal if key foundational questions and answers are lacking.

Once in a while you will have unique exhibits that have to be disclosed to the state prior to trial, and the prosecutor may try to use (or even introduce) your exhibits before you get a chance. To address this, mark your exhibits before trial, so that even if the DA introduces them, they will be marked with a defense sticker. Also, allow the DA to see your materials before trial, but store them at your office until the day you plan to introduce them.

C. Use the Courtroom Clerk (or Not)

Some courts require attorneys to use the courtroom clerk to handle exhibits during trial. This system makes it easier for the attorney to track the status of various exhibits. On the other hand, some attorneys may prefer to control the presentation of exhibits and thereby emphasize certain exhibits.

D. Use Specific Referents

When using your exhibits, do not permit witnesses to describe things as “this much” bigger, smaller, louder, etc., or as “right here” or “right there.” For someone reviewing your case’s paper record, that is no description at all. Use colors and specific markings and refer to them during testimony. Always ask witnesses to identify the exhibit by number when they are referring to it.

E. Consider Stipulations and Limiting Instructions

Prior to trial, consider stipulating to uncontested issues in order to reduce the impact of potentially prejudicial exhibits of your opponent. See State v. Zimmerlee, 261 Or 49, 492 P2d 795 (1972). A proposed stipulation that does not cover all purposes for which an exhibit is offered preserves the exhibit’s admissibility. See State v. Rose, supra.

Also consider limiting instructions when particularly gruesome or frightening exhibits have come in. If an exhibit is capable of prejudicing a juror forced to sit with it while deliberating, ask that the exhibit remain in the courtroom unless and until summoned by the jury.

F. Voir Dire about Inflammatory Physical Evidence

If your case contains gruesome photos, you can ask jurors about whether they have particular sensitivity to explicit or violent content that might interfere with their ability to be objective and fair to your client. If they say yes, this can be a basis for a cause challenge and it is also a chance to preview the concepts to the jury so the state loses some of the shock value when the actual images are shown.

G. Inspect and Use the State’s Evidence

Do not rely on the police reports to adequately describe the physical evidence. Go to the police property room and view the evidence yourself. You can learn all kinds of good information – i.e., in an arson case the police seized a lighter alleged to have been used to start a fire, but inspection of the seized and bagged lighter revealed that it was broken and inoperable.

When the evidence is sensitive, dangerous or potentially inflammatory, it is important for counsel to get familiar with it and handle it before the evidence is shown to the jury. With the court’s permission, you should open exhibit bags, handle firearms and other weapons to inspect and get used to them so you are at ease when the jury sees you handling them.

H. Take Note of How the Evidence Is Stored

For example, storage in plastic bags can compromise fingerprints on surfaces, so if the state seized an object and stored it in a plastic bag, they may have destroyed important fingerprint evidence.

I. Publish to the Jury

If a piece of evidence will help the jury understand the testimony (like a map or photo), you should obtain the court’s permission to publish the exhibit to the jury during testimony.

J. Redact in Advance

If you plan to use a document or other recorded statement and know that a portion is objectionable, or if you know the state plans to use such an exhibit and part of it is objectionable, create your own redacted copy in advance and have it available to use if the court allows part of the evidence in.

K. Use Transcripts to Impeach

Impeachment with a recorded prior inconsistent statement can be a time-consuming nightmare that aggravates the judge and the jury unless counsel either:

• prepares audio- or video-clips in advance and has each segment marked and ready to play; or

• uses a written, certified transcript of the audio- or video-recording.

Tactics will dictate the best choice, but be sure to prepare in advance to impeach with prior statements.

IV. Exhibits as Demonstrative Evidence

Once you have cleared the hurdles of admissibility, exhibits make immediate and lasting impressions. Studies have demonstrated that jurors retain much more information derived from a visual exhibit compared to verbal testimony. Use of exhibits can also help the litigator organize and focus case presentation. Be creative and use exhibits as early and often as possible.

A. Opening Statement

Assuming you have a stipulation or judicial permission, use pictures, diagrams and lists to help set the stage for your case. Tactics might require holding some exhibits back.

B. Direct and Cross-Examination

After a photograph or any piece of demonstrative evidence has been received, pass it to the jury. Delay only diminishes the impact.

C. Closing Argument

Reuse previously received exhibits. Consider enlarging specific jury instructions concerning key elements and defenses.

D. Specific Techniques

Demonstrative evidence (as opposed to “real” evidence – tangible evidence that is historically connected to the case) can be used to illustrate just about anything.

1. Foam core and enlarging for photos, maps and diagrams. Available at photo stores, print shops and legal support businesses. You can even email the exhibits to them for enlargement/production.

2. Maps—from MapQuest, Google Maps, Google Earth, Microsoft Streets & Trips and many others. Google Earth and similar satellite photo programs can zoom in to street level and produce great overhead “scene” photos.

3. Booking photos—easily available from the police and often useful in cases such as resisting arrest, DUII, and domestic violence.

4.Your own photos. There is no substitute for your own visit to the scene. It is almost always helpful in giving you a sense of the space and location, and you can often take your own photos to portray the location in a way that shows the things you want the jury to see: how level the FST surface really was, how close the porn store was to the place where your client was stopped for prostitution, the damage to your client’s vehicle, how dark the scene of the eyewitness I.D. was, etc. [Editor’s note: Also see the OCDLA Investigator’s Manual, Chapter 8, “Forensic Photography.”]

5. Acetate overlays for diagrams. These are simple to prepare and avoid cluttered or overly marked up exhibits. A creative example of this technique was used by OCDLA member Ken Lerner and his investigator Wendy Kunkel in an eyewitness identification case. Mr. Lerner displayed a large picture of the human eye to the jury and added layers of cloudy acetate over the eye corresponding to the witnesses’ various problems in observing the incident. Eventually, the eye was almost completely invisible.

6. PowerPoint and related computer software products.

• Accident reconstruction animation can be prepared as a demonstrative exhibit for use in vehicular homicide or assault cases.

• Genogram to show client’s family history and relevant traits (drug abuse, mental illness, suicide, physical and sexual abuse, etc.) for sentencing proceedings.

• Relationship chart showing co-defendants, witnesses or family relationships – for use in complex cases with lots of parties, witnesses, snitches, etc.

  • Timelines – simple diagrams or complex ones using software like Timemap.

Counsel can arrange a PowerPoint lesson from another lawyer, office staff or professional trainer, or order the materials on effective use of PowerPoint from OCDLA’s 2011 Annual Conference, “Using PowerPoint to Win Cases” by Tom Plunkett.

7. Video and audio devices. Don’t count on the court to have playback devices. Bring your own tested equipment. Rental equipment is available from legal support businesses, and technicians for trial can be hired.

8. Models and replicas. Everything from anatomically correct models to the Styrofoam heads found in wig shops. You can also bring in a can of Malt Liquor like the one the witness drank, or a tire iron like the one the police claim was used.

9. A tape measure or other measuring device. When distance is an issue, bring your tape measure and show the equivalent distance in the courtroom.

Many of these items are more affordable than you think. They are readily available through local retail stores, copy or printing shops and blueprint companies.
