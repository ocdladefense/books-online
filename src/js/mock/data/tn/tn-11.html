Chapter 11. Making a Record and Offers of Proof

By Office of Public Defense Services

I. The Rule of Preservation

“No matter claimed as error will be considered on appeal unless the claim of error was preserved in the lower court and is assigned as error in the opening brief in accordance with this rule; provided that the appellate court may consider an error of law apparent on the face of the record.” ORAP 5.45(1).

II. The Purpose of Preservation

The preservation requirement is grounded in concerns of fairness to the parties and the efficient administration of justice. The adversary should be permitted to present its position, and the trial court should be permitted to understand and correct any error, thereby obviating the need for an appeal. State v. Brown,310 Or 347, 356, 800 P2d 259 (1990); State v. Hitz,307 Or 183, 188, 766 P2d 373 (l988).

III. Statutory Limitations on the Ability to Appeal

A. Guilty and No Contest Pleas

1. Issues that Can Not be Appealed

ORS 138.050 provides:

[A] defendant who has pleaded guilty or no contest may take an appeal …only when the defendant makes a colorable showing that the disposition:

(a) Exceeds the maximum allowable by law; or

(b) Is unconstitutionally cruel and unusual.

Example 1: The Court of Appeals held that a defendant cannot appeal a trial court’s legally erroneous refusal to consider a Measure 11 opt-out because the Measure 11 sentence actually imposed did not exceed the statutory maximum. State v. Anderson, 197 Or App 193, 104 P3d 1175,rev den, 338 Or 583 (2005).

Practice Tip

In cases where defendant will seek an opt-out, trial counsel should consider a stipulated facts trial instead of a guilty plea.

Example 2: The trial court’s imposition of a longer sentence on an amended judgment raised the question of the lower court’s jurisdiction to impose the longer sentence, which was appealable. State v. Easton, 204 Or App 1, 126 P3d 1256 (2006).

2. Limitations on Issues that May be Reviewed

ORS 138.222 limits what issues the courts may review, e.g.,stipulated sentences and any sentence within the guidelines’ presumptive range may not be appealed.

A disposition that “exceeds the maximum allowable by law” under ORS 138.050 does not include a sentence that was imposed by means that violate the Due Process clause of the federal constitution; the colorable claim that the sentence exceeds the legal maximum must relate a statutory maximum. State v. Cloutier, 351 Or 68, 261 P3d 1234 (2011).

B. Conditional Pleas for Appeals of Pretrial Rulings

ORS 135.335 provides:

(3) With the consent of the court and the state, a defendant may enter a conditional plea of guilty or no contest reserving, in writing, the right, on appeal from the judgment, to a review of an adverse determination of any specified pretrial motion. A defendant who finally prevails on appeal may withdraw the plea.

1. In Writing

The conditional plea must be in writing and the writing must specify which pretrial ruling is being appealed. See City of Lake Oswego v. Albright,222 Or App 117, 193 P3d 988 (2008) (holding that a notation on the plea petition that a defendant wish [for] a “reservation of appeal rights” was not sufficient to specify the ruling he wished to appeal, and thus the appellate court could not review the denial of the motion to suppress after the defendant’s guilty plea. The best practice is to make sure that the plea petition and the judgment explicitly state that it is a conditional plea and specify the particular pretrial ruling being challenged. Be sure to cross out any language in the petition that may be inconsistent with a conditional plea. The defendant probably can appeal only issues that relate to a count to which he or she is pleading guilty. For example, if the issue at the pretrial hearing relates to Count 1, and your client conditionally pleads guilty to Counts 2 and 3, the appellate court may say any error is harmless. It may also find no harm if it can’t tell which count the pretrial issue relates to. So if your case involves multiple counts, you need to be clear that your pretrial issue relates to a count that was pleaded to and not dismissed.

2. Possible Advantage

Unlike an appeal from a stipulated facts trial, the appellant does not have to show that the error was harmful. State v. Mastin, 205 Or App 528, 134 P3d 1052 (2006); State v. Dinsmore, 182 Or App 505, 49 P3d 830 (2002) (Dinsmore I). An erroneous ruling is an automatic reversal.

3. Possible Disadvantages

Because a conditional plea is still a plea, ORS 138.050 limits what sentencing issues can be appealed. In addition, be careful what you state in the plea agreement.State v. Dinsmore, 200 Or App 432, 116 P3d 226 (2005) (Dinsmore II), affirmed, 342 Or 1 (2006) (Dinsmore III). In Dinsmore I, the defendant entered a conditional plea to negligent homicide, the lesser-included offense of the charged second-degree manslaughter. Three other charges were dismissed. After the defendant’s successful appeal, the state brought back all of the original charges and the jury convicted. In Dinsmore II, the Court of Appeals held that the three dismissed charges could not be revived absent a provision for that in the original plea petition. The state, however, could bring back the charge of manslaughter, because it had not been dismissed. The Oregon Supreme Court held that the terms of the plea agreement controlled Dinsmore III. The state agreed to dismiss charges in exchange for defendant’s conditional plea to negligent homicide. Therefore, the state was entitled to retry her only on the charge of negligent homicide (not manslaughter as the Court of Appeals had concluded). 342 Or at 9.

On a related issue, the Supreme Court allowed a defendant to withdraw his conditional guilty plea to two charges even though the issue underlying the conditional plea involved only one count. State v. Tannehill, 341 Or 205, 141 P3d 584 (2006) (statute intended to permit the defendant to withdraw the entire plea if the premise underlying one part of the plea turns out not to be valid).

Practice Tip

Stipulated facts trials and conditional pleas have different consequences. Weigh the advantages and disadvantages of both depending on each individual situation. Depending on the wording of the plea agreement, the court could consider the withdrawal of a conditional plea after a successful appeal as a withdrawal of the entire plea agreement. Accordingly, trial counsel should warn clients of any possible adverse consequences.

IV. How to Preserve Issues for Appeal

A. The Record

The court will not look outside the record to find objections. State v. Terry, 333 Or 163, 167, 37 P3d 157 (2001). So discussions in chambers don’t count. If discussions do occur off the record regarding a disputed issue, make sure that you later summarize them for the record and submit exhibits if necessary. Tapes played in court are usually not transcribed, so offer those as exhibits.

B. Timing

Even the best of objections must be timely in order to be “preserved.” OEC103(1)(a). An objection is “timely” if it is made as soon as its applicability to the offered evidence is known to the opponent of the evidence. State v. Keller,315 Or 273, 283, 844 P2d 195 (1993). Timeliness may also be determined by application of statutes, the Uniform Trial Court Rules (UTCRs) or by Supplemental Local Rules. UTCR 4.010, for example, requires filing pretrial motions under ORS 135.037 (omnibus hearing statute) and ORS 135.805–135.873 (discovery statutes) not less than 21 days before trial or within 7 days after arraignment, whichever is later, unless otherwise permitted by the trial court, on good cause shown. Failure to adhere to UTCR timetables can result in a motion being denied as not timely, and therefore not preserved for appellate review.

No objection is required when a party is not allowed to make it. For example, a defendant is not required to object to the trial court’s sua spontemodification of his sentence on the ground that it lacked jurisdiction and defendant was entitled to be present. State v. DeCamp, 158 Or App 238, 973 P2d 922 (1999) (“A party cannot be expected to make an objection contemporaneously with a trial court’s ruling or other action when the party is not on notice of the trial court’s intended action and had no opportunity”); State v. Whitlock, 187 Or App 265, 65 P3d 1114 (2003). Similarly, a party is not required to preserve an issue that arises for the first time when the court issues its written order. McCarthy v. Oregon Freeze Dry, Inc.,327 Or 84, 95 n 6,modified on recons.,327 Or 185 (1998).

C. Specificity

Our state supreme court hasdistinguished raising an issueat trial vs. identifying a source for a claimed position vs. making a particular argument. “The first ordinarily is essential, the second less so, the third the least.” Hitz,supra,307 Or at 188.

Frustration with the Hitz template prompted Judge Landau writing for the Court of Appeals to describe the preservation specificity inquiry as, “Did the trial court have a realistic opportunity to make the right call?” State v. Doern, 156 Or App 566, 579, 967 P2d 1230 (1998) (Landau, J., dissenting), rev den,328 Or 666 (1999).

In State v. Stevens, 328 Or 116, 122, 970 P2d 215 (1998), the court seems to have endorsed Judge Landau’s view:

Although [the Hitztemplate] imparts some degree of liberality to the preservation requirement, it does not transform that requirement into a cursory search for some common thread, however remote, between an issue on appeal and a position that was advanced at trial. Instead, in considering whether an objection at trial raised the “issue” being advanced on appeal, an appellate court must view the facts in light of the purposes of fairness and efficiency that underlie the requirement.

The court in Stevens reviewed a trial-level objection to evidence of Battered Woman Syndrome (BWS). Trial counsel objected to the state’s BWS evidence, claiming it was inadmissible profile evidence—an impermissible comment on witness credibility—and challenged the state expert’s qualifications. The court held those objections insufficient to challenge on appeal; the admissibility of BWS testimony under the framework for admitting scientific evidence having been established in State v. Brown, 297 Or 404, 687 P2d 751 (l984) (“Of particular relevance [on appeal], defendant never objected [at trial] to the state’s evidence regarding the general acceptance of BWS as a diagnosis, its 25-year history, the wide judicial usage of BWS evidence, or the rate of error involved in BWS diagnosis”). Stevens,328 Orat 123.

''Compare State v. Phillips,314 Or 460, 840 P2d 666 (1992) (claim preserved when defense counsel informed trial court that certain evidence was offered for impeachment, but failed to cite governing rule of evidence).

However, despite the acknowledged difficulties with the Hitzframework, the court continues to analyze specificity that way and has used that framework to articulate the best practice for trial counsel. Recently, in State v. Haugenthe court cited Hitzwhen it said that “[a] party ordinarily may preserve an issue for review merely by raising an issue at trial; alternatively (and preferably), a party may preserve an issue by raising the issue, identifying a source for the party’s position, and advancing a particular argument. State v. Haugen,349 Or 174 (2010).

D. Prejudice (Harmless Error) and Offers of Proof

OEC 103(1) provides, “Evidential error is not presumed to be prejudicial. Error may not be predicated upon a ruling which admits or excludes evidence unless a substantial right of the party is affected[.]” An appellate court will not reverse if there is little likelihood that the error affected the verdict. State v. Davis, 336 Or 19, 77 P3d 1111 (2003); Oregon Constitution Article VII (Amended), section 3. Although harmlessness is not strictly an issue of preservation, trial counsel should understand the concepts of prejudice and harmless error to guide the way issues are presented to the trial court.

Trial counsel must make an offer of proof of the evidence at issue. State v. Smith,319 Or 37, 43, 872 P2d 966 (1994); OEC 103(1)(b). Offers of proof are required in cases tried to the court. Offers are required even as to evidence not permitted on cross-examination. State v. Affeld, 307 Or 125, 764 P2d 220 (1988). An offer of proof may be in the form of witness testimony or witness affidavits. OEC 103(2). It is also permissible for counsel to orally relate proposed evidence. Phillips, 314 Or at 466. The purpose of an offer of proof is to enable the appellate court to determine whether the ruling was erroneous and, if so, harmful. Offers of proof should be made outside the presence of the jury. OEC 103(3).

Refusal of a trial court to allow an offer of proof is itself error. State v. Rodriguez,115 Or App 281, 287, 840 P2d 711 (1992).

Practice Tip

A trial court ruling excluding evidence will be sustained on appeal if any part of the offer of proof contains inadmissible evidence. Smith v. White,231 Or 425, 372 P2d 483 (l962).'' See also State v. Thomas,149 Or App 557, 945 P2d 1056 (1997). So if your offer of proof includes, for example, hearsay evidence from different people, segregate the offers of proof and the theories of admissibility. If your offer of proof includes both hearsay evidence and character evidence, segregate the evidence and the theories of admissibility. That way the trial court’s rulings on the various parts of the evidence can be reviewed separately and the client may ultimately prevail on at least some of it.

Similarly, your opponent’s evidence may not be entirely inadmissible. Therefore, separate objections may need to be made to specific portions of the evidence. State v. Brown, 310 Or 347, 359, 800 P2d 259 (1990) (a general objection to the admission of evidence is insufficient to preserve that issue for appellate review if any part of the evidence is admissible); State v. Hasson, 153 Or App 527, 958 P2d 163 (1998) (because defendant made no objection to the latter portion of a 911 tape, the admission of the entire tape was not error).

V. Specific Preservation Issues

A. Pretrial Preservation Issues

1. Demurrers

A demurrer is a challenge to the accusatory instrument. Challenges to a particular sentencing scheme, therefore, may not be litigated by demurrer. ''See State v. Pinnell, 319 Or 438, 877 P2d 635 (1994); State v. Ysasaga,146 Or App 74, 78, 932 P2d 1182 (1997). A demurrer “shall be in writing.” ORS 135.610.

A demurrer to an indictment, on the ground it is not sufficiently definite and certain, is properly raised under ORS 135.630(2). The same challenge to any other accusatory instrument is properly raised under ORS 135.630(6). State v. Morgan, 151 Or App 750, 951 P2d 187 (1997).

Demurrers claiming the facts stated do not constitute an offense may be made pretrial or post-trial via a motion in arrest of judgment. State v. McKenzie,307 Or554, 560, 771 P2d 264 (l989); ORS 135.640. If made mid-trial in a motion for acquittal, courts may treat such motions as “premature motions in arrest of judgment.” Id. at 561. Such a motion “should be denied, with leave to renew it after the verdict.” Id.; ''see State v. Metcalf, 328 Or 309, 974 P2d 1189 (1999) (motion of judgment of acquittal improper after verdict). A claim that a statute is vague or overbroad is considered a claim that the charging instrument fails to state an offense.

Trial counsel should then make a post-verdict motion in arrest of judgment to preserve the issue. State v. Hankins, 342 Or 258, 151 P3d 149 (2007).

2. Motions in Limine

Generally, trial counsel need not object during trial to the receipt of evidence ruled admissible pretrial. State v. Cole, 323 Or 30, 35, 912 P2d 907 (1996).

However, if your trial judge rules your pretrial motion premature (or more likely says something like he or she needs to hear some of the evidence before ruling), a timely objection must be made at trial. State v. Coleman,130 Or App 656, 663, 883 P2d 266 (1994), rev den,320 Or 569 (1995). And, even where a particular argument was made in a pretrial motion in limine, failure to raise that ground when objecting at trial constitutes a failure to preserve the issue properly for appeal. State v. Fanus,336 Or 63, 88 n 27, 79 P3d 847 (2003).

3. Motions to Suppress (also see Section E, Constitutional Issues)

Motions to suppress must specify not only the grounds upon which you are seeking suppression, but also what you seek to suppress. If counsel does not identify the illegally obtained evidence either in the motion or at the hearing, there is no way to show harm on appeal. Consequently, if the initial search was illegal, counsel must identify what evidence was obtained by exploiting that initial search. See State v. Hall, 339 Or 7, 115 P3d 908 (2005) (to show exploitation, defendant must establish a causal “but for” connection between the subsequently discovered evidence and the preceding illegality).

The usual rule that you must separate admissible evidence from inadmissible evidence does not apply in cases where the state bears the burden of proof, e.g., voluntariness of statements. State v. Roble-Baker, 340 Or 631, 136 P3d 22 (2006). In that case, the defendant’s motion argued that at the time she made incriminating statements, she was under compelling circumstances and it became the state’s burden to show that compelling circumstances did not exist at that time. On appeal, defendant was not precluded from arguing alternative points in time where Miranda warnings were required. The court noted that the rule of State v. Brown, 310 Or 347 (a general objection is insufficient to preserve that issue for appeal if any part of the evidence is admissible), which applies to the admission of evidence, did not apply.

The Catch-22: evidence from an erroneously denied motion to suppress may be rendered harmless if the defendant tries to explain that evidence later in the trial. State v. McGinnis, 335 Or 243, 64 P3d 1123 (2003). The same rule does not apply where a defendant is compelled to testify in response to an involuntary confession. In that case, the appellate court does not consider the trial testimony, unless the state can show that the defendant would have testified regardless of the erroneous admission of the confession.

4. OEC 412 (Rape Shield Law) Motions

A defendant charged with any degree of rape, sodomy or sex abuse, or with attempting any of those crimes, who wishes to offer evidence of a victim’s past sexual behavior mustfile a motion requesting admission of such evidence, together with a written offer of proof, at least 15 days before trial. State v. Lajoie,316 Or 63, 849 P2d 479 (1993), upheld the exclusion of evidence for failing to meet OEC 412’s time requirement. In Lajoie v. Thompson,201 F3d 1166 (9th Circuit 2000), however the federal court said exclusion of 412 evidence for violation of the 15-day rule violates the Sixth Amendment compulsory process and confrontation guarantees.

B. Trial Preservation Issues

1. Motions for Mistrial

A motion for mistrial must be made contemporaneously with the allegedly objectionable event. State v. Walton,311 Or 223, 248, 809 P2d 81 (1991). If the incident or remark is allowed to pass, the motion is too late. See State v. Kolbe,115 Or App 268, 273, 838 P2d 612 (1992),rev den,315 Or 644 (1993); Simpson v. Coursey,224 Or App 145, 197 P3d 68 (2008). This is true even in case of prosecutor’s opening or closing statements. State v. Cooper,130 Or App 209, 880 P2d 514,rev den, 320 Or 325 (1994); State v. Neal,143 Or App 188, 193–94, 922 P2d 717,rev den,'' 324 Or 488 (1996).

2. Motions for Acquittal

To challenge the sufficiency of the evidence on appeal, trial counsel must have made a motion for a judgment of acquittal in the trial court. ORS 136.445; State v. Carter, 105 Or App 483, 485, 805 P2d 721 (1991). This is true even in cases tried without a jury. State v. Johnson, 116 Or App 252, 841 P2d 643 (1992); State v. Maestas,113 Or App 744, 833 P2d 1348 (1992). However, in a bench trial, a closing argument that the state did not introduce sufficient evidence of the elements of the crime functions as the equivalent of a motion for judgment of acquittal for the purposes of appellate review. State v. Gonzalez,188 Or App 430, 71 P3d 573 (2003).

Whether tried to the court or to a jury, the standard of proof is the same. State v. Jost/Oregon-Washington Recovery Co.,122 Or App 531, 858 P2d 881 (1993).

3. Evidentiary Issues

When the question to a witness is not objectionable but the answer is, counsel must move to strike the testimony. An objection to the question is not sufficient. Keller, 315 Or at 273.

To move for a limiting instruction trial counsel must have objected to the evidence in the first place. State v. Stevens, 328 Or 116, 970 P2d 215 (1998) (asking for a limiting instruction to prohibit use of prior bad act as character evidence unpreserved where there was no objection to the evidence itself). For example, if the state offers hearsay evidence intended to impeach a witness and trial counsel does not object, the jury may consider the evidence for the truth of the matter asserted. You may not later ask for a limiting instruction.

4. Jury Instructions

ORCP 59H requires trial counsel to “except” to giving or the failure to give jury instructions immediately after the court instructs the jury. Trial counsel must do so in nearly all cases in order to preserve a jury instruction issue for appellate review. (Under the old rule, the trial court’s failure to give a requested written jury instruction did not require an exception.) The only exception, as articulated in State v. Toth,is that ORCP 59H does not preclude review when a trial court fails to give an instruction that was not requested but is required by law. State v. Guardipee,239 Or App 44, 47-48, 243 P3d 149, ''citing State v. Toth,213 Or App 505, 509–10, 162 P3d 317 (2007).A defendant does not preserve an error relating to the trial court’s failure to give a requested instruction where the trial court agreed to give an instruction, forgot to do so, and the defendant did not except. State v. Derrick, 198 Or App 358, 108 P3d 608 (2005).

Always submit jury instructions in writing pretrial or as soon as the need for the instruction becomes apparent. ORCP 59A; UTCR 6.060(1).

Be sure an instruction accurately states the law and is supported by the facts. Hernandez v. Barbo Machinery Co.,327 Or 99, 106, 957 P2d 147 (1998) (“there is no error if the instruction is not correct in all respects”); State v. Moses,165 Or App 317 (2000) (where allegations in indictment include necessary elements and evidence supports the instruction requested, it was error to refuse to give the instruction); State v. Engen,164 Or App 591 (1999) (no error where proposed jury instruction did not accurately state the law).

Practice Tip

Do not assume uniform jury instructions are uniformly accurate. Cf. State v. Foote,154 Or App 227, 960 P2d 900 (1998) (holding that UCrJI No. 2718—failure to perform duties of a driver when property is damaged—incorrectly stated law); State v. Amini,154 Or App 589, 963 P2d 65 (1998) (giving UCrJI No. 1122 about consequences of finding of guilty but insane over defendant’s objection violated Article I, section 11),reversed, ''331 Or 384 (2000); State v. Lopez-Minjarez,350 Or 576, 260 P3d 439 (2011) (holding that UCrJI 1053 on accomplice liability for the “natural and probable consequences” of the intended crime incorrectly stated the law).

5. Stipulated Facts Trials

Do not concede guilt at a stipulated facts trial. For example, do not stipulate to what is in essence a recitation of the indictment. (For example, “Defendant stipulates that on January 1, 2004, he knowingly caused physical injury to Jane Doe.”) The state will argue that any error in the pretrial hearing was harmless because the defendant agreed to all the elements of guilt. Instead, stipulate that if the case were to proceed to trial, the state could produce evidence consistent with the police reports (that you will make sure are introduced as exhibits). You can even stipulate that the state could produce evidence that would prove the elements of the offense. However, don’t forget that the ruling from the pretrial hearing must be harmful. That can be very difficult to show on appeal if the facts were never presented at trial. If it is not apparent how the object of the pretrial hearing fits in with the evidence against defendant, the defendant will not be able to show harm on appeal.

C. Post-trial Preservation Issues

1. Verdict Issues

Make objections to the verdict immediately upon its receipt. State v. Kelley,114 Or App 262, 265, 835 P2d 145 (1992), rev den, 315 Or 312 (1993) (motion for new trial based on inconsistent verdicts ten days after verdicts filed untimely).

Object to the form of the verdict at the time the verdict form is proposed. Id.(Waiver found where defendant stated he had no objection to verdict form.)

When contesting the validity of the verdict, move for a mistrial and a new trial. State v. Vann,158 Or App 65, 973 P2d 354 (1999) (mistrial is an allowable post-trial order for irregular verdict because error was not known until three weeks after verdict). ''But see State ex rel Turner v. Frankel,322 Or 363, 908 P2d 293 (1995) (mistrial enabling retrial on charges is inappropriate where an irregularity in the verdict and unusual jury polling procedure prevented a finding of whether the jury was unable to reach a verdict); State v. Metcalf, 328 Or 309 (motion in arrest of judgment and motion for new trial are the only proper post-verdict motions).

2. Jury Polling

Jury polling must happen immediately after the verdict is rendered. Object if the trial court denies a timely motion to conduct a jury poll, and move for a mistrial. Once the jury is dismissed, the trial court may take no further action to administer or substantiate a jury poll. State v. Vann,158 Or App 65, 973 P2d 354 (1999).

3. New Trial Motions

Let appellate counsel know if you file a motion for new trial. If appellate counsel files a notice of appeal, it will divest the trial court’s authority to hear your motion.

Be sure your motion is filed within 10 days after the entry of judgment. The motion also must be decided within 55 days of the judgment’s entry. If the motion is not decided within 55 days, it “shall conclusively be considered denied.” ORS 136.535(3); ORCP 64 A, B, D to G.

To move for a new trial based upon events occurring at trial, trial counsel must have objected to the event when it happened or when trial counsel first became aware of it. State v. Longoria,17 Or App 1, 9, 520 P2d 912 (l974).

Motions for new trial based upon grounds discovered post-trial must be supported with affidavits. You must detail why evidence supporting a new trial could not have been discovered pre- or during trial with due diligence. The evidence later discovered must be so compelling that, had it been discovered and admitted, it may have changed the result of the trial. State v. Arnold,320 Or 111, 879 P2d 1272 (1994); ORCP 64.

D. Sentencing Preservation Issues

1. The Judgment

Always review the written judgment. Be sure it accurately reflects the sentence announced in open court. Pay particular attention to the imposition of fees and fines, and to conditions of probation. Regardless of what the judge said on the bench, the written judgment controls the sentence. State v. Swain/Goldsmith,267 Or 527 (1974).

2. Motions to Correct the Judgment

Counsel may move to correct any errors in the judgment under ORS 138.083. This is so even if a case is on appeal. Beware, though, that motions to correct the judgment do not “cure” the failure to object to portions of a sentence at the sentencing hearing, and therefore do not revive previously waived issues for appeal. State v. Layton,163 Or App 37 (1999), rev den, 330 Or 252 (2000). Furthermore, if the motion is successful and results in a corrected or amended judgment, be sure to alert appellate counsel to the new judgment so that an amended notice of appeal can be filed if necessary.

3. Presentence Report

Written motions to correct the criminal history contained in the presentence report should be filed prior to sentencing to preserve issues for appeal. ORS 137.079(4); 137.079(5)(c).

4. Merger

To preserve an argument for merger, you must move the court for merger andexplain why convictions merge. A reviewing court will not review a merger argument that was not advanced in the trial court.

5. Consecutive Sentences

When a defendant is sentenced for crimes arising from separate acts, the judge may order the sentences to run consecutively. ORS 137.123(2).

When a defendant is sentenced for crimes arising out of the same and uninterrupted course of conduct, the sentences shall be concurrent unless the court makes required findings. ORS 137.123(5).

To preserve an issue about consecutive sentencing for appeal, you must object to the consecutive sentencing, request findings on the record, and then object to the findings (i.e., not supported by the record). See State v. Racicot,106 Or App 557 (1991). Merely requesting concurrent sentences will not preserve an issue for appeal.

6. Departure Sentences

To preserve an objection to a departure sentence, counsel must object to the fact of and reasons for the departure. Each departure basis must be specifically addressed. State v. Orsi/Gauthier,108 Or App 176 (1991); State v. Guzman,121 Or App 673 (1993).

7. Restitution

To preserve a restitution issue for appeal, it is insufficient to simply object to restitution. A specific objection must instead be lodged based on either the amount of restitution assessed, or the causal connectionbetween the criminal conduct and the damaged sustained. ORS 137.106; State v. Edson,329 Or 127 (1999).

8. SB 936 Credits

SB 936 allows the judge to grant or deny various prison programs and credits. A silent judgment will be interpreted by the Department of Corrections as a denial of all programming, including good time credits. ORS 137.750(2). (“The executing or releasing authority may consider the defendant for the programs described in subsection (1) of this section only upon order of the sentencing court appearing in the judgment.”) So, again, be sure to check your judgment!

E. Constitutional Issues

To properly preserve a constitutional issue, trial counsel should be specific about which constitutional provision he or she is relying on. For example, when trial counsel used catchphrases from United States Supreme Court cases, but he did not specifically cite the Sixth Amendment, he did not preserve any Sixth Amendment argument. State v. Page, 197 Or App 72, 104 P3d 616 (2005). On the other hand, appellate courts have considered issues preserved when trial counsel did not cite to specific provisions. State v. McCoin, 190 Or App 532, 79 P3d 342 (2003), rev den 336 Or 422 (2004) (when defendant argued that “double counting is improper” under state and federal constitutions, he preserved argument under Article I, section 12, of the Oregon Constitution and the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution); State v. Cookman, 127 Or App 283, 873 P2d 335 (1994) (when defendant referred to “fundamental fairness,” he preserved argument under the Due Process Clause). Again, the key is to provide an explanation of the issue with sufficient clarity so that the trial court has a chance to consider the issue and correct the error.

In addition, counsel should specify whether he is relying on the federal or state constitution or both. For example, when the defendant framed his argument in the trial court in terms of the Oregon Constitution, he did not preserve any federal constitutional arguments, and when the defendant framed his argument in terms of the federal constitution, he did not preserve any state constitutional arguments. State v. Baker, 154 Or App 358, 363 n 2, 961 P2d 913 rev den 327 Or 553 (1998) (no federal argument preserved); State v. Warren, 195 Or App 656, 98 P3d 1129 (2004) (no state constitutional argument preserved); ''but seeFreedom Socialist Party v. Bradbury, 182 Or App 217, 225, 48 P3d 199 (2002) (Landau, J., concurring) (positing that court should analyze issue first under state constitution, even when trial counsel relied exclusively on the federal constitution).

When courts analyze comparable state and federal constitutional provisions differently, merely citing to the specific constitutional provision is not enough to preserve an issue. Appellate courts require trial counsel to provide “analytic amplification.” State v. Hayes, 186 Or App 49, 57, 61 P3d 960 (2003), rev den 339 Or 230 (2005) (citing to Fourth Amendment but offering no separate analysis under that provision does not preserve Fourth Amendment issue); State v. Riggs, 143 Or App 427, 923 P2d 683 (1996) (citing Article I, section 12, while making argument based exclusively on a federal analysis does not preserve any issue under Article I, section 12).

Remember—segregate admissible evidence from inadmissible evidence if it appears your offer includes both. If your offer of proof contains inadmissible evidence, the court’s ruling excluding the evidence will be sustained on appeal
