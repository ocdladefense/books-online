Chapter 3. Defenses

By Martha Spinhirne

I. Introduction

A.Theory and Theme of Defense

''' A defense attorney’s goal is to create a compelling story for the jury negating the prosecution’s theory and promoting the defense’s theory of the case as the most viable.

In the context of trial, persuasive storytelling contains the following characteristics:

It is told about people who have reasons for the way they act.

It accounts for or explains all of the known or undeniable facts.

It is told by credible witnesses.

It is supported by details.

It is understood through common sense and contains no implausible elements.

It is organized in a way that makes each succeeding fact increasingly likely.

Lubet, Steve, Modern Trial Advocacy, (NITA 2004).

A defense is organized around the “theory of the case.” A theory of the case should be expressed in no more than a paragraph and should combine the facts and legal issues in a way that compels the trier of fact to vote “not guilty.” A theory of the case should contain these elements:

The theory should be logical.

The theory should speak to the legal elements of your case.

The theory should be simple making maximum use of undisputed facts.

The theory should be easy to believe.

Id. Before preparing the defense of a case the defense attorney must have a theory of the defense/case. The theory will guide the defense preparation.

After developing the theory of the case the defense attorney must develop a theme that justifies the morality of the theory and appeals to the justice of the defense case. A theme best presented in a single sentence which can be repeated throughout the trial gives persuasive force to the legal arguments. For example, in a self-defense case in which the defendant shot the victim, a cohesive and persuasive theme could be, “The same bullet that killed Mr. Jones saved Mr. Smith’s life.”

It is desirable in the beginning of each case to consider as many theories of defense as possible. In the early stages of any case a lawyer will not know what the investigation will reveal. If the lawyer narrows the focus of the defense too soon the lawyer may miss an important defense theory. If several defenses apply to the facts of a single case, then all may be raised, although raising more than one defense can be confusing to the trier of fact. A lawyer must analyze the advisability of raising all available defenses before actually raising any defense and putting the state on notice. Inconsistent defenses are generally a bad idea.

Before formulating a defense strategy, the lawyer must be familiar with the law defining the offenses as well as with general provisions of the criminal code defining criminal liability and parties to a crime. A lawyer preparing a defense in any case should always cite-check the statute of the offense the accused is charged with violating. With today’s computer options cite checking can be done in a relatively short period of time. The lawyer should read and be familiar with all the cases that discuss the statute defendant is charged with violating. In reading those cases, the lawyer will be alerted to both statutory and factual defenses that might apply in any given case.

Additionally, a lawyer preparing a case should review the Uniform Criminal Jury Instruction of the offense. The jury instruction will enumerate the elements the state must prove beyond a reasonable doubt. The jury instruction will also reference other legal concepts the lawyer must be familiar with in defending a particular crime. Finally, the jury instruction will cite some relevant case law.

After the above is completed the lawyer, before formulating a plan for the defense, needs to review all the discovery provided by the state and confer with the client. Listen to your client. Until the investigation is completed the client will have the best information about the offense and the circumstances surrounding the offense. Try not to be judgmental. Treat the client with respect. The rest of the criminal justice system views your client as a liar; you are the client’s only advocate in almost all cases. Your client does not need to be called a liar by their sole advocate. Your client needs to know the reality of the evidence against them and what the likely outcome will be if a case is pursued to trial under any particular defense. Most importantly, your client needs to make important decisions concerning their case and can only make an informed decision if their lawyer adequately and completely advises them of their options and the wisdom of their choices.

A good, thorough investigation is the soul of any successful defense or any successfully negotiated plea. Failure to investigate a case is considered to be incompetent representation. Rodriguez-Moreno v. State, 208 Or App 659, 145 P3d 256 (2006), ''quoting Hill v. Lockhart, 474 U.S. 52, 59, 106 S Ct 366, 88 L Ed 2d 203 (1985). With the information gleaned from discovery, the client, and the investigation, a lawyer can then advise a client of the best possibilities for a defense.

The Library of Defense also has an entire section dedicated to defenses at https://libraryofdefense.org/legal/defenses.

B. Generally Denying the Charge with a Plea of Not Guilty

A defense is anything that prevents the conviction of our clients. A plea of not guilty is a general denial of all material allegations in the charging instrument. A criminal case may be defended based on the lack of proof beyond a reasonable doubt of any element. Many criminal cases are defended in exactly this way.

For example, in a prosecution for misdemeanor assault in the fourth degree, a defendant may successfully assert that there is no proof beyond a reasonable doubt that the act was committed in the county charged (venue); that the state has not proved that it was the defendant committed the assault (identity); that the state has not proved that the defendant committed the assault with the required mental state; or that the complainant did not suffer a physical injury as a result of defendant’s actions.

C. Mental State

Typically the state must prove the voluntary commission of an act with a culpable mental state to obtain a conviction for an offense. Often, the first question a case presents is whether there is sufficient proof of intent, knowledge, receklessness or criminal negligence to prove the crime charged.

Oregon recognizes four culpable mental states: Intentionally, Knowingly, Recklessly and Criminally Negligent. ORS 161.085. Proof of a higher mental state constitutes proof of every lower mental state. A person who acted intentionally also acted knowingly, recklessly, and with criminal negligence. ORS 161.115(3). The mental states relate to the elements contained within the definition of the crime. State v. Rutley, 343 Or 368 (2007). If a statute prescribes a mental state but does not specify the element to which it applies, “ the prescribed culpable mental state applies to each material element of the offense that necessarily require a culpable mental state.” ORS 161.115(1).

For crimes that are not part of the Oregon Criminal Code (Revision of 1971), determining whether a culpable mental state applies and to which elements of the crime a culpable mental state applies can be a difficult and confusing inquiry. Unless there is a clear legislative intent to dispense with a culpable mental state, the state is required to prove a culpable mental state. State v. Rutley, Id.; State v. Rainoldi,351 Or 486, 268 P3d 568 (2011). For crimes defined outside the Oregon Criminal Code, determining which mental state applies and to which element generates much litigation.

D. Specific Defenses (Affirmative and Non-Affirmative)

Oregon statutes and case law provide for specific defenses. These defenses are different than a general denial of the accusation as outlined in the preceding section. Specific defenses relate to a particular offense, or set of offenses, and usually excuse criminal liability if certain conditions exist.

Defenses are divided into two categories, affirmative and non-affirmative defenses. When a defense—either affirmative or non-affirmative—has been raised, the state has the burden of disproving the defense beyond a reasonable doubt. ORS 161.055(1). It is reversible error for the court to fail to instruct the jury that the state has the burden of disproving a defense beyond a reasonable doubt. State v. McMullen,34 Or App 749, 579 P2d 879 (1978).

This chapter will focus on these defenses and generally categorize them as affirmative and non-affirmative defenses.

II. Raising a Defense

A. Either Written Notice or Case-in-Chief

A defense may be raised by written pretrial notice or by affirmative evidence by a defense witness in the defendant’s case-in-chief. ORS 161.055(3); State v.Honzel, 177 Or App 35, 33 P3d 346 (2001) (conviction affirmed where defendant did not file pretrial notice of defense and no evidence of defense introduced in state’s case to support defense so therefore state was not required to disprove the defense. Evidence raised in defense’s case-in-chief did not support that defendant fell within statutory exception and therefore jury instruction not warranted). ButseeState v. Dudley, 29 Or App 471, 475, 564 P2d 711 (1977) (defense of alibi can be raised through testimony of prosecution witness). Although the court in State v. Williams, 12 Or App 21, 503 P2d 1254 (1972), held that regardless of pretrial notice some evidence must be introduced to entitle a defendant to a jury instruction, later cases have indicated that written pretrial notice is sufficient to raise a defense. State v. Keaton, 15 Or App 477, 516 P2d 490 (1973); State v.Siens, 12 Or App 97, 504 P2d 1056 (1973).

When written notice is used to raise a defense or affirmative defense, the defense may be withdrawn from the jury’s consideration only at the defendant’s request or if no evidence in the record supports the elements of the defense''. State v. Brown, 306 Or 599, 761 P2d 1300 (1988); State v. Boldt, 116 Or App 480 (1992) (conviction reversed when trial court refused to give choice-of-evils instruction when some evidence supported defense).

When a defendant relies upon a defense or an affirmative defense, counsel generally should prepare the jury for the defense during the voir dire examination and opening statement. Counsel should also make certain that any requests for jury instructions cover that defense thoroughly. See Uniform Criminal Jury Instructions (UCrJI) Nos. 1101 to 1123.

B. The Constitutional Right to Present a Defense

The constitutional right to present a defense is grounded in due process and fundamental fairness guaranteed to any individual under the Oregon and federal constitutions. The various provisions of the federal Constitution and the Oregon Constitution, particularly the right to compulsory process for obtaining witnesses, the right to counsel, the right to be heard, and the right to confront and cross-examine witnesses provide the basis for a defendant’s right to present a defense.

The United States Supreme Court in Chambers v.Mississippi,410 U.S. 284, 93 S Ct 1038, 35 L Ed 2d 297 (1973), explaining the essence of a defendant’s constitutional right to present a defense, stated:

The right of an accused in a criminal trial to due process is, in essence, the right to a fair opportunity to defend against the State’s accusations. The rights to confront and cross-examine witnesses and to call witnesses on one’s own behalf have long been recognized as essential to due process.

The constitutional analysis for Oregon is similar if not the same as the federal analysis. See State v. Huffman, 65 Or App 594 (1983).

But many of the “rights” to present a defense rely on other grounds. For example, it is often noted that a defendant isentitled to have his or her theory of the case presented to the jury if there is evidence to support it. State v. Lockwood, 43 Or App 639 (1979). Furthermore it is an abuse of discretion for the court to refuse to instruct the jury where there is evidence supporting the defense theory. State v. Davis, 65 Or App 83 (1983). This right to instruct the jury includes the statutory right to have an appropriate written jury instruction submitted to a jury where notice has been given to the court in a timely fashion. ORCP 59B.

The statutory and constitutional right to present a defense will often conflict with other statutory provisions and “legitimate interests in the criminal trial process.” See State v. Beeler, 166 Or App 275 (2000). In those cases where the state has a legitimate interest in excluding certain evidence, the court is required to balance the state’s interest in excluding the evidence against the infringement upon defendant’s constitutional right to present exculpatory evidence if the evidence is to be excluded. State v.Muyingo,171 Or App 218, 15 P3d 83 (2000).

Where the presentation of a defense is at issue, counsel should make a thorough record that the defense is constitutionally required under both Oregon and federal constitutional provisions and in related cases. The following list of provisions and cases is not exhaustive but illustrates the scope and detail of the right to present a defense in a criminal case.

III. Affirmative Defenses

An affirmative defense is a defense established by statute that requires the defendant to prove the existence of a condition or issue by a preponderance of the evidence once the defense has been raised. The state must prove every element of an offense. If the state does not, then the jury need not consider any affirmative defense raised by the defendant. State v. Lyon, 65 Or App 790, 672 P2d 1358 (1983). An affirmative defense does not serve to negate any of the facts the state must prove in regard to each element of the offense to convict a defendant. In fact, any affirmative defense that has the effect of shifting the burden of disproving an element of the offense charged from the state to the defendant is unconstitutional. Patterson v. New York, 432 U.S. 197, 97 S Ct 2319, 53 L Ed 2d 281 (1977); State v. Burrow, 293 Or 691, 653 P2d 226 (1982). An affirmative defense is a separate circumstance, often a mitigating circumstance for which the defendant initially bears the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence.

A. Defenses Requiring Written Pretrial Notice

1. Alibi

Absent good cause, a defendant must provide detailed alibi notice not less than five days before trial. ORS 135.455, but see State v. Douglas, 292 Or 516 (1982).

2. Insanity, Including Diminished Responsibility

Absent just cause, mental defenses must be raised by notice at the time of entering a not guilty plea. ORS 161.309.

3. Extreme Emotional Disturbance

Notice is required at time of entry of not guilty plea or later for just cause. ORS 163.135.

B. What to Argue if Notice Not Given

A failure of discovery reciprocity will deny defendant’s right to due process if his defense is stricken. Wardius v. Oregon,412 U.S. 470 (1973). Furthermore, excluding defense testimony because of a statutory notice or discovery violation may violate a defendant’s right to be heard and to compel witnesses in his favor under Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution. See State v. Douglas,292 Or 516, 520–538, 641 P2d 561 (1972) (Lent, J., concurring); State v. Mai,294 Or 269, 656 P2d 315 (1982). Before a court can exclude defense evidence because of a discovery violation, the court must find actual prejudice to the state and it must determine that no other sanction short of exclusion would remedy the prejudice to the state. State v. Johanesen,110 Or App 348, 822 P2d 154 (1991).

C. Burden of Proof

A defendant bears the burden of proving an “affirmative defense” by a preponderance of evidence. ORS 161.055(2). An affirmative defense must be given to the jury as long as there is some evidence to support it. State v Brown, 306 Or 599, 605, 761 P2d 1300(1988). The court should not weigh the evidence presented of the defense, it can only decline to give the requested instruction if there is no evidence of the defense. State v. Ramsey, 184 Or App 468 (2002).

D. Specific Affirmative Defenses

1. Guilty Except for Insanity

A person is guilty except for insanity if, as a result of mental disease or defect at the time of engaging in criminal conduct, the person lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality of the conduct or to conform the conduct to the requirements of law. ORS 161.295.

Some mental conditions specifically do not qualify. Under ORS 161.295, the term mental disease or defect does not include an abnormality manifested only by repeated criminal or otherwise antisocial conduct. Alcohol and drug dependence alone does not qualify as a mental disease or defect. Ashcroft v. PSRB, 338 Or 448, 111 P3d 1117 (2005). However, the long-term effects of extended use may produce a mental condition that could qualify for an insanity defense. State v. Smith, 260 Or 349, 490 P2d 1262 (1971).

Consultation with a qualified expert is necessary to determine whether a defendant’s particular mental condition is appropriately termed a mental disease or defect.

Defense counsel must give written notice of intent to rely upon this defense. Written notice must be filed at the time the defendant pleads not guilty unless just cause appears for failure to file the notice at that time, in which case notice may be filed prior to trial. ORS 161.309(1) and (3).

If defendant files notice of intent to raise the mental disease or defect defense, the state has the right to conduct its own examination. ORS 161.315. The state’s examination of the defendant may include commitment of the defendant for up to 30 days. ORS 161.315.

Raising this defense requires careful consideration because if successful the result will put your client under the supervision of the Psychiatric Review Board for the statutory maximum period allowed for incarceration for the crime. This could also mean your client can be placed at the Oregon State Hospital for a substantial period of time. Your client needs to understand these consequences prior to raising this defense.

For motion forms, please refer to the OCDLA Criminal Law Formbook, OCDLA, 2012, chapters 4 and 6.

For more information, please refer to Major Crimes and Defenses, OCDLA, 2010, Chapter 30.

Also consult Bassos, Mental Health and Criminal Defense (OCDLA 2010).

2. Drug Cases

In any prosecution for tampering with drug records or frequenting places where drugs are used, any exception contained in ORS 475.005–475.285 and 475.940–475.995 shall be an affirmative defense.

It is an affirmative defense to a criminal charge of possession or manufacturing of marijuana or any other criminal offense in which possession or manufacturing of marijuana is an element that the person meets the criteria for the medical use of marijuana. ORS 475.319. The criteria are:

(a) The person has been diagnosed with a debilitating medical condition within 12 months prior to arrest and the person has been told by the primary attending physician that use of marijuana would mitigate the symptoms of the medical condition;

(b) The person is engaged in the medical us of marijuana; and,

(c) Possess or manufactures marijuana in accordance to the statutory guidelines for the medical use of marijuana. ORS 475.319(1)

It is not necessary for a person asserting an affirmative defense to have received a registry identification card in order to assert the defense. ORS 475.319(2).

For an analysis of who constitutes an “attending physician” under the Oregon Medical Marijuana Act (OMMA) ''see State v. Miles197 Or App 86, 104 P3d 604rev den338 Or 488 (2005) (a physician who merely reviews written records and documents is not an attending physician as defined by OMMA).

For more information, please refer to Major Crimes and Defenses, Chapter 26.

3. Ownership of Forest Products ORS 164.828 puts the burden on defendant to prove he was the owner of timber cut or transported without a permit, or of hay transported without a transportation certificate.

4. Murder Extreme emotional disturbance is an affirmative defense to intentional murder only.

ORS 163.115(1)(a) provides that extreme emotional disturbance (reducing murder to manslaughter I) is an affirmative defense.

The defense is meant to refer to a loss of self-control due to intense feelings. State v.Ott,297 Or 375, 686 P2d 1001 (1984). The affirmative defense of extreme emotional disturbance does not apply to aggravated murder. State v. Running,336 Or 545, 87 P3d 661 (2004).

Both subjective and objective criteria are part of the standard. A three-part test is set out in State v. Counts,311 Or 616, 816 P2d 1157 (1991):

a. Did the defendant commit the homicide under the influence of an extreme emotional disturbance? (Subjective test.)

b. Was the disturbance the result of the defendant’s intentional, knowing, reckless, or criminally negligent act?

c. Was there a reasonable explanation for the disturbance? (Objective test.) In other words, would a reasonable person have been provoked under the same circumstances?

See UCrJI 1307 for guidance on jury instructions.

For more information, please refer to Major Crimes and Defenses, Chapter 2.

5. Felony Murder

ORS 163.115(3) puts the burden on a defendant charged with felony murder to prove that:

he was not the only participant in the underlying crime

he neither killed nor contributed to the killing

he wasn’t armed

he had no reason to believe any other participant was armed, and

had no reason to believe any other participant intended to engage in conduct likely to result in death.

See State v. Burrow,293 Or 691, 693, 653 P2d (1982) (Linde and Lent, JJ, dissenting, asserts that the burden placed on the defendant by ORS 163.115(2) violates due process and fundamental rights protected by Article I, section 33, of the Oregon Constitution).

For more information, please refer to Major Crimes and Defenses, Chapter 2.

6. Obscenity

ORS 167.085 provides for various affirmative defenses for obscenity offenses involving minors. Note: Portions of the statutes defining these offenses have been stricken for over breadth. See State v. Frink,60 Or App 209, 653 P2d 553 (1982); State v. Woodcock, 75 Or App 659, 706 P2d 1012 (1985).

7. Renunciation

Renunciation is an affirmative defense to charges of attempt, ORS 161.430, solicitation, ORS 161.440, and conspiracy, ORS 161.460. The defense should be raised whenever the defendant has completely and voluntarily renounced criminal intent and has done everything possible to thwart completion of the criminal scheme.

8. Sex Offenses

Reasonable belief that a child is above a specified age at the time of the offense is an affirmative defense to sex offenses where the apparently consenting minor is between 16 and 18 years old. ORS 163.325(2).

It is also an affirmative defense if the accused did not know that the victim was mentally defective, mentally incapacitated or physically helpless. ORS 163.325.

Finally, it is an affirmative defense if the accused is less than three years older than the victim for certain sex offenses where the incapacity to consent was due solely to the victim’s age. ORS 163.345.

For more information, please refer to Major Crimes and Defenses, chapters 21–25.

9. Driving While Suspended

ORS 811.180 provides affirmative defenses to DWS:

(a) An injury or immediate threat of injury to human life or animal life under urgent circumstances making it necessary to drive. Although the injury must be to human or animal life (rather than inanimate property), the injury threatened need not be life-threatening. State v. Brown, 306 Or 599, 761 P2d 1300 (1988).

(b) No notice of suspension received as required by ORS 809.430.

Not available if:

(a) Defendant refused to sign a receipt for certified mail containing the notice

(b) The notice was not delivered because defendant failed to notify DMV of change of address as required by ORS 807.560

(c) Defendant was told of suspension by a court, or

(d) Defendant had actual knowledge of suspension (often established through notice of suspension as sanction for failure to appear on a traffic violation, printed on back of the violation summons).

IV. Non-Affirmative Defenses

For a “defense” other than an “affirmative defense,” the state bears the burden of disproving the defense beyond a reasonable doubt. ORS 161.055(1); Hood River County v. Mazzara, 193 Or App 272, 89 P3d 1195 (2004) (defense adequately raised defense of appropriate farm use to justify herding dog barking for six hours and the county offered no evidence to the contrary resulting in reversal of conviction for county’s failure to disprove defendant’s defense). The state gains this burden when noticed in writing prior to trial, or raised by a defense witness in the defense case-in-chief. ORS 161.055(3). However, where no evidence is given at trial in support of a defense raised in pretrial notice, the defense need not be submitted to jury. State v Williams, 12 Or App 21, 23, 503 P2d 1254 (1972),rev den,(1973); State v Davis, 14 Or App 422, 429, 512 P2d 1366,rev den,(1973). A defendant is entitled to have the jury instructed as to the defense as long as there is some evidence to support it. State v McMullen, 34 Or App 749, 751, 579 P2d 879 (1978).

A. Diminished Responsibility/Disordered Mental State

The Diminished Responsibility—or, as more accurately titled, Disordered Mental State— defense allows testimony concerning a defendant’s mental disease or defect whenever it is relevant to the issue of whether he did or did not have the intent which is an element of the crime. ORS 161.300. The defense is available for crimes where “knowledge” is the mental state alleged. State v. Smith,154 Or App 37, 960 P2d 877 (1998).

In order to introduce expert testimony regarding partial responsibility, the defendant must give written notice at the time the defendant pleads not guilty. The defendant may give notice at a later time if just cause is shown. ORS 161.309(2) and (3). If the defendant is not calling an expert witness to testify regarding partial responsibility a pretrial notice is not required. This defense can be established through the use of lay witnesses and other evidence of defendant’s mental state at the time of commission of the offense. State v. Gallegos174 Or App 549, 28 P3d 1178 (2001) (reversible error for trial court to exclude a letter evidencing defendant’s mental state and written by defendant at the time of commission of the offense).

The defense should request a jury instruction on diminished capacity whenever such an instruction would be helpful to the jury. However, courts have found that it is not error to refuse such an instruction. State v. Gallegos,174 Or App 549, 28 P3d 1178 (2001); State v. Stockett,278 Or 637, 565 P2d 739 (1977) (failure to give a partial responsibility instruction was harmless error); State v. Booth,284 Or 615, 588 P2d 614 (1978) (instruction on the state’s burden to prove intent was adequate. A separate instruction on diminished responsibility was not essential).

B. Intoxication

Voluntary intoxication is not a defense, but may be offered whenever it is relevant to negate an element of the crime charged. Evidence regarding intoxication is usually concerned with whether the defendant’s intoxication rendered him or her incapable of forming a particular mental element of the alleged crime. Intoxication is not sufficient to negate the element of recklessness if the defendant, because of voluntary intoxication, is unaware of a risk he or she would have been aware of if sober. ORS 161.125(2); State v. Rabago,24 Or App 95, 544 P2d 1061 (1976).

Intoxication is not a defense to strict liability offenses such as DUII since the defendant’s mental state is not an issue. State v. Miller,309 Or 362, 788 P2d 974 (1990).

Practice Tip

Defense counsel needs to be aware of any potentially prior bad acts evidence the state may try to introduce if a particular defense is raised. The defense counsel also needs to be careful about opening the door to prior bad acts evidence when putting on evidence of any defense. In State v. Orr,197 Or App 327, 105 P3d 904 (2005), the defendant filed a pretrial notice of intent to rely on an intoxication defense. The state sought to introduce evidence that defendant had previously attempted to blame having sex with a 15-year-old girl on his intoxication and then subsequently confessed in great detail to the act. The trial court allowed the evidence without making a Johns analysis based on State v. Johns, 301 Or 535 (1986). The Court of Appeals said it was not necessary to determine if the trial court erred in failing to perform a Johns analysis on the proffered evidence because any error was harmless as the evidence was merely cumulative in light of the defendant’s testimony about the incident during the defendant’s case-in-chief. Orr, Id.

C. Choice of Evils

Conduct otherwise criminal is justifiable when it is necessary to avoid a greater, imminent harm under ORS 161.200. Examples include breaking into an unoccupied house in a remote and isolated area to make an emergency telephone call for the purpose of securing rescue assistance.

See the Library of Defense section on Choice of Evils and Necessity: https://libraryofdefense.org/legal/choice-evilsnecessityduress.

Jury instructions on this defense should be submitted whenever there is any evidence in the record to support it. State v. Matthews, 30 Or App 1133, 569 P2d 662 (1977); State v. Brown, 306 Or 559, 761 P2d 1300 (1988); State v. Costanzo, 94 Or App 516, 766 P2d 415 (1988).

The trial court granted a pretrial motion preventing defendant, charged with escape in the third degree, from presenting evidence for choice of evils defense in case where defendant escaped in order to avoid being returned to county jail where he had been beaten by other prisoners and forced to commit oral sodomy. The rationale of case law that duress, under ORS 161.270, requires danger to be present, imminent and impending, is equally applicable to choice of evils under this section. State v. Whisman,33 Or App 147, 575 P2d 1005 (1978).

Choice of evils defense is available to defendant charged with being ex-convict in possession of firearm. State v. Burney, 49 Or App 529, 619 P2d 1336 (1980).

Choice of evils defense may be available to a defendant in possession of or manufacturing marijuana under the Oregon Medical Marijuana Act if the defendant lacks a medical marijuana card. ''See State v. Miles,197 Or App 86, 104 P3d 604,rev den,338Or 488 (2005) (defendant not entitled to jury instruction on choice of evils where defendant failed to present evidence that his conduct was necessary to avoid a threatened injury).

Defendant was entitled to a jury instruction on choice of evils defense when evidence demonstrated that the defendant put her companion’s gun in her purse because of his disturbed state of mind and her fear that he might use the gun. State v. Lawson,37 Or App 739, 588 P2d 110 (1978).

Where defendant, charged with eluding police officer, was allegedly seeking to avoid assault by police officer and further delay in returning to care for his mother, trial court’s refusal to instruct on necessity was error. State v. Matthews,30 Or App 1133, 569 P2d 662 (1977).

Choice of evils defense is not available in prosecutions for driving while revoked in violation of ORS 484.740. State v. Neubauer, 68 Or App 885, 683 P2d 136 (1984).

Defendant may justify otherwise criminal act by showing it was “necessary as an emergency measure to avoid an imminent public or private injury.” State v. Olson, 79 Or App 302, 719 P2d 55 (1986) (threatened injury may be to property, animals or people).

Choice of evils defense is not limited to actions taken to protect life, but also may be invoked by defendant who has acted unlawfully in order to protect property. State v. Webber, 85 Or App 347, 736 P2d 220 (1987).

Defendant charged with FTA at trial was entitled to jury instructions on defense of choice of evils and duress; jury could infer that defendant was under continuing threat that if he appeared in court his legs would be broken or he would be murdered, and thus that threat was imminent. State v. Boldt, 116 Or App 480, 841 P2d 1196 (1992).

Defense under this section to charge of criminal trespass was unavailable to abortion protesters because defense is inconsistent with other provision of law. State v. Clowes, 310 Or 686, 801 P2d 789 (1990).

Choice of evils defense could not exonerate defendants charged with contempt for violating injunction arising from demonstration to prevent abortions because defense is available only if defendants’ necessary conduct is not inconsistent with other provisions of law. Downtown Women’s Center v. Advocates for Life,Inc.,111 Or App 317, 826 P2d 637 (1992).

D. Duress

Except for murder, an act is not criminal if the actor was coerced by the use or threatened use of unlawful physical force on him or a third person, if the force or threat was of such nature or degree as to overcome earnest resistance. ORS 161.270.

The defense is not available if the accused intentionally or recklessly placed himself in a situation where it was probable that he would be subjected to duress. ORS 161.270(2).

Evidence relevant to this defense may also serve to negate a mens rea requirement.

Duress requires that danger must be “present, imminent, and impending.” State v. Fitzgerald, 14 Or App 361, 513 P2d 817 (1973).

Under evidence that defendant, convicted of armed robbery, had several opportunities to escape, he could not object to jury instruction stating that duress is not available as defense if person intentionally or recklessly placed himself in duress situation. State v. Fowler, 37 Or App 299, 587 P2d 104 (1978).

Where threat of injury, if there was one, existed on the day defendant was scheduled to appear in court and was conditioned on what he might do on that date, threat was “imminent.” State v. Boldt, 116 Or App 480, 841 P2d 1196 (1992).

“Earnest resistance” is generalized standard not measured by defendant’s submissiveness or other individual personality traits. State v. VanNatta, 149 Or App 587, 945 P2d 1062 (1997).

There is a discussion on Duress on the Library of Defense page: https://libraryofdefense.org/legal/duress.

E. Entrapment

A person who is not predisposed to commit a crime, and who is induced to do so by a law enforcement official or by a person acting in cooperation with an official, has the defense of entrapment. ORS 161.275. The defendant need not admit the crime to assert the defense. State v. McBride, 287 Or 315, 599 P2d 449 (1979). Additionally, the defendant is entitled to have the jury instructed on this defense if there is any evidence supporting it. ''McBride; State v. Smith,107 Or App 647, 813 P2d 1086 (1991).

Merely providing defendant an opportunity to commit an offense that defendant was predisposed to commit does not constitute entrapment. ORS 161.275(2).

Because entrapment is not an affirmative defense, it must be disproved by the state beyond a reasonable doubt once it is raised. Jacobson v United States, 503 U.S. 540 (1992) (“...the prosecution must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant was disposed to commit the criminal act prior to first being approached by the Government agents”).

In Theft I and UUV case, trial court erred in refusing to give entrapment instruction. Remanded for new trial. State v. Mitchell, 59 Or App 151, 650 P2d 992 (1982).

Prior dissimilar offense may not be used to show propensity. ''See State v. Barr, 62 Or App 46, 660 P2d 169 (1983) (error to instruct jury they could consider prior burglary as to predisposition to commit theft).

F. Immaturity

A person under 12 lacks the capacity, in adult court, for criminal responsibility. ORS 161.290. Defense of incapacity due to immaturity set forth in this section is not applicable in juvenile proceedings under ORS 419.476, State ex rel Juv. Dept. v. Wicks, 97 Or App 390, 776 P2d 582 (1989).

G. Impossibility

Impossibility generally is not a defense in a case charging an attempt to commit a crime. ORS 161.425.

In solicitation or conspiracy cases, there is a defense of legal impossibility. ORS 161.475(2) provides that impossibility is a defense to a charge of solicitation or conspiracy to commit a crime when if the criminal object were achieved, the actor would not be guilty of a crime under the law defining that offense.

Defendant can be properly convicted of attempted theft-by-receiving even though the subject property was not stolen. State v. Niehuser,21 Or App 33, 533 P2d 834 (1975); State v. Mack,31 Or App 59 (1977).

H. Alibi

Evidence that defendant was elsewhere at the time of the offense can be admitted, if notice is given not less than five days before trial. ORS 135.455. For content of notice and effect of failure to supply notice, see ORS 135.455.

Requirement that a defendant disclose his alibi defense, without the reciprocal requirement that the prosecutor disclose the evidence to be used to refute the alibi, is an unconstitutional violation of due process. Wardius v. Oregon,412 U.S. 470, 93 SCt 2208, 37 L Ed 2d 82 (1973).

This section requires that criminal defendants specifically identify which witnesses will be used to establish alibi defense and, given that reciprocal discovery is constitutionally essential, state must specifically identify which witnesses will be used to rebut alibi. State v. Frye, 34 Or App 871, 581 P2d 528 (1978).

Where defendant complied with this section by providing notice of her intent to present an alibi defense but failed to give proper notice of the witnesses she intended to call in support of her alibi, it was error for trial court to preclude her from giving testimony regarding her alibi. State v. Douglas, 292 Or 516, 641 P2d 561 (1982).

Preclusion sanction of this section excludes only evidence that defendant was at particular place other than location of crime. State v. Redwine, 79 Or App 25, 717 P2d 1239 (1986).

This section does not prohibit defendant’s own testimony about location and activities at time in question, notwithstanding failure to give prior notice of intention to present alibi evidence. State v. Edgmand, 306 Or 535, 761 P2d 505 (1988).

The defense probably does not apply where defendant is charged as an accessory, not necessarily present at the time and place of the crime. State v. Meek, 35 Or App 649, 653–654, 582 P2d 464 (1978).

Statements made by defendant in pre-sentence investigation interview were statements made during “proceeding relating to guilty plea” and as such were correctly suppressed. State v. Hubbard, 113 Or App 587, 833 P2d 1313 (1992)

I. Syndrome Evidence

Syndrome evidence, such as evidence of Battered Women’s Syndrome, can be used to build the defense of self-defense, duress, necessity or entrapment. Use expert testimony to explain typical reactions of similarly situated individuals. Reactions must be demonstrated to be typical and reliable before syndrome testimony will be admitted. State v. Milbradt,305 Or 621, 631, 756 P2d 620 (1988). ''See also State v. Brown, 297 Or 404, 687 P2d 751 (1984); 64 Oregon Law Review 393 (1988).

The 1997 legislature amended the Oregon Evidence Code to allow evidence establishing a pattern, practice, or history of abuse of a person if the evidence is relevant and not otherwise inadmissible. OEC 404(1).

For more information, see Chapter 15, Expert Witnesses, in this manual and please refer to Major Crimes and Defenses, chapters 27–29.

J. Discipline

Parents and guardians may use non-deadly, reasonable physical force if they reasonably believe it necessary to maintain discipline and to promote the welfare of a minor or incompetent person. Similarly, teachers may use reasonable physical force to maintain order; jailers to maintain order and discipline; and anyone to prevent suicide. ORS 161.205; See State v. Waller, 22 Or App 299, 538 P2d 1274 (1975).

K. Self-Defense

1.Defense of Self and Defense of Others A person is permitted to use physical force upon another person for self-defense or to defend a third person from what the person reasonably believes to be the use or imminent use of unlawful physical force. The degree of force permissible is the amount which the person reasonably believes to be necessary for the purpose. ORS 161.205–161.209.

a.Defense of Others. This defense is based on the reasonable belief of the accused concerning the necessity of force and the amount of force needed. However, a person cannot use more force in defense of a third party than the third party could have used in defense of himself. State v. Gibson, 36 Or App 111, 583 P2d 584 (1978).

b.Threat to Use Force. When a defendant is privileged to use physical force under the self-defense statutes, threats to use force are also protected. State v. Lockwood, 43 Or App 639, 603 P2d 1231 (1979) (defendant charged with menacing was entitled to a self-defense jury instruction).

c.Other Non-Assault Cases. Self-defense can be a defense to cases other than assault cases. State v. DeLaura, 75 Or App 655, 706 P2d 1011 (1985) (defendant was charged with disorderly conduct based on fighting. He was justified under self-defense statutes in fighting. The court said self-defense is a defense to disorderly conduct under these circumstances)

d.Limitations. A person is not justified in using physical force if he first provoked the force resisted; if he was the initial aggressor, and had not withdrawn and effectively communicated his intent to withdraw; or if he was engaged in combat by agreement. ORS 161.215. One who provokes or initiates an assault cannot escape criminal liability by invoking self-defense. However, if, after beginning the assault, the aggressor withdraws from the conflict and lets his opponent know this in some way, his right to claim self-defense is restored if his opponent attacks him later. 55 ALR 3d, at 1000; see State v. Ware, 26 Or App 675, 554 P2d 609 (1976).

e.Mutual Combat. The rule is similar for mutual combat. The defendant is justified in using self-defense in a mutual combat situation only if he withdrew, communicated the withdrawal and his opponent then attacked. ORS 161.215(3).

f.Deadly Physical Force. The limitations on the use of deadly physical force in self-defense are stricter: A person is not justified in using deadly physical force unless the person reasonably believes that the other person is:

i.committing or attempting to commit a felony involving the use of physical force against a person,

ii.committing or attempting to commit a burglary in a dwelling, or

iii.using or about to use unlawful deadly physical force against a person and there must be no reasonable way to escape and avoid the conflict. State v.Charles, 293 Or 273, 647 P2d 897 (1982).

iv.A threat to use deadly physical force is not use of deadly physical force and it is reversible error to instruct the jury on the use of deadly physical force when such force was only threatened. ''See State v. Taylor,182 Or App 243, 48 P3d 182 (2002).

2. Defense of Property

A person may use non-deadly physical force upon another to defend property or premises. The person using force must reasonably believe it is necessary to prevent either the commission of a theft, criminal mischief of property or to prevent the commission of a trespass on premises. ORS 161.225–161.229. Deadly physical force in defense of property or premises can only be used when the person reasonably believes it is necessary to prevent arson or a felony by force and violence. A person defending premises has a duty to retreat before using deadly force if the person can do so without endangering himself. A person is not required to retreat if he is in his own dwelling or place of business. State v. Charles, supra.

In determining whether a person using physical force in defense of property has a reasonable belief that the property will be stolen, the court must look at the reasonable belief of the person at the time he acted. State v. Lockwood, supra.

3. Raising the Defense

The defendant may raise self-defense by either giving written notice to the state before trial or by presenting affirmative evidence through a defense witness in the defendant’s case-in-chief. ORS 161.055(3). Once raised, the state has the burden of disproving the defense beyond a reasonable doubt. ORS 161.055(1). If the defense is not raised, the state has no burden to disprove it and it will not be considered by the jury. State v. Keaton, 15 Or App 477 (1973). When raised by written notice, the defense may be withdrawn from the jury’s consideration only if there is not evidence in the record to support the defense. State v. Brown, 306 Or App 599 (1988).

4. Evidentiary Issues

Evidence of the victim’s character for violence may be introduced by the defendant to support a claim of self-defense. State v. Griffin, 19 Or App 822, 522 P2d 399 (1974).See also State v. Bishop, 49 Or App 1023, 1032 P2d 1196 (1980); State v. Lunow, 131 Or App 429, 885 P2d 731 (1994). The prosecution may rebut this evidence with proof of the victim’s character for peacefulness.See OEC 404 and 405. Evidence of one’s character for violence can be testimony about reputation or opinion testimony. When a witness has testified about one’s reputation for peacefulness, that witness can be impeached by the defendant through the witness’ knowledge of specific instances of conduct of the victim, including prior convictions. State v. Williams, 44 Or App 387, 605 P2d 1361 (1980). Reputation evidence is admissible even when the victim’s reputation for violence was unknown to the defendant.State v. Williams,supra.''

Under OEC 405(1), specific bad acts are not admissible until cross-examination unless the character or trait is an essential element of the defense, or is used for proof of motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or accident. OEC 404(1); OEC 404(3). The state may introduce evidence of the victim’s reputation for peacefulness. OEC 404(2) (b). However, it may not show specific instances of conduct until cross-examination unless such conduct is an essential element of a charge or claim. OEC 405(1).

5. Duty to Retreat

Oregon does not have a “rule” regarding retreat. The duty to retreat depends upon the threat posed and the facts of each case. State v. Charles, 293 Or App 273, 647 P2d 897 (1982). The rule is that self-defense is founded upon necessity, and there is no duty to retreat when the defendant is where he or she has a right to be. For instance, one place where a defendant definitely has a right to be is in his home.

While there is no duty to retreat, the danger justifying self-defense must be imminent and there must be no other means of avoiding combat.

Before using deadly physical force, a defendant must retreat unless retreat would sacrifice the person’s safety.

6. Self-Defense Against Police Officers

When an officer uses unreasonable force to arrest a person who is offering no resistance, that person is entitled to defend himself. He is, also, entitled to a self-defense jury instruction. State v. Castle, 48 Or App 15, 19, 616 P2d 510 (1980); State v. Burger, 55 Or App 712, 717–18, 639 P2d 706 (1982). See also UCrJI 1227.

A person is not entitled to a jury instruction stating that any person has a right to resist an arrest made with excessive force, according to State v. Wright, 310 Or 430, 799 P2d 642 (1990), overturning in part State v. Crane, 46 Or App 547, 612 P2d 735 (1980). If a peace officer uses excessive force against a citizen, the citizen has a right to use physical force in self-defense, but he or she does not have a right to use physical force in resisting the arrest. The degree of force permissible, as in other self-defense cases, is limited to the degree of force reasonably necessary under the circumstances.State v. Wright,supra''.

Neither passive resistance nor flight is prohibited by ORS 162.315. State v. Allison, 104 Or App 149, 799 P2d 676 (1990); State v. Crane, supra; State v. Hutchinson, 94 Or App 441, 765 P2d 248 (1988).

A person can be found guilty of resisting arrest by aiding and abetting another’s crime of resisting arrest. State v. Hasan, 93 Or App 142, 760 P2d 1377 (1988).

The prohibition against resisting arrest is applicable to the entire course of events during which an officer effectuates and maintains custody over an arrestee for purposes of charging the person with resisting arrest. State v. Bolden, 104 Or App 356, 801 P2d 863 (1990).

7. Jury Instructions

A defendant is entitled to have a self-defense instruction presented to the jury if there is any evidence to support it. State v. Dahrens192 Or App 283, 84 P3d 1122 (2004); State v. Davis,65 Or App 83, 670 P2d 192 (1983). See also State v. Burns, 15 Or App 552, 516 P2d 748 (1973); State v. Freeman, 109 Or App 472, 820 P2d 764 (1991). Additionally, a defendant is entitled to an instruction on the state’s burden to disprove the defense of self-defense beyond a reasonable doubt. State v. McMullen, 34 Or App 749, 579 P2d 879 (1978). ''See UCrJI 1107–1114.
