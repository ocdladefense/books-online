I. Arrest-level Seizures in Public

A. Probable Cause of a Crime Can Justify an Arrest-level Seizure

1. What is an arrest?

An “arrest” is a seizure that infringes on a person’s liberty to a greater extent than a “stop” and must be supported by probable cause. State v. Morgan, 106 Or App 138, 141, 806 P2d 713 (1991). By statute, an “[a]rrest’ means to place a person under actual or constructive restraint or to take a person into custody for the purpose of charging that person with an offense.” ORS 133.005(1). Under Article I, section 9, whether an officer’s conduct amounts to mere conversation, a stop, or an arrest does not turn on the officer’s subjective motivations. See State v. Tucker, 286 Or 485, 493, 595 P2d 1364 (1979) (“Nor do we believe that determining the validity of an otherwise authorized stop on the basis of the officer’s purpose, or primary purpose, in making it would be either practical or desirable”); State v. Koester, 117 Or App 139, 141-42, 843 P2d 968 (1992) (holding that the defendant was under arrest even though the officer told the defendant at the time he was handcuffed and placed in a patrol car that he was being handcuffed for safety reasons). Regardless of the officer’s intentions, “[t]he duration of the detention or intensity of the officer’s actions can convert a stop into an arrest under Article I, section 9.” State v. Hebrard, 244 Or App 593, 598, 260 P3d 759 (2011).

In most cases, handcuffing a person automatically converts a stop into an arrest. Id.; see also Koester, 117 Or App at 141-42; State v. Quinn, 112 Or App 608, 612, 831 P2d 48 (1992).

The Oregon Court of Appeals has on occasion first determined whether handcuffing a person was justified by the officer-safety exception; if not, it then concludes that the defendant was “arrested” and then determines whether the police had probable cause. See Hebrard, 244 Or App at 598-99 (“Because officer safety concerns did not justify the handcuffing, we conclude that the handcuffing converted the stop into an arrest of defendant.”); State v. Medinger, 235 Or App 88, 93, 230 P3d 76 (2010) (concluding that defendant was “arrested” only after determining that handcuffing defendant and placing him in back of patrol car was not justified by safety concerns); State v.
Werowinski, 179 Or App 522, 528, 40 P3d 545 (2002) (same) State v. Johnson, 120 Or App 151, 158, 851 P2d 1160, rev den 318 Or 26 (1993) (same) Morgan, 106 Or App at 142 (same).

In State v. Ayvazov, 246 Or App 641, 645, 267 P3d 196 (2011), this court acknowledged in dicta that the more persuasive analytical framework would be to acknowledge that handcuffing an individual entails the imposition of actual restraint and is thus an arrest regardless of its justification. It then suggests that the arrest may be justified by one of two exceptions to the warrant requirement: the officer safety exception or probable cause. That is, the first issue is to determine the level of the seizure; the second is to determine whether that level of seizure was justified. That method of analysis acknowledges the actual level of restriction imposed—actual physical restraint.

2024 Update

The fact of prior handcuffing—even if justified by the officer-safety exception—is highly relevant to establishing that the defendant was “arrested” even after handcuffs were removed. Other relevant factors include the number of police officers involved, their purpose in being on the scene, and the kinds of questions they posed to the defendant. State v. Madden, 315 Or App 787, 793, 502 P3d 746 (2021).

2. What is probable cause?

“Probable cause to arrest exists if there is an objective basis for believing that it is more likely than not that the person is committing or has committed an offense. In addition * * * the arresting officer must possess a subjective belief that he or she has lawful authority to restrain the individual’s liberty.” State v. Koroteev, 222 Or App 596, 600, 194 P3d 842, 844 (2008) (citing ORS 131.005(11) (emphasis added); State v. Miller, 345 Or 176, 185, 191 P3d 651 (2008) (“The degree of certainty required to establish probable cause to arrest is whether it is ‘more likely than not [that] an offense has been committed and [that] a person to be arrested has committed it.’”).

2024 Update

Officers lacked probable cause to believe that a hand-to-hand drug transaction had occurred, where officers observed a person holding a small, unidentified item shaking hands with another person in a manner common to drug transfers, while looking around furtively, in an area of town known for both legal and illegal activities. State v. Lebanno, 314 Or App 172, 497 P3d 1280 (2021). However, such behavior may amount to reasonable suspicion. State v. Hollins, 312 Or App 682, 493 P3d 535 (2021).

3. To what extent can officers rely on their training and experience?

Similar to reasonable suspicion, “whether an officer has probable cause to arrest a person depends on the totality of circumstances known to the officer at the time of the arrest, viewed in light of the officer’s experience.” State v. Martin, 260 Or App 461, 317 P3d 408 (2014) (citing State v. Heckathorne, 347 Or 474, 484-85, 223 P3d 1034 (2009)). And as with reasonable suspicion, “an officer’s experience cannot form the entire basis for probable cause.” Id.

4. How incriminating must the defendant’s conduct be?

Although an officer need not “eliminate all possible lawful explanations for a person’s conduct before arresting the person, it must be more likely than not that the unlawful explanation is the actual explanation.” Id. (citing State v. Foster, 350 Or 161, 173, 252 P3d 292 (2011)).

In Martin, the court explained:

“Evidence that a person is in a high-crime area, is engaged in ambiguous conduct, and appears to want to avoid police observation does not give rise to reasonable suspicion to stop the person. If those circumstances fail to give rise to reasonable suspicion to stop a person, so much more do they fail to give rise to probable cause to arrest—the standard at issue here.”

Id. at '''' 472-73. It also clarified that the incriminating nature of the conduct is significant:

“‘The fact that the person’s conduct is consistent with criminal activity is not necessarily enough to give rise to reasonable suspicion.’ State v. Alvarado, 257 Or App 612, 629, 307 P3d 540 (2013). ‘If a behavior is consistent with criminal activity, but is ‘not too remarkable,’ it will not support a stop.’ Valdez, 277 Or at 628; Moya, 97 Or App at 378.”

Id. 476. In State v. Jacobs, 187 Or App 330, 67 P3d 408 (2003), officers were driving in downtown Salem, a “high drug traffic area,” when they saw three men “huddled together” near an automated teller machine. They were looking around “in a nervous manner” when the defendant passed what looked like money to one of the others. The officers suspected that a drug transaction was occurring, so they approached the men and said, “[I]t looks like you guys just did a drug deal.” Id. The defendant denied the accusation. One of the officers searched the defendant and found a plastic bag containing marijuana.

The state argued that the search was lawful because police may conduct a search incident to an arrest if the officer has probable cause to arrest and the search “relates to a crime which there is probable cause to believe the arrestee has committed.” State v. Owens, 302 Or 196, 204, 729 P2d 524 (1986).

The Court of Appeals held that there was “no evidence that the specific location was a site of continuous, hand-to-hand drug traffic. There was only a vague reference to the entirety of downtown Salem as a ‘high drug traffic area.’” Id. at 335. It continued, “[F]urtiveness in the act of engaging in what may nevertheless be entirely lawful conduct does not establish an objectively reasonable basis for a belief that a crime has been committed.” Id. at 336. Consequently, the state failed to establish probable cause.

5. How specific must a description of the suspect be?

In Medinger, 235 Or App at 93-95, the Court of Appeals concluded that the following combination of facts failed to establish probable cause to arrest:

“(1) [The] defendant’s behavior was ‘strange and furtive’ when [the officer] approached him; (2) [the officer] encountered defendant one-third of a mile from the Cascade Inn, where [he] knew a crime had recently been committed; (3) defendant wore a gray hooded sweatshirt just as the suspect reportedly wore; (4) defendant was the only person seen outside; and (5) defendant was intoxicated.”

Id. at 94. That the defendant tried to avoid an interaction with the officer, stopped when ordered to do so, and then refused to answer questions, does not constitute strange or furtive behavior. Defendant had a constitutionally protected right to refuse to answer the officer’s questions. See State v. Davis, 234 Or App 106, 113, 227 P3d 204 (2010) (holding that individuals have a constitutionally protected right not to speak with police officers); see also State v. Brown, 110 Or App 604, 610-11, 825 P2d 282 (1992) (to allow “the police to conduct a search on the basis of the assertion of a privacy right would render the so-called right nugatory”).

None of the remaining circumstances was independently sufficient to establish probable cause. The description of defendant was generic, and defendant’s presence one-third of a mile from the crime scene cannot give rise to probable cause by itself, even if he was the only person seen within one-third of a mile of the crime scene. See State v. Vasquez–Villagomez, 346 Or 12, 25-26, 203 P3d 193 (2009) (concluding that a witness’s description of two suspects, “a taller shooter and his shorter companion—both identified by race, gender, age, weight, and height,” did not establish probable cause because the description was too generic); State v. Gressel, 276 Or 333, 338, 554 P2d 1014 (1976) (holding that officer lacked probable cause based on a generic suspect description and the defendant’s presence one-quarter to one-half of a mile from the crime scene). Medinger, 235 Or App at 95-96.

6. What is the collective knowledge doctrine?

As a final point, under the “collective knowledge doctrine,” the officer conducting the stop or arrest need not have personal knowledge of the facts amounting to probable cause, so long as the arresting officer was acting at the behest of another officer who had both subjective and objective probable cause. State v. Soldahl, 331 Or 420, 428, 15 P3d 564, (2000). (“[O]fficers may direct one another to carry out lawful police activities. However, the state retains the obligation at trial to establish that police action was initiated by an officer who had both objective and subjective probable cause.”).

B. Officer Safety Arrests

Reasonable suspicion that a suspect is presently armed and dangerous may justify an arrest-level seizure on officer-safety grounds.

In Ayvazov, 246 Or App at 645, the court set forth the most persuasive analytical framework for evaluating arrest-level detentions. That court recognized that handcuffing an individual entails the imposition of significant physical restraint and is thus an arrest regardless of its justification. The Ayvazov court then suggested that any such arrest could be justified by one of two exceptions to the warrant requirement: the officer safety exception or probable cause of a crime.

Unfortunately, several other Court of Appeals cases apply a much more convoluted analysis. In those cases, where the level of police restraint exceeds that of a stop, the court turns first to whether the restrictive action was justified by officer safety concerns; next, if the police conduct cannot be justified on that basis, it then concludes that the defendant was “arrested”; lastly, it determines whether the police had probable cause to justify the arrest. See Hebrard, 244 Or App at 598-99 (“Because officer safety concerns did not justify the handcuffing, we conclude that the handcuffing converted the stop into an arrest of defendant.”); Medinger, 235 Or App at 93 (concluding that defendant was “arrested” only after determining that handcuffing defendant and placing him in back of patrol car was not justified by safety concerns); Werowinski, 179 Or App at 528 (same); State v. Johnson, 120 Or App 151, 158, 851 P2d 1160, rev den 318 Or 26 (1993) (same); Morgan, 106 Or App at 141, (same).

1. When is handcuffing for officer-safety reasons justified?

Absent credible evidence that the suspect is armed with a deadly weapon and presenting an immediate risk of serious physical injury to those present, handcuffing is unreasonable. That is, handcuffing is not authorized without probable cause unless the circumstances present an extreme danger to law enforcement or others present. For example, in State v. Rickard, 150 Or App 517, 526, 947 P2d 215, rev den, 326 Or 234, 952 P2d 61 (1997), during a “high risk,” night-time stop, the police reasonably believed that an occupant of the suspect vehicle had brandished a gun at the passengers of another car, the officer safety exception permitted police to order the occupants out of the vehicle at gunpoint, handcuff them, and empty their pockets. And in State v. Barnett, 132 Or App 520, 524, 888 P2d 1064 (1995), during the execution of a residential search warrant for controlled substances, police had probable cause to believe that the homeowner was paranoid, armed and dangerous, and that guns were readily available in the living room, consequently it was reasonable to enter the residence with guns drawn and handcuff all occupants prior to searching the residence.

By contrast, in Johnson, 120 Or App 151, rev den 318 Or 26 (1993), an officer was executing an arrest warrant in a motel room when he saw a man lying on the bed, fully clothed, with one of his hands under a pillow. Id. at 153-54. The officer knew that the man had a history of weapons charges. The man smelled like a “meth lab.” Id. The officer handcuffed him for officer-safety purposes. Id.

This court held that although the officer reasonably believed that the man was armed and dangerous, id. at 157, immediately handcuffing him without first performing a pat-down search violated Article I, section 9.

2. When is handcuffing justified based on the conduct of defendant’s companions?

The case law suggests that unless police reasonably believe that a gun is involved, they cannot detain an entire group based on the conduct of a member in the group. See State v. Hendricks, 213 Or App 360, 367, 160 P3d 1014 (2007) (officer responding to an armed robbery in progress, where one of the suspects was wanted, and the officer could not determine which of two suspects had the gun, it was reasonable to sequentially order one man to sit down and then order the other man to put his hands on the counter).

II. Arrest-level Seizures in a Private Residence

Probable cause plus exigent circumstances are required before police can enter a residence to make an arrest or order a defendant to come out of the residence.

Absent hot pursuit or some other pre-existing, exigent circumstances, police officers with probable cause to arrest a suspect are prohibited from entering a suspect’s house without a warrant in order to make a routine felony arrest of the suspect. State v. Olson, 287 Or 157, 165, 598 P2d 670 (1979). In such circumstances, an arrest warrant, based on probable cause, is constitutionally required. Id.

Similarly, “Permitting the police, without a warrant based on probable cause or probable cause plus exigent circumstances, to seize a person inside his house by ordering that person to emerge from his house would be inconsistent with that well-established constitutional principle [from Olson].” State v. Dahl, 323 Or 199, 209, 915 P2d 979 (1996); State v. Hudson, 253 Or App 327, 342, 290 P3d 868 (2012) (applying rule and concluding, “Whether [police] chose to physically enter the house to secure the premises or to accomplish the same goal by simply instructing defendant to come out of the house, their actions were legally permissible” because probable cause and exigent circumstances existed).

Article I, section 9, plainly lacks a “crime scene” exception separate from the exigent circumstance exception. Thus, police violate the state constitution if they enter a house without a warrant to seize a person therein in order to “freeze the scene” unless the entry is justified under some other exception to the warrant requirement. State v. Matsen/Wilson, 287 Or 581, 589, 601 P2d 784 (1979) (“We conclude that ‘securing the premises’ prior to arrests and without a warrant is not lawful absent unforeseen and compelling circumstances which would independently justify a warrantless entry and search.”).
