Warrantless Seizures

Introduction

The vast majority of all search and seizure motions to suppress turn on two legal questions: Did the police “search” or “seize” the defendant? If so, was the search or seizure justified?

Chapter 1 dealt with the first of those inquires while Chapters 2 and 3 focus on the second, specifically on situations where the police failed to obtain a warrant prior to conducting the search or seizure.

A search or seizure conducted under a judicially authorized warrant is presumptively justified; whereas, a warrantless search or seizure is presumptively unconstitutional. For that reason, if the police searched or seized your client without a warrant, the state bears the burden of establishing that a warrant was not necessary. That is, upon the filing of a timely motion to suppress, the state must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the police action was justified by “one of the few specifically established and carefully delineated exceptions to the warrant requirement.” State v. Bridewell, 306 Or 231, 235, 759 P2d 1054 (1988).

As described in Chapter 1, there are generally three types of police-citizen encounters, and they very in the degree to which the police infringe upon a person’s freedom of movement. The first is “mere conversation,” which does not qualify as a seizure under Article I, section 9. The second two are both categories of “seizures”—the first being a “stop,” which occurs when a state actor temporarily restrains a person’s liberty through either physical force or some other “show of authority.” State v. Ashbaugh, 349 Or 297, 308-09, 244 P3d 360 (2010). Stops require reasonable suspicion that the defendant has committed a crime. State v. Newton, 286 Or App 274, 279, 398 P3d 390 (2017).

The second type of seizure is an “arrest,” which involves “protracted custodial restraint and require[s] probable cause.” State v. Fair, 353 Or 588, 593, 302 P3d 417 (2013). The exceptions to the warrant requirement that permit police to make an arrest require the state to meet more demanding standards. The following chapter focuses on different types of seizures and the various ways to challenge their legality.

Chapter 2A

Criminal Stops: Warrantless Seizures of People

I. Stop-level Seizures of People in Public

A. When can police “stop” a person on the street without a warrant?

The police must have “reasonable suspicion” that a particular person “has committed or is about to commit a crime” to justify a stop-level seizure for the purpose of questioning the person about the suspected crime. State v. Belt, 325 Or 6, 12, 932 P2d 1177 (1997).

Article I, section 9, typically requires that the level of justification for a seizure correlate with the extent to which the seizure infringes on a citizen’s liberty. State v. Fair, 353 Or 588, 603, 302 P3d 417 (2013). Thus, to arrest a citizen, an officer must have probable cause; however, a temporary detention for investigation—a “stop”—requires a lower standard: reasonable suspicion of criminal activity. Id.

Often police will not intend to stop a person. Instead, police will attempt to engage a person in mere conversation, but by their words or conduct they convert the encounter into a stop. See Section 1C (defining a stop and outlining the myriad ways in which a police officer may effect a stop). Whether a police-citizen interaction rises beyond “mere conversation” to the level of a seizure under Article I, section 9, depends on whether a reasonable person would believe that the officer has intentionally and significantly restricted, interfered with, or otherwise deprived a citizen of his or her liberty or freedom of movement. State v. Ashbaugh, 349 Or 297, 316, 244 P3d 360 (2010). “What distinguishes a seizure (either a stop or an arrest) from a constitutionally insignificant police-citizen encounter ‘is the imposition, either by physical force or through some “show of authority,” of some restraint on the individual’s liberty.’” State v. Backstrand, 354 Or 392, 399, 313 P3d 1084 (2013) ' (citing Ashbaugh, 239 Or at 309).' “A constitutionally significant ‘show of authority’ can be inferred from ‘the content of the questions [asked by a police officer], the manner of asking them, or other actions that police take (along with the circumstances in which they take them).’” State v. Charles, 263 Or App 578, 583, 331 P3d 1012 (2014) (quoting Backstrand, 354 Or at 412).

To prove that an officer has reasonable suspicion of a crime, the state must show that the objective facts known to the officer would permit a reasonable person to conclude that the defendant was engaged in criminal activity. Belt, 325 Or at 12. Thus, to determine whether police lawfully interfered with a person’s liberty or freedom of movement such that it creates a stop, the trial court should scrutinize the officer’s testimony at the suppression hearing for specific and articulable facts sufficient to give rise to an objectively reasonable inference that the suspect has “committed or was about to commit a specific crime or type of crime.” State v. Maciel-Figueroa, 361 Or 163, 165, 182 (2017) (emphasis added).

Said another way, “For a court to determine that an investigative stop was lawful under Article I, section 9, the court (1) must find that the officers actually suspected that the stopped person had committed a specific crime or type of crime, or was about to commit a specific crime or type of crime, and (2) must conclude, based on the record, that the officers’ subjective belief—their suspicion—was objectively reasonable under the totality of the circumstances existing at the time of the stop.” Id. at 182.

B. To what extent can police rely on training and experience?

An officer’s experience is “relevant to what inferences the officer may draw from the circumstances. But, an officer’s experience cannot form the entire basis for reasonable suspicion.” State v. Martin, 260 Or App 461, 470, 317 P3d 408 (2014); see State v. Alvarado, 257 Or App 612, 631, 307 P3d 540 (2013) (“[T]he phrase ‘training and experience’ is not a magical incantation with the power to imbue speculation, stereotype, or pseudoscience with an impenetrable armor of veracity.”). Accordingly, an officer’s intuitive sixth sense, instinct, and experience alone are insufficient. State v. Valdez, 277 Or 621, 628, 561 P2d 1006 (1977). “The trial court must determine whether the standard of reasonable suspicion has been met by the objective test of observable facts, not by reliance on the special intuitions of the officer.” Id. at 629.

“An officer’s training and experience may help an officer interpret a specific and articulable fact in a given situation or event, but training and experience is not, in and of itself, a specific and articulable fact that can provide the ‘something more’ to establish reasonable suspicion.” State v. Schmitz, 299 Or App 170, 178, 448 P3d 699 (2019) (quoting State v. Davis, 286 Or App 528, 534, 400 P3d 994 (2017) (holding that “something more” beyond impairment by a controlled substance was necessary to establish reasonable suspicion that the defendant possessed a controlled substance)).

C. Can reasonable suspicion be based on the actions of the defendant’s companions?

The officer’s suspicion must be particularized to the person based on that person’s conduct, demeanor, or appearance—not that of the person’s companions. State v. Kingsmith, 256 Or App 762, 769, 302 P3d 471 (2013); State v. Miglavs, 337 Or 1, 12, 90 P3d 607 (2004) (“A police officer’s suspicion must be particularized to the individual based on the individual’s own conduct.”).

D. Must the officer personally observe the suspicious conduct?

Reliable citizen reports may “furnish the required ‘specific and articulable facts’ to support an inference that criminal activity is afoot.” State v. Goss, 219 Or App 645, 650, 184 P3d 115, rev den, 345 Or 94 (2008).

1. Reliability of Informant

Where the officer’s suspicion is based solely on a citizen informant’s report, that report must contain some indicia of reliability. State v. Shumway, 124 Or App 131, 133, 861 P2d 384 (1993). Three factors to consider in determining the reliability of a citizen informant’s report are: (1) whether the informant is exposed to possible criminal and civil prosecution if the report is false. That factor is satisfied if the informant gives his or her name to law enforcement or if the informant delivers the information to the officer in person. State v. Bybee, 131 Or App 492, 495, 884 P2d 906 (1994). (2) Whether the report is based on the personal observations of the informant. An officer may infer that the information is based on the informant’s personal observations if the information contains sufficient detail to infer that fact. Shumway, 124 Or App at 136. (3) Whether the officer’s own observations corroborated the informant’s information. The officer may corroborate the report either by observing the illegal activity or by finding the person, the location, or the circumstances substantially as described by the informant. Bybee, 131 Or App at 495.

The totality of the circumstances also includes what an officer does not observe. If an officer observes activity that contradicts an informant’s report, that contradiction can weigh against a finding of reasonable suspicion. State v. Hames, 223 Or App 624, 635, 196 P3d 88 (2008). Thus, if the scene observed upon the officer’s arrival is not “substantially as described by the informant” that fact cuts against the reliability of the report. Id.

2. Content of Informant’s Report

The fact that a reliable report is made to police is not alone sufficient to create reasonable suspicion; the court must assess the report’s content to determine whether the facts contained within it reasonably create a suspicion of criminal activity. See Lichty, 313 Or at 585 (assessing separately whether informant was reliable and whether her report established objective reasonable suspicion).

State v. Greer, 93 Or App 409, 411-12, 763 P2d 158 (1988), is illustrative. In Greer, police received a citizen report that two “suspicious people” were in a “suspicious vehicle” at a fast-food restaurant’s drive-up window at 3:00 a.m. The business was closed, the restaurant and its driveway were unlit, and no employees were present. The responding officers knew that burglars sometimes gained entry through drive-up windows. The Greer court held that the informant’s tip, even if reliable, was insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion because it did not provide any specific and articulable facts that would connect the defendant’s presence at the drive-up window to criminal activity. “[T]he citizen informant’s report that ‘two suspicious’ persons in a ‘suspicious’ vehicle were at that location did not supply a factual basis for a stop; it merely alerted the police to the situation.” 93 Or App at 412; see also Maciel-Figueroa, 361 Or at 184 (holding that an informant’s report did not recite facts sufficient to give rise to reasonable suspicion that the defendant had committed a crime).

Reasonable suspicion can also be based on an informant’s conclusory report, but only if the conclusion is within the common experience of the average citizen. Lichty, 313 Or at 581. In Lichty, the defendant argued that a witness’s statement to police, “I just found [the defendant’s] wallet * * * a bag of coke fell out of it,” could not establish reasonable suspicion because it was conclusory. The court held that because “members of society have a general knowledge regarding the appearance of cocaine,” the informant’s statement was merely shorthand for “[I] saw a transparent bag, small enough to be put in a wallet, that contained a white powdery substance.” Id. at 585. That statement combined with the officer’s expertise concerning how illegal drugs are carried amounted to reasonable suspicion. Id.; see Bybee, 131 Or App at 497 (“Because members of the general public have a common knowledge about whether a person is under the influence of alcohol, a statement from a named informant, based on his own personal observations, that a person is a ‘drunken driver’ is sufficient to establish reasonable suspicion.’”); See also Goss, 219 Or App at 651 n 3 (same); State v. Perrin, 143 Or App 123, 128 n 3, 923 P2d 1249 (1996) (same).

2024 Update

A 911 call reporting a dispute with a gun-possessing suspect near a motel’s vending machines did not provide reasonable suspicion to stop defendant and his visibly upset companion, who were in a car in the motel parking lot near the vending machines. State v. Cage, 324 Or App 430 (March 8, 2023). The first factor from State v. Bybee, 131 Or App 492 (1994)—whether the informant identified him or herself or was otherwise exposed to liability for filing a false report—was “neutral.” The informant did not provide his or her name to law enforcement when making the report, did not greet the officers upon arrival, and the officers did not identify the reporting party before stopping defendant, even though police arrived right away after receiving the call and reasonably believed that the report had been made by a motel employee and that the person would have been readily identifiable.

The second Bybee factor—whether report contained sufficient detail to infer personal observations—was not satisfied. A motel employee had made a report secondhand from information that the employee had received from one guest, the employee did not personally verify the report, and the report did not contain any significant detail—simply that a dispute involving a firearm occurred near the vending machines. Thus, the details provided to the police were not “sufficient to show that neither the front desk clerk nor the guests had likely fabricated the report.

The third Bybee factor—whether the officer’s own observations corroborated the informant’s information—was not satisfied either. When the officer arrived shortly after receiving the report, he observed two people sitting in a car near the vending machines. But the report made no mention of a vehicle, which could have arrived before, during, or after the report. Neither the fact that one of the occupants was evasive upon seeing the officer nor that she had been crying, were sufficient to suggest that she had been in a dispute with defendant involving a firearm. Finally, the fact that defendant attempted to conceal “something” under his shirt was not enough.

In summary, the officer “did not find ‘the person, the vehicle, and the location substantially as described by the informant’ when the informant had made no mention of the parties involved in the dispute or of a vehicle, the officers did not observe any parties in a dispute, and the officers did not observe a firearm.” Id. at 441 (emphasis in Cage).

E. If an officer has reasonable suspicion of one crime, can the officer prolong the stop to investigate another crime for which the officer lacks reasonable suspicion?

Even when a stop is initially lawful, it may endure only for the time it takes an officer to complete the parts of the investigation that are reasonably related to the reasons for the stop. State v. Sherman 74 Or App 764 (2015). “[A]n officer’s reasonable suspicion about certain crimes does not justify the officer’s extension of a stop to conduct an investigation of another crime for which the officer does not also have reasonable suspicion.” State v. Kentopp, 251 Or App 527, 534, 284 P3d 564 (2012) (emphasis in original) (stating that even if the court were to assume that the officer had reasonable suspicion of unauthorized use of a motor vehicle, that would not justify the extension of the traffic stop to investigate a crime for which the officers did not have reasonable suspicion—drug possession); see also State v. Pichardo, 263 Or App 1, 6, 326 P3d 624 (2014) (same).

Whether a reasonable relationship exists between the officer’s questions and the nature of the stop is a lower standard than reasonable suspicion. The state need only show a “reasonable, circumstance-specific” relationship between the question and the purpose of the stop. Pichardo, 360 Or at 759-60.

That principal was also applied in State v. Klein, 234 Or App 523, 228 P3d 714 (2010), where the court held that although the officer developed reasonable suspicion that the defendant possessed burglar’s tools during the course of a valid traffic stop, the officer’s inquiry about drugs “unconstitutionally extended the duration of the stop as to either the traffic offense or unlawful possession of burglary tools.” Id. at 532.

F. Does an officer need to rule out noncriminal explanations for conduct that reasonably appears to violate the law?

The mere possibility that an activity may have a criminal explanation is insufficient to give an officer justification to seize a defendant or conduct a search if there are other, more reasonable noncriminal explanations for the activity. See, e.g., [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=12782449452189549402&q=.,+State+v.+Khoshnaw,+234+Or+App+24,&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 State v. Khoshnaw, 234 Or App 24, 33, 227 P3d 1188 (2010)] (finding that officer had no reasonable suspicion to stop the defendant based on possession of soda and chips by the defendant leaving a convenience store despite testimony by the officer that robbers “sometimes make purchases at convenience stores before robbing them in order to see how much money is in the till”). Thus, the more innocuous a particular fact, the less likely that fact is to add weight to the reasonable suspicion determination, and the more evidence the state must put forth to show that the fact has criminal significance. For example, even where an officer testified that people engaged in criminal enterprises often use prepaid cellular phones, the fact that defendant had two prepaid cellular phones carried little weight because such phones are readily available to both criminals and noncriminals. State v. Maciel, 254 Or App 530, 539, 295 P3d 145 (2013).

At the same time, an officer need not “eliminate all possible lawful exceptions for conduct that reasonably appears to violate the law.” State v. Bourget-Goddard, 164 Or App 573, 578, 993 P2d 814 (1999) (internal quotations and citations omitted) (so holding as to probable cause). Nor must an officer “eliminate the possible applicability of defenses or statutory exemptions that do not describe a necessary element of the offense itself.” Id.

G. Reasonable Suspicion Summarized

In summary, if a police officer is able to testify to “specific and articulable facts” (based on his own observations or the report of a reliable informant) and those facts give rise to a reasonable inference that a particular person has committed or is about to commit a “specific crime or type of crime,” the officer has “reasonable suspicion” and hence may stop the person to investigate that crime. Maciel-Figueroa, 361 Or at 165, 182 (emphasis added); Martin, 260 Or App at 469.

II. Common Fact Patterns That Do Not Justify a Stop

A. Suspect’s Appearance Indicative of Prolonged Drug Use

State v. Heater, 263 Or App 298, 328 P3d 714 (2014) (holding that where the defendant’s “overall demeanor” and “conversation style” were consistent with prolonged drug use; the defendant’s speech and movements were “erratic”; and the defendant was not communicating in full sentences, the officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the defendant possessed illegal drugs at the time of the stop).

Kentopp, 251 Or App 527 (2012) (reasoning that an officer’s observation that the defendant’s gray, rotting teeth were indicative of past methamphetamine use was not sufficient to support reasonable suspicion of current drug possession).

State v. Frias, 229 Or App 60, 65, 210 P3d 914 (2009) (“Simply put, whatever the inference that could be reasonably drawn about defendant’s past drug use, there was no evidence of a current or imminent crime[.]”).

State v. Holcomb, 202 Or App 73, 121 P3d 13 (2005) (holding that an officer’s observation of a dirty, unkempt woman dancing along the side of a highway with track marks on her arms, but who was not presently intoxicated, was insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion of present drug possession and explaining that the state’s argument “would effectively sanction nonparticularized, status-based stops of habitual drug users”).

B. Associating With Known Drug Users or Dealers

Heater, 263 Or App at 306 (“We have squarely rejected the proposition that a person’s association with an individual involved with criminal drug activity is a basis for reasonable suspicion of that person’s own involvement in criminal drug activity.”).

State v. Wiggins, 262 Or App 351, 362, 324 P3d 626 (2014) (stating that the circumstances established nothing more than that the defendant, who had admitted to using drugs months before the stop, was present in high drug-use areas and was associating with others who had been or may have been involved in drug-related activities. “If that were sufficient to establish reasonable suspicion, we would be effectively sanctioning nonparticularized status-based stops of individuals who have used drugs in the past”).

State v. Bertsch, 251 Or App 128, 134, 284 P3d 502 (2012) (“It is not reasonable to conclude that a person is involved in drug crimes because he or she is in the company of a known drug user or dealer.”).

C. Recent Drug Use/Intoxication (Distinguishing Holdorf from Kolb and Farrar)

State v. Kolb, 251 Or App 303, 313-15, 283 P3d 423 (2012) (holding that an officer’s reasonable suspicion that a defendant is presently under the influence of a controlled substance does not permit a reasonable inference that the defendant presently possesses a controlled substance).

State v. Farrar, 252 Or App 256, 260-61, 287 P3d 1124 (2012): The defendant was fumbling through documents, fidgety, extremely nervous, and showed symptoms of bruxism, all of which the officer had learned through “training and experience” was indicative of methamphetamine use. The officer also testified that “people that show symptoms of the use of methamphetamine will usually have some sort of way to use the drug, needles, snort tubes, etcetera * * * or possibly have more methamphetamine in the vehicle.” 

The court found that present use was insufficient to establish a reasonable inference of present possession:

“Even if the officer correctly concluded that defendant’s behavior was consistent with methamphetamine use, his subjective belief—that defendant’s methamphetamine use provided a sufficient basis for concluding that she presently possessed methamphetamine—was not objectively reasonable.”

Id. at 261. State v. Holdorf, 355 Or 812, 829, 333 P3d 982 (2014): Finding reasonable suspicion that the “defendant committed the crime of possession of methamphetamine where the defendant was actively “tweaking” on methamphetamines and riding with a known felon who had an outstanding warrant and who was under investigation as a suspect in a local methamphetamine distribution ring.

The court in Holdorf framed the inquiry as follows:

“Was [the officer’s] observation that [the] defendant appeared to be under the influence of methamphetamine, based on his training and experience, together with other information on which the officer properly relied, sufficient to establish a reasonable suspicion that [the] defendant had or was about to commit a crime?”

Id. at 825. Consequently, Holdorf should not be interpreted as destroying either of the two bedrock principles of Court of Appeals case law: (1) mere association with drug dealers and users alone is insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion; (2) present intoxication alone is insufficient to establish reasonable suspicion of possession.

In Holdorf, the court wrote at length about the significance of the information the police had gathered about the defendant’s companion. And when that information was combined with the defendant’s present intoxication, the court concluded that the officer had reasonable suspicion that the defendant “committed the crime of possession.”

It may be that enough law-abiding citizens interact with drug users and dealers (possibly in efforts to help them change their ways) that the appellate courts have refused to find reasonable suspicion based on mere association alone. But Holdorf suggests that when a person is riding in a car with a drug dealer and the person is visibly under the influence of methamphetamines, it is reasonable to conclude that the person possesses methamphetamine or did so in the very recent past.

Although the Court of Appeals found that inference unduly speculative, Holdorf may be limited to the following proposition: when a person is tweaking on methamphetamine and riding in a car with a reputed methamphetamine dealer, it is reasonable to infer that the intoxicated person has recently purchased or possessed methamphetamine.

D. Nervousness or Evasive Action

State v. Meza-Garcia, 256 Or App 798, 804, 303 P3d 975 (2013) (stating that a defendant’s nervousness “adds little, if any, weight toward reasonable suspicion”).

State v. Espinoza-Barragan, 253 Or App 743, 750, 293 P3d 1072 (2012):

“[N]ervousness during a traffic stop contributes little, if any, weight toward reasonable suspicion that the driver is engaged in criminal activity. As we held in State v. Berry, 232 Or App 612, 618, 222 P3d 758 (2009) * * * , ‘[T]here is nothing inherently suspicious about * * * being nervous when pulled over by a police officer, particularly at [a very early morning] hour.’ State v. Juarez–Godinez, 135 Or App 591, 607, 900 P2d 1044 (1995), aff’d on other grounds, 326 Or 1, 942 P2d 772 (1997) (the defendant’s nervous reaction to officer’s questioning did not, alone or in combination with other factors, give rise to reasonable suspicion of drug trafficking).”

Frias, 229 Or App at 65-66 (holding no reasonable suspicion where the defendant became “evasive” when questioned about why he had been visiting his friend and “hemmed and hawed” before finally answering; the court noted that the “defendant’s evasive reaction to questioning that he [was] constitutionally entitled to refuse to answer” did not, combined with knowledge of the defendant’s previous drug use, provide reasonable suspicion of current drug use”).

Kentopp, 251 Or App at 532 (stating that the defendant’s “(1) furtive movements; (2) nervous demeanor; (3) gray, rotting teeth; (4) conduct consistent with trying to flee the scene; (5) failure to have registration and proof of insurance for the car that he was driving; and (6) statement that he was borrowing the car from another person” were insufficient to give rise to a reasonable suspicion that the defendant possessed drugs).

State v. Juarez–Godinez, 135 Or App 591, 607, 900 P2d 1044 (1995) (holding that officer lacked reasonable suspicion where the defendant, among other things, was “visibly nervous, turning pale, agitated, perspiring, and exhibit[ing] furtive eye movements”).

E. Entering Premises Known to be Associated With Drug Activity

Bertsch, 251 Or App at 134 (concluding that police did not have reasonable suspicion that the defendant possessed drugs merely because the defendant was visiting an apartment building suspected of drug activity, the defendant left the apartment shortly after arriving, and the defendant was accompanied by a person known to associate with drug dealers and drug users).

State v. Rutledge, 243 Or App 603, 610, 260 P3d 532 (2011) (holding that facts were insufficient to justify reasonable suspicion where the defendant “had just left a motel that the police believed was involved in drug activity, [and] was in a car with a person suspected of drug activity”).

State v. Zumbrum, 221 Or App 362, 364-70, 189 P3d 1235 (2008) (stating that although the defendant was present in the same apartment as a person who participated in “mid- to upper-level drug deals,” that the apartment was located in a “high-crime area,” and that the apartment was the subject of tips about illegal drug activity, those facts did not “tell [the court] whether [the] defendant * * * was involved in criminal activity”).

F. Presence in High-Crime Area of the City

State v. Acuna, 264 Or App 158 168, 331 P3d 1040 (2014) (“As an initial matter, that [the] defendant was present in an area known for drug activity is of little relevance; a person’s presence in a high-crime area does not alone justify reasonable suspicion.”).

Martin, 260 Or App at 472-73 (“Evidence that a person is in a high-crime area, is engaged in ambiguous conduct, and appears to want to avoid police observation does not give rise to reasonable suspicion to stop the person.”).

State v. Moya, 97 Or App 375, 775-77 P2d 927 (1989): Although the defendant was in an area of “intense” drug activity and parked in front of a location nicknamed the “Heroin Hotel”:

“[t]here is nothing inherently suspicious about parking in or ‘frequenting’ such an area. The location of [the] defendant’s car and her earlier presence in the area may have assumed heightened significance had the officers noticed any other indications of likely criminal activity. However, the officers did not see any drugs or drug paraphernalia before stopping her. There is nothing remarkable about examining something in one’s hands, appearing startled at a sudden confrontation with the police or denying the suggestion that one is involved in wrongdoing.”

Id. at 378. Therefore, the officers lacked objectively reasonable suspicion to stop the defendant. Id.

G. Officer Subjectively Believes that the Defendant Engaged in a Drug Transaction

State v. Loud, 149 Or App 250, 942 P2d 814 (1997): Officers observed an individual pacing on the street corner in front of a known drug house for two hours attempting to wave down passing cars, which led the officer to suspect that the individual was selling drugs. Id. at 252. At 1:45 a.m., the defendant parked his car, honked his horn, and turned off his headlights. Id. The individual who had been pacing on the street corner in front of the drug house approached the car and leaned into the driver’s side window for approximately one minute, such that the officers could not tell whether the individual and the defendant were conducting a hand-to-hand exchange. Id. The suspected drug dealer stepped away from the car and the defendant sped away. Id.

The Court of Appeals held that the “defendant’s brief visit with a suspicious but unidentified person in an area known for drug sales” did not give rise to reasonable suspicion that the defendant was engaged in criminal activity. Id. at 254.

State v. Aguilar, 139 Or App 175, 912 P2d 379 (1996): Five weeks prior to the stop at issue, an officer witnessed Stephens, the suspected operator of a drug house, get into a car that was parked in front of his drug house. Stephens got out after one minute and then ran back into the house. Id. at 177-78. The officer stopped the car after it pulled away from the drug house, and the driver admitted that he had just sold cocaine and heroin to Stephens. Id. at 177.

Five weeks later, the same officer observed the defendant get into a car parked in front of Stephens’s drug house. Id. “As [the defendant’s] car pulled away from the curb * * * a [ ] man, whom [the officer] believed to be Stephens, the reputed operator of the drug house, jog[ged] from the area where the car had been parked, up the stairs, and into the suspected drug house.” Id. “[The officer] did not see any contact between the two men.” Id.

The officer suspected that a drug transaction had just occurred. He stopped the defendant’s vehicle for a traffic infraction and extended the stop to investigate the suspected drug transaction. Id. at 177-78, 181. The Court of Appeals held that the objective facts recited by the officer failed to establish reasonable suspicion needed to extend the traffic stop:

“We * * * do not believe defendant’s proximity to the [drug] house combined with [the officer’s] belief that the man seen running from the curb into the house was Stephens, the purported operator of the drug house, provided reasonable suspicion to believe that defendant had committed a drug-related offense.”

Id. at 183. State v. King, 67 Or App 749, 680 P2d 10 (1984): Officers observed two cars parked door to door in a secluded area of a freeway rest stop. Id. at 752. The officers knew that there had been instances of drug use in the rest area. Id. Two men in their early twenties were sitting in one of the cars. The other car was unoccupied. The officers observed the defendant get out of the occupied car and walk to the vacant car carrying a brown paper bag, which could have contained drugs. Id. The officers subjectively believed that they had just witnessed a “drug buy.” Id. The police stopped the defendant and obtained an admission that the bag contained drugs. Id. at 751. The Court of Appeals concluded that those facts failed to establish an objectively reasonable suspicion of a drug transaction. Id.

State v. Jacobs, 187 Or App 330, 67 P3d 408 (2003): ' Officers were driving through downtown Salem, a “high drug traffic area,” when they saw three men “huddled together” near an automated teller machine. They were looking around “in a nervous manner” when the defendant passed what looked like money to one of the others. The officers suspected that a drug transaction was occurring, so they approached the men and said, “[I]t looks like you guys just did a drug deal.” The defendant denied the accusation. One of the officers searched the defendant and found a plastic bag containing marijuana.'

The court held that there was “no evidence that the specific location was a site of continuous, hand-to-hand drug traffic. There is only a vague reference to the entirety of downtown Salem as a ‘high drug traffic area.’” Id. at 335. The court continued, holding, “furtiveness in the act of engaging in what may nevertheless be entirely lawful conduct does not establish an objectively reasonable basis for a belief that a crime has been committed.” Id. at 336.

H. Religious Symbols

Maciel, 254 Or App at 538 (declining to consider a “Christian necklace * * *  [and the officer’s] experience [that] ‘people transporting narcotics often have certain religious symbols hanging in their vehicle[]’ because of the serious constitutional concerns raised by reliance on such evidence.”) (emphasis added).

State v. De La Rosa, 228 Or App 666, 674 n 2, 208 P3d 1012 (2009) (declining to consider observation of a “Jesus Malverde” medallion, despite “detailed testimony” regarding the significance of that medallion to drug dealers).

State v. Alvarado, 257 Or App 612, 628, 307 P3d 540 (2013) (declining to consider the defendant’s religious symbol in the reasonable suspicion analysis). 

I. Air Fresheners

Meza-Garcia, 256 Or App at 806: Where an officer smelled a strong odor of air freshener that had no visible source and testified that “vehicles that transport illegal narcotics attempt many means to mask any odors that could be potentially detected by police canines” those facts neither alone nor in combination with other circumstances establish reasonable suspicion of drug activity.

Juarez–Godinez, 135 Or App at, 606-07 (explaining that facts, including “heavy odor of air freshener in the vehicle,” did not give rise to reasonable suspicion of drug-related criminal activity (internal quotation marks omitted)).

Alvarado, 257 Or App at 628 (officer lacked reasonable suspicion where defendant’s vehicle contained numerous air fresheners, two bottles of cologne, and smelled strongly of both; defendant had “religious symbols” in his vehicle; defendant had an open energy drink; defendant was traveling with both a cell phone and a pager; during his conversation with Turner, defendant looked down at his cell phone; defendant moved the pager to his glove box; defendant became nervous when asked about the presence of drugs).

J. Implausible or Bizarre Story

Maciel, 254 Or App at 540: The following did not create reasonable suspicion of drug activity:

“Defendant left Woodburn in an unspecified car and drove to Oakland to visit Junior. While in Oakland, defendant decided that he wanted to buy the vehicle in which he was stopped, and Junior introduced defendant to Monzo on a street corner. There, defendant ‘bought’ the vehicle and, leaving his original car in Oakland, began driving back to Woodburn on a “test drive” of approximately 600 miles. Defendant had no contact information for Monzo, had no idea how he would reclaim his other car, had no idea how he would send Monzo payment, and had not agreed to a purchase price for the vehicle.”

Although “certainly suspicious,” the state offered no evidence to connect the defendant’s “bizarre story” with the crime of drug trafficking.

Alvarado, 257 Or App at 628-29: The defendant’s story that he was “going to Pendleton to pay his cousin’s taxes—may be unusual, but it does not suggest criminal activity; Pendleton is a county seat, and people pay taxes there. The state offers no explanation of the connection, if any, between these facts and the crime of drug trafficking”).

K. Lack of Luggage

Maciel, 254 Or App at 540 (“Regarding the lack of visible luggage, we have on several occasions held that a lack of visible luggage adds nothing to the reasonable suspicion inquiry if luggage may have readily been stored out of the officer’s plain view. Because the officer could not have seen into the trunk, ‘the lack of visible luggage in no way affects our reasonable suspicion analysis.’”).

Meza-Garcia, 256 Or App at 805 (“[T]he absence of luggage in a vehicle is of no consequence where the luggage may have been stored out of plain view.”).

Espinoza-Barragan, 253 Or App at 752 (“An officer cannot conclude that a driver is not carrying any luggage if the officer does not, or cannot, look into places in the driver’s vehicle that are likely to hold luggage.”).

Juarez–Godinez, 135 Or App at 607, aff’d on other grounds, 326 Or 1, 942 P2d 772 (1997) (“The lack of visible luggage also adds nothing, because, with three adults riding in the vehicle, any luggage would likely have been placed in the trunk.”).

L. Conduct “Consistent” With Criminal Activity

Martin, 260 Or App at 469-70 (“An officer may not stop a person simply because the person’s conduct is consistent with criminal conduct; the nature of the conduct matters.”).

Alvarado, 257 Or App at 628-29: For three reasons the state argued that the totality of the circumstances were consistent with the transportation of narcotics: “(1) the car defendant was driving was owned by somebody else; (2) defendant was carrying a cell phone and a pager; and (3) the car contained multiple air fresheners and two bottles of cologne.” The Court of Appeals explained,

“[A] set of facts will not always create a reasonable suspicion just because those facts are consistent with, but do not necessarily suggest, a crime being committed. Wearing clothing while driving, to use an extreme example, is also consistent with the transportation of narcotics. Less extremely, so too (for all we know) is wearing cologne. This consistency, however, would not be enough to support reasonable suspicion. See State v. Maciel, 254 Or App 530, 540, 295 P3d 145, (2013) (holding that notwithstanding officer’s testimony that people engaged in criminal enterprises often use prepaid cellular phones, the presence of two prepaid cellular phones carried little weight because such phones can be easily lawfully acquired by criminals and noncriminals alike).”

Id. at 629.

M. Officer Lacks Sufficient Basis to Draw an Adverse Inference

Espinoza-Barragan, 253 Or App at 752 (“An officer cannot conclude that a driver is not carrying any luggage if the officer does not, or cannot, look into places in the driver’s vehicle that are likely to hold luggage.”); see also Chapter 2(a)(2)(j) (more lack of luggage cases).

N. Refusal to Cooperate, or Assertion of Constitutional Rights

“A person’s assertion of a constitutional right cannot support a reasonable suspicion of criminal activity.” State v. Rutledge, 243 Or App 603, 610, 260 P3d 532 (2011).

A defendant tightly clutching her purse and refusing a search manifested a desire to protect the privacy of the item and, as such, was an assertion of her constitutional rights. State v Barker, 271 Or App 63 (2015).

“[T]he fact that defendant closed her purse and pulled it away from the officers could not support probable cause to believe that she had committed a crime. By that act, defendant exhibited her intention to protect the privacy of her purse. The assertion of a constitutionally protected right against warrantless searches cannot be a basis for such a search.” State v. Lavender, 93 Or App 361, 764 P2d 1027 (1988).

When an individual seeks to protect an item and openly asserts his or her privacy rights, that behavior and assertion is neither innately shifty nor sinister—rather, it is constitutionally protected. State v Barker, 271 Or App 63, 348 P3d 1138 (2015). And, “[a]llowing the police to conduct a search on the basis of the assertion of a privacy right would render the so-called right nugatory.” State v. Brown, 110 Or App 604, 611, 825 P2d 282 (1992).

Although furtive behavior may contribute to probable cause, asserting a constitutionally protected privacy right cannot. State v. Gressel, 276 Or 333, 339, 554 P2d 1014 (1976) (“[A] suspicion, however well founded, having been aroused merely on the basis of an assertion of one’s constitutional rights [not to be searched], can play no part in creating probable cause for a search.”); State v. Farrar, 252 Or App 256, 262, 287 P3d 1124 (2012) (the defendant’s refusal to consent to a search of her purse did not contribute to establishing reasonable suspicion); State v. Medinger, 235 Or App 88, 94, 230 P3d 76 (2010) (the defendant’s refusal to answer the officer’s questions was not “strange” or “furtive”; rather, it was the exercise of a constitutionally protected right, and therefore did not affect the probable cause analysis); and State v. Wise, 72 Or App 58, 62 n 3, 695 P2d 68 (1985) (“A refusal to consent to a search cannot aid in showing probable cause for a search.”).
