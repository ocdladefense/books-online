I. Police Conduct During Traffic Stops

A. When can officers extend the subject matter or duration of a stop?

“[F]or the purposes of Article I, section 9, all investigative activities, including investigative inquiries, conducted during a traffic stop are part of an ongoing seizure and are subject to both subject-matter and durational limitations. State v. Areola-Botello, 365 Or 695, 712, 451 P3d 939.

Officers’ investigative activities and inquiries during a traffic or investigatory stop “must be reasonably related to the purpose that permits the officer to stop [the] individual in the first place” or must have an independent constitutional justification. Id. at 711-12.

An officer who stops a driver for a traffic infraction must focus their actions on investigating the infraction, ascertaining the identity of the driver, and issuing a traffic citation:

“Police authority to detain [the person] dissipates when the investigation reasonably related to that traffic infraction, the identification of persons, and the issuance of a citation (if any) is completed or reasonably should be completed. Other or further conduct by the police, beyond that reasonably related to the traffic violation, must be justified on some basis other than the traffic violation.”

An officer can extend the durational or subject-matter scope of a traffic stop if they develop independent constitutional justification to do so—for example, when an officer develops reasonable suspicion of a crime while writing a traffic citation. Arreola-Botello, 365 Or at 714.

Practice Tips

Dog sniffs are unquestionably “investigative activities”; thus, you should argue that Arreola-Botello prevents police from subjecting a stopped person or vehicle to a dog sniff while another officer lawfully and expeditiously processes the stop.

Arreola-Botello is not limited to “traffic stops” but applies to all constitutionally significant seizures. 365 Or at 711. Thus, you should argue that any investigative inquiries or activities must be reasonably related to the justification for the stop. For example, if police claim to justify a seizure under the “emergency aid” exception, the officer’s questions must be related to the perceived emergency—the officer cannot stray into investigative inquiries. Similarly, if the justification for a seizure is the “officer-safety” exception, the officer’s questions must be related to the perceived threat and cannot stray into inquiries about drugs or other criminal activity.

2024 Update

In State v. Swafford, 331 Or App 308 (2024), an officer unlawfully expanded subject matter scope of a traffic stop to investigate a hit and run by asking several questions about a theft that had just occurred nearby. The court rejected the state’s argument that the questions about the theft were permissible as “reasonably necessary” to effectuate the purpose of the stop, under State v. Jimenez, 357 Or 417, 353 P3d 1227 (2015), ' and State v. Watson, 353 Or 768, 774, 305 P3d 94 (2013), because they were designed to ascertain the motive that led the defendant to commit the hit and run. Id. at 311-12. According to the court, to allow police to inquire into any potential motive that a person may have for committing a crime would allow an infinite number of inquiries; yet, Article I, section 9, requires a closer connection between the purpose of the stop and the questions that an officer poses. Id. at 312. In other words, under State v. Arreola-Botello, 365 Or 695, 714, 451 P3d 939 (2019),' an officer may not ask about new crimes during an investigatory stop even if the new crimes form the “motive” for the crime or traffic violation that the officer is investigating.

B. What are examples of actions or inquiries that extend the durational or subject matter scope of a traffic stop?

A request for consent to search made during a seizure has “constitutional significance,” thus the request violates the Oregon Constitution if it is unrelated to the justification for the stop. Arreola-Botello, 365 Or at 711.

State v. Williams, 297 Or App 384, 441 P3d 242 (2019)

Where an officer has reasonable suspicion of DUII, the officer may ask whether there is any alcohol or drugs in the car and request consent to search the car without extending the stop because the presence of alcohol or controlled substances is reasonably related to a DUII investigation.

State v. Rondeau, 295 Or App 769, 436 P3d 49 (2019)

During a DUII stop, the officer smelled a strong odor of marijuana and asked the defendant if she had any marijuana on her bike. The defendant handed him a small bag of marijuana. The officer asked for consent to search because he did not believe the bag that the defendant produced was consistent with the odor. The court held that the request for consent unlawfully extended the DUII stop. It reasoned, to the extent that current possession of marijuana was relevant to recent consumption for purposes of DUII, the first bag of marijuana established current possession, and the discovery of any additional marijuana would not have advanced the DUII investigation or prosecution.

State v. Aguirre-Lopez, 291 Or App 78 (2018)

In the absence of reasonable suspicion for unlawful use of a vehicle or a related crime, questioning about the ownership of a car during a traffic stop exceeds the permissible scope of the stop.

State v. Rosales, 291 Or App 762, 423 P3d 112 (2018)

A dog sniff extends a traffic stop under the Fourth Amendment if it adds even 30 seconds to the stop.

The defendant was a passenger in a car that was stopped for a traffic violation. Officers deployed a drug-sniffing dog during the stop and the dog alerted to the car, leading to the discovery of drugs. Passengers are not seized during a traffic stop as a matter of law under Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution. Furthermore, the deployment of the dog did not escalate the encounter into a stop because the presence of a drug-sniffing dog would not lead a reasonable person to believe that he or she was under criminal investigation.

But “under the Fourth Amendment [to the United States Constitution], a police officer ‘effectively seizes everyone in the vehicle, the driver and all passengers’ for the duration of a traffic stop.” State v. Evans, 284 Or App 806, 814, 397 P3d 42 (2017) (quoting Arizona v. Johnson, 555 US 323, 327, 129 S Ct 781, 172 L Ed 2d 694 (2009)). '''' Therefore, the defendant was seized under the Fourth Amendment for the entire traffic stop.

A dog sniff is not an ordinary part of a traffic stop, so any sniff that increases the duration of a stop is unlawful. Here, the dog deployment added time to the traffic stop because there was evidence that the officer conducting the stop delayed processing the traffic violation for about 30 seconds, “a measurable amount of time,” to cover the K-9 officer. There was no independent justification for an extension of the stop. As a result, the dog alert occurred during an unlawful extension, and all evidence discovered as a result of the alert had to be suppressed.


State v. Decker, 290 Or App 321, 417 P3d 449 (2018)

An officer lacked reasonable suspicion to extend a traffic stop to investigate the defendant for (1) possession of controlled substances, or (2) being a felon in possession of a firearm, based on the following observations:

“(1) defendant’s delay in pulling over, (2) his movement inside his vehicle, including repeatedly looking at the center console, (3) his nervousness during the stop, including repeatedly looking at the center console, (4) his odd story about where he was driving, and (5) the fact that he was driving a vehicle that belonged to a person known to be involved with drugs.”

Id. at 328.

The court noted that nervousness during a stop can only contribute to reasonable suspicion of drug possession if there are facts connecting the defendant to illegal drug activity, such as the defendant’s presence in an area associated with high levels of drug activity. Furthermore, none of the other behaviors observed by the officer were associated with past or current drug use.

The record did not reflect when the officer learned that the defendant was a convicted felon. Therefore, the defendant’s status as a felon could not contribute to reasonable suspicion of felon in possession, and none of the defendant’s other behaviors specifically related to that crime.

2024 Update

During a stop for illegally tinted windows, officer lacked constitutional justification to look inside the defendant’s car “to see if there was something illegal * * * in view.” By investigating whether defendant had “something illegal” in his car—and not whether the defendant’s windows were excessively tinted, the officer exceeded the subject-matter scope of the stop. City of Portland v. Gonzalez, 314 Or App 401, 406, 499 P3d 95 (2021).

“[A]bsent an independent constitutional justification, a drug-detecting dog * * * generally cannot, consistent with Article I, section 9, sniff a car for drugs during a traffic stop.” State v. Soto-Navarro, 309 Or App 218, 223, 482 P3d 150 (2021).

During a stop for speeding, officer lacked constitutional justification to ask the defendant (1) “Do you live in this area?”; (2) “What are you doing up here?”; (3) “Where are you coming from today?”; and (4) if the officer could see a citation that was visible in defendant's car. The questions were unrelated to the speeding infraction and the officer lacked reasonable suspicion of DUII when the questions were asked. State v. Krieger, 318 Or App 441, 446-47, 508 P3d 62 (2022).

Officer unlawfully extended a traffic stop by asking the defendant questions about his incarceration history and tattoos; the questions were not reasonably related to the traffic infraction, nor were the questions justified by a circumstance-specific officer safety concern. State v. Gilkey, 317 Or App 752, 505 P3d 1029 (2022).

In State v. Vannoy, 326 Or App 11 (May 17, 2023), the court held that an officer’s immediate perception of “a strong odor of marijuana” coming from inside of a vehicle during a traffic stop failed to support reasonable suspicion of a marijuana crime to justify asking the passenger “about marijuana,” asking her to step out of the car so that it could be searched pursuant to the driver’s consent, and asking the passenger for consent to search her bag to essentially prove that she did not possess an unlawful quantity.

Practice Tip

Under Vannoy, the Court’s 2016 decision in State v. Vennell, 274 Or App 94 (2015), which held that the smell of marijuana can create reasonable suspicion of a marijuana crime, has been severely undermined and is likely no longer good law. The Vannoy ' court quoted with approval the state’s acknowledgement that Vennel' had been “severely undermined” (if not overturned), and Vannoy '''' reaffirmed that the odor of marijuana is no longer “remarkable” and is afforded little weight in a reasonable suspicion analysis.

Calling for a drug dog at the outset of a traffic stop can unlawfully extend the stop. State v. GABR, 324 Or App 588 (March 15, 2023) (Police unlawfully extended a traffic stop by calling a drug detection dog at the initiation of a traffic stop without reasonable suspicion).

2024 Update

In State v. Bradley, 329 Or App 736 (2023), after the defendant agreed to perform FSTs during a DUII stop, the officer asked, “Do you have anything on you that you’re not supposed to have?” The court held that the question was not “reasonably related” to the DUII stop, because it was not limited to an inquiry about “controlled substances,” which may be permissible during a DUII investigation, State v. Williams, 297 Or App 384, 441 P3d 242 (2019), nor was it limited to a question about the presence of a “weapon,” which may be permissible if the officer testifies to a circumstance specific danger that the weapons inquiry was intended to remediate. State v. Miler, 363 Or 374, 383, 422 P3d 240 (2018). Rather, the question was so broad that it encompassed many possessory offenses entirely unrelated to the purpose of the stop, see, e.g., ORS 164.095 (stolen property); ORS 164.235 (burglar’s tools); ORS 163.689 (materials depicting sexually explicit conduct of a child), and thus unconstitutionally expanded the stop.

C. Unrelated inquiries that take place during an unavoidable lull in processing a citation.

Prior to Arreola-Botello, officers could make inquiries unrelated to the issuance of a citation during ' an “unavoidable lull” in the stop, which occurred during a period of time in which the officer could not proceed with the investigation—for example, while awaiting the results of a records check. See, e.g.,' State v. Nims, 248 Or App 708, 713, 274 P3d 235 rev den, 352 Or 378, 290 P3d 814 (2012).

Arreola-Botello disavowed the unavoidable-lull doctrine and held that all inquiries and actions during a traffic stop must have independent justification, even if the inquiries do not extend the duration of the stop. 365 Or at 712.

II. Failure to Carry or Present a Driver’s License

Are there any special limitations on officers’ authority to detain drivers for the crime of failure to carry or present a driver’s license?

Failure to carry or present a driver’s license to a police officer, ORS 807.570, is a class C misdemeanor. The failure to present statute provides, “A police officer may detain a person arrested or cited for the offense described in this section only for such time as reasonably necessary to investigate and verify the person’s identity.” ORS 807.570(4).

Detaining a driver for failure to carry or present a driver’s license beyond the time authorized in ORS 807.570 is a violation of the state constitutional provision against unreasonable searches and seizures. State v. Middleton, 294 Or App 596, 432 P3d 337 (2018).

An officer can request consent to search a car during a traffic stop if the request was made for the purpose of investigating and verifying the identity of the person being detained for failure to carry or present a driver’s license. State v. Claxton, 140 Or App 168, 915 P2d 421 (1996). But if the driver of the stopped car refuses to consent, officers cannot search a car for evidence of the defendant’s identity incident to arrest for that crime. Id.; State v. Krause, 281 Or App 143, 146, 383 P3d 307 (2016); State v. Durando, 262 Or App 299, 304, 323 P3d 985 (2014); State v. Bishop, 157 Or App 33, 43 n 4, 967 P2d 1241 (1998).

Furthermore, an officer unlawfully extends a traffic stop by asking for consent to search after the driver’s identity has already been verified. State v. Middleton, 294 Or App 596, 432 P3d 337 (2018).

However, a search for a defendant’s identification may be permissible incident to an arrest for the crime of providing false information to a police officer, ORS 162.385; ORS 807.620. But even in that situation, the state must still establish that the search was reasonable in time, scope, and intensity. State v. Ramirez, 305 Or App 195, 218, 468 P3d 1006 (2020). Specifically, the state bears the burden to establish that the officer was actually (that is, subjectively) searching for the arrestee’s identification and that it was reasonable to conclude that the identification could be found in the location searched. Id. at 217-18.
