I. When is property seized?

Property is “seized” for the purposes of Article I, section 9, when the police significantly interfere, even temporarily, with a person’s ownership or possessory interests in the property. State v. Owens, 302 Or 196, 207, 729 P2d 524 (1986). A “seizure” of property “clearly” occurs when police physically remove property from a person’s possession. State v. Juarez-Godinez, 326 Or 1, 6, 942 P2d 772 (1997). However, just as the seizure of a person can be accomplished without physically touching anyone, a nonphysical “show of authority” can result in the seizure of property if it is objectively reasonable for the defendant to believe that the police significantly interfered with his possessory rights. Id. at 7-8. A warrantless seizure of property is unconstitutional unless it is justified under one of a few carefully circumscribed exceptions to the warrant requirement. Id. at 9.

2024 Update

“[A] ‘seizure’ of property occurs when police physically remove property from a person’s possession.” State v. Wilcox, 323 Or App 271, 276 (December 14, 2022), rev allowed, 371 Or 60 (2023).

2024 Update

“[W]hen, after seizing a person, an officer removes items of clothing from the person, or items from the person's pockets, to separate those items from the individual, even temporarily, or holds those items as evidence or otherwise, those items have been seized. Removing those items and reducing them to exclusive control of law enforcement has interfered with the person’s possessory rights—their right to possess the item themselves, and their right to exclude others from possessing the items.” Id.

“Whether something is seized is determined by considering the state’s interference with a person’s possessory or ownership interests, not the rationale for the interference[.]” Id. at 763.

“When the state interferes with possessory or ownership interests, even temporarily, the state has effectuated a seizure, regardless of whether defendant objected to, or protested, that interference.” Id. at 764.

II. When can officers seize property without a warrant?

A. Items in Plain View

The plain view exception to the warrant requirement allows an officer to seize an object without a warrant if the officer encounters the object in plain view, while in a place where the officer is entitled to be, and the incriminating character of that object is immediately apparent. State v. Currin, 258 Or App 715, 718-19, 311 P3d 903 (2013); see also State v. Sargent, 323 Or 455, 463 n 5, 918 P2d 819 (1996) (stating that “the [initial] intrusion must be valid and it must be immediately apparent that the items [seized] are crime evidence”); State v. Ford, 220 Or App 247, 251, 185 P3d 550 (2008) (“If a police officer is at a lawful vantage point * * * obviously incriminating evidence in plain view may be seized without a warrant.”).

In Horton v. California, 496 US 128, 110 S Ct 2301, 110 L Ed 2d 112 (1990), the United States Supreme Court held that the Fourth Amendment does not prohibit the warrantless seizure of evidence in plain view even though the discovery of the evidence was purposeful, thereby jettisoning the “inadvertence” requirement of some constructions of the plain view doctrine. Although that case involved a seizure under the Fourth Amendment, the Oregon Supreme Court adopted the same approach in State v. Ainsworth, 310 Or 613, 621, n 9, 801 P2d 749 (1990). The Ainsworth court concurred with the Supreme Court’s statement:

“[E]ven handed law enforcement is best achieved by the application of objective standards of conduct, rather than standards that depend upon the subjective state of mind of the officer. The fact that an officer is interested in an item of evidence and fully expects to find it in the course of a search should not invalidate its seizure * * * .”

Id. (quoting Horton, '''' 496 US at [129]).

B. Probable Cause Plus Exigent Circumstances

“Police may search or seize property without a warrant without violating the Oregon or federal constitutions when they have probable cause to believe that the property is evidence of a crime, upon a showing of exigent circumstances.” In re J.C.L., 261 Or App 692, 697, 325 P3d 740 (2014).

Article I, section 9, thus, permits a search or seizure under exigent circumstances, i.e., “a situation that requires the police to act swiftly to prevent danger to life or serious damage to property, or to forestall a suspect’s escape or the destruction of evidence.” State v. Stevens, 311 Or 119, 126, 806 P2d 92 (1991) (when officers are “presented with both probable cause to believe that a crime had occurred and an exigent circumstance,” warrantless action may be justified).

Exigent circumstances exist when a reasonable person in the police officer’s position would determine under the circumstances that immediate action is necessary to prevent the disappearance, dissipation, or destruction of evidence. State v. Snow, 337 Or 219, 94 P3d 872 (2004); State v. Meharry, 342 Or 173, 177 149 P3d 1155 (2006); State v. Miller, 300 Or 203, 229, 709 P2d 225 (1985). It is the state’s burden to show exigency by establishing both that the destruction of evidence was imminent and that a warrant could not have otherwise been expeditiously obtained. In re J.C.L., 261 Or App at 697. Once the officer has a container in his possession, he cannot search the container even if he reasonably believes that it contains a weapon. State v. Booker, 110 Or App 6, 820 P2d 1378 (1992).

The following cases illustrate situations that are not considered exigent. In State v. Price, 92 Or App 669, 759 P2d 1130 (1988), officers received information that defendant was bringing marijuana to work in her purse. When confronted by police, she refused to submit to a search. The police seized the purse and applied for a warrant to search it. Because probable cause existed, the only issue was whether exigent circumstances were also present. The court found no exigent circumstances because police knew that the defendant was carrying the marijuana on a continuing basis. And an officer cannot create exigent circumstances by his own action or inaction. Accordingly, the seizure of the purse and the fruits of that seizure were suppressed.

In State v. Ali, 105 Or App 193, 803 P2d 1231 (1991), the defendant was stopped for a traffic violation. The officer saw beer cans protruding from under the front seat. He further saw the passenger roll up a small piece of paper and place it under the seat. The officer seized the beer cans and the paper wad. He testified that there was nothing about the appearance of the wad that indicated to him that it contained drugs and nothing about the appearance of the passengers that indicated to him that they were using drugs. He testified that he simply suspected that defendant “was hiding something.” The Court of Appeals concluded that the officer lacked probable cause and thus, the state could not rely on the exigent circumstances exception to justify the seizure of the paper.

2024 Update

The exigency exception permits police to seize and retain property only while the corresponding exigent circumstances last. State v. Thompson, 370 Or 273 (October 13, 2022). In Thompson, the defendant was shot while trying to rob the victim. The defendant sought treatment at a hospital. At the hospital, police warrantlessly seized the defendant’s cellphone as likely containing evidence of the shooting and other crimes, on the basis that defendant might destroy the phone or its contents. Police held the phone for five days before seeking a warrant.

The court held: (1) The warrantless retention of the cellphone for five days was not justified by the exigent circumstances exception, because any exigency had dissipated and was attributable only to the state’s failure to act sooner; (2) The unlawful seizure tainted the subsequent warrant and some subsequent interview statements that defendant made when confronted with the “call logs” from his phone, requiring suppression.

C. Officer Safety Seizure

Where the officer-safety exception initially authorizes a patdown of the defendant, and the officer feels a container in the defendant’s pockets:

“Seizure of a closed container on officer safety grounds is valid only if there exists ‘reasonable suspicion that the object “might” contain a weapon.’ Id. at 244, 920 P.2d 1131. Such reasonable suspicion exists where: (1) the container has the physical capacity to conceal a weapon; and (2) ‘under the totality of the circumstances, there was a reasonable suspicion that it did contain a weapon.’ State v. Blevins, 142 Or App 237, 920 P2d 1131 (1996), rev den 327 Or 521, 971 P2d 408 (1998).”

State v. Gilkey, 172 Or App 95, 100, 18 P3d 402 (2001). Additionally, when officers discover a closed container during an officer-safety patdown that may contain a weapon, the seizure of the container is sufficient to protect officer safety but a warrantless search of the container is not justified by officer safety concerns. For example, in State v. Jones, 103 Or App 316, 319 797 P2d 385 (1990), the court explained:

“Even if the officer was justified in removing the box for his own protection or to prevent defendant from escaping * * * once it was in his possession * * * If he thought it contained a razor blade that could be used as a weapon or means of escape, he could simply have withheld it from defendant.”

(Citations omitted); see also State v. Meyer, 120 Or App 319, 331, 852 P2d 879 (1993) (“Any threat that the box might have posed and, accordingly, any authority to act for officer safety, dissipated as soon as the box was in the control of the police.”).

That rule has existed for a quarter century and was well expressed in State v. Schellhorn:

“[O]nce the officer had seized the purse, he no longer had any reason to believe that it still posed an immediate threat to him. The fact that the purse was unusually heavy may have given the officer reason to believe that it contained a weapon. However, the officer had the purse in his possession and, if he believed that it contained a weapon, he could have taken steps to protect himself without opening it. Before the officer could open the purse and inspect its contents, he was required to have probable cause to believe that it contained evidence of a crime and there had to be exigent circumstances to justify not obtaining a search warrant. Neither of those conditions existed. Thus, the officer’s inspection of the contents of the purse after seizing it was unlawful.”

95 Or App 297, 301, 769 P2d 221 (1989).

E. Automobile Exception

The Oregon Supreme Court explained in State v. Brown, 301 Or 268, 278, 721 P2d 1357 (1986), that probable cause that a vehicle contains contraband would authorize the seizure of the automobile. The court explained:

“We agree with the proposition that if police have probable cause to believe that a person’s automobile, which is mobile when stopped by police, contains contraband or crime evidence, the privacy rights of our citizens are subjected to no greater governmental intrusion if the police are authorized to conduct an immediate on-the-scene search of the vehicle than to seize the vehicle and hold it until a warrant is obtained. The police ticket to admission into a stopped mobile vehicle is probable cause. The test is whether a magistrate could issue a constitutionally sound search warrant based on the probable cause articulated by the officers.

“* * * * *  

“We * * * do not address in this opinion whether a warrant for the search and seizure of a parked or impounded automobile is required.

“* * * * *

“As we said in State v. Greene, supra, and State v. Quinn, supra, we agree with the reasoning of the United States Supreme Court that for constitutional purposes no difference exists between, on the one hand, seizing and holding a car before presenting the probable cause issue to a magistrate and, on the other hand, carrying out an immediate search without a warrant. Given probable cause to search, either course is reasonable under the Oregon Constitution.

Id. at 276-78.

The automobile exception does “does not permit a warrantless search of a defendant’s vehicle when the vehicle is parked, immobile, and unoccupied at the time that the police encounter it in connection with a crime.” State v. Kurokawa-Lasciak, 351 Or 179, 181, 263 P3d 336 (2011). The exception only applies if the car to be searched was mobile when the officers first encountered it in connection with a crime or a traffic violation. See id.; see also State v. Bliss, 283 Or App 833, 841, 390 P3d 1099 (2017) (holding that the “in connection with a crime” requirement in Kurokawa-Lasciak is satisfied when a vehicle is mobile when officers see the driver commit a traffic violation).

The reasoning behind the automobile exception is peculiar to mobile automobiles, which by their nature create an imminent risk that any evidence contained inside will be driven away before police can obtain a warrant. The idea that seizing property and awaiting a warrant is no more intrusive than searching the property without a warrant has never been extended beyond the unique circumstances of a mobile automobile.

F. Packages Seized From the Stream of Mail

Postal employees can take a package out of the stream of mail and let a drug-detection dog sniff it without seizing the package. State v. Sholedice/Smith, 364 Or 146, 161-62, 431 P3d 386 (2018). Law enforcement officers or postal employees seize the package if they remove it from the stream of mail, take it to the recipient’s address, and ask for consent to search the package without delivering it first. Id. at 167. But the seizure of the package is reasonable if officers believe that the package contains contraband. Id. at 169-70.

III. Can police seize property if the officer has reasonable suspicion that it contains contraband?

In State v. Juarez-Godinez, 326 Or 1, 942 P2d 772 (1997), the court implicitly rejected the proposition that “reasonable suspicion” is sufficient to permit investigative detentions of personal property under Article I, section 9. The court did so by first analyzing the constitutionality of the property seizure under the exception recognized by Article I, section 9, and—finding none applicable—holding that the police therefore violated the Oregon Constitution. Id. at 9.

The court then concluded that the officers had also violated the Fourth Amendment—expressly noting that it contains a reasonable-suspicion–based exception to the warrant requirement—but held the state’s evidence insufficient to justify that exception:

“Although the question is a close one, it does not appear to us that the result in this case would be different under the Fourth Amendment. Although the United States Supreme Court holds that investigative detentions of personal property can be constitutionally valid, United States v. Place, 462 US 696, 706, 103 S Ct 2637, 2644, 77 L Ed 2d 110 (1983)[(permitting police to detain items of property for investigation within the parameters established by Terry v. Ohio, if the officer has reasonable suspicion that the property contains contraband or evidence of a crime)], the facts arousing the officer’s suspicions here were too minimal, and the duration of the seizure too lengthy, to meet the requirements of the Place rule.”

Consequently, the court held, “trial court’s order suppressing evidence was, on the facts found by that court, constitutionally correct under both state and federal constitutions.”

The Oregon Supreme Court did not in Juarez-Godinez, nor has it in any other case, suggested that the Oregon Constitution contains an exception analogous to the federal rule from Place.

IV. When can officers seize a place?

A warrantless seizure of premises and its various contents is invalid unless it falls within one of the few established and well-delineated exceptions to the warrant requirement, such as the doctrine of probable cause plus exigent circumstances. State v. Hudson, 253 Or App 327, 342, 290 P3d 868 (2012); see also State v. White, 211 Or App 210, 216, 154 P3d 124 (2007) (acknowledging that police could not order the defendant to stay out of his house, which was involved in an arson investigation, unless the defendant consented to staying out of his house). The Oregon Supreme Court has held that entering premises to secure them prior to making a warrantless arrest of an occupant is not lawful absent unforeseen and compelling circumstances which would independently justify a warrantless entry and search. State v. Matsen/Wilson, 287 Or 581, 589, 601 P2d 784 (1979).

Even where the seizure of a residence and its contents is justified initially, if the seizure continues “beyond a reasonable time after the arrest warrant and related matters [have been] completed,” any evidence discovered during the unreasonable period of time should be suppressed. State v. Sargent, 323 Or 455, 462, 918 P2d 819 (1996).
