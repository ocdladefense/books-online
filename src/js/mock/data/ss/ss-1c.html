I. Was the Government Action a Seizure?

Both Article 1, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution and the Fourth Amendment require that seizures be reasonable. What constitutes an unreasonable seizure differs depending on whether it is analyzed under the State or the Federal Constitution and whether it involves the seizure of a person or property.

A. Article 1, Section 9, Seizures

1. Article 1, Section 9, Seizures of a Person

“[T]here are three generally recognized categories of street encounters between policeman and citizen. In descending order of justification, they are: (1) arrest, justified only by probable cause; (2) temporary restraint of the citizen’s liberty (a ‘stop’), justified by reasonable suspicion (or reliable indicia) of the citizen’s criminal activity; and (3) questioning without any restraint of liberty (mere conversation), requiring no justification.” State v. Warner, 284 Or 147, 161, 585 P2d 681, 689 (1978). “The thing that distinguishes ‘seizures’—that is, ‘stops’ and ‘arrests’—from encounters that are ‘mere conversation’ is the imposition, either by physical force or through some ‘show of authority,’ of some restraint on the individual’s liberty.” State v. Ashbaugh, 349 Or 297, 309, 244 P3d 360, 366 (2010). Thus, “[a] ‘seizure’ of a person occurs under Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution: (a) if a law enforcement officer intentionally and significantly restricts, interferes with, or otherwise deprives an individual of that individual’s liberty or freedom of movement; or (b) if a reasonable person under the totality of the circumstances would believe that (a) above has occurred.” Ashbaugh, 349 Or at 316.

“Under that test, the lodestar for determining whether an officer has seized a defendant under Article I, section 9, is whether the officer restricted the defendant’s liberty or freedom of movement by a show of authority, State v. Rodgers/Kirkeby, 347 Or 610, 621–22, 227 P3d 695 (2010), which may be established through, among other circumstances, the content of the officer’s questions to the defendant or the officer’s manner or actions during the encounter, State v. Levias, 242 Or App 264, 266–67, 255 P3d 611 (2011). Moreover, a reasonable person would believe that an officer’s actions amounted to such a show of authority ‘if the person knew that he or she was the subject of a criminal investigation.’ State v. Radtke, 242 Or App 234, 239, 255 P3d 543 (2011).”

State v. Zaccone, 245 Or App 560, 565, 261 P3d 1287, 1289-90 (2011) rev den, 355 Or 381 (2014). “Because of the diversity of potential police-citizen encounters, the inquiry necessarily is fact-specific and requires an examination of the totality of the circumstances involved.” State v. Backstrand, 354 Or 392, 399, 313 P3d 1084, 1091 (2013). Thus, “[a] seizure exists if, under the totality of the circumstances, an officer’s conduct would be reasonably perceived ‘as coercive in the sense that it would cause the citizen to reasonably believe that the officer is intentionally restraining the citizen’s liberty or freedom of movement in a significant way—that is, in a way that exceeds the bounds of ordinary social encounters between private citizens.’” State v. Zamora-Martinez, 264 Or App 50 (2014) (citing Ashbaugh, 349 Or 297). Thus, the question is whether, based on the totality of the circumstances, a reasonable person would believe that an officer had explicitly or implicitly conveyed to defendant that he was not free to terminate the encounter or otherwise go about his ordinary affairs. Zamora-Martinez, 264 Or App 50 . “The duration of the detention or intensity of the officer’s actions can convert a stop into an arrest under Article I, section 9.” State v. Hebrard, 244 Or App 593, 598, 260 P3d 759 (2011). “A seizure ends when a reasonable person under the totality of the circumstances would no longer believe that his or her liberty is being restricted.” State v. Lowell, 275 Or App 365, 373, 364 P3d 34 (2015).

a. Stops

i. Requests for Identification

In 2013 the Oregon Supreme Court addressed what facts can transform a mere conversation into a stop-level seizure in a series of cases. In Backstrand, Highley, and Anderson, the court addressed whether an officer’s request for identification automatically converted a police-citizen encounter into a seizure under Article I, section 9.

Prior to those cases, the Court of Appeals had consistently held that “[w]hen an officer takes and retains an individual’s identification, the officer has stopped the person.” State v. Smith, 252 Or App 518, 522-23, 287 P3d 1210 (2012) (citing State v. Ayles, 348 Or 622, 628, 237 P3d 805 (2010); State v. Thompkin, 341 Or 368, 378-79, 143 P3d 530 (2006); State v. Lay, 242 Or App 38, 44, 252 P3d 850 (2011); Radtke, 242 Or App at 239–40).

The supreme court had also held that “[w]hen an officer conducts a warrant check or other investigation that could result in the defendant’s immediate citation or arrest, the officer has stopped the person.” Id. (citing State v. Hall, 339 Or 7, 10, 115 P3d 908 (2005); State v. Parker, 242 Or App 387, 394, 255 P3d 624 (2011)).

In Backstrand, however, the court held that requesting identification from a citizen and verifying that identification do not alone effectuate a per se seizure, but that the “content of the questions, the manner of asking them, or other actions that police take (along with the circumstances in which they take them) [might] convey to a reasonable person that the police are exercising their authority to coercively detain the citizen[.]” 354 Or at 412. That is, the court announced the following general rule:

“[P]olice requests for information or cooperation do not implicate Article I, section 9, as long as the officer does no more than seek the individual’s cooperation through noncoercive questioning and conduct. A request for identification, in and of itself, is not a seizure. Nor is an officer’s act of checking the validity of that identification, in and of itself, a seizure. For a request and verification of identification to amount to a seizure, something more is required on an officer’s part. Either through the context, the content or manner of questioning, or the other circumstances of the encounter, the officer must convey to a reasonable person that the officer is exercising his or her authority to significantly restrain the citizen’s liberty or freedom of movement.”

Id. at 417.

In Backstrand, the court held that the defendant was not seized when a police officer approached him inside an adult store because he thought the defendant was a minor, asked him how old he was, and asked to see his identification and after holding it for 10–15 seconds returned it to defendant. Id. at 414-416. The court concluded that, because there was no “accompanying exercise of authority to restrain,” and the officer was not “overbearing, intimidating, or coercive in his demeanor or behavior,” a reasonable person would not believe “that the officer had significantly restricted his or her liberty” (emphasis in original). Id. at 414. The court reasoned that a reasonable person engaged in an age-restricted activity would expect to be questioned about his or her age. For example, “proof-of-age requests and examinations are customarily made * * * by private proprietors of businesses (bartenders, clerks of stores where alcohol or tobacco are sold) as well as by law enforcement personnel.” Id. at 415. Accordingly, asking a person’s age is not conduct that is “significantly beyond that accepted in ordinary social intercourse.” Id. at 416.

Importantly, the court affirmed its prior holding in Hall by expressly treating it as an example of a properly analyzed seizure and distinguishing it from Backstrand '' on its facts.Id.at 411-12. The court explained that inHallan officer had parked next to the defendant as the defendant was walking along a street and motioned for the defendant to approach. As the defendant neared the patrol car, the officer got out of his car and asked for the defendant’s identification, which he briefly retained while requesting a warrant check. The officer then returned the identification, and while awaiting the results of the warrant check, asked defendant if he was carrying any weapons, knives, or illegal drugs and then for consent to search.Id.at 411 (recounting the facts inHall'').

Hall held that the encounter became a seizure when the officer asked for the defendant’s identification and ran the warrants check. The Backstrand '' court explained that, at that point, the defendant was aware that he was the subject of a pending warrant check and, because of that fact, it was “difficult to posit” that a reasonable person would have felt free to leave (discussing the legal analysis inHall).Id.'' at 412.

In addition to that fact, the officer “‘did nothing to dispel what would have been an objectively reasonable belief that defendant was restrained from leaving until [the officer] had received the results of the warrant check. Instead, immediately upon returning [the] defendant’s identification card, [the officer] questioned [the] defendant about whether [the] defendant was carrying any weapons, knives, or illegal drugs, and he asked [the] defendant for consent to search [his] person.’” Id. (quoting Hall).

Thus, while a warrant check alone may not constitute a per se stop in every case, if the officer fails to inform the person that they are free to leave and then asks potentially incriminating questions or for consent to search, the circumstances taken together may amount to a stop.

In State v. Highley, 354 Or 459, 313 P3d 1068 (2013), the police officer’s request for the defendant’s identification, probationary status check, and request for consent to search did not constitute a seizure. The officer called out to the driver of a vehicle that the defendant was in and the officer then talked only to the driver. The defendant and another occupant walked away from the car, went to an apartment, and then returned of their own accord. The officer said nothing to the defendant and other person during this time while the defendant and the other person “milled” around the car. As they milled around, the officer asked the defendant if he was still on probation. The defendant replied that he was not. The officer then asked the defendant for his identification, and held the defendant’s license only long enough to write down the license number. The officer informed the defendant that he had confirmed he was no longer on probation and then asked the defendant for consent to search his pockets. None of these interactions elevated the encounter to a seizure.

In State v. Anderson, 354 Or 440, 313 P3d 1113 (2013), the content of officers’ requests for identification from the occupants of a stopped vehicle, the manner in which the requests were made, and the overall context of the contact did not convey to the occupants that they were not allowed to leave, as required to elevate the encounter to level of seizure. Their brief verbal exchanges and inquiries did not communicate an exercise of authority to restrain the occupants of the vehicle; the officers’ tone and manner were not overbearing or controlling; the content of the brief verbal exchange was not coercive; the officers took no physical action other than to approach the parked car; and the fact that officers outnumbered occupants by one would not cause a reasonable person to believe that officers were significantly restricting his or her liberty.

After that trio of cases, the Court of Appeals concluded in State v. Thompson, that a request for identification and warrants check are now to be treated as factors in the totality-of-the-circumstances analysis, but do not “necessarily and always mean that the person was stopped.” 264 Or App 754, 759-60, 333 P3d 1125 (2014). The Thompson court noted that Backstrand left open the possibility that if the officer does not promptly return the identification, that might still amount to a per se seizure:

“The one possibly remaining per se rule is this: When an officer takes a person’s identification card and retains it for more than a reasonable time, the encounter is a stop. Briefly retaining an identification card is not a ‘significant’ restraint of a person’s liberty or freedom of movement, but at several places, the court in Backstrand implies that an unreasonably extended retention is.”

Thompson, 264 Or App at 760 (emphasis in original). The Thompson court did not ultimately resolve the question of whether such conduct amounts to a per se seizure, because the state had failed to meet its burden of establishing that the officer had only briefly retained possession of the identification. That fact when combined with the other factors in the case (three officers present, officer asked the defendant to change her location, officer asked for her identification, officer told her that they suspected drug activity on the premises, and officer asked if she was a drug user) allowed the court to conclude with “no difficulty” that the defendant had been seized.

Practice Tips

Winning the Stop Despite Backstrand

Develop the facts relevant to establishing the “show of authority.” Many important facts will not be included in the police report, making it necessary to elicit them at the suppression hearing.

Establish, e.g., number officers, number of cars, lights, sirens, air horn or other loud or intimidating noises, hand gestures, body language, tone of voice, specific questions, topic of the questions, police conduct toward other people at the scene, implication of wrongdoing, accusation of wrongdoing, location of the encounter, requests, directives or instructions, attempts to control defendant’s movements or requests to change defendant’s location or position.

After incorporating the newly elicited facts, it may be necessary to argue that the stop occurred earlier in the encounter than the point identified in the written motion to suppress.

In that scenario, first argue that the stop occurred at the earliest moment at which it is arguable; then argue that the stop occurred by the time that the show of authority was at its height.

If an attorney fails to identify the conduct constituting the initial stop, the Court of Appeals may conclude that the officer gathered enough evidence to establish reasonable suspicion between the time that the initial stop occurred and the later time that the attorney argued that the stop occurred. In that situation, the client may lose on appeal, even if the officer lacked reasonable suspicion at the time of the initial stop.

The Court of Appeals has since identified several factors which, in addition to a request for I.D., amount to a stop:

·      Accusing the defendant of a law violation. State v. Jackson, 268 Or App 139 (2014) (officer accused the defendant of committing a traffic infraction).

·       Openly investigating a potential law violation. State v. Rodriguez-Perez, 262 Or App 206, (2014).

·       Age- or identity-related crime where the circumstances support a reasonable inference that defendant’s I.D. was being checked to confirm its validity. Rodriguez-Perez, 262 Or App 206.

·       The defendant’s awareness that the I.D. was being used to run a warrants check might be sufficient. State v. Campbell, 263 Or App 315, 319 (2014) (“An individual’s awareness that the officer is using the individual’s identification to run a warrant check is among the circumstances that may contribute to a reasonable belief that an individual’s liberty is significantly restricted.”).

·       Holding onto a defendant’s I.D. for an unreasonable amount of time. Thompson, 264 Or App 754.

·       The context in which the request for I.D. was made would cause a reasonable person to think he or she could not continue to go about his or her affairs without providing it. , Zamora-Martinez, 264 Or App 50.

·       An officer’s “terse” or “firm” tone of voice can convey an assertion of authority that, combined with investigatory questioning, indicates that the person is not free to leave. State v. Norton, 270 Or App 584, 349 P3d 576 (2015).

2024 Update After requesting and obtaining the defendant’s license, the officer did not proceed with verifying the identification or asking any questions about the identification, as a person might reasonably expect; rather, he retained the identification and questioned the defendant about the contents of his bag. State v. McKibben, 320 Or App 26, 31–32, 512 P3d 464 (2022). A reasonable person in the defendant’s position would understand that the officer’s retention of the defendant’s identification while questioning him was a show of authority that restrained his “liberty or freedom of movement.”

ii. Retention of Identification

2024 Update Retaining a person’s driver’s license or ID for longer than reasonably necessary to verify the person’s identity, driving status, and warrant status—or not promptly returning it after receiving the results of those checks—can result in the seizure of the person. State v. Orman, 322 Or App 707 (2022) (November 16, 2022). There, the defendant, a passenger in a car, was stopped without reasonable suspicion when an officer retained his ID “past a ‘reasonable period for purposes of examining and verifying it[,]’” and in particular, failed to return it after the officer was informed by dispatch that the license was “clean.” Id. at 716-17. In addition, multiple officers reported to the scene and essentially guarded the defendant, and the questions that the police asked defendant conveyed to defendant that he was suspected of criminal activity. 

iii. Directives

2024 Update The courts have held time and again that directives restricting hand movement are Article I, section 9, seizures. State v. Najar, 287 Or App. 98, 106, 401 P3d 1205 (2017) (officers seized the defendant when they told him, “Can I—keep your hands where I can see them. Go in with one hand”); State v. Rudnitskyy, 266 Or App. 560, 564, 338 P3d 742 (2014), rev den, 357 Or 112, 346 P3d 1213 (2015) (the defendant was seized by order to place his hands on the dashboard); State v. Courtney, 242 Or App 321, 333, 255 P3d 577, 584 (2011) (Stating that the factor that weighed most significantly in the court’s determination that the defendant was seized was that “[the officer] directed defendant to put his hands on top of his head, with his fingers interlaced.”); State v. Shaw, 230 Or App. 257, 264-65, 215 P3d 105, rev den, 347 Or 365, 222 P3d 1091 (2009) (the defendant was seized by order to show his hands to the officer); State v. Ruiz, 196 Or App. 324, 326-27, 101 P3d 824 (2004), rev den, 338 Or 363, 109 P3d 797 (2005) (the defendant was seized by an order to remove his hand from his pocket). “In our ‘ordinary affairs,’ we have the freedom to move our hands freely, and a command from an armed, uniformed officer limiting that freedom most certainly communicates to a person that she is not free to go about her ordinary affairs (at least not without provoking a responsive use of force).” State v. Soto-Navarro, 309 Or App 218, 228–29, 482 P3d 150, 156–57 (2021).

Similarly, an officer may seize a person by directing the person’s movements. See, e.g., State v. Mastin, 203 Or App 366, 371, 124 P3d 1275 (2005), adh’d to on recons, 205 Or App 528, 134 P3d 1052 (2006) (police officer’s directive to defendant to “get back in the vehicle” from which he had just exited constituted a stop); State v. Crandall, 197 Or App 591, 595, 108 P3d 16, 18 (2005), rev’d on other grounds, 340 Or 645 (2006) (defendant was stopped when officer “told him to ‘stop’ and to ‘come here’ to where [the officer] was standing”).

Moreover, even relatively casual questioning by an officer can convert a “mere conversation” into a “stop” when the questioning requires a person to change directions, move at the officer’s request, or alter his or her course of conduct. See State v. Rocha-Ramos, 161 Or App 306, 985 P2d 217 (1999) (officer stopped defendant when she asked the defendant if he could sit down and wait rather than use the bathroom and blocked his route of travel to the bathroom); State v. Johnson, 105 Or App 587, 590, 805 P2d 747 (1991) (holding that the defendant was seized after police encountered the defendant in a parking lot and asked if he knew anything about a fight that had been reported in the area, whether he had anything in his pocket, and then said, “I can’t see you back there, can you step out [from behind the bush]?”); but see State v. Cline, 264 Or App. 293, 299, 330 P3d 1255 (2014) (officer’s request to “stay where you are” or “stay there,” as defendant began to approach the police cruiser, was not a show of authority necessary for a seizure, but a de minimis request that, at most, slightly altered the location of an ongoing, casual, and consensual conversation).

iv. Accusations of Wrongdoing/Subject of an Investigation

2024 Update “[W]hen an officer ‘makes a direct and unambiguous accusation’ that an individual has committed a violation or crime, the officer has stopped that individual.” Jackson, 268 Or App at 149. Similarly, an officer seized a defendant when the officer asked the defendant if she possessed illegal drugs, the defendant took offense to the question, and the officer told her “[i]t would be easy to prove me wrong * * * [b]y showing me that your purse doesn’t contain drugs.” State v. Nelson, 294 Or App 793, 798, 433 P3d 370 (2018).

Additionally, police conduct rises to the level of a stop “if a reasonable person in defendant’s position would be aware that he was the subject of a police investigation.” Levias, 242 Or App at 268; Radtke, 242 Or App at 239 (a reasonable person would believe that an officer’s actions amounted to such a show of authority “if the person knew that he or she was the subject of a criminal investigation”). Where an officer did not tell the defendant (who had expressed concern about a possible warrant) that the warrant check came back clear and instead directed the defendant to stand at the front of the patrol car, it was reasonable to infer that the defendant was the subject of a continuing investigation, and hence stopped. Zaccone, 246 Or App at 567.

Similarly, in State v. Brown, 293 Or App 772, 774-76, 427 P3d 221 (2018), the court held that an officer seized the defendant when the officer followed the defendant’s car for several minutes and, when the defendant parked in a vacant lot, the officer approached the defendant, asked if she had permission to be there, and asked whether she had drugs. The court concluded that a reasonable person in the defendant’s position would understand that the officer “was intent on questioning her and, having pursued her conspicuously for an extended time and tracked her down to the back side of a deserted building, would not allow her to leave until he did so.” Id. at 781. That is, even if an officer does not accuse the defendant of a crime, an officer’s targeted scrutiny of the defendant can effect a seizure. In State v. Leiby, 293 Or App 293, 294-95, 427 P3d 1141 (2018), an officer, acting on a hunch, followed the defendant’s car for several minutes and, when defendant parked and got out, approached defendant and asked, “Do you want to tell me why you’re trying to avoid me?” The court concluded that the officer seized defendant. Id. at 299. The court reasoned that two facts turned the encounter into a seizure. First, the officer “followed defendant in an obvious fashion on a road,” which would convey to a reasonable person in defendant’s position that the officer was scrutinizing him. Id. at 298. Second, the officer’s statement about the defendant avoiding him, “[a]lthough framed as a question,” was “confrontational in the context of this interaction.” Id. The court explained that the officer’s question implied that defendant had “a duty to stop and interact with” the officer and that the defendant’s behavior was “nefarious and warrant[ed] explanation.” Id.

Although the “subject of a police investigation” factor remains valid, the Nelson court drew a fine distinction between a direct accusation of criminal activity and conduct conveying mere suspicion. Nelson, 294 Or App '''' at 798.

“Other cases in which officers accused persons of drug use or possession are instructive here. In State v. K. A. M., 361 Or 805, 807-08, 812-13, 401 P3d 774 (2017), the Supreme Court held that an officer stopped a youth when he effectively accused the youth’s friend of methamphetamine use and entered, without explanation, a bedroom in which the youth and his friend were waiting. The combination ‘created a coercive atmosphere that reasonably conveyed that [the youth and his friend] were suspected of illegal drug use and were not free to leave until [the officer] had completed his inquiry.’ Id. at 811, 401 P3d 774. The court also noted ‘other circumstances’ supporting a stop, including that the officer asked the youths whether they ‘had anything illegal on them, a question that, given [the officer’s] prior accusation of methamphetamine use, reasonably added to the coercive pressure.’ Id. Similarly, in Allen, 224 Or App. at 531, 198 P3d 466, we held that an officer had stopped the defendant when he told her that he ‘knew she was coming from a dope house’ and ‘that if she was honest and gave [him] the dope [he] would give her a citation’ (brackets in original). We explained that the officer’s statements were tantamount to an announcement that ‘the officer had just seen defendant break the law.’ Id. In contrast, in State v. Baker, 154 Or App. 358, 360, 961 P2d 913, rev den, 327 Or 553, 971 P2d 409 (1998), we held that the officer did not stop the defendant, whom he saw coming out of a known ‘crack house,’ when the officer asked defendant, ‘[D]id you buy any good crack in there?’ Baker is distinguished from Allen because ‘the officer asked the defendant a question and did not announce that he had seen the defendant break the law.’ State v. Morfin-Estrada, 251 Or App. 158, 166 n. 4, 283 P3d 378, rev den, 352 Or 565, 291 P3d 737 (2012) (internal quotation marks and brackets omitted).” Id.

2024 Update In State v. Reyes-Herrera, 369 Or 54, 67-68, 500 P3d 1 (2021), the Oregon Supreme Court held that questions posed by an officer can effectuate a stop even if they do not amount to a “direct and unambiguous accusation” as the Court of Appeals had seemed to suggest was required in State v. Nelson, 294 Or App 793, 798, 433 P3d 370 (2018). Rather, a stop occurs whenever police convey that they are investigating criminal activity under circumstances that would cause a reasonable person to believe that the person must remain and respond to the police.

In Reyes-Herrera, a uniformed police officer approached the defendant in an alley to investigate a possible drug deal. The officer told the defendant he was not in trouble and was free to leave. The officer then told the defendant that he had observed him walk away from a conversation with another man who was counting money. He then asked the defendant whether he had purchased drugs from the man and whether he possessed drugs. The defendant answered, “no,” twice, and the officer asked for consent to search him.

The court concluded that the totality of the circumstances was such that reasonable people in the defendant’s position would believe that they “must remain where they are and respond.” Id. at 67-68. As such, the defendant was seized. Id. at 66-68.

In distinguishing some of the court’s prior cases that had held certain police questioning amounted to “mere conversation,” the court noted that, when the officer approached the defendant, he did not explain that he had a noninvestigative purpose. Id. at 66. Nor did the officer simply ask for the defendant’s identification. Id. Instead, the officer asked the defendant questions that conveyed that the officer suspected him of drug activity, which were “compounded” by requesting the defendant’s consent to search after the defendant had twice answered “no” to the officer’s questions. Id. at 66-67.

Notably, the court rejected the state’s argument that “the line between relaxed conversation and coercive questioning depends on whether a court determines that an officer made a declarative statement or asked a question, or whether the officer’s comments fit the textbook definition of an accusation.” Id. at 61. Instead, the totality of circumstances determines whether the encounter amounted to a stop. Id. at 68. A person is stopped if a reasonable person would believe that they must “remain where they are and respond” to the questioning. Id.; see also State v. Bryars, 319 Or App 464, 473-74, 510 P3d 261 (2022) (drug-related questioning followed by request for consent to search equates to a seizure).

In State v. Alcaraz, 318 Or App 179, 508 P3d 13 (2022), rev allowed, 370 Or 197 (2022), the court held that the defendant was stopped when a sheriff’s deputy in a marked patrol boat asked the defendant, who was actively fishing, if he had the required fishing license; moreover, fishing regulations obligated the defendant to submit to a fishing license inspection. According to the court,

“A reasonable person in defendant’s position—that is, a person actively fishing from a boat on a public lake, who is required to have a fishing license and submit to its inspection upon request—would not believe that, upon a sheriff’s deputy asking him if he had a license, he could simply start up his boat and motor away without answering.”

Id. at 188; see also State v. Almahmood, 308 Or App 795, 482 P3d 88 (2021) (holding that a passenger on a TriMet train was seized when uniformed police officers boarded the train, announced a fare check, told passengers to display proof of their paid fares, and began checking passengers’ fares row by row).

Vague expression of generalized suspicion does not necessarily amount to a stop. State v. Thier, 322 Or App 680 (November 16, 2022). In Thier, the court held that an officer did not seize the defendant when he approached her as she stepped out of her car, asked “what was going on,” told her that they “had a lot of problems in the area,” and asked if she had her ID “with her by chance.” Citing State v. Anderson, 354 Or 440, 450 (2013), '' the court noted that police officers are free to approach people in public spaces and request information or question them—without it amounting to a seizure—even where the person is discomforted by the officer’s presence or questions. Thier, 322 Or App at 683. Such an encounter may become a stop, however, where the officer “makes a direct and unambiguous accusation” that the individual committed a crime. State v. Nelson, 294 Or App 793, 797 (2018). But the officer can make “statements conveying possible suspicion” or general “inquir[ies] about criminal activity,” without necessarily converting the encounter into a stop.Id.''

The Thier court concluded that the officer’s initial statements, in conjunction with his actions, and his request for the defendant’s identification, were not sufficient—in the totality of the circumstances—to cause a reasonable person to believe that their freedom of movement was significantly restrained. In particular, the officer did not retain the defendant’s ID nor ask any further questions while in possession of it, because he observed the defendant’s meth pipe before she handed over her ID.

By contrast in State v. Elbinger, 322 Or App 498 (October 26, 2022), the court held that an officer had seized the defendant when the officer approached the defendant, who was sitting in a parked car, and asked the defendant about his relationship to a shoplifter, whom the officer told the defendant had just been arrested, and radioed a description of the defendant to another officer. The Elbinger court explained that “probing the defendant’s connection” to a person that police had just taken into custody created a “more coercive atmosphere” that amounted to seizure, despite the officer joking with defendant and speaking jovially with him.

That holding is significant because it expands the principle that a person is stopped when an officer conveys a belief that the person committed a crime, but under Elbinger, a stop also exists when officers probe a person’s “connection” to some other person who is accused of a crime.

2024 Update When an officer approaches a person on the street and engages the person in conversation, the officer must make clear the non-investigatory nature of the contact or request for information; otherwise, even mildly accusatory questioning may amount to a stop. State v. Winter, 328 Or App 747 (2023). In Winter, the officer observed the defendant talking to another woman in front of a residence and then leave on foot. Id. at 748. The officer turned his car around, parked, and called out to the defendant by asking “how she was doing.” Id. The officer did not turn on his overhead lights or block defendant’s path. Id. The officer asked why the defendant had parted ways from the other person when he drove by. Id. The defendant said that she had been dropping off some clothes to be washed. Id. The officer asked if the defendant was “staying clean,” and she admitted that she had not been, because she was an addict. Id. The officer asked if she had “anything” on her person, and she admitted to having needles. Id. The officer asked to search a zippered pouch in her bag, she consented, and he found methamphetamine residue inside. Id. at 749.

In concluding that the officer’s questions leading up to the request for consent constituted a stop, the court distinguished this case from State v. Ashbaugh, 349 Or 297, 309, 244 P3d 360, 366 (2010) ' where the officer had made clear that he had a non-investigatory basis for engaging the defendant in conversation as well as State v. Anderson, 354 Or 440, 450 (2013),' where the officer’s conduct “conveyed that the officers were interested in whatever information the [defendant] might be able to provide.” Id. at 751-52.

Here, the officer did not ask defendant about her “current condition” out of concern for her health or merely to get a better understanding of the circumstances; id. at 752, he did not articulate any non-investigatory reason for contacting her; id. at 752, rather, he hailed the defendant from the street, inquired about her drug use, and asked if she had “anything on her.” Id. at 753. That is a stop under Reyes-Herrera. Id.

b. Traffic Stops

i. Overhead Lights

Using overhead lights to require a motorist to pull over will nearly always constitute a stop. State v. Middleton, 302 Or App 339, 345, 459 P3d 918, 923 (2020) (“[I]t is undisputed that [the officer] acted in a manner that, in most circumstances, would constitute a seizure, specifically a ‘stop’” by following a vehicle with his overhead lights on.”). Specifically, if an officer has “observed someone commit a traffic violation and engaged in the same conduct to investigate the traffic violation, there would be no question that [the officer] had ‘stopped’ the person.” Id. (citing State v. Watson, 353 Or 768, 774, 305 P3d 94 (2013)). See also [https://1.next.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=Y&serNum=2021330937&pubNum=0000641&originatingDoc=I107acd00545811ea851bfabee22f40c8&refType=RP&fi=co_pp_sp_641_622&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.Search) Rodgers/Kirkeby, 347 Or at 622-23, 227 P3d 695] (citing several criminal statutes that apply when a driver fails to stop when directed to do so by a law enforcement officer).

ii. Non-Targeted Efforts to Impart Community-Safety Related Information or Provide Assistance (Gerrish and Holmes)

In State v. Holmes, 311 Or 400, 813 P2d 28 (1991), and State v. Gerrish, 311 Or 506, 815 P2d 1244 (1991), the supreme court created a limited exception to the general rule that police use of overhead lights to stop a vehicle is a seizure. In Holmes, the court held that the defendant-driver was not seized when a police officer standing in the middle of the highway flagged down the defendant’s car to inform him of a detour due to a car accident. 311 Or at 412. Importantly, the officer had set up a roadblock with a pattern of flares and a patrol car, and the accident scene was in view of approaching drivers. Id. at 402. The court reasoned that, because the accident scene was “lit up like a Christmas tree,” the limited and specific nature of the police encounter would have been immediately clear to motorists. Id. at 411. Further, the officer did not question the defendant or request any identification, and the “intrusion was tailored in direction and manner to be insignificantly intrusive.” Id. Gerrish involved a similar roadblock, where police blocked the only road out of a resort in an attempt to find witnesses to a shooting. 311 Or at 508. This court, relying on the reasoning it employed in Holmes, held that the officer did not seize the driver of a car that stopped at the roadblock. Gerrish, 311 Or at 513.

“Together, Holmes and Gerrish stand for the limited proposition that a law enforcement officer constitutionally may halt and briefly detain a person passing through a public area as a means to engage the citizen long enough to impart information or seek the citizen’s cooperation or assistance.” State v. Fair, 353 Or 588, 598, 302 P3d 417, 425 (2013).

Additionally, where an officer uses overhead lights in circumstances that objectively indicate that the officer is doing so to warn oncoming traffic of a roadway hazard or where the defendant indicates that he or she needs assistance, the use of overhead lights does not necessarily establish that the officer is exercising his authority to restrain. State v. Blair, 171 Or App 162, 170-71 (2000), rev den, 332 Or 137 (2001).

iii. Spotlight

In State v. Aronson, 247 Or App. 422, 424, 271 P3d 121 (2011), rev den, 352 Or 33, 281 P3d 611 (2012), a deputy watched the defendant, who appeared to be intoxicated, drive into a shopping center parking lot and park. The deputy stopped his patrol car at an angle behind the defendant’s vehicle, “leaving between one and a half and three car lengths between the two cars. The distance between the two cars would have allowed defendant to pull out of the parking space had he chosen to do so.” Id. The deputy did not activate his lights after stopping but, for officer safety reasons, directed his spotlight at defendant’s car to illuminate it. The court relied on two cases: State v. Deptuch, 95 Or App 54, 767 P2d 471, adh’d to as modified on recons., 96 Or App 228, 772 P2d 442 (1989), and State v. Calhoun, 101 Or App 622, 624, 792 P2d 1223 (1990), to hold that the use of the spotlight did not constitute a seizure.

In both cases the court had held that the shining of a spotlight did not convert the encounter into a seizure because it was not a show of authority that directly restrained liberty or prevented the defendants from leaving.

In light of those cases, the court held that because the officer in Aronson had not parked so close to the defendant’s vehicle to actually prevent the defendant from leaving, the officer had not stopped the defendant. The court did not resolve the question of whether it would have been a stop had the use of the light impaired the defendant’s ability to safely drive away, because those facts had not been elicited at the suppression hearing.

Under a more modern analysis, the focus of any spotlight-based argument should not be on whether the defendant was physically prevented from leaving but whether the spotlight communicated to the defendant that he or she was the subject of a criminal investigation. That is, the light alone may not constitute a seizure (assuming it does not impair the defendant’s vision and ability to safely drive away), but in the context of questions related to criminal activity or other circumstances may amount to a seizure if the totality of the circumstances convey that the defendant was the target of a criminal investigation.

iv. Physically Blocking a Vehicle

Blocking a vehicle in a parking lot or driveway usually amounts to a stop. In State v. Dendy, 294 Or App 539, 542, 432 P3d 357, 359 (2018), the defendant argued that “at the time of the stop—that is, when they blocked in his car—the officers lacked reasonable suspicion that defendant had committed DUII or was about to commit DUII.” The state did not contest that defendant was stopped as soon as the officers blocked in his car, and the court did not disagree, citing State v. Thacker, 264 Or App. 150, 156, 331 P3d 1036 (2014). See also State v. Guest, 207 Or App. 395, 397, 142 P3d 482 (2006), (“The question whether [the officer] stopped defendant upon first pulling into the parking lot depends, in turn, on whether [the] patrol car blocked defendant’s truck, preventing him from leaving the scene.”). Id. at 400; cf. Aronson, 247 Or App at 427-28 (holding that no stop occurred, relying in part of the fact that “the officer did not park his car in a way that prevented defendant from leaving—there was sufficient space between the patrol car and defendant’s vehicle that defendant could have backed out of the parking space and driven away”).

v. Passengers in the Vehicle

Passengers in cars that are stopped for traffic violations are not themselves automatically stopped for purposes of Article I, section 9. State v. Stevens, 364 Or 91, 100, 430 P3d 1059 (2018). Unlike the federal constitution, the Oregon Constitution does not contain a “categorical” rule that such passengers are seized. Id. Despite the absence of a categorical rule, however, the circumstances of the stop may effectuate a seizure of both driver and passenger. A passenger may be seized when police stop a car if there is “something more than the bare fact that the driver was pulled over for a traffic violation.” [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=13534434842727509996&q=State+v.+T.+T.,+308+Or+App+408&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 State v. T. T., 308 Or App 408, 418, 479 P3d 598 (2021)]. The need for “something more” stems from the courts’ rationale behind the absence of a categorical rule in Oregon’s constitution, which is premised on “the proposition that the passengers in a car stopped for a traffic or criminal offense would not understand that the officer’s show of authority in stopping the driver extended to them.” Stevens, 364 Or at 100.

However, “If passengers, under the totality of the circumstances, would understand that the officer’s show of authority in stopping the driver extended to them or that the officer was independently restricting their movement apart from the stop of the driver, the passenger is seized.” State v. Payne, 310 Or App 672, __P3d __ (April 14, 2021) (where driver failed to pull over immediately, the officer “flanked” the vehicle and motioned to the driver to pull over, and, after the driver ultimately complied, the officer approached the vehicle with his gun drawn, the passenger was seized).

2024 Update Police questioning or accusations that implicate all passengers in a vehicle can effectively seize everyone in the vehicle. '''' State v. Vannoy, 326 Or App 11 (2023) (May 17, 2023) In that case, the defendant, a passenger in a car, was stopped under Article I, section 9, when an officer stopped the car, asked the driver how much marijuana was in the vehicle, asked the defendant “about marijuana,” asked the defendant to step out of the car so that he could search it pursuant to the driver’s consent, picked up the defendant’s purse from inside the car and asked for her consent to search it.

Practice Tip

Vannoy ' focused heavily on the fact that the officer’s question about drugs in the vehicle “requested information that implicated all occupants of the vehicle.” When an officer asks questions that implicate all occupants of a vehicle (e.g., asking whether there are guns or drugs in the car), attorneys should be prepared to cite Vannoy' and argue that the questioning created a seizure of all occupants.

vi. Walking a Drug Dog Around a Vehicle

The Court of Appeals has held that walking a drug detection dog around the outside of a vehicle does not automatically constitute a seizure of the passengers because it would not cause a reasonable person to believe that the officer had “intentionally and significantly deprived defendant of liberty or freedom of movement.” State v. Sexton, 278 Or App 1, 7, 378 P3d 83 (2016). This court has further held that a momentary blocking of the passenger door while a drug-detection dog sniffs that area of the vehicle is insufficient to establish a stop of the passenger and convers only “possible suspicion” of criminal activity—not a direct accusation. State v. Rosales, 291 Or App 762, 766, 423 P3d 112 (2018). Nonetheless, the dog sniff is a factor in the totality-of-circumstances analysis.

2024 Update “[A]bsent an independent constitutional justification, a drug-detecting dog * * * generally cannot, consistent with Article I, section 9, sniff a car for drugs during a traffic stop.” State v. Soto-Navarro, 309 Or App 218, 223, 482 P3d 150 (2021).

vii. Hand Signals

When a fully uniformed police officer holds up his hand and waves down a moving vehicle, the failure to stop exposes the driver to being arrested for attempting to elude, ORS 811.540(1)(b)(A), a Class C felony. That statute provides:

(1) A person commits the crime of fleeing or attempting to elude a police officer if:

(a) The person is operating a motor vehicle; and

(b) A police officer who is in uniform and prominently displaying the police officer’s badge of office * * * gives a visual or audible signal to bring the vehicle to a stop, including any signal by hand, voice, emergency light or siren * * * .

ORS 811.540 (1)(b)(A) (emphasis added).

Moreover, failing to stop for an officer’s hand signal could constitute the Class A misdemeanor of interfering with a peace officer, ORS 162.247. A person commits that crime if, knowing that the other person is a peace officer, the person “[r]efuses to obey a lawful order by the peace officer.” ORS 162.247(1)(b). Consequently, when an officer uses a hand signal to direct a person to stop their vehicle, the officer has “stopped” the person. State v. Turner, 221 Or App 621, 625, 191 P3d 697 (2008) (holding that a bicyclist’s liberty was significantly restricted when a uniformed officer motioned with his hand for the bicyclist to pull over.)

viii. Scope of a Traffic Stop

Under State v. Arreola-Botello, 365 Or 695, 714, 451 P3d 939 (2019), an officer who stops a driver for a traffic infraction must focus their actions on investigating the infraction, ascertaining the identity of the driver, and issuing a traffic citation. Officers cannot extend the duration or subject-matter scope of a traffic stop without independent constitutional justification. In other words, if an officer does not have reasonable suspicion of some other crime, the officer cannot use a traffic stop to conduct a criminal investigation or take other investigative actions that are unrelated to the purpose of the stop.

c. Arrests

An “arrest” is a seizure that infringes on a person’s liberty more than does a “stop” and must be supported by probable cause. State v. Morgan, 106 Or App 138, 141, 806 P2d 713 (1991). By statute, an “‘[a]rrest’ means to place a person under actual or constructive restraint or to take a person into custody for the purpose of charging that person with an offense.” ORS 133.005(1).

i. Guns Drawn

Where police approached a vehicle with guns drawn and issued orders, they effected an arrest. State v. Shaw, 3 Or App 346, 348, 473 P2d 159 (1970).

The court assumed that defendant was “arrested” when police surrounded his home with guns drawn and ordered him out of his house. State v. White, 115 Or App 104, 110, 838 P2d 605, 608 (1992).

ii. Handcuffing

In most cases, handcuffing a person automatically converts a stop into an arrest. State v. Hebrard, 244 Or App 593, 598, 260 P3d 759 (2011); see also State v. Koester, 117 Or App 139, 141-42, 843 P2d 968 (1992); State v. Quinn, 112 Or App 608, 612, 831 P2d 48 (1992).

When confronting a handcuffing case, the court has on occasion looked first at whether the handcuffing was justified by the officer-safety exception; and then, only after concluding it was not, declared that the defendant was “arrested” and determined whether the police had the necessary probable cause. See Hebrard, 244 Or App at 598-99 (“Because officer safety concerns did not justify the handcuffing, we conclude that the handcuffing converted the stop into an arrest of defendant.”); State v. Medinger, 235 Or App 88, 93, 230 P3d 76 (2010) (concluding that defendant was “arrested” only after determining that handcuffing defendant and placing him in back of patrol car was not justified by safety concerns); State v. Werowinski, 179 Or App 522, 528, 40 P3d 545 (2002) (same), State v. Johnson, 120 Or App 151, 158, 851 P2d 1160, rev den, 318 Or 26 (1993) (same), Morgan, 106 Or App at 142 (same).

In State v. Ayvazov, 246 Or App 641, 645, 267 P3d 196 (2011), this court acknowledged in dicta that a more precise analytical framework would be to acknowledge that handcuffing an individual entails the imposition of actual restraint and is thus an arrest regardless of its justification. Such an arrest may be justified by one of two exceptions to the warrant requirement: the officer safety exception or probable cause. That is, the first issue is to determine the level of the seizure, and the second is to determine whether that level of seizure was justified. That analysis acknowledges the real level of restriction imposed—actual physical restraint.

Moreover, regardless of whether the officer is subjectively “detaining” the person in handcuffs for officer safety purposes or for the purpose of taking the person to jail, the state must establish the elements of the two potential justifications (officer-safety exception to the warrant requirement or probable cause of a crime). Koester, 117 Or App at 141-42 (holding that the defendant was under arrest even though the officer told the defendant at the time he was handcuffed and placed in a patrol car that he was being handcuffed for safety reasons). That is, regardless of the officer’s subjective intentions, “[t]he duration of the detention or intensity of the officer’s actions can convert a stop into an arrest under Article I, section 9.” Hebrard, 244 Or App at 598.

2024 Update The fact of prior handcuffing—even if justified by the officer-safety exception—is highly relevant to establishing that the defendant was “arrested” later after the handcuffs were removed. Other relevant factors include the number of police officers involved, their purpose in being on the scene, and the kinds of questions they posed to the defendant. State v. Madden, 315 Or App 787, 793-94, 502 P3d 746 (2021).

iii. Physical Restraint

The state conceded and the court agreed that the “defendant was in custody when the state concedes that he was—when the officers went ‘hands on’ with him.” State v. Beeson, 307 Or App 808, 819, 479 P3d 576, 583–84 (2020), opinion adh’d to as modified on recons, 309 Or App 787 (2021) (holding that the defendant was in custody for purposes of Miranda warnings when officers restrained him from confronting the victim of the hit and run incident at issue).

iv. Verbal Assertion: “You’re under arrest.”

At least for the purposes of establishing the offense of third-degree escape, telling a person that he or she is under arrest (or using “words manifesting the intent to arrest”) are sufficient to impose “constructive restraint” and thus sufficient to establish that the person was in “custody.” State v. Thomas, 229 Or App 453, 456, 211 P3d 979, 980 (2009). Thomas thus provides a basis to argue that the officer must have probable cause prior to announcing that a person is under arrest.

2. Article 1, Section 9, Seizures of Property

“A seizure of property occurs when there is a significant interference with a person’s possessory or ownership interests in property” by the government. State v. Owens, ' 302 Or 196, 206-07, 729 P2d 524, 530 (1986)' (“The seizure of an article by the police and the retention of it (even temporarily) is a significant intrusion into a person’s possessory interest in that ‘effect.’”). In addition, the fact that a seized item may be contraband does not prevent a defendant from having a cognizable property interest, as well as a privacy interest, in the item. State v. Elkins, 245 Or 279, 290, 422 P2d 250 (1966). State v. Galloway, 198 Or App 585, 109 P3d 383 (2005) (police infringed on defendants’ possessory interests in garbage that they left in garbage cans outside their homes for curbside collection when police, without a warrant, seized and searched the garbage for evidence of drugs; defendants retained protected possessory interests in the contents of the garbage cans while the cans sat at the curbside awaiting collection, until collection by the trash collection companies actually occurred, and therefore the evidence found by police was subject to suppression). State v. Lien/Wilverding, 364 Or 750, 778, 441 P3d 185, 200 (2019) (“Defendants had privacy interests in their garbage as their bin sat at the curb for regular collection by the sanitation company * * * Because the police directed that private actor, Republic’s manager, to segregate and then to bring their private information in their garbage to the police for exposure in a search, the police bear responsibility for invading defendants’ privacy interests in their garbage.”).

Under Oregon Constitution, where defendant placed paper wad under his automobile seat, police officer “seized” that paper wad when he picked it up). State v. Ali, 105 Or App 193, 803 P2d 1231 (1991). Thus, an officer must actually take possession of the property in order for the action to constitute a seizure. State v. Finch, 86 Or App 315, 739 P2d 66 (1987) (no “seizure” occurred, for purpose of state or federal prohibition against unreasonable searches and seizures, when police officer unsuccessfully attempted to remove cigarette case from defendant’s pocket; nothing in records suggested that officer did not intend to allow defendant to retain case once defendant grabbed it back from officer).

Property may also be “seized” by show of authority without physical force. The test is what an objectively reasonable person would believe under the circumstances, and whether the property has been seized necessarily involves a fact-specific inquiry. State v. Juarez-Godinez, 326 Or 1, 942 P2d 772 (1997) (although, after driver’s arrest, driver retained possessory interest in the car and, in normal circumstances, could have transferred possession of it to one of his passengers and directed that it be driven away, car was “seized” where, after driver denied consent to search, officer told driver and his companions that another officer and his dog were coming to check the car, as officer’s statements were and reasonably could have been interpreted as curtailing, by a show of authority, driver’s rights to transfer possession of, and direct the movements of, the car). However, the Court of Appeals determined that no seizure of defendant’s family residence occurred by show of authority, for purposes of state constitutional guarantee against unreasonable searches and seizures, because the interference was threatened, but never effected. State v. Berg, 223 Or App 387, 196 P3d 547 (2008) opinion adh’d to as modified on recons, 228 Or App 754, 208 P3d 1006 (2009), rev den, 346 Or 361 (2009). On the other hand, when police actually “secure[d]” a defendant’s car and motel room for a significant period of time following a lawful stop, the car and room were seized. State v. Dowdy, 117 Or App 414, 418, 844 P2d 263, 265 (1992).

If a person abandons property, they do not retain any possessory or privacy interests in that property. Thus, a police officer can take the items and it will not constitute a seizure. Compare State v. Brown, 348 Or 293, 232 P3d 962 (2010) (defendant relinquished possessory and privacy interest in bags that defendant disclaimed ownership of and voluntarily left in a hotel room, and therefore the search of bags did not violate defendant’s state constitutional rights in identity theft prosecution; defendant abandoned the bags in a locked hotel room to which she would not have later had access to) with State v. Cook, 332 Or 601, 34 P3d 156 (2001) (defendant did not lose or relinquish his constitutionally protected possessory and privacy interests in duffel bag and clothing by leaving such items on ground and walking away from them when he agreed to talk to police officer, even though defendant repeatedly denied ownership of the items, where defendant told officer that he was sorting through clothing to find useable items, and he only relinquished immediate physical possession of the items when officer instructed him to step out of the area near the dumpster where he was doing the sorting). Furthermore, “no warrant is required for the opening and seizure of the contents of transparent containers or containers that otherwise announce their complete contents. State v. Heckathorne, 347 Or 474, 483, 223 P3d 1034, 1039 (2009).

B. Fourth Amendment Seizures

1. Fourth Amendment Seizures of a Person

A person is “seized” under the Fourth Amendment only when, by means of physical force or show of authority, the person’s freedom of movement is restrained. The test is whether, in view of all of the circumstances surrounding the incident, a reasonable person would have believed that they were not free to leave; as long as person to whom questions are put remains free to disregard questions and walk away, there has been no intrusion upon that person’s liberty or privacy as would under the Constitution require some particularized and objective justification. United States v. Mendenhall, 446 US 544, 100 S Ct 1870, 64 L Ed 2d 497 (1980). Therefore, a person must be confined physically or submit to a show of authority in order for a seizure to have occurred. [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=California+v.+Hodari+D.,+499+US+621&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38&case=3128854250640310684&scilh=0 California v. Hodari D., 499 US 621, 111 S Ct 1547, 113 L Ed 2d 690 (1991)] (even if police officer’s pursuit of defendant on suspicion of narcotics transaction was a “show of authority” enjoining defendant to halt, defendant was not “seized” until officer physically tackled him since defendant did not comply with that show of authority and, thus, cocaine abandoned by defendant while he was running was not fruit of seizure and was not subject to exclusion); United States v. White, 295 Fed Appx 186 (9th Cir 2008) (not reported) (encounter between defendant and police officer prior to defendant’s flight was not a seizure, but rather a consensual encounter, and, thus, defendant’s voluntary abandonment of the handgun during his flight was not the fruit of a seizure; although officer did not explicitly tell defendant that he was free to leave during encounter, the officer merely asked questions, and defendant was free to leave the encounter at any time).

“An arrest occurs when, in view of all the circumstances, a reasonable person would have believed he was not free to leave. Florida v. Royer, 460 US 491, 502-03 (1983). No bright line rule is established, but important factors include whether the agents drew their weapons, the threatening presence of several officers, the use of abusive language, the volume of the agents’ voices, physical touching of the individual, and the length of time the individual is detained. United States v. Sharpe, 470 US 675, 685 (1985); Mendenhall, 446 U.S. 544. Our review must consider ‘the law enforcement purposes to be served by the [detention] as well as the time reasonably needed to effectuate those purposes.’ Sharpe, 470 US at 685.”

United States v. Dadd, 963 F2d 380 (9th Cir 1992). However, something short of an actual arrest may still be a seizure.

Like the Oregon Constitution, the Fourth Amendment distinguishes between arrests, stops, and casual encounters. Therefore, not all personal interaction between police and citizens involves a seizure, and a seizure exists only when an officer, by means of physical force or show of authority, has in some way restrained a citizen’s liberty. “Even when law enforcement officers have no basis for suspecting a particular individual, they may pose questions, ask for identification, and request consent to search luggage—provided they do not induce cooperation by coercive means.” United States v. Drayton, 536 US 194, 201, 122 S Ct 2105, 2110, 153 L Ed 2d 242 (2002). But, “whenever a police officer accosts an individual and restrains his freedom to walk away, he has ‘seized’ that person,” and, regardless of whether it is a full-blown arrest, Fourth Amendment protections will apply. Terry v. Ohio, 392 US 1, 16, 88 S Ct 1868, 1877, 20 L Ed 2d 889 (1968). Likewise, whether the encounter is an arrest, stop, or mere encounter will depend on the facts of the particular case because the proper inquiry necessitates consideration of all the surrounding circumstances. Drayton, 536 US 194 (plainclothes police officers did not “seize” passengers on bus when, as part of routine drug and weapons interdiction effort, they boarded bus at rest stop and began asking passengers questions, notwithstanding officers’ failure to explicitly inform passengers that they were free to refuse to cooperate, and notwithstanding that one officer maintained presence at front of bus near exit, where officers did not draw or brandish their weapons, made no intimidating movements, left aisle free so that passengers could exit, and spoke to passengers one by one and in polite, quiet voice). In Fourth Amendment terms, a traffic stop entails a “seizure” of the driver and passengers even though the purpose of the stop is limited and the resulting detention quite brief. Brendlin v. California, 551 US 249, 127 S Ct 2400, 168 L Ed 2d 132 (2007) (passenger in automobile that was pulled over by police officer for traffic stop was “seized” under the Fourth Amendment from moment automobile came to halt on roadside and, therefore, was entitled to challenge constitutionality of traffic stop; any reasonable passenger would have understood police officers to be exercising control to the point that no one in the automobile was free to depart without police permission). However, the government does not violate a person’s right not to be stopped when its agents do not effect the stop, or when the person voluntarily stops. United States v. Al Nasser, 555 F3d 722 (9th Cir 2009).

2. Fourth Amendment Seizures of Property

Under the Fourth Amendment, “[a] ‘seizure’ of property occurs when there is some meaningful interference with an individual’s possessory interests in that property.” United States v. Jacobsen, 466 US 109, 113, 104 S Ct 1652, 1656, 80 L Ed 2d 85 (1984). For example, in Soldal v. Cook County the Court found that a seizure had occurred when a trailer home was forcibly removed. 506 US 56, 113 S Ct 538, 121 L Ed 2d 450 (1992). Furthermore, destruction of property is “seizure” under the Fourth Amendment. Fuller v. Vines, 36 F3d 65 (9th Cir 1994) overruled on other grounds by [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=Robinson+v.+Solano+Cnty.,+278+F3d+1007&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38&case=9025313375118238787&scilh=0 Robinson v. Solano Cnty., 278 F3d 1007 (9th Cir 2002)]. On the other hand, in Arizona v. Hicks, no “seizure” occurred, for purposes of Fourth Amendment, where officer merely recorded serial numbers of stereo system he observed in plain view. 480 US 321, 107 S Ct 1149, 94 L Ed 2d 347 (1987). Additionally, persons “who voluntarily abandon property lack standing to complain of its search or seizure.” United States v. Nordling, 804 F2d 1466, 1469 (9th Cir 1986); United States v. Ferguson, 33 Fed Appx 849 (9th Cir 2002) (not reported) (defendant had abandoned residence, where his actions were not consistent with any reasonable expectation of privacy, and therefore he lacked standing to complain of search. Prior to police entry into residence in search of medical information, defendant told aid personnel that house and its contents were not his, defendant produced identification listing his address as being in another city and told police the information was correct, and when released by police defendant got into his car and drove away). Whether property has been abandoned is a question of intent and is to be determined in light of the totality of the circumstances. One factor is physical relinquishment of the property. United States v. Knaub, 81 F3d 171 (9th Cir 1996), cert den, 519 US 875, 117 S Ct 194, 136 L Ed 2d 131 (1996)[1]  (physical abandonment of vehicle). However,

“In the ordinary case, the Court has viewed a seizure of personal property as per se unreasonable within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment unless it is accomplished pursuant to a judicial warrant issued upon probable cause and particularly describing the items to be seized. See, e.g., Marron v. United States, 275 US192, 196, 48 S Ct 74, 76, 72 L.Ed. 231 (1927). Where law enforcement authorities have probable cause to believe that a container holds contraband or evidence of a crime, but have not secured a warrant, the Court has interpreted the Amendment to permit seizure of the property, pending issuance of a warrant to examine its contents, if the exigencies of the circumstances demand it or some other recognized exception to the warrant requirement is present. See, e.g., Arkansas v. Sanders, 442 US753, 761, 99 S Ct 2586, 2591, 61 L Ed 2d235 (1979); United States v. Chadwick, 433 US1, 97 S Ct 2476, 53 L Ed 2d538 (1977); Coolidge v. New Hampshire, 403 US443, 91 S Ct 2022, 29 L Ed 2d564 (1971). For example, ‘objects such as weapons or contraband found in a public place may be seized by the police without a warrant,’ Payton v. New York, 445 US573, 587, 100 S Ct 1371, 1380, 63 L Ed 2d639 (1980), because, under these circumstances, the risk of the item’s disappearance or use for its intended purpose before a warrant may be obtained outweighs the interest in possession.”

United States v. Place, 462 US 696, 701-02, 103 S Ct 2637, 2641, 77 L Ed 2d 110 (1983) (agents made a “seizure” of traveler’s luggage for purposes of the Fourth Amendment when, following his refusal to consent to a search, a government agent told the traveler that he was going to take the luggage to a federal judge to secure the issuance of a search warrant and held it for 90 minutes without informing the defendant of the place to which they were transporting the luggage, the length of time he might be dispossessed of the luggage, and what arrangements would be made for the return of the luggage if the investigation dispelled their suspicions).

“In determining the reasonableness of a detention, we look to both (1) the absolute length of the detention, ‘regardless of the police officers’ assiduousness in seeking probable cause,’ and (2) the context of the Terry stop length, i.e., ‘whether law enforcement officers unduly extended the detention by their lack of diligence.’” United States v. Macias-Encinas, 430 Fed Appx 582 (9th Cir 2011) (citations omitted); United States v. $191,910.00 in USCurrency, 16 F3d 1051 (9th Cir 1994) (police officers failed to act with reasonable diligence to obtain dog to perform drug sniff on airplane passenger’s luggage, so that officers’ detention of luggage without warrant and without probable cause violated passenger’s Fourth Amendment rights, even assuming that length of detention was not, in absolute terms, excessive, where officers knew more than one hour before arrival of passenger’s flight that passenger would be on plane, and that passenger was suspected of narcotics activity, but failed to make any arrangements for dog to conduct drug sniff test until after seizure of luggage). Conversely, a brief detention of airline passenger’s luggage after it had been checked but before it was delivered to cargo hold did not constitute “seizure” for Fourth Amendment purposes, as detention was insufficient to constitute meaningful interference with possessory interest. United States v. Brown, 884 F2d 1309 (9th Cir 1989); United States v. Johnson, 990 F2d 1129 (9th Cir 1993) (airline passenger’s luggage was not seized, for Fourth Amendment purposes, when narcotics officers removed luggage from airport tarmac and took it to airline office so that dog could sniff it for narcotics, as sniff test did not interfere with passenger’s custody of luggage; passenger had checked his luggage late and agreed to allow airlines to send it on next plane if necessary and drug sniffing procedure was completed prior to time luggage would have been placed on airplane, absent indication of presence of narcotics). An addressee’s possessory interest boils down to an interest in the package’s timely delivery. “[L]aw enforcement interactions with packages that cause a de minimis interference with the flow of delivery do not implicate the Fourth Amendment.” United States v. Villalobos, 2007 WL 2688123 (D Or Sept. 5, 2007) (citations omitted) (not reported) (when a letter was removed from the outgoing inmate mailbox for a few minutes while a detective photocopied the envelope, the process did not interfere with the flow of the delivery of the letter because it occurred after normal business hours. Accordingly, the Court found that the detective’s interference with the individual’s possessory interest in the timely delivery of the letter was de minimis, and did not violate her Fourth Amendment rights); United States v. England, 971 F2d 419 (9th Cir 1992) (Detention of mailed packages for dog-sniff test for narcotics was not “seizure” within meaning of Fourth Amendment, even though packages were removed from assigned holding areas in preparation for inspection; one package arrived on schedule despite detention, and other package could easily have been placed on regularly scheduled flight had no cocaine been discovered).
