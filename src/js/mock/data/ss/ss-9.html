If the trial court denies a motion to suppress and the defendant is subsequently convicted and desires to appeal, the practices and procedures outlined in this chapter will ensure that the defendant has the best chance of prevailing on appeal.

 

I. Preserving Issues in a Motion to Suppress

As a general rule, the appellate courts will not consider claims of error that were not first presented to the trial court. State v. Wyatt, 331 Or 335, 343, 15 P3d 22 (2000); ORAP 5.45(1). This is the requirement known as “preservation,” which requires a party to offer “an explanation of his or her objection that is specific enough to ensure that the court can identify its alleged error with enough clarity to permit it to consider and correct the error immediately.” Wyatt, 331 Or at 343. The three primary purposes of the preservation rule are (1) to allow the trial court to consider the contention and correct any error in the first instance so as to obviate need for a costly and protracted appeal (judicial economy); (2) to ensure fairness to opposing parties, by requiring that the positions of the parties are presented clearly to the initial tribunal so that parties are not taken by surprise, misled, or denied opportunities to meet an argument (fairness); and (3) to foster full development of the record, which aids the trial court in deciding the issue and aids the appellate courts in reviewing it (record development). Peeples v. Lampert, 345 Or 209, 219-20, 191 P3d 637 (2008) (internal quotations and citations omitted).

 

The court has drawn “distinctions between raising an issue at trial, identifying a source for a claimed position, and making a particular argument. The first ordinarily is essential, the second less so, the third least.” State v. Hitz, 307 Or 183, 188, 766 P2d 373 (1988) (internal citation omitted) (emphasis in original). “Ultimately, the preservation rule is a practical one, and close calls * * *inevitably will turn on whether, given the particular record of a case, the court concludes that the policies underlying the rule have been sufficiently served.” State v. Parkins, 346 Or 333, 341, 211 P3d 262 (2009).

 

There are two schools of thought regarding the written motion to suppress. On the one hand, the more narrowly tailored the argument and the more on-point authority cited, the more likely the trial judge will not misunderstand the argument and the more likely the argument will be preserved for appeal. On the other hand, by filing a “skinny” motion, the defense does not tip off the state about potential theories for suppression, leaving the state to come up with its own justifications for the search and/or seizure. Both of these approaches have their merits and will often depend on the personalities at issue in the particular courtroom and other practical considerations. But it is important to recognize the possible benefits and pitfalls of either approach.

 

Even a “skinny” motion to suppress must meet certain minimum requirements set forth by UTCR 4.060(1).

 

A defendant’s motion to suppress conforms with UTCR 4.060(1) as long as it does the following four things: (1) generally identifies a particular search or seizure by the state; (2) asserts that the search or seizure was unreasonable (for example, by simply stating that it was warrantless and therefore per se unreasonable); (3) cites authority in support of the motion (for example, Article 1, section 9); and (4) requests suppression of evidence obtained as a result of the search or seizure. The motion need not set out specific facts beyond identifying the search or seizure, and it need not detail the argument beyond an assertion that the search was per se unreasonable. State v. Oxford, 287 Or App 580, 403 P3d 528 (2017).

 

In Oxford, as required by UTCR 4.060(1)(a), the defendant’s motion cited authority on which he was relying—specifically, Article I, section 9, and State v. Miller, 269 Or 328, 334, 524 P2d 1399 (1974). It apprised the court and the state that defendant was challenging specific searches and seizures: the state’s “stop and seizure of the defendant,” and the state’s “search of the defendant’s residence and personal room.” The motion set forth the defendant’s position that the searches and seizures had been warrantless and that “[w]arrantless searches and seizures are per se unreasonable.” And it laid out the evidence that defendant sought to have suppressed: “any and all evidence obtained as a result” of the warrantless search and seizure of defendant and the warrantless search of defendant’s residence and personal room, “including the firearms and explosive device, and all oral derivative evidence.” According to the Oxford court, “Defendant’s assertions in his motion and brief in support ‘sufficiently apprise[d]’ the court, UTCR 4.060(1)(b), and were sufficient to satisfy the requirements of the rule, ‘at least for the purpose of imposing upon the state the burden to show the legality of the search.’ Miller, 269 Or at 334. Under the circumstances, the trial court erred in striking defendant’s motion for failure to comply with UTCR 4.060(1).” Id. at 587.

 

But, in State v. Jacinto-Leiva, 287 Or App 574, 404 P3d 1118 (2017), the defendant’s one-page motion to suppress failed to comply with UTCR 4.060(1), and so the trial court did not err in striking the motion to suppress because the defendant failed to cite any statutory or constitutional provision (such as Article 1, section 9) as grounds for the motion. Instead, the only authority that the motion cited was State v. Miller, which dealt with the sufficiency of a motion to suppress under a local rule similar to UTCR 4.060, but was not itself authority for the defendant suppression argument.

 

Lastly, in State v. Sepulveda, 288 Or App 632, 406 P3d 169 (2017), the defendant preserved, in a one-page motion to suppress without accompanying memorandum or affidavit, his argument that the warrantless “search and seizure” of his person was unreasonable. The motion sufficiently alerted the court that the search and seizure was unreasonable because even though it was not accompanied by facts detailing the search and seizure, the testimony at the suppression hearing revealed a simple factual scenario involving only one potential seizure and one potential search. The motion also asserted that the search and seizure occurred without a warrant and was therefore per se unreasonable. The defendant preserved his argument that the seizure was unlawful because at the hearing on the motion, the parties litigated at length the question whether and when the defendant was seized. The state had not challenged the motion on the ground that it failed to comply with the UTCR, so the only issue in Sepulveda was one of preservation.

 

So, at a bare minimum, to meet the filing requirements of UTCR 4.060(1) a motion must identify the unlawful searches or seizures, cite authority (even if just Article I, section 9), assert why the search was unlawful (e.g., a warrantless search is per se unreasonable), and request suppression of evidence. To meet the appellate preservation requirements, one must generally develop an argument at the suppression hearing that identifies the unlawful search or seizure and the basis for suppression unless a detailed written motion to suppress has already covered that issue. That is, including an argument with supporting authority in a written motion to suppress generally will preserve it, even if that argument is not addressed at all at the hearing. State v. Walker, 350 Or 540, 258 P3d 1228 (2011). In Walker, the court further acknowledged that, “[p]articularly in criminal cases, in which there is a premium on considerations of cost and speed, the realities of trial practice may be such that fairly abbreviated short-hand references suffice to put all on notice about the nature of a party’s arguments.” Id. at 550.

 

Practice Tip

If in doubt about whether your motion/argument will preserve an issue, don’t hesitate to consult with an attorney at OPDS Appellate Division, there is always one on duty. Moreover, it is important to recognize the distinction between meeting the requirements of a motion to suppress under UTCR 4.060(1) and meeting the requirements of preservation under ORAP 5.45(1). So long as the requirements of URCR 4.060(1) are satisfied, it is technically possible to win suppression in the trial court even if your argument is not sufficiently well developed to preserve it for appeal. In that case you would be taking a big gamble—win now or never. That is because if the trial court rules against you, you will not be able to prevail on appeal for lack of preservation. State v. Hallam, 307 Or App 796, 802–03, 479 P3d 545, 550 (2020) (“[A]ttorneys who craft motions to just barely cross the minimal threshold of UTCR 4.060 play a risky game. Points and authorities motions that offer no legal argument, like the motion here, while potentially complying with UTCR 4.060 in some instances, may nevertheless be inadequate to preserve an argument for appeal.”). There may be situations where this is a reasonable approach but it will most likely involve cases where the merits of the correct legal argument are very weak and the best chance of prevailing is hoping that the state fails to recognize its own best argument. In that way, it might be possible to win by the state’s “own-goal” in the trial court. But that course is certainly not recommended where the defense believes that they have a winning argument. Generally speaking, in those situations, you should do all you can to clearly communicate your position to the trial court so as to have the best chance of winning in the trial court—and failing there—getting the trial court reversed in the Court of Appeals.

 

II. Setting Up an Appeal if the Defendant Waives a Jury Trial

A. Conditional Pleas

 

Whenever the court denies a motion to suppress potentially dispositive evidence, defense counsel should consider a conditional plea. A conditional plea allows the client to preserve the issue for appeal but also to negotiate aspects of the sentence or the number of convictions. Conditional pleas also come with a huge advantage on appeal—no harmless error analysis.

 

ORS 135.335(3) states:

 

“With the consent of the court and the state, a defendant may enter a conditional plea of guilty or no contest reserving, in writing, the right, on appeal from the judgment, to a review of an adverse determination of any specified pretrial motion. A defendant who finally prevails on appeal may withdraw the plea.”

 

There are a few things that ORS 135.335 requires. For example, the issue must be specifically identified and reserved “in writing.” It must be approved by the prosecutor and trial judge. While approval by the prosecutor and trial judge can be done orally, it may be safest to obtain their signatures on the form you use.

 

The key is to clearly list, on the plea petition itself or an attachment, each pretrial motion being reserved for appeal (there can be more than one). And then clearly state that the plea is conditioned upon reserving the right to appeal those decisions. It really is that simple, but the Court of Appeals can be unforgiving if the statutory requirements are not followed to the letter. (See Lake Oswego v. Albright, 222 Or App 117 (2008)). Recently, in State v. Slight, 301 Or App 237, 241-43, 456 P3d 366 (2019), this court clarified the requirements for preserving a pretrial ruling pursuant to a conditional plea. This court explained that while the “reservation of appellate rights” must be included in the plea petition, a defendant may specify the ruling at issue in other ways, including in the record of proceedings. “The statute does not require that the specification of which pretrial motions are at issue occur in a particular form.” Id. at 243.

 

If the appellate courts find error, ORS 135.335(3) provides that “[a] defendant who finally prevails on appeal may withdraw the plea,” even if the error might have been harmless. See State v. Dinsmore, 182 Or App 505, 519, 49 P3d 830 (2002). It is hard to overstate what an advantage this is on appeal. Unlike a bench trial, or even a stipulated facts trial, because a defendant has pleaded before any evidence is introduced, it is impossible for the Court of Appeals to determine if the error in denying the motion to suppress would have harmed the defendant. Therefore, on appeal, the defendant does not have his usual burden of establishing a prejudicial error.

 

The defendant in a conditional plea case is also at an advantage because he does not have to withdraw the plea and proceed to trial if the case is returned to the trial court and the prosecutor is unwilling to renegotiate. In certain situations, the client may prefer the sentence and let the number of convictions stand rather than risk a worse result after a trial. And, the state cannot revive counts that it dismissed pursuant to a conditional plea, even if the defendant ultimately withdraws it. 

 

B. Stipulated Facts and Bench Trials

 

If the court or the prosecutor will not agree to a conditional plea, it may make sense to have a bench trial rather than risk the ire of the trial judge by wasting time and resources on a jury trial. But defense lawyers will often encourage their clients to go further and simply agree to a stipulated facts trial. A stipulated facts trial is, of course, a bench trial where certain stipulations are made to avoid the time-consuming nature of live testimony. But the name is a misnomer—defense attorneys should never stipulate to facts. For example, if the defendant stipulates that drugs were found in his pocket, he has inadvertently entered a guilty plea by another name, and there is nothing the appellate lawyer can do to win the appeal.

 

While there is no one way to do this, a safe course of action is to stipulate as follows:

 

“The defendant stipulates that if the arresting officer were to testify at trial, he would testify consistently with his testimony in the motion to suppress hearing.”

 

This language is better because it does not stipulate to the accuracy of the facts. Rather, it is a stipulation to the evidence that will be presented.

 

Note, if in doubt about how to do stipulated-facts trials correctly, a non-stipulated bench trial is always an option. Moreover, there is always the possibility with live testimony that some mistake will be made by a witness or the prosecutor, which would lead to an unexpected acquittal or dismissal.

 

C. Holding All or Part of the Judgment in Abeyance Pending Appeal

 

A trial court (but not the Court of Appeals) has statutory authority to suspend a sentence during the pendency of an appeal. ORS 138.285 (set forth below) provides the factors for the trial court to consider. A defendant cannot appeal from the denial of a motion to stay. State v. Wimber, 108 Or App 1, 5, 814 P2d 169 (1991).

 

As a practical matter, these motions are rarely granted. A number of factors will certainly impact whether the judge will seriously consider suspending any or all of the sentence pending appeal. One of the most significant factors would be whether the judge herself believes the issue is viable or meritorious on appeal. If so, the trial judge might be convinced that it would be unfair and unjust for the defendant to serve a lengthy sentence on a charge that stands a reasonable possibility of being reversed. The chances that a defendant would be compensated for the time he or she spent unlawfully in custody are virtually nil in Oregon. Also, if the sentence includes both a substantial payment of restitution and prison time, the judge may be convinced that suspending the imposition of the prison time would allow the defendant the opportunity to make restitution payments more quickly. While this argument does not often persuade judges to forego imposing any prison time, the defendant is not asking to avoid prison altogether but simply to postpone it pending appeal, during which he or she could actually make the payments. If the appeal is unsuccessful, the client would still serve his or her sentence.

 

ORS 138.285 provides:

 

“(1)(a) A justice, municipal or circuit court may enter an order in a criminal action as defined in ORS 131.005 staying execution of a sentence, or a portion of a sentence, pending the resolution of an appeal.

“(b) Except for good cause shown, a motion for an order under this section must be filed in the trial court no later than the filing of a notice of appeal. The trial court retains jurisdiction to enter an order under this section irrespective of whether a notice of appeal has been filed.

“(c) Except as otherwise required by this section, the trial court may impose conditions on a stay that the trial court determines are appropriate.

“(2) In determining whether to enter an order staying the execution of all of a sentence, or a portion of a sentence, the trial court shall consider the following factors:

“(a) The nature of the offense;

“(b) The severity of the sentence imposed;

“(c) The health of the defendant;

“(d) The character and strength of the evidence;

“(e) The criminal history of the defendant;

“(f) If the sentence, or the portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed includes a term of incarceration, the likelihood that the defendant will:

“(A) Appear in court at all appropriate times;

“(B) Comply with any other conditions of release; and

“(C) Complete, or substantially complete, serving the term of incarceration before the appeal is decided;

“(g) The likelihood that an appellate court will reverse the sentence, or the portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed or will reverse the judgment of conviction that includes the sentence, or the portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed; and

“(h) If an appellate court has issued a decision reversing the sentence, or a portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed or reversing the judgment of conviction that includes the sentence, or the portion of the sentence, sought to be stayed:

“(A) Whether the reversal or any other relief described in the appellate decision will result in the defendant having completed serving the term of incarceration imposed; and

“(B) Whether the appellate decision remands the case for a new trial.

“(3) If the trial court enters an order staying a term of incarceration, the court:

“(a) May order that the conditions of the release agreement and any posted security stand pending resolution of the appeal or may order an increase or reduction in the amount of security.

“(b) Shall order that the defendant, as a condition of release:

“(A) Duly prosecute the appeal of the defendant as required by ORS 138.005 to 138.500;

“(B) Appear at such time and place as the court may direct;

“(C) Not depart this state without leave of the court; and

“(D) If the judgment is affirmed, or the judgment is reversed and the case is remanded for a new trial, immediately appear as required by the trial court.

“(4) If the court enters an order staying the payment of a monetary obligation, the court may order the defendant:

“(a) To deposit, pending resolution of the appeal, the whole or any part of the monetary obligation with the clerk of the trial court;

“(b) To file an undertaking with sufficient sureties;

“(c) To submit to an examination of assets; or

“(d) To refrain from dissipating the assets of the defendant.”

 

III. When the Case Comes Back from a Successful Appeal

Naturally, when a case comes back from an appeal that was successful in whole or in part, it is absolutely necessary to read the opinion, paying particular attention to the last paragraph in which the court orders what needs to be done on remand. On occasion, the order will not grant exactly the relief that was requested. Or the order is ambiguous regarding what exactly the appellate court wants the trial court to do. If you are unsure about what exactly the appellate court intended, contact the appellate attorney who handled the case.

 

Regardless, it is important to stay in touch with the appellate attorney. For one, the appellate briefs may be highly useful as there may be additional issues that would have had a good chance of success (such as evidentiary issues or sentencing issues) but were either unpreserved or unresolved due to the success on the suppression issue. Remand will provide a new opportunity to argue those issues. The briefs are often accessible online at http://digitallawlibrary.oregon.gov/.

 

However, note that a remand for resentencing does not reopen issues that challenge the entry of conviction. See State v. Claborn, 237 Or App 488 (2010).

 

The appellate attorney may also have spotted additional issues that were not included in the briefs that she believes should be raised in the retrial.

 

It is also imperative to obtain a transcript of the original hearing.

First Appearance Checklist for Cases on Remand

 

When you make your first appearance following a successful appeal, it may or may not be in front of the original trial judge. Here are the steps you should ask the judge to take when the appeal does not arise from a conditional plea:

 

·      Enter an order reversing the convictions that the Court of Appeals ordered reversed (The COA may have ordered it, but an additional order must be entered by the trial court).

·      If the appellate court ruled that the trial judge erred in denying the motion to suppress, enter an order granting the motion to suppress.

·      If the state reports it is unable to proceed without the evidence that has now been suppressed, enter an order of dismissal or acquittal of whatever counts the state cannot proceed on.

·      If there are charges that were not reversed, or the state can proceed on some of the charges even after evidence is suppressed, set a further proceedings date or retrial date, as appropriate.

·      If the state intends to proceed on certain charges, and the defendant is not serving any other sentence, move for the defendant’s immediate release from custody pending trial under a release agreement, as the case is now back in its standard pretrial posture.

·      If no charges remain, seek an order returning to the defendant any fines or fees that have already been paid.

 

Note, if the defendant was convicted pursuant to conditional plea, you may not want the judge to do anything at that first appearance except set a further proceeding date. During the interim, you can attempt to negotiate with the prosecutor, identify the location of witnesses, evaluate the chances of success on a retrial, etcetera. You will also need to determine what previously dismissed charges would be reinstated if she withdraws her plea. In other words, you will want your client to have the benefit of as much information as possible before she decides to withdraw her plea.

 

If all the charges are dismissed, you will want to discuss with your client the opportunity for expungement. Please check out the expungement rules that can be found online at the Library of Defense.

 

Also, keep in mind that the appellate opinion, with your client’s name on the first page, is now online and subject to an internet search. You may want to discuss with your client the possibility of requesting the COA redact the online edition of the opinion by referring to your client only by his initials. (The same can be sought if your client is not actually a party but is discussed in the opinion as a witness or co-defendant.) The rules for seeking that change can be found at the Court of Appeals’ website.
