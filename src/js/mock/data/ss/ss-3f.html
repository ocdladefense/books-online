Under the emergency aid doctrine, law enforcement officers may lawfully enter private premises without a warrant if they “have an objectively reasonable belief, based on articulable facts, that a warrantless entry is necessary to either render immediate aid to persons, or to assist persons who have suffered, or who are imminently threatened with suffering, serious physical injury or harm.” State v. Bistrika, 262 Or App 385, 392-93, 324 P3d 584 (2014) (quoting State v. Baker, 350 Or 641, 649, 260 P3d 476 (2011)).

“Although there is an ‘emergency aid’ exception to the warrant requirement, there is not a ‘community caretaking’ exception. State v. Martin, 222 Or App 138, 146, 193 P3d 993 (2008) (‘ORS 133.033 does not independently establish an exception to the warrant requirement. Rather, it provides legislative authorization for (among other things) a particular class of searches, subject to many of the same constitutional constraints that operate to limit other searches, including the warrant requirement. A lawful community caretaking search, in other words, must first be within the universe of police action described in [the community caretaking statute,] ORS 133.033, and then it must also fall within one of the constitutional exceptions to the warrant requirement.’).” Bistrika, 262 Or App at 407 (internal quotation marks and citation omitted in Bistrika).

The “emergency aid” exception and the “exigent circumstances exception” are not coterminous:

“The emergency aid exception and the exigent circumstances exception differ in at least one key way. The exigent circumstances exception ‘requires both probable cause and an exigency.’ The emergency aid exception does not: It permits warrantless entry, search, or seizure, regardless of whether the officer has probable cause to believe that a crime has been or is being committed, as long as the officer reasonably believes it necessary to ‘render immediate aid to persons * * * who have suffered, or who are imminently threatened with suffering, serious physical injury or harm.’ Emergency aid requires only ‘an objectively reasonably belief, based on articulable facts’ that such an emergency exists.

“The exceptions also may differ in scope. Although this court has not decided the question, it has, as noted, described exigent circumstances as including those in which swift action is necessary to prevent serious damage to ‘property.’ The emergency aid doctrine, on the other hand, has been described as applying to situations in which immediate action is necessary to render aid to ‘persons.’”

State v. Fessenden, 355 Or 759, 765, 333 P3d 278 (2014) (declining to decide question of whether emergency aid doctrine would have permitted a warrantless entry in order to render aid to a starving animal).

Moreover, “the emergency aid exception requires more than a speculative concern that someone may need assistance; rather, police must actually have a subjective belief that a person is seriously injured and in need of immediate assistance.” State v. Stanley, 287 Or App 399, 404 P3d 1100 (2017) (reversing where police entered home after responding to domestic violence call to determine whether the caller was injured).

The appellate courts have held a relatively firm line, requiring the state to establish that the officer had a reasonable belief that there was an imminent need to render near life-saving aid. State v. Martin, 222 Or App 138, 193 P3d 993 (2008). In that case, a witness reported a hit-and-run accident and followed the car to defendant’s address. The police responded and observed the vehicle. They knocked on the door. Eventually, the defendant, completely naked, opened the door. She then turned and ran upstairs. The officers followed and eventually arrested the defendant for DUII. The Court of Appeals held that the mere opening of a door to a residence was not a consent to enter that residence. The emergency aid doctrine was not applicable because there was no life-threatening situation.

In State v. Salisbury, 223 Or App 516, 196 P3d 1017 (2008), an officer followed the defendant, whom he knew lacked a valid license, to the defendant’s house. The officer attempted to engage the defendant, but the defendant rapidly entered his residence and closed the door. The officer then heard a woman scream. Eventually he and other officers broke down the door and entered the residence. The court held that there was no objective evidence in this case that the officers’ entry was required to protect a person from peril.

In State v. Fredricks, 238 Or App 349, 243 P3d 97 (2010), police overheard a loud argument, unaccompanied by any sounds of a physical struggle or an indication that an act of violence had occurred. Warrantless entry into the defendant’s residence was not authorized by the emergency aid exception. See also State v. Mazzola, 238 Or App 201, 242 P3d 674 (2010) (evidence of verbal dispute insufficient to trigger emergency aid doctrine).

Practice Tip

When the state seeks to rely on the emergency aid exception, defense counsel should generally make the following arguments:

  1. The officer entered to determine whether there was an emergency—not to render immediate aid.
  2. The facts known to the officer were insufficient to give rise to an objectively reasonable belief that someone on the property was experiencing a serious medical emergency.
  3. Even if the officer subjectively believed there was an emergency, the officer’s belief that his or her entry or assistance was necessary was not objectively reasonable, because the officer lacked the training and experience necessary to render the type of aid called for by the circumstances.
  4. Even if one or more officers entered to render emergency aid and it was reasonable for them to do so, it was not objectively reasonable for more officers than was reasonably necessary to render such aid to enter the premises.
  5. The police did not leave the property within a reasonable time after the emergency had dissipated.
