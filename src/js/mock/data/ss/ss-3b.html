I. The Test and General Principles: Mobility Plus Probable Cause

The “automobile exception” is “a subset of the exigent circumstances exception” under which the “[m]obility of the vehicle, by itself, creates the exigency.” State v. Brown, 301 Or 268, 276, 721 P2d 1357 (1986). Brown was the first case to establish Oregon’s automobile exception, and the Supreme Court has since upheld it, further defined it, and sometimes stressed that it will one day be obsolete.

The automobile exception allows law enforcement to search a vehicle if (1) the officer has probable cause to believe that the vehicle contains “contraband or evidence of a crime[,]” and (2) the vehicle is mobile when officers lawfully stop it or when they first encounter it in connection with a criminal investigation. State v. Bliss, 363 Or 426, 438, 423 P3d 53 (2018), State v. Kurokawa-Lasciak, 351 Or 179, 263 P3d 336 (2011).

2024 Update

In State v. McCarthy, 369 Or 129, 177, 501 P3d 478 (2021), the court eliminated the automobile exception. Prior to McCarthy, the automobile exception was a per se exigency, in which police could search a car without a warrant if they had probable cause that the car contained evidence of a crime and if the car was mobile when the police encountered it in connection with a crime. State v. Brown, 301 Or 268, 278, 721 P2d 1357 (1986), overruled by McCarthy, 369 Or 129.

In disavowing the automobile exception, the court criticized its reasoning in Brown as overly reliant on Fourth Amendment jurisprudence. McCarthy, 369 Or at 153-56. The court also reasoned that the per se rule was meant to provide clarity to police and lower courts but that the subsequent case law demonstrated that it had failed to do either. Id. at 157, 168. Further, the Brown court expressly intended the automobile exception to be a temporary rule that would expire when technology developed enough to allow warrants to be issued more quickly and easily, and the McCarthy court was satisfied by the post-Brown technological developments and legislative changes to warrant application procedures. Id. at 147, 173-76. Finally, the court reasoned that the scope of the exception impermissibly exceeded its underlying justification and thus permitted warrantless searches under an exigency rationale despite the absence of any actual exigency. Id. at 168.

For those reasons, the court overruled Brown, disavowed the automobile exception, and held that, “in order to justify a warrantless seizure or search of a vehicle based on exigent circumstances, the state must prove that exigent circumstances actually existed at the time of the seizure or the search[.]” Id. at 177. To prove that exigency, “the state must prove that it could not obtain a warrant through reasonable steps, which include utilizing available processes for electronic warrants” without allowing the loss or destruction of the evidence sought. Id.

Further, the court held that a seizure and a search of a car must be “separately analyzed”: while an exigency “may justify the seizure of a vehicle,” “the seizure itself may eliminate any exigency that would justify proceeding further without a warrant. Once officers have seized a vehicle, their control over it may eliminate the need to search it before a warrant application can be processed.” Id. at 178.

A. The Mobility Requirement

1. “Mobile” Means “Moving,” Not “Operable”

Brown held that police may search a “lawfully stopped car” provided there exists probable cause. 301 Or at 275. The Supreme Court subsequently held that a car that is parked, immobile and unoccupied when police first “encounter” it may not be searched under the automobile exception—even if it is operable.

The Supreme Court later clarified that “bright line rule” in Kurokawa-Lasciak: “mobile” does not mean “operable,” and a defendant need not show that a car was inoperable in order to overcome the automobile exception. 351 Or at 193. The court reasoned that even if it were just as possible an operable but parked vehicle could be driven away, the automobile exception is a bright-line rule and must stay that way in order to “benefit both the police and the citizens of this state.” Id.

2. The Timing of Mobility: When “First Encountered in Connection With a Crime”

Provided a vehicle is not actually stopped lawfully by police (further explained below), then in order for the automobile exception to apply, the vehicle “must be mobile at the time that the police encounter it in connection with a crime.” Kurakawa-Lasciak, 351 Or at 192.

In State v. Meharry, 342 Or 173, 149 P3d 1155 (2006), the Supreme Court concluded that the officer had “encountered” a vehicle in connection with a crime when the officer—who already had reasonable suspicion that the defendant was driving under the influence of intoxicants—saw the defendant driving the vehicle. Even though the officer did not stop the defendant—he caught up with her after she had parked at a store—the automobile exception justified the search, because the officer had encountered her moving vehicle in the course of the criminal investigation of DUII.

The Court of Appeals has emphasized the distinction between merely “encountering” a vehicle and “encountering it in connection with a crime.” State v. Pirtle, 255 Or App 195, 296 P3d 625 (2013). In that case, a police officer happened to observe the defendant’s truck in motion, but did not realize it was connected to any crime until later, when it was parked and unoccupied. Id. at 201. The court held that the automobile exception did not apply—there was no concurrence of mobility and connection with a crime. Id. Similarly, in State v. Groom, 249 Or App 118, 274 P3d 876 (2012), police did not realize that the defendant’s moving vehicle was connected to any crime when they randomly checked its license. By the time the officers had discovered the vehicle owner had an outstanding warrant, the car had turned down a side street and parked. Then, by the time the officer relocated the car, the driver and one of the passengers were standing on the sidewalk. The officer developed probable cause that a purse inside the car contained drugs, searched it, and found drugs. But the Court of Appeals held that, in light of Kurokawa, the automobile exception did not authorize the search of the car. It held that the vehicle was only “mobile” while the officer was “randomly” running license plates and so the encounter was not “in connection with a crime.” Instead, the nexus with a crime occurred later, after the car had been parked, the defendant was standing outside of it, and the officer uncovered additional information that led him to suspect that the car contained drugs. In other words, the officer first encountered the vehicle while it was mobile; but he first encountered the vehicle in connection with a crime while it was parked.

The police do not have to observe the automobile actually being driven to establish “mobility” where it is obvious that the car just came to rest after having been driven. State v. Andersen, 361 Or 187, 390 P3d 992 (2017). In Andersen, two officers were waiting for the defendant’s car to arrive at a parking lot to complete a drug sale with an informant. One officer was listening in on the defendant’s passenger’s cell phone conversation, and learned that the car was just arriving at the parking lot. But the other officer left to check on whether the defendant was pulling into a different part of the lot. By the time the officer returned a minute later, the defendant’s car was parked. The engine was running and the defendant was in the driver’s seat, but the officer had never actually seen the car moving. Under those circumstances, the court held that the car was “mobile.” The officers had encountered it moving (via the cell phone conversation and other circumstances) during their criminal investigation, even if not with their sight.

3. Mobile When Lawfully Stopped

In Bliss, the Supreme Court made one exception to the rule that a car must be moving when encountered in connection with a crime: The automobile exception justifies searching a car that is parked and immobile when police connect it to a criminal investigation, so long as the automobile was lawfully stopped—e.g., for a traffic violation. The court took its reasoning from the language in Brown, Oregon’s case establishing the automobile exception, allowing the search of “lawfully stopped” vehicle. Bliss, 363 Or at 438 (quoting Brown, 301 Or at 278). In other words, to meet the mobility prong of the automobile exception, the automobile need only be mobile at the time it is lawfully stopped by police—for whatever lawful reason. If police stop the car for a traffic violation, and then develop probable cause (while the car is parked) of a crime or contraband, they may search under the automobile exception. In that circumstance, the car does have to be mobile when first encountered in connection with a crime.

Note: Though it was not a central issue, the Supreme Court repeatedly states that the automobile must be “lawfully stopped” in order to meet the mobility prong of the automobile exception.


Practice Tip

If an officer lawfully stops a car for a traffic violation, then unlawfully extends the stop to develop probable cause of a crime or contraband, defendants should argue that the automobile exception cannot justify a search: Because the vehicle must be “lawfully stopped” under Brown and Bliss for the automobile exception to apply (if it was not mobile when first encountered in connection with a crime), then the unlawful extension of the stop takes the search out of the automobile exception’s bright-line rule.

B. The Probable Cause Requirement

Probable cause must still exist at the time of the search. Bliss, 363 Or at 438. This is true even though officers may stop the car for a traffic violation and then search pursuant to the automobile exception: they may only do so if they develop probable cause of a crime or contraband. Strictly speaking, probable cause is not really a prong of the automobile exception, but is an overall prerequisite for all searches. The automobile exception is an exception to the warrant requirement; all searches must be justified by probable cause and either a warrant or an exception to the warrant requirement.

II. Challenging the Per Se Nature of the Rule and Requiring Actual Exigency

A. Has the Supreme Court cast doubt on per se exigency?

Andersen would appear to open the door to arguments that the state must show actual exigency, and that the automobile exception may not provide a per se rule of exigency for much longer. But the Court of Appeals has interpreted Bliss as having “retreated from” any doubt that Andersen may have cast on the per se nature of the automobile exception. State v. McCarthy, 302 Or App 82, 90, 459 P3d 890, rev all’d, 366 Or 691, 466 P3d 958 (July 2, 2020). The Supreme Court has granted review of 
McCarthy. 

The issue in McCarthy is whether current technological realities coupled with legislative changes that expedite the warrant application process are sufficient to trigger Brown’s sunset provision, such that the state would bear the burden to establish that an actual exigency existed at the time of the search.

When the Supreme Court created Oregon’s automobile exception, it included a sunset provision on the rule. “[T]he court did not anticipate that the police would rely on the automobile exception when advances in technology permitted quick and efficient electronic issuance of warrants.” Kurokawa-
Lasciak, 351 Or at 188. In fact, the Brown court “fores[aw] a time in the near future when the warrant requirement of the state and federal constitution [could] be fulfilled virtually without exception.” 301 Or at 278 n 6. Brown envisioned a future where technological realities made it practicable for police to call a magistrate on a recorded line to state “the facts, given under oath, constituting the purported probable cause” and, if sufficient, “the magistrate would immediately issue an electronic warrant authorizing the officer on the scene to proceed.” Id. 

There should be little doubt that the advances in technology now available to law enforcement should bring an end to the per se nature of the automobile exception.

B. Must the defendant prove that there was no actual exigency?

So far, the Court of Appeals acknowledges that a search may be unlawful if there was no actual exigency—even if the automobile exception would otherwise apply. McCarthy squared Supreme Court precedent with that acknowledgement by stating that it is the defendant’s burden to show that there was no exigency—for instance, by establishing that the state could have quickly obtained a telephonic warrant. McCarthy, 302 Or App at 89 (“Andersen appears to cast the theoretical exigency that underlies the automobile exception as a rebuttable presumption.”). This conflicts with the general rule that the state ordinarily must prove that an exception to the warrant requirement exists. Again, the Supreme Court has granted review of McCarthy.

C. Keeping the Bright Line Rule Narrow

It is important to note that the Supreme Court’s decisions on the automobile exception are, in the opinion of some of the justices, somewhat in conflict, making the automobile exception ripe for further challenge. When Justice Walters (joined by Justice Nakamoto) dissented in Bliss, she opined that Bliss strays from the hard line drawn earlier by Kurokawa-Lasciak—and also by Meharry, State v. Kock, 302 Or 29, 725 P2d 1285 (1986), and Brown. Bliss, 363 Or at 439 (Walters, J., dissenting). Of course, the majority opinion in Bliss does not purport to conflict with that earlier jurisprudence; instead, the majority opinion states that Brown in the first place justified searching a “lawfully stopped” car and that no Supreme Court case had squarely confronted whether that lawful stop could be for a traffic violation rather than for a crime.

But the dissent would hold that Bliss was an expansion of the rule, because the former cases could not be interpreted to apply to stops for traffic violations. The reason that the dissent finds a necessary distinction between traffic stops and criminal investigations is this: “[T]he fact that exigent circumstances may be established does not provide a reason to expand the circumstances in which an exigency must be assumed.” Bliss, 363 Or at 439 (Walters, J., dissenting). In other words, it is one thing to allow a per se exigency for vehicles that are the subject of a criminal investigation. But to establish a per se exigency to traffic stops, which are far more common, would be an expansion of the rule, in the eyes of the dissent.

This means that at least some justices view the automobile exception as a narrow, bright-line rule that does not justify expansion. All justices seem to agree on that principle. But it is important to see where some justices would perceive an “expansion” that other justices see as application. Attorneys should argue that new applications of the automobile exception are expansions of the bright-line rule, where possible.

III. Common Issues Within the Automobile Exception

A. Must Police Actually Stop the Car?

Not if officers observe the vehicle in motion while there is a contemporaneous connection between it and a crime. State v. Finlay, 257 Or App 581, 591, 307 P3d 518 (2013). In other words, “lawfully stopping” a mobile car and “first encountering a vehicle in connection with a crime” while the vehicle is moving are two alternative ways to establish mobility under the automobile exception.

B. Limitations on Scope of the Search

Police may search “any area of the vehicle or any container within the vehicle in which they have probable cause to believe that the contraband or crime evidence may be found.” State v. Smalley, 233 Or App 263, 267, 225 P3d 844 (2010).

Nothing in Brown, or the cases that have followed, extends the automobile exception to a search of a defendant’s person while the defendant is standing outside the car. State v. Jones, 253 Or App 246, 247, 289 P3d 360 (2012).

The police cannot wait until after the car has been impounded to conduct a search pursuant to the automobile exception. State v. Quinn, 290 Or 383, 392, 623 P2d 630 (1981) (overruled in part on other grounds, State v. Hall, 339 Or 7, 28,115 P3d 908 (2005)):

“Here, however, the delay was not occasioned by the impracticability of searching the car immediately. Rather it was for the convenience of the police and the owner of the stolen property [suspected to be inside the vehicle]. If the requirement that decisions to search must be judicial is to have any effect and if the exigent circumstances doctrine is to have any temporal limitation, then a deferred warrantless search must be commenced as promptly after the seizure as is reasonable in the circumstances. This one was not. The expeditious sequence was broken, cf. Mincey v. Arizona, 437 US 385, 98 S Ct 2408, 57 L Ed 2d 290 (1978). There was time and opportunity to seek judicial authorization indeed the officer was ready to do so, but he understood the deputy district attorney to have advised him otherwise. The failure to have obtained a warrant invalidates the delayed search.”

Quinn, 290 Or at 392.

C. “Contraband” and Violation-level Controlled Substances

When possession of less than an ounce of marijuana was reduced from a crime to a violation, the Court of Appeals held that “contraband” included marijuana. State v. Smalley, 233 Or App 263, 265, 225 P3d 844, rev den, 348 Or 415 (2010). There, the driver consented to a search of the pickup, and in that search an officer located a backpack from which a strong odor of marijuana emanated. Id. Based on the strength of the smell emitting from the backpack, the officer believed that the backpack contained a large amount of marijuana. Id. The Court of Appeals concluded that based on 1) the smell of actual marijuana, 2) coming from a particular location, 3) that was consistent with a large quantity of marijuana based on the officer’s training and experience, the officer had probable cause to believe that some quantity of marijuana was in the backpack. Id. at 268.

Where the officer only testifies to an odor of burned marijuana coming from the car, Smalley does not control. To conclude that the smell of burned marijuana signifies the presence of unburned marijuana is comparable to concluding that the smell of alcohol on a driver’s breath signifies the presence of unconsumed alcohol in the driver’s car. Because such smells can linger long after the substance is initially consumed, it is too speculative to conclude that the car contains additional drugs or alcohol.

Additionally, where the officer merely observes drug paraphernalia, such as a pipe—the pipe itself is not contraband—thus, that alone cannot support a search under the automobile exception. State v. Sunderman, 304 Or App 329, 344-46, 467 P3d 52 (2020).

This means that where an officer has probable cause to believe the car contains a violation-level amount of controlled substance, Smalley probably applies and the search could be justified under the automobile exception. But the state cannot get there by merely showing evidence of recent drug consumption, or of drug paraphernalia.

This also means that, post-legalization of marijuana, attorneys should argue that Smalley no longer applies to searches for marijuana possession, because marijuana no longer qualifies as “contraband.”

D. Suppressing Statements

The automobile exception alone does not authorize detaining a car’s occupants outside the car to await the results of a search, unless police have reasonable suspicion to stop the particular individual. That means that even if the prongs of the automobile exception are met, any incriminating responses made under police questioning during such a detention are subject to suppression. State v. Tovar, 256 Or App 1, 299 P3d 580 (2013); State v. Martinez, 275 Or App 645, 365 P3d 659 (2015).

IV. Dog Sniffs and the Automobile Exception

A. A Questionable Premise

When conducted from a public place, “dog sniffs are not searches.” State v. Smith, 327 Or 366, 374, 963 P2d 642 (1998) (emphasis in original). That conclusion followed from the following reasoning:

“[S]ome police techniques clearly do involve technological or figurative invasions of privacy interests. High-powered telescopes, parabolic sound gathering devices, and infrared cameras all are able to gather ordinarily imperceptible information (in the form of light rays, sound waves, or heat waves) from the interior of an enclosed space as it emanates from that space. Although such devices can operate from an external and public place, their use is invasive nevertheless, because they provide information from an enclosed space about the enclosure’s contents that a police officer, standing at a lawful vantage point, cannot detect with ordinary human powers of perception. That is, they constructively move the observing police officer into the private space.

“Superficially, one might argue that dog sniffs fall into that category—that trained police dogs perceive odor molecules as they emanate from an enclosed space and then indicate the contents of the enclosure by their reactions. But dog sniffs do not provide the kind of direct access to the interiors of enclosed spaces that the aforementioned device provide. Dog sniffs operate on a sample of air whose origins are unknown. One may infer from the fact that a dog detects the presence of a substance in the public airspace surrounding an enclosure that the enclosure contains that substance. However, that would only be an inference. For the purposes of both the dog and its human interpreter, the substance that is detected remains an ambient substance in the public airspace. As such, dog sniffs do not extract information from the interior of a private space, either literally or figuratively. They are not invasive in any sense of the word.

“The clear import of the foregoing is that, at least when they are conducted in a public place, dog sniffs are not searches. And, because they are not, the protections of Article I, section 9—including the warrant requirement—do not apply.”

Smith, 327 Or at 374.

The government’s use of a trained police dog to investigate is a “search” within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. Florida v. Jardines, 569 US 1, 133 S Ct 1409, 185 L Ed 2d 495 (2013) (holding that the officer’s entry onto the defendant’s curtilage (the front porch) was not explicitly or implicitly invited, and that the use of a dog sniff there was an unreasonable search).

A person who is not the target of the investigation is not necessarily seized just because a drug dog walks around the perimeter of a car. State v. Sexton, 278 Or App 1, 378 P3d 83 (2016). But a dog sniff might impermissibly extend the scope of a stop under Article I, section 9, andthe Fourth Amendment, even though dog sniffs are not per se seizures. See State v. Soto-Navarro, 309 Or App 218, 229-30, 482 P3d 150 (2021) (dog sniff extended scope of stop of seized passenger); see also State v. Kamph, 297 OR App 687, 442 P3d 1129 (2019) (passenger is seized under the Fourth Amendment when a car is stopped, but not under Article I, Section 9). (See, e.g., Arizona v. Johnson, 555 US 323, 327, 129 S Ct 781, 172 L Ed 2d 694 (2009)).

Practice Tip

Because dog sniffs are searches under the Fourth Amendment and Jardines, and extensions under Article I, section 9 (unless supported by reasonable suspicion), attorneys should argue that a dog sniff impermissibly extends the traffic stop when there is no constitutionally sufficient justification. In the automobile exception context, attorneys should argue that a “lawfully stopped” car cannot be searched under the automobile exception if that lawful stop is unlawfully extended.

B. A Drug Dog’s Alert Does Not Establish Probable Cause

A drug dog’s alert, standing alone, does not provide probable cause to search. State v. Foster, 350 Or 161, 177, 252 P3d 292 (2011). Determining whether an alert provided probable cause in a given situation requires “an individualized inquiry into the reliability of the particular dog involved.” Id. at 171. In determining the reliability of a particular alert, courts must consider the dog and handler team’s training, testing, and certification. See, e.g[https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=17990926071008329344&q=.,+State+v.+Snyder,+281+Or+App+308,+&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 ., State v. Snyder, 281 Or App 308, 383 P3d 357 (2016)] (evidence was insufficient to demonstrate the dog’s training and certification).

C. State Must Prove Reliability Based on Dog’s Training, Testing, and Certifications

The types of training, testing, and certification used by the dog-handler team are important to the court’s consideration. Id. The training procedures used by the handler-dog team must “ensure[] that the dog alerts in response to drug odors, as opposed to, for example, a desire for a reward, non-drug odors, or handler cues of physical or scent trails left by the person who hid the drugs.” Id. Likewise, a reliable team must undergo testing “in a controlled environment, where precautions against human cuing have been taken and the dog’s accuracy can be assessed because the persons conducting the test know where the dog should alert and where it should not.” Id.

For example, the Oregon Supreme Court held that a drug-detecting dog alert was reliable where the K-9 officer testified at some length and with great specificity regarding the dog’s training and certification procedures as well as its strong performance in training exercises. Foster, 350 Or at 164- 66. Conversely, an alert by a different drug-detecting dog did not give rise to probable cause where a K-9 officer testified about the training of the dog in a generalized way without providing specific information about the training methods used or the performance of the dog during training. State v. Helzer, 350 Or 153, 157-59, 252 P3d 288 (2011); see also State v. Farmer, 258 Or App 693, 714, 311 P3d 888 (2013) (holding that an alert from a drug-detecting dog was not sufficiently reliable to give officers probable cause where the handler did not provide specific details regarding the training procedures used to ensure the dog’s accuracy).

That the state failed to present sufficient evidence of the dog’s reliability for its alert to establish probable cause by itself does not necessarily mean that the alert is wholly irrelevant. Helzer, 350 Or at 156 n 2. If the state falls short in establishing the reliability of a drug dog alert, evidence that the alert had some reliability may allow officers to use it as a factor in establishing probable cause to conduct a search. Id.

V. The Federal Automobile Exception

The federal automobile exception does not justify the search of an automobile that is parked on the curtilage, even if it would otherwise justify the search of that same automobile at that same time were it parked in a place open to the public. The federal automobile exception only justifies searches inside the automobile; it does not extend to houses or curtilage on which automobiles are parked. A search of an automobile on curtilage is a search of the curtilage and, therefore, the home. In general, the federal automobile exception is far less restrictive on searches than is Oregon’s, but it is important to note that it cannot apply to vitiate the protections from search of other areas that are more private under the Fourth Amendment.
