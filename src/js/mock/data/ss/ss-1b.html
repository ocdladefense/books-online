Search

 

 

 

I.   What Qualifies as a “Search” Under Article 1, Section 9

Article I, section 9, protects “the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable search, or seizure.” The Article 1, section 9, search analysis involves one basic question: has a state actor infringed upon the privacy to which one has a right? State v. Ainsworth, 310 Or 613, 619 n 8, 801 P2d 749, 753 (1990). That is determined by the “'objective test of whether the government’s conduct would significantly impair an individual’s interest in freedom from scrutiny.’” State v. Wacker, 317 Or 419, 425, 856 P2d 1029 (1993), quoting State v. Dixson/Digby, 307 Or 195, 211, 766 P2d 1015 (1988). Again, “[T]he privacy protected by Article I, section 9, is not the privacy that one reasonably expects but the privacy to which one has a right.” State v. Campbell, 306 Or 157, 164, 759 P2d 1040 (1988) (emphasis in original) (citing State v. Tanner, 304 Or 312, 321-2 n 7, 745 P2d 757 (1987) “[A] defendant is not required to assert a protected property or privacy interest on which the state intruded. Rather, consistent with ORS 133.693(4), the burden is on the state to prove that [a state actor] * * * did not violate a protected interest of the defendant.” State v. Tucker, 330 Or 85, 88-89, 997 P2d 182 (2000) (emphasis in original). Although searches are usually considered to be a function of sight, hearing, smell, and touch, many different kinds of information-gathering activities may be searches if these efforts invade a protected privacy interest under Article 1, section 9.

 

A.   Physical Invasions of Private Spaces

 

“The privacy interests that are protected by Article I, section 9, commonly are circumscribed by the space in which they exist and, more particularly, by the barriers to public entry (physical and sensory) that define that private space.” State v. Smith, 327 Or 366, 373, 963 P2d 642, 646 (1998). Therefore, the first step is to determine whether an individual has a general privacy interest, and second, whether the individual’s privacy interest was invaded.

 

1.   Private Dwellings

 

For constitutional purposes, the home is sacrosanct. State v. Davis, 295 Or 227, 243, 666 P2d 802, 809 (1983). (“The very purpose of [Article I, section 9] was to protect a person’s home from governmental intrusions.”). State v. Fair, 353 Or 588, 600–01, 302 P3d 417, 426–27 (2013) (“An ultimate objective of the constitutional protections, both state and federal, against unreasonable searches and seizures is ‘to protect the individual in the sanctity of his [or her] home[.]’”); See also Silverman v. United States, 365 US 505, 512, 81 S Ct 679, 683, 5 L Ed 2d 734 (1961) (“A sane, decent, civilized society must provide some such oasis, some shelter from public scrutiny, some insulated enclosure, some enclave, some inviolate place which is a man’s castle.”).

 

The degree to which law enforcement conduct intrudes on a citizen’s protected interest in privacy and liberty is significantly affected by where the conduct occurs, such as in the home, in an automobile, or on a public street. See State v. Tourtillott, 289 Or 845, 865, 618 P2d 423 (1980). A government intrusion into the home is at the extreme end of the spectrum: “Nothing is as personal or private. Nothing is more inviolate.” Id.

 

Indeed, even if police observed evidence within an arm’s reach just over the threshold of an open front door, the officer could not reach inside the home without effecting a search; that is, without a warrant or an exception to the warrant requirement, such conduct would be unreasonable. State v. Lorenzo, 356 Or 134, 139, 335 P3d 821, 824 (2014) (“The state concedes that the officer violated Article I, section 9, when he reached inside defendant’s apartment to knock on his bedroom door.”); State v. Owens, 302 Or 196, 218, 729 P2d 524, 537–38 (1986) (“police may lawfully be able to observe the entire contents of a small garage through a window that opens onto a public street, but this fact is irrelevant to whether an entry into the garage is a ‘search’”).

 

“Residence in a house is uniformly deemed to be a sufficient basis for concluding that the violation of the privacy of the house violated the residents’ privacy interests, but there is no reason to assume that the class of persons with privacy interests in a house is limited to residents.” Tanner, 304 Or at 321 (the defendant had an expectation of privacy in another person’s residence by virtue of the fact that the home was the repository of the defendant’s collateral on a loan); City of Eugene v. Silva, 198 Or App 101, 106-107, 108 P3d 23 (2005) (the defendant was an invited guest at the residence, so when the police trespassed into the backyard of the house it constituted a search and a violation of the defendant’s Article 1, section 9, rights). Furthermore, “[t]he state, as proponent of the evidence, has the burden of proving an implicit invitation to public entry sufficient to overcome the presumption of trespass.” State v. Pierce, 226 Or App 336, 345, 203 P3d 343, 349-50 (2009).

 

2.   Curtilage of Private Dwellings

 

A person also has a privacy interest in the curtilage of the dwelling. State v. Breshears/Oliver, 98 Or App 105, 111, 779 P2d 158 (1989). The curtilage includes the area adjoining the house and all outbuildings used in connection with it, such as garages, sheds and barns. State v. Lee, 120 Or 643, 648–49, 253 P 533 (1927). “Whether the place searched is within the curtilage is to be determined from the facts, including its proximity or annexation to the dwelling, its inclusion within the general enclosure surrounding the dwelling and its use and enjoyment as an adjunct to the domestic economy of the family.” State v. Russo, 68 Or App 760, 763, 683 P2d 163, 165 (1984) (citing Care v. United States, 231 F2d 22, 25 (10th Cir 1956), cert den 351 U.S. 932, 76 S Ct 788, 100 L Ed 1461 (1956)). However, a person impliedly consents to visitors going to the front door of the person’s house, provided the person has not manifested intent to forbid the intrusion of casual visitors onto the property. State v. Ohling, 70 Or App 249, 688 P2d 1384, rev den, 298 Or 334, 691 P2d 483 (1984) (entry to knock on the front door is common in this society “unless there are posted warnings, a fence, a moat filled with crocodiles, or other evidence of a desire to exclude casual visitors”). “In order to exclude visitors from going to the front door, a person must make a greater showing than that which would be required to exclude individuals who would use the property for their own purposes, such as hiking.” State v. Gabbard, 129 Or App 122, 126-27, 877 P2d 1217, 1220 (1994) rev den, 320 Or 131 (1994). “Each case depends on its factual circumstances.” State v. Cam, 255 Or App 1, 6, 296 P3d 578, 582 (2013) as modified on recons, 256 Or App 146, 300 P3d 208 (2013) rev den, 354 Or 148 (2013). A gated driveway and signs stating “private property” do not sufficiently manifest a defendant’s intent to exclude uninvited visitors, and thus a police officer's warrantless entry onto a defendant’s property does not violate the state constitutional prohibition on unreasonable searches because the gate had been left open, and the sign indicating that the property was private did not in and of itself suggest that visitors to the property were excluded. Id. at 6. On the other hand, “No Trespassing” signs, posted on and near a driveway gate, and a four- to five-foot boundary fence around defendant’s rural property, were sufficient to show that defendant intended to exclude public from entering his property, and to establish constitutionally protected privacy interest in land outside the curtilage, even though gate was open at time police entered driveway. State v. Roper, 254 Or App 197, 294 P3d 517 (2012) rev den, 353 Or 714 (2013).

 

Furthermore, “intrusions onto a home’s curtilage for purposes other than to contact persons at the front door are presumptively trespasses, unless the circumstances so strongly evince an invitation to the public that it can be said that the homeowner has implicitly invited entry.” State v. Olinger, 240 Or App 215, 221, 246 P3d 20 (2010) rev den, 350 Or 423 (2011) (officers seeking to contact homeowner were entitled to use the driveway that led to the house, but could not go back onto an “offshoot” of the driveway that went behind the house). However, “the intrusion to which an occupant impliedly consents is limited. By impliedly consenting to one form of intrusion, an occupant does not necessarily consent to being subjected to other forms of scrutiny as well.” State v. Portrey, 134 Or App 460, 465, 896 P2d 7 (1995) (action of officer in picking up and examining boots which he observed in plain view on top of box on front porch of the defendant’s apartment constituted an illegal search even though the defendant impliedly consented to visitors coming to his front door). “The rebuttable presumption of implied consent to approach the front door of the home is bounded by two considerations: location and behavior. An officer exceeds the implied consent as to location when the officer deviates from the path to the front door and explores other areas of the curtilage where, according to social norms, visitors would not have an implied invitation * * * Those same social norms constrain the behavior of an officer, even when he is present in a permissible area of the curtilage.” State v. Goldberg, 309 Or App 660, 662 (2021) (holding that an officer’s actions constitute an unlawful search if, either by location or action, the officer exceeded the reasonable scope of the implied consent, as defined by the expected social norms of behaviors for persons to enter onto the property for purpose of making contact with the occupant). The court further determined that the officer exceeded those reasonable social expectations with regard to “action” when in furtherance of a hit-and-run investigation, he took a piece of broken car bumper from the scene, walked onto the defendant’s driveway, and crouched down to hold the broken piece up to the defendant’s vehicle like a jigsaw puzzle piece. Id. at 669.

 

Although officers may enter onto a person’s property to knock on the front door, they commit a trespass when they “begin randomly walking” around the remainder of the property and invade an individual’s privacy interest. State v. Malvern, 230 Or App 370, 374, 215 P3d 114, 116 (2009) (detectives’ observations of defendant’s marijuana plants inside the greenhouse were made from an unlawful vantage point while “‘meandering’ around the property”). “Going to the back of the house is a different matter. Such an action is both less common and less acceptable in our society. There is no implied consent for a stranger to do so.” Ohling, 70 Or App at 252. Entry to other areas of the curtilage is presumptively a trespass, but it “can be overcome by evidence that the homeowner has sufficiently implicitly or explicitly invited entry.” State v. Somfleth, 168 Or App 414, 425, 8 P3d 221, 227 (2000) (presumption of trespass was not overcome when officers entered through an open backyard gate and there was a path leading to the back door); See also Silva, 198 Or App at 107-08 (officer’s entry into ungated but fenced backyard was a trespass); State v. Hayes, 186 Or App 49, 61 P3d 960 (2003) rev den, 339 Or 230 (2005) (officer’s approach to the back door of defendant’s home constituted unlawful trespass when the back door was not visible from the public street providing access to defendant’s home, there was no doorbell on the back door, and with the exception of the officers in this case, all other strangers who visited the property, including solicitors, used only the front door). On the other hand, the presumption of trespass has been overcome when an officer attempts to contact a defendant from a side door, rather than front door. State v. Glines, 134 Or App 21, 894 P2d 516 (1995) rev den, 321 Or 512 (1995) (the side entry was about eight feet from front wall of defendant’s house, it was adjacent to common driveway that defendant shared with his neighbor, it was visible from public sidewalk, it was equipped with doorbell, area in which it was located was not fenced, and there was no evidence of any “no trespassing” or “no solicitors” signs).

 

3.   Curtilage or Common Areas Surrounding Multi-Tenant Dwellings

 

“Because of the differences between a typical single-family dwelling and multiple-family dwellings, a strict application of the traditional curtilage doctrine to apartment dwellings should not be determinative of whether privacy rights exist.” State v. Larson, 159 Or App 34, 40, 977 P2d 1175, 1178, rev den, 329 Or 318 (1999). Instead, the physical layout of the living units and the residents’ use of the area in question is of particular significance when assessing whether a particular area of an apartment complex is one in which an individual has privacy rights under the state constitution. Id. at 40. For example, in Larson, the officer’s entry into ground-level back area of apartment complex to peer up at defendant’s second-floor window violated defendant’s privacy interests under state constitution because the area was not available for use by people other than tenants and perhaps their guests, and ground-level tenant had posted “keep out” sign. See also Breshears/Oliver, 98 Or App 105 (defendants had a privacy interest in side yard of their apartment which was surrounded by a fence, apartment buildings, and brush and reached by walking across a grassy area off the walkway); State v. Roles, 75 Or App 63, 705 P2d 227 (1985) (because roof area was accessible only from defendant’s second-floor apartment, it was a protected area). Even if an officer obtains consent from a resident to be in a common area their actions may still invade a privacy interest. State v. Fortmeyer, 178 Or App 485, 491-92, 37 P3d 223 (2001) (officers investigating narcotics tip conducted a search of defendants’ residence by kneeling down at a particular angle in common area adjacent to residence and peering through cracks in an otherwise obstructed basement window, even though they obtained permission to enter the common area). Conversely, an officer had implied consent to walk through center door of porch of a fourplex, where the door was the entryway to two upstairs units, marked by two address numbers, was unlocked, there was no doorbell, intercom or buzzer for members of the public to use to let the upstairs residents know they wished to gain entry, and upstairs residents took no action to show they intended to exclude visitors from the stairway. State v. Stafford, 184 Or App 674, 678, 57 P3d 598 (2002) rev den, 335 Or 181 (2003); Portrey, 134 Or App 460 (the defendant had impliedly consented to visitors coming to his apartment door). A police officer’s entrance into private condominium driveway in parking lot did not invade privacy interest of resident even though parking lot location was isolated and “residents parking only” sign was posted at entrance of driveway; neither isolated location nor sign provided objective evidence of intent to exclude public from parking lot for all purposes. State v. Erb, 135 Or App 421, 426, 899 P2d 716 (1995) rev den, 322 Or 421 (1995).

 

4.   Open Fields

 

There is no open fields exception to the Oregon constitutional limitation on searches and seizures, but “[a] person who wishes to preserve a constitutionally protected privacy interest in land outside the curtilage must manifest an intention to exclude the public by erecting barriers to entry, such as fences, or by posting signs.” Dixson/Digby, 307 Or at 208, 211-12. Thus, if a person manifests such an intention by, for example, posting signs or erecting fences, a law enforcement officer cannot enter the property based solely on reasonable suspicion; the officer’s entry must be justified by a warrant or an exception to the warrant requirement. State v. McKee, 272 Or App 372 (2015).

 

A court will consider all surrounding circumstances to determine a residents’ intent. State v. McIntyre/Pereira, 123 Or App 436, 440, 860 P2d 299 (1993), rev den, 318 Or 351, 870 P2d 220 (1994). For example, “No Trespassing” signs, posted on and near driveway gate, and a four- to five-foot boundary fence around the defendant’s rural property, were sufficient to show that the defendant intended to exclude public from entering his property, and to establish a constitutionally protected privacy interest in land outside the curtilage, even though gate was open at time police entered driveway. Roper, 254 Or App at 200. A driveway sign stating “private road no unauthorized persons beyond this point” did not allow entry and required authorization from the property owner, and thus, trooper’s entry into the property violated the defendant’s constitutional right to privacy. State ex rel. Juvenile Dept of Coos County. v. Reeves, 163 Or App 497, 988 P2d 433 (1999). An owner of rural property took sufficient steps to manifest prohibition against public entry when the owner’s mailbox and newspaper box were on highway at base of driveway, “before entering driveway, visitor was told not to trespass, that admonition was followed by warning sign for guard dog, and that warning, in turn, was followed by command to ‘stop.’” State v. Poulos, 149 Or App 351, 942 P2d 901 (1997). On the other hand, posting a “No Trespassing” sign facing the road in front of property is insufficient to exclude the public or police from entering where no signs, barriers, or fences indicate the property boundaries. Gabbard, 129 Or App at 128 (where an officer does not see a “No Trespassing” sign while making no deliberate effort to avoid seeing it, the sign does not adequately manifest defendant’s intent to exclude visitors). Additionally, a single strand of barbed wire lying on the ground is not sufficient to show intent to exclude public or police from property; even if officers had seen the “No Trespassing” sign, they could have reasonably concluded that the sign was intended only to exclude those who might put property to their own uses, but that it did not apply to visitors who desired to contact residents. State v. Poppe, 131 Or App 14, 22, 883 P2d 905 (1994) rev den, 320 Or 492 (1994). “No trespass signs on the adjacent field did not establish that defendant intended to restrict access to his driveway * * * [when] [t]here was a fence separating the field from defendant’s driveway, and defendant had left the gate to the driveway open.” State v. Gorham, 121 Or App 347, 353, 854 P2d 971, 975 (1993) rev den, 318 Or 171 (1993).

 

5.   Vehicles

 

Opening the door of a vehicle constitutes a search. State v. Wetzell, 148 Or App 122, 125, 939 P2d 106, 107 (1997) rev den, 325 Or 621 (1997). A police officer’s act of completely opening door of defendant’s truck, which was already open three or four inches, was a search under state constitution where officer physically grasped door handle and moved door to expose truck’s passenger compartment to visual inspection of scope and intensity that could not have been made without opening door. State v. Rhodes, 315 Or 191, 196-97, 843 P2d 927 (1992). Physical intrusions into vehicles will often invade a privacy interest. See State v. Kosta, 304 Or 549, 554-55, 748 P2d 72 (1987) (opening of car’s trunk was a “search”); State v. Kock, 302 Or 29, 725 P2d 1285 (1986) (opening of car door, reaching in and seizing package to identify it, was a “search”); State v. Brown, 301 Or 268, 721 P2d 1357 (1986) (entry into car’s passenger compartment, glove box, and truck was a “search”); State v. Hendricks, 151 Or App 271, 948 P2d 740 (1997) (officer conducted an unreasonable search when he inserted his head into the open window of the defendant’s vehicle and then noticed alcohol on the defendant’s breath). In addition to physical intrusions into a vehicle ordering occupants out of a vehicle is a search. State v. Finlay, 170 Or App 359, 12 P3d 999 (2000). Furthermore, a person also retains a privacy interest in items in the open bed of a truck. State v. Guynn, 162 Or App 594, 986 P2d 1190 (1999). The search and seizure protections provided by state constitution also apply to the boarding of boats. State v. Lecarros, 187 Or App 105, 110, 66 P3d 543 (2003).

 

6.   Closed Containers

 

Even if the officer has probable cause to believe that a closed container contains contraband, the officer may not open it to examine its contents without a warrant or an exception to the warrant requirement. State v. Fugate, 210 Or App 8, 150 P3d 409 (2006) (police officer’s opening folded piece of tinfoil that defendant handed to officer constituted “search” that had to be supported by probable cause and either warrant or exception to warrant requirement; tinfoil was opaque container and there was no evidence presented that such containers invariably contain controlled substances and nothing else); State v. Haney, 153 Or App 642, 958 P2d 192 (1998) (police officer’s intentional patdown of book bag was a “search” within meaning of State Constitution’s prohibition against unreasonable search or seizure, where contents of book bag were not open to public view).

 

However, examination of the contents of containers that announce their complete contents is not a search at all, and thus an officer needs neither probable cause nor an exception to the warrant requirement to examine the container’s contents. State v. Kruchek, 156 Or App 617, 969 P2d 386 (1998), aff’d, 331 Or 664, 20 P3d 180 (2001).

 

The court has cautioned against using the “announce its contents” phrasing for fear that it inappropriately places the focus on the container as opposed to the sensory perceptions of the officer:

 

“We use the terms ‘may be revealed,’ as opposed to ‘may announce its contents,’ because we believe that the latter phrase, while felicitous in some respects, slightly skews the appropriate inquiry by adding an anthropomorphic element. It is not the container—an inanimate object—that ‘announces’ its contents. Rather, it is the person who observes the container who perceives the likely contents of the container by virtue of sensory clues. We encourage future litigants to concentrate on sensory clues, without suggesting that the container somehow intended to present them.”

 

State v. Heckathorne, 347 Or 474, 485 fn. 11, 223 P3d 1034, 1040 (2009).

 

According to the court, “(1) the contents of a container may be revealed, not only visually, but also by the container’s feel or smell, and, possibly, by its taste or sound; and (2) individual expertise and training may provide the knowledge that turns various sensory clues into probable cause to search a container. Id. at 484-85. (footnote omitted).

 

A container can announce its contents through sound, although it is unlikely to extinguish a person’s privacy interest without more. State v. Walker, 173 Or App 46, 50-51, 20 P3d 834 (2001) (plastic bubble-soap container that rattled when shaken announced “I contain bubble soap” not “I contain drugs” because “the bottle could have contained any number of small, hard objects that would have caused it to rattle when shook, including the bubble-blowing wand”).

 

The “announces its contents” rule is predicated on the plain view doctrine. Owens, 302 Or at 206 (discussing plain view justification). Thus, the entire contents of the container must be readily observable or any intrusion into the container constitutes a search. Said another way, when it is “[p]roperly understood, Owens stands for the proposition that the police do not conduct a search by opening a container that has already announced all of its contents” and “[u]nless it is apparent that container at issue holds nothing other than contraband, a warrant is required before police lawfully can open it, unless exception to warrant requirement applies.” Kruchek, 156 Or app 617 (warrantless opening of opaque plastic cooler from which the odor of marijuana emanated was a search because the cooler did not reveal that marijuana was the only item it contained).

 

However, some opaque containers (such as balloons or tinfoil bundles) “may be so uniquely associated with the storage and transportation of controlled substances that their unique packaging alone might provide, to an officer with training and experience in the area of drug detection, probable cause to believe they contain a controlled substance[,]” which could trigger the search incident to arrest exception. State v. Stock, 209 Or App 7, 13, 146 P3d 393 (2006). The factors to evaluate probable cause to search based largely on evidence of a particular container that could contain drugs “include the nature of the container itself, the context in which the container was found, and the knowledge and experience of the investigating police officer.” Id. State v. Edmiston, 229 Or App 411, 416-17, 211 P3d 340 (2009). Evidence of officer’s view of small plastic bag in defendant’s day planner, together with that of his experience and training, was insufficient to establish probable cause because “[t]he fact that this type of baggie is commonly used to package methamphetamine does not determine whether this particular baggie contained anything, much less controlled substances”). State v. Currin, 258 Or App 715, 311 P3d 903 (2013) (plain envelope was not uniquely associated with drugs and did not tend to announce its contents sufficiently to give officer probable cause to believe that it contained contraband). Other containers, like film canisters, may often be connected with drugs, but do not necessarily give rise to probable cause without additional evidence. Compare State v. Lanig, 154 Or App 665, 669, 963 P2d 58 (1998) (film canister “is not so uniquely associated with the storage and transportation of drugs that, by itself, it suggests that it contains drugs”); and State v. Lane, 135 Or App 233, 242, 898 P2d 1358 (1995) (film canister in same car as marijuana pipe does not provide probable cause in the absence of “specific conduct toward the unopened containers”); with State v. Poulson, 150 Or App 164, 945 P2d 1084 (1997) (film canister found directly alongside numerous items of drug paraphernalia provides probable cause). 

 

7.   Papers and Effects

 

“Papers” are specifically protected under Article I, section 9. Therefore before police read a person’s private papers, the police must have a warrant or recognized exception to the warrant requirement that authorizes intrusion. State v. Barnum, 136 Or App 167, 176 n 1, 902 P2d 95, 101 (1995) rev den, 323 Or 336 (1996) (reading a notebook is a search). Article I, section 9, protects both possessory and privacy interests in effects. Owens, 302 Or at 206. Examination of a person’s private videotape is a “search.” State v. Munro, 339 Or 545, 550, 124 P3d 1221, 1223 (2005).

 

8.   Public Restrooms

 

“‘The final bastion of privacy is to be found in the area of human procreation and excretion’ and ‘[if] a person is entitled to any shred of privacy, then it is to privacy as to these matters.’” State v. Casconi, 94 Or App 457, 461, 766 P2d 397, 399 (1988) (quoting Sterling v. Cupp, 44 Or App 755, 761, 607 P2d 206 (1980), aff’d as modified, 290 Or 611, 624, 625 P2d 123 (1981)) (allowing the police to conduct hidden surveillance of a doorless toilet stall significantly impairs freedom from scrutiny). “A restroom is a place that, by its very nature, excludes unlimited observation. A person in a public restroom anticipates that another person might enter and see what is going on. What a person does not anticipate is that his activity will be seen by concealed officers or recorded by concealed cameras. That police surveillance, which allows no ready means for the person to determine that he is being watched, significantly impairs the people’s freedom from scrutiny.” State v. Owczarzak, 94 Or App 500, 504, 766 P2d 399, 401 (1988) (although defendant’s conduct took place in an area open to the view of others in the restroom, he had a protected privacy interest). Furthermore, an officer conducts a search under the state constitution by unlocking door and entering public restroom to arrest a defendant. State v. Holiday, 258 Or App 601, 310 P3d 1149 (2013). However, in State v. Wilson, 291 Or App 581, 586–88, 422 P3d 402 (2018), the court held that the officers did not violate a protected privacy right of the defendant’s when they entered a public restroom and saw defendant lying on the floor of a closed stall that had a 12–inch gap between the stall partition and the floor. Both officers bent over at the waist at a 90–degree angle and immediately saw defendant masturbating.

 

9.   Tents, Other Structures, and Homeless Encampments

 

Oregon law has not recognized a right to privacy in temporary residences, such as tents or homeless encampments where those structures have been erected in unlawful locations and are subject to abatement and removal by law. State v. Tegland, 269 Or App 1, 344 P3d 63, 65 (2015). There, the defendant had constructed a make-shift shelter on a public sidewalk comprised of a shopping cart, a wooden pallet, and multiple tarps. A police officer lifted one of the tarps and observed defendant inside with drugs.

 

The Court of Appeals held that the officers’ conduct did not impair “the people’s freedom from scrutiny,” and was thus not a search, for three reasons: “First, defendant’s structure violated the city code prohibition against temporary structures on a public right-of-way. Second, the police had authorization, under the city code, to summarily abate any such obstruction, meaning that the officers were authorized under the city code to summarily deconstruct and remove the encroaching structure. And, third, the police had previously informed defendant that he could not camp in that spot.” Id. at 7-8.

 

The Washington Court of Appeals, however, has taken a more sympathetic view to the realities of homelessness. State v. Pippin, 200 Wash App 826, 845, 403 P3d 907, 917 (2017). The Washington court focused heavily on the purpose of its search and seizure provision related to the protection of intimate information from government scrutiny: i.e., items revealing a person’s familial, political, religious, or sexual associations or beliefs, hopes, and fears. According to the court, “the temporary nature of Pippin’s tent does not undermine [that] privacy interest * * * For the homeless, this may often be the only refuge for privacy in the world as it is.” The court further rejected the primary basis underlying the rationale in Tegland, concluding that “Pippin’s privacy interests are not diminished by his lack of permission to camp at that location.” And the court rejected the notion that Pippen had voluntarily chosen to live in a tent on public land and therefore had in some way acquiesced to the intrusion: “to call homelessness voluntary, and thus unworthy of basic privacy protections, is to walk blindly among the realities around us. Worse, such an argument would strip those on the street of the protections given the rest of us directly because of their poverty. Our constitution means something better.” Id. at 845. See also Carrie Leonetti, The Wild, Wild West: The Right of the Unhoused to Privacy in Their Encampments, 56 Am. Crim. L. Rev. 399, 410 (2019) (noting factors that could be helpful in distinguishing Tegland from future cases, for example “whether the dwelling was trespassory (as opposed to, at least implicitly, authorized), the identity of the victim of the trespass (federal government, local government, private property owner), whether the trespasser had notice of the trespass and the nature of the notice”).

 

B.   Visual Invasions of Privacy Interests

 

1.   “Special Efforts”

 

Police observation of an item in “plain view” from a “lawful vantage point” is not usually considered a search because no privacy right is violated. E.g., State v. Gohring, 311 Or 33, 39, 803 P2d 1189 (1991) (unaided aerial observation of marijuana plants).

 

Although police observation from a lawful vantage point is not generally considered a search, the determination of whether the officer’s conduct amounted to search depends on whether the police relied on “special efforts” to observe the defendants’ conduct in an otherwise protected area and whether the officer’s conduct violated social norms. State v. Louis, 296 Or 57, 61, 672 P2d 708 (1983) (“The question is when observation becomes a ‘search’ within the legal meaning of that term. Persons may conduct themselves in otherwise protected areas in such a way that their words or acts can plainly be seen or heard outside without any special effort.”); See State v. Castillo–Salgado, 186 Or App. 605, 611, 64 P3d 1169, rev den, 336 Or 60, 77 P3d 635 (2003) (police officer did not engage in a search when he “observed illegal activity from a lawful vantage point through a three-inch gap in the blinds after his attention was drawn to the interior of the room by movement inside the apartment as he approached the front door” and “took no extraordinary efforts to look into the apartment” and his conduct did not offend social norms); State v. Rodriguez–Ganegar, 186 Or App. 530, 537-38, 63 P3d 1225 (2003) (police officer did not engage in a search when he observed, without any “special effort,” illegal activity through a three-fourths to one-inch vertical gap in the curtains of a motel room window after “his attention happened to be drawn * * * by a series of loud noises” from inside the room, and the activity inside the room would have been “plainly visible to any passerby whose attention might be drawn to activities inside the room”).

 

The action of a five-foot-ten-inch police officer standing on rock to peer over solid, six-foot fence did not violate resident’s privacy interest, where others could have peered into backyard without standing on rock; “defendant does not have privacy interest which applied only against short people.” State v. Corra, 88 Or App 339, 342, 745 P2d 786 (1987) rev den, 305 Or 331 (1988).

 

In Wilson, 291 Or App at 586, the court noted that the officers “did not look under and up the stall partition but bent at the waist from several feet away. “ Thus, the court concluded, although the defendant was inside the stall with the door closed, which typically secures a protected right to privacy, he nevertheless exposed himself to the common area of the restroom when he lay down on the floor such that any child upon entering the restroom would have seen defendant exposing his genitals without bending, the police did not engage in any “special efforts” by briefly bending over to better observe defendant, and the conduct—briefly leaning over to glance under a stall door to determine whether it is occupied does not necessarily violate any social norms.

 

However, in Fortmeyer, 178 Or App at 491-92, the court determined that the police violated the defendants’ right to privacy after an officer obtained a neighbor’s permission to access a common area outside the defendants’ residence and observed unlawful activity by kneeling down at the defendants’ basement window and peering through a crack between a door panel leaned against the window on the outside of the building and a piece of cardboard covering the window on the inside of the building. The court noted that it was “uncommon” and socially “unacceptable” to kneel down and lean one’s head against a window of a private residence to see through a gap in cardboard placed to block off the interior from view).

 

Moreover, if an officer’s action exposes what would otherwise be hidden from observation it is a search. Portrey, 134 Or App at 465. (Action of officer in picking up and examining boots which he observed in plain view on top of box on front porch of defendant’s apartment constituted an illegal search even though he impliedly consented to visitors coming to his front door.)

 

An officer’s use of flashlight to discern contents of film canister found in defendant’s purse during course of otherwise lawful search for weapons revealed evidence not otherwise exposed to public view, amounting to unlawful search in violation of defendant’s state constitutional privacy interest in contents of her purse. State v. Reed, 169 Or App 456, 9 P3d 738 (2000).

 

2.   Visual Inspections of Vehicles

 

Courts have been less protective of vehicles, but the same considerations should control the outcome in any particular case—did the police engage in special efforts to inspect the interior of a vehicle and did their conduct violate social norms?

 

Police officers observing the defendant while he was in parked automobile did not conduct “search,” as officers did not invade the defendant’s protected privacy interests; the automobile was in the parking lot of an open tavern while tavern patrons passed regularly within a few feet of automobile and automobile’s interior light was on. Wacker, 317 Or at 426-27. “An officer who has lawfully stopped a vehicle does not violate any occupant’s rights in walking around the vehicle and looking through the windows of the vehicle to observe that which can be plainly seen.” State v. Jackson, 296 Or 430, 438, 677 P2d 21 (1984). Where officer was properly on sidewalk, anything which he saw when he looked in window of truck was in plain view. State v. Orlovski, 146 Or App 632, 933 P2d 976 (1997).

 

C.   Tactile Invasions of Privacy Interests

 

“When additional activity beyond that available to an ordinary observer is required to obtain information, a ‘search’ under Article I, section 9, has occurred.” Haney, 153 Or App at 646. Therefore, touching or feeling by an officer can constitute a search if it invades an individual’s privacy interests. State v. Cardell, 180 Or App 104, 109, 41 P3d 1111 (2002) (police officer had implied consent to walk up driveway and past suspect vehicle to reach the front door of the defendant’s residence, officer exceeded that consent when he touched vehicle’s tires in an attempt to obtain information that would aid him in his investigation of report that cars had been racing on city streets); Portrey, 134 Or App at 465 (action of officer in picking up and examining boots which he observed in plain view on top of box on front porch of the defendant’s apartment constituted an illegal search even though the defendant impliedly consented to visitors coming to his front door); State v. Dickerson, 135 Or App 192, 898 P2d 193 (1995) (opening pocketknife is search because it revealed evidence on blade not otherwise exposed to public view). Taking a person’s pulse is a search. State v Stowers, 136 Or App 448, 453, 902 P2d 117 (1995) (person’s pulse is private and not subject to examination absent warrant or constitutionally recognized exception to warrant requirement). The court has also held that although “[a] patdown is an intrusion (indeed, it is a search), it normally is sufficient to identify those objects that are relevant to officer safety concerns while leaving other information and objects relatively private * * * [b]ut intrusion into a suspect’s clothing requires something more—either probable cause or some greater justification.” State v. Rudder, 347 Or 14, 24–25, 217 P3d 1064 (2009) (emphasis added). Opening and inspecting contents of a purse which officer had lawfully seized from the defendant on ground that purse could have been used as a weapon against officer was a search. State v. Schellhorn, 95 Or App 297, 769 P2d 221 (1989). Police officer’s intentional patdown of book bag that he was removing the bag from a car that was to be towed because of the defendant’s lack of insurance was a “search” where contents of book bag were not open to public view. Haney, 153 Or App at 642.

 

D.  Medical Testing, Field Sobriety Tests, Chemical Substance Tests

 

A blood test for alcohol content is a search. State v. Machuca, 347 Or 644, 657, 227 P3d 729, 736 (2010) (“The evanescent nature of a suspect’s blood alcohol content is an exigent circumstance that will ordinarily permit a warrantless blood draw * * * [h]owever...particular facts may show, in the rare case, that a warrant could have been obtained and executed significantly faster than the actual process otherwise used under the circumstances.”). A breath test is a search. State v. Girod, 245 Or App 642, 263 P3d 1113 (2011) (applying Machuca to a breath test).

 

Additionally, a urine analysis is a search subject to the same warrant or probable cause and exigent circumstances requirements discussed in Machuca. State v. Fuller, 252 Or App 245, 254, 287 P3d 1147 (2012).

 

A field sobriety test is a search and must be based on probable cause in which the officer subjectively believes that a crime has been committed and that belief must be objectively reasonable. State v. Stroup, 147 Or App 118, 124, 935 P2d 438 (1997) (police officer lacked objective probable cause to subject motorist to field sobriety test based on slight odor of alcohol, bloodshot eyes, and motorist’s admission to drinking alcoholic beverages ten hours earlier; motorist was not stopped for erratic driving, nor did she exhibit typical characteristics of person who was under the influence such as slurred speech or impaired balance). Police officers can lawfully administer FSTs if (1) they have probable cause of DUII combined with exigent circumstances justifying a warrantless search, or (2) the defendant voluntarily consents to the search.

 

State v. Forrest, 174 Or App 129, 134-35, 25 P3d 392, 395 (2001) (as the proponent of the results of the warrantless administration of FSTs, the state must establish both that the search was supported by probable cause that the suspect/defendant was driving under the influence of intoxicants, and that a recognized exception to the warrant requirement-generally, exigent circumstances-applied).

 

E.   Invasions of Privacy Interests by Smell

 

When odors emanate into public places and are smelled and identified by police officers, the information gained is not the product of a search. State v. Brown, 109 Or App 636, 820 P2d 878 (1991) rev den, 313 Or 210 (1992) (detective’s observations of strong odor of methamphetamine at defendant’s residence was not product of warrantless search of the defendant’s house, where detectives smelled odor when they went to door of house in attempt to interview defendant regarding unrelated homicide investigation).

 

The Supreme Court has also held that using a trained drug-detection dog to signal to trained police dog handlers that controlled substances are present within a closed container is not a search. State v. Smith, 327 Or 366, 963 P2d 642 (1998). But the court’s reasoning in Smith does not withstand scrutiny and should be directly challenged on the following grounds:

 

In 1998, the Oregon Supreme Court aligned Oregon with then-existing Fourth Amendment jurisprudence by holding that a drug-dog sniff does not constitute a search under Article I, section 9. Smith was wrong then and is even less defensible today.

 

When Smith was decided, the United States Supreme Court had held that a drug-dog sniff is not a search because it “discloses only the presence or absence of narcotics” within an enclosure and because individuals have no “legitimate” interest in possessing controlled substances, such conduct does not infringe any expectation of privacy that “society is prepared to consider reasonable.” United States v. Place, 462 US 696, 707, 103 S Ct 2637, 2644, 77 L Ed 2d 110 (1983); United States v. Jacobsen, 466 US 109, 123, 104 S Ct 1652, 1662, 80 L Ed 2d 85 (1984); but see Florida v. Jardines, 569 US 1, 11–12, 133 S Ct 1409, 1417–18, 185 L Ed 2d 495 (2013) (“The government’s use of trained police dogs to investigate the home and its immediate surroundings is a ‘search’ within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment.”).

 

However, the same conclusion is incompatible with the Oregon Constitution because “the privacy protected by Article I, section 9, is not the privacy that one reasonably expects but the privacy to which one has a right.” Campbell, 306 Or at 164 (emphasis in original). To determine whether a government practice is a search within the meaning of Article I, section 9, the question is whether “the practice, if engaged in wholly at the discretion of the government, will significantly impair ‘the people’s’ freedom from scrutiny, for the protection of that freedom is the principle that underlies the prohibition on ‘unreasonable searches’ set forth in Article I, section 9.” Id. at 171.

 

Plainly, granting police the unfettered power to tactically “deploy” narcotics detection dogs on persons, their vehicles, and their possessions without individualized suspicion, puts racial and religious minority groups at risk that such surveillance techniques will be disproportionately directed against them—as well as those who live in less affluent or more crime-plagued neighborhoods. To hold that a drug-dog sniff is not a search is to authorize police to conduct both randomized as well as targeted drug-detection raids of people congregating on street corners, park benches, church entrances, political rallies, and the common areas of certain apartment buildings. The state could even assign a trained drug dog and police handler to every city block and maintain relatively complete and constant counter-drug surveillance of the entire population. Because such a practice would significantly impair Oregonians’ freedom from scrutiny, the Oregon courts should recognize that it unquestionably meets the definition of a “search” under Article I, section 9.

 

Numerous state supreme courts have held that drug-dog sniffs are searches under their respective state constitutions for precisely that reason. People v. Dunn, 77 NY2d 19, 25-26, 564 NE2d 1054, (1990) (“To hold otherwise, we believe would raise the specter of the police roaming indiscriminately through the corridors of public housing projects with trained dogs in search of drugs. Such an Orwellian notion would be repugnant under our State Constitution.”); Com. v. Johnston, 515 Pa 454, 465, 530 A2d 74, 79 (1987) (“[I]t is our view that a free society will not remain free if police may use [narcotics detection dogs], or any other crime detection device, at random and without reason.”).

 

Other courts have been offended by the prospect that “a person stopped for a minor traffic offense, while in view of passing neighbors or friends, could have his vehicle sniffed without any basis for suspecting that it contains contraband.” State v. Pellicci, 133 NH 523, 544, 580 A2d 710, 723 (1990). “Such a public spectacle unfolding in a residential neighborhood will invariably entail a degree of public opprobrium, humiliation and embarrassment for the [target of the sniff], for such dramatic government activity in the eyes of many—neighbors, passers-by, and the public at large—will be viewed as an official accusation of crime.” Jardines v. State, 73 So 3d 34, 36 (Fla 2011), aff’d, 569 US 1, 133 S Ct 1409, 185 L Ed 2d 495 (2013).

 

The Oregon Supreme Court’s reasoning in Smith is flawed. Smith differentiated drug dogs from “[h]igh-powered telescopes, parabolic sound gathering devices, and infrared cameras [that] are able to gather ordinarily imperceptible information (in the form of light rays, sound waves, or heat waves) from the interior of an enclosed space as it emanates from that space.” 327 Or at 374. The court acknowledged that the use of those devices constituted searches, “because they provide information from an enclosed space about the enclosure’s contents that a police officer, standing at a lawful vantage point, cannot detect with ordinary human powers of perception.” Id.

 

The court continued:

 

“Superficially, one might argue that dog sniffs fall into that category—that trained police dogs perceive odor molecules as they emanate from an enclosed space and then indicate the contents of the enclosure by their reactions. But dog sniffs do not provide the kind of direct access to the interiors of enclosed spaces that the aforementioned devices provide. Dog sniffs operate on a sample of air whose origins are unknown. One may infer from the fact that a dog detects the presence of a substance in the public airspace surrounding an enclosure that the enclosure contains that substance.”

 

Id. (emphasis in original). However, the same could be said for any of the aforementioned devices. A receiver designed to detect certain light or sound waves is placed in a public space, the device interprets the information that reaches the receiver, and it provides that information to the officer. The officer then draws an inference about the criminal significance of the signal and its origin. There is nothing constitutionally or scientifically different between that and deploying a drug dog on particular car or particular container and having it smell the molecules in the air surrounding the container. A drug-dog sniff is a search, and the Oregon courts should declare as much.

 

F.   Invasions of Privacy Interests by Listening

 

Individuals have a cognizable privacy interest in the content of their telephone calls. See ORS 133.724 (setting out requirement that police obtain judicially issued warrant to intercept a telephonic communication). Listening to a cordless telephone conversation on a “scanner” is a search. State v. Carston/ Sage/ Sage/ Gralla, 323 Or 75, 913 P2d 709 (1996). However, a person does not have a privacy interest in records that are kept by a third party that do not reveal the contents of a conversation. State v. Johnson, 340 Or 319, 336, 131 P3d 173, 184 (2006) cert den, 549 US 1079 (2006) (“[W]e cannot identify a source of law that establishes that defendant also had some interest in keeping private any records kept by a third party, his cellular telephone provider, respecting his cellular telephone usage.”).

 

Unless exigent circumstances are present, a court order is required to record a conversation between an undercover police officer or an informant and a suspect. State v. Miskell, 351 Or 680, 697-98 277 P3d 522 (2012) (citing US Const, Amend IV; Or Const Art 1, § 9; ORS 133.726(7)(b)) (“We conclude that, in ORS 133.726(7)(b), the phrase ‘circumstances of such exigency that it would be unreasonable to obtain a court order,’ refers to ‘exigent circumstances’ in the specialized legal sense—as it has been used by this court in discussing exceptions to the search warrant requirement under Article I, section 9, and by the federal courts in discussing exceptions to the warrant requirement under the Fourth Amendment.”). However, if a person is informed that they are being recorded no privacy interest is violated. State v. Wischnofske, 129 Or App 231, 236, 878 P2d 1130 (1994) (the defendant had no privacy interests in statements he made while he was seated alone in back seat of patrol car following his arrest and, thus, tape-recording statements was not a “search” within meaning of State Constitution’s protection against unreasonable searches and seizures; defendant had been arrested and told that his conversations would be recorded and tape recorder was visible on passenger’s side of front seat).

 

Testimonial responses to field sobriety tests are the product of a constitutionally significant search. State v. Gile, 147 Or App 469, 936 P2d 1008 (1997).

 

G.   Technological or Figurative Invasions of Privacy Interests

 

A technological or figurative invasion is a search if it infringes upon an individual’s protected privacy interests:

 

“S]ome police techniques clearly do involve technological or figurative invasions of privacy interests. High-powered telescopes, parabolic sound gathering devices, and infrared cameras all are able to gather ordinarily imperceptible information (in the form of light rays, sound waves, or heat waves) from the interior of an enclosed space as it emanates from that space. Although such devices can operate from an external and public place, their use is invasive nevertheless, because they provide information from an enclosed space about the enclosure’s contents that a police officer, standing at a lawful vantage point, cannot detect with ordinary human powers of perception. That is, they constructively move the observing police officer into the private space.”

 

Smith, 327 Or at 374 (emphasis in the original) (suggesting that the use of technology like high-powered telescopes, parabolic sound gathering devices, and infrared cameras that are able to gather ordinarily imperceptible information from the interior of an enclosed space as it emanates from that space would infringe on a person’s privacy interest). The Oregon test to determine if the technology used violates a protected privacy interest is “whether the practice, if engaged in wholly at the discretion of the government, will significantly impair ‘the people’s’ freedom from scrutiny, for the protection of that freedom is the principle that underlies the prohibition on ‘unreasonable searches’ set forth in Article I, section 9.” Campbell, 306 Or at 171 n 8 (quoting United States v. White, 401 US 745, 786, 91 S Ct 1122, 1143, 28 L Ed 2d 453 (1971) (Harlan, J., dissenting) (“The critical question * * * is whether under our system of government, as reflected in the Constitution, we should impose on our citizens the risks of the electronic listener or observer without at least the protection of a warrant requirement.”). In focusing on that question, the court must consider “the particular context in which the government conduct occurred” and also “consider the interest for which defendant asserts constitutional protection and determine whether that interest is ‘private’ within the meaning of Article I, section 9.” Holiday, 258 Or App at 607 (quoting State v. Meredith, 337 Or 299, 306, 96 P3d 342, 345 (2004)). Generally, technology used by police to obtain an unusual but otherwise lawful vantage point is not a search. Gohring, 311 Or at 39 (the use of a fixed-wing aircraft or a helicopter at a lawful altitude to observe what could not be seen from the ground is not a search). In addition, the use of technology to observe what is readily observable without enhancement or to confirm an already lawful observation is not a search because it does not involve an intrusion into an interest that has been kept private. For example, telephoto photographing of the defendant in his living room by the police was not a “search” since it merely recorded what could be seen and had been seen without a camera, “did not shed light into darkness and provided only modest enlargement.” Louis, 296 Or 57. Similarly, in State v. Wacker, police officers observing the defendant, using passive night vision system and video camera, while the defendant was in a parked automobile did not conduct a “search,” as the officers did not invade defendant’s protected privacy interests; the car was in the parking lot of an open tavern while tavern patrons passed regularly within a few feet of it and the car’s interior light was on. 317 Or at 426-27. Police officer’s use of camera with telephoto lens during aerial drug surveillance of defendant’s yard did not constitute a “search” for purposes of search and seizure provisions of state constitution; officer positively identified marijuana plants on defendant’s property with no visual aids other than his eyeglasses before using telephoto lens to document his discovery. State v. Rodal, 161 Or App 232, 985 P2d 863 (1999). Conversely, a police officer’s use of a spotting scope to observe a marijuana plant located in interior of the defendant’s residence, was a search where plant was placed in a second story room in the defendant’s house, a large tree obscured the window, and plant was not visible to an outside observer off the premises with the naked eye. State v. Blacker, 52 Or App 1077, 630 P2d 413 (1981). Thus, “[i]ntrusions and technologically enhanced observations into “protected premises” infringe privacy interests protected by Article I, section 9, but the question whether an individual’s privacy interests have been infringed by an act of the police cannot always be resolved by reference to the area at which the act is directed.” Campbell, 306 Or at 169 (attaching a tracking device to defendants vehicle without his consent and monitoring his activities “day [and] night, over a period of several days, is a significant limitation on freedom from scrutiny”). However, “Unlike the monitoring of the defendant as a member of the general public in Campbell, the monitoring of the transmitter at issue [in Meredith, 337 Or at 306] occurred in a factually unique context and employment relationship. Unlike the defendant in Campbell, defendant here was using a truck that her employer had provided to perform her work duties. Defendant had no right to privacy with respect to that truck’s location, and the transmitter never disclosed anything other than that location. Put differently, defendant did not have a protected privacy interest in keeping her location and work-related activities concealed from the type of observation by her employer that the transmitter revealed.” Id.

H.  Invasions into Animals

 

Where the state has lawfully seized a dog or other animal on probable cause to believe the animal has been neglected or otherwise abused, a medically appropriate procedure for diagnosis and treatment (blood draw and testing) does not constitute a search under Article I, section 9. State v. Newcomb, 359 Or 756 (2016).

 

I.    Records Held by Third Parties

 

Voluntary disclosure of information to a third party, such as a bank, hospital, or cell phone carrier has traditionally been viewed to remove the information from the protections of by Article I, section 9. State v. Delp, 218 Or App 17, 178 P3d 259 (2008) rev den, 345 Or 317 (2008) (customer of internet service provider (ISP) did not have a protected privacy interest, for purposes of Oregon Constitution’s prohibition of unreasonable searches, in the customer’s non-content subscriber information held by ISP). Similarly in State v. Gonzalez, 120 Or App 249, 852 P2d 851 (1993), the court held that the defendant did not have a privacy interest in his medical records that “were owned, made, kept and guarded by the hospital.” Id. at 256. That rule has led to a categorical approach that any time a person reveals otherwise private information to a third-party business, including hospitals, the person permanently extinguishes his privacy interest in that information. See, e.g., State v. Ghim, 267 Or App 435, 441, 340 P3d 753 (2014) (explaining that “an individual has no protected privacy interest in business records held by a third-party service provider—whether a phone carrier, an internet provider, or a hospital”); see also Johnson, 340 Or 319 (holding that the defendant had no cognizable privacy interest in records kept by his cellular telephone provider of numbers dialed form his account, because the records were the telephone company’s business records and the defendant had voluntarily disclosed the numbers dialed to the company by dialing them).

 

However, the Oregon Supreme Court granted review in Ghim, affirmed the Court of Appeals on different grounds, and left “for another day the question whether and in what circumstances a defendant will have a protected privacy interest in information that a third party maintains.” Ghim, 360 Or at 444.

 

Thus, the Supreme Court has signaled its willingness to revisit the third-party records doctrine, considering that people frequently reveal private information to third parties and do not expect that such information is somehow transformed into something public or something that the government can demand without a warrant. Indeed, people often provide confidential information to their “accountants, lawyers, stock brokers, psychotherapists, ministers, [and] bankers” and would not expect that using those services—which are largely premised on confidentiality—means that the information is no longer private and can be subject to government scrutiny without probable cause that the person had committed a crime and without a warrant or exception to the warrant requirement.” Gonzalez, 120 Or App at 262 (Warren, J., dissenting).

 

Under the Court of Appeal’s rule, the legislature could simply enlist each of those private parties (accountants, lawyers, etc.) to act on behalf of the government and turn over private information to law enforcement without implicating privacy interests under Article I, section 9. That rule is far too broad and subverts the protections of Oregon’s constitution, as would significantly impair the peoples’ right to be free from governmental scrutiny.

 

Advocates should ask the court to adopt a multifactor approach to determine when a defendant has relinquished his privacy interest in information provided to third parties. Those factors should include, at a minimum, the nature and scope of the information at issue and the nature of the relationship between the person asserting the privacy interest and the third party.

II. What Qualifies as a Fourth Amendment Search?

The Fourth Amendment provides: “The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.” US Const, Amend IV. A search is considered a governmental intrusion onto private property for the purpose of obtaining information, which is based on the common law rule of trespass, or a governmental invasion of a person’s legitimate expectation of privacy. See U.S. v. Jones, 132 S. Ct. 945, 950 (2012) (trespass); Oliver v. U.S., 466 US 170, 177-78 (1984) (invasion of privacy). The Supreme Court adopted the two-part test in Katz v. United States to determine whether a person has an expectation of privacy that is legitimate. 389 US 347 (Harlan, J., concurring)). The test was announced by Justice Harlan as follows:

 

“[F]irst that a person have exhibited an actual (subjective) expectation of privacy and, second, that the expectation be one that society is prepared to recognize as ‘reasonable.’ Thus a man’s home is, for most purposes, a place where he expects privacy, but objects, activities, or statements that he exposes to the ‘plain view’ of outsiders are not ‘protected’ because no intention to keep them to himself has been exhibited.”

 

 Id. at 361. Therefore, “[o]fficial conduct that does not ‘compromise any legitimate interest in privacy’ is not a search subject to the Fourth Amendment” and “any interest in possessing contraband cannot be deemed ‘legitimate,’ and thus, governmental conduct that only reveals the possession of contraband ‘compromises no legitimate privacy interest.’ This is because the expectation ‘that certain facts will not come to the attention of the authorities’ is not the same as an interest in ‘privacy that society is prepared to consider reasonable.’” Illinois v. Caballes, 543 US 405, 408-09, 125 S Ct 834, 837-38, 160 L Ed 2d 842 (2005) (citing Jacobsen, 466 US at 122-23). Furthermore, the concept of an interest in privacy that society is prepared to recognize as reasonable is different from the individual’s subjective expectation, and as such the government may utilize information voluntarily disclosed to a governmental informant in an individual’s home, despite the criminal’s reasonable expectation that his associates would not disclose confidential information to the authorities. White, 401 US at 751-52 (plurality opinion). On the other hand, “[w]here ‘eavesdropping was accomplished by means of an unauthorized physical penetration into the premises occupied’ by the defendant, although falling short of a ‘technical trespass under the local property law,’ the Fourth Amendment was violated and any evidence of what was seen and heard, as well as tangible objects seized, was considered the inadmissible fruit of an unlawful invasion.” Id. at 748 (citing Alderman v. United States, 394 US 165, 177-78, 89 S Ct 961, 22 L Ed 2d 176 (1969)). In addition, unlike in Oregon where “the burden is on the state to prove that the warrantless search did not violate a protected interest of the defendant,” the proponent of a motion to suppress has the burden of establishing that his own standing with respect to the item or property searched or seized. Compare Tucker, 330 Or at 88-89 (emphasis in original) with Simmons v. United States, 390 US 377, 389–394, 88 S Ct 967, 973-974, 19 L Ed 2d 1247 (1968).

 

A.  Physical Invasions of Private Space

 

“[W]arrants are generally required to search a person’s home or his person unless ‘the exigencies of the situation’ make the needs of law enforcement so compelling that the warrantless search is objectively reasonable under the Fourth Amendment.” Mincey v. Arizona, 437 US 385, 393-394, 98 S Ct 2408, 57 L Ed 2d 290 (1978). “[F]or most of our history the Fourth Amendment was understood to embody a particular concern for government trespass upon the areas (“persons, houses, papers, and effects”) it enumerates [and] Katz did not repudiate that understanding.” Jones, 556 US 400 Therefore, the first step is to determine whether there has been an invasion into a protected area, and if not to determine whether a legitimate expectation of privacy has been violated.

 

1.   Private Dwellings

 

It is a “basic principle of Fourth Amendment law that searches and seizures inside a home without a warrant are presumptively unreasonable.” Groh v. Ramirez, 540 US 551, 559, 124 S Ct 1284, 157 L Ed 2d 1068 (2004). “[T]he Fourth Amendment has drawn a firm line at the entrance to the house. Absent exigent circumstances, that threshold may not reasonably be crossed without a warrant.” Payton v. New York, 445 US 573, 100 S Ct 1371, 1373, 63 L Ed 2d 639 (1980); “With few exceptions, the question whether a warrantless search of a home is reasonable and hence constitutional must be answered no.” Kyllo v. United States, 533 US 27, 31, 121 S Ct 2038, 2042, 150 L Ed 2d 94 (2001) (“At the very core stands the right of a man to retreat into his own home and there be free from unreasonable governmental intrusion * * * [and] [t]his Court has never held that a federal officer may without warrant and without consent physically entrench into a man’s office or home, there secretly observe or listen, and relate at the man’s subsequent criminal trial what was seen or heard.” The action of police officers in attaching electronic device, a so-called “spike mike,” to heating duct of house used by the defendant, and, in effect, turning duct into a gigantic microphone running throughout entire house, violated the Fourth Amendment). Silverman v. United States, 365 US 505, 511-12, 81 S Ct 679, 683, 5 L Ed 2d 734 (1961). However, “The government may utilize information voluntarily disclosed to a governmental informant in an individual’s home, despite the criminal’s reasonable expectation that his associates would not disclose confidential information to the authorities.” United States v. White, 401 U S 745, 751-52, 91 S Ct 1122, 1125-1126, 28 L Ed 2d 453 (1971) (plurality opinion). In addition, Article 1, section 9, and the Fourth Amendment provide protections against searches and seizures in the home for a resident. However, unlike the Oregon Constitution, which provides protections against searches and seizures for short-term guests of a home or even for a person who has property stored in another person’s home, the Fourth Amendment only provides protection for overnight guests. Compare City of Eugene v. Silva, 198 Or App 101, 106-107, 108 P3d 23 (2005) (invited guest); State v. Tanner, 304 Or 312, 321, 745 P2d 757, 762 (1987) (home was repository of defendant’s collateral on a loan); with Minnesota v. Carter, 525 US 83, 90, 119 S Ct 469, 473, 142 L Ed 2d 373 (1998) (“[A]n overnight guest in a home may claim the protection of the Fourth Amendment, but one who is merely present with the consent of the householder may not.”).

 

2.   Curtilage of Dwellings

 

“[C]ourts * * * have defined the curtilage, as did the common law, by reference to the factors that determine whether an individual reasonably may expect that an area immediately adjacent to the home will remain private.” Oliver v. United States, 466 US 170, 180, 104 S Ct 1735, 1742, 80 L Ed 2d 214 (1984) (citations omitted). “[C]urtilage questions should be resolved with particular reference to four factors: the proximity of the area claimed to be curtilage to the home, whether the area is included within an enclosure surrounding the home, the nature of the uses to which the area is put, and the steps taken by the resident to protect the area from observation by people passing by.” United States v. Dunn, 480 US 294, 301, 107 S Ct 1134, 1139, 94 L Ed 2d 326 (1987). “Because the curtilage is part of the home, searches and seizures in the curtilage without a warrant are also presumptively unreasonable.” United States v. Perea-Rey, 680 F3d 1179, 1184 (9th Cir 2012). However, “[t]hat the area is within the curtilage does not itself bar all police observation. The Fourth Amendment protection of the home has never been extended to require law enforcement officers to shield their eyes when passing by a home on public thoroughfares.” California v. Ciraolo, 476 US 207, 213, 106 S Ct 1809, 1812, 90 L Ed 2d 210 (1986). A person also impliedly consents to visitors going to the front door of their house under the Fourth Amendment. McKee v. Gratz, 260 US 127, 136, 43 S Ct 16, 67 L Ed. 167 (1922) (Holmes, J.) (“A license may be implied from the habits of the country.”).

 

“When law enforcement officers who are not armed with a warrant knock on a door, they do no more than any private citizen might do. And whether the person who knocks on the door and requests the opportunity to speak is a police officer or a private citizen, the occupant has no obligation to open the door or to speak * * * And even if an occupant chooses to open the door and speak with the officers, the occupant need not allow the officers to enter the premises and may refuse to answer any questions at any time.”

 

Kentucky v. King, 131 S Ct 1849, 1862, 179 L Ed 2d 865 (2011). Thus, the implied license to enter is limited, and any activity which would not be customarily accepted will constitute a search. Jardines, 569 US at 6-8 (this implicit license typically permits the visitor to approach the home by the front path, knock promptly, wait briefly to be received, and then (absent invitation to linger longer) leave). Furthermore, officers can approach any part of a building where uninvited visitors could be expected to contact the residents.

 

“The constitutionality of such entries into the curtilage hinges on whether the officer’s actions are consistent with an attempt to initiate consensual contact with the occupants of the home. Officers conducting a knock and talk also need not approach only a specific door if there are multiple doors accessible to the public. [T]he law does not require an officer to determine which door most closely approximates the Platonic form of main entrance and then, after successfully completing this metaphysical inquiry, approach only that door. An officer [initiating] a knock and talk visit may approach any part of the building...where uninvited visitors could be expected.”

 

Lingo v. City of Salem, 2014 WL 1347468 (D Or Apr. 4, 2014) (not reported) (citing United States v. Perea–Rey, 680 F3d 1179, 1187 (9th Cir 2012)). [1] Thus, under the knock and talk exception to the Fourth Amendment officers can enter the curtilage of a residence to contact the resident, but their implied license to enter is limited.

 

3.   Open Fields

 

“[T]he special protection accorded by the Fourth Amendment to the people in their ‘persons, houses, papers, and effects,’ is not extended to the open fields. The distinction between the latter and the house is as old as the common law.” Hester v. United States, 265 US 57, 59, 44 S Ct 445, 446, 68 L Ed 898 (1924). Unlike the Oregon constitution in which “the privacy protected by Article I, section 9, is not the privacy that one reasonably expects but the privacy to which one has a right,” the Fourth Amendment protects “expectation[s] that society is prepared to recognize as ‘reasonable.’” Campbell, 306 Or at 164 (citing Tanner, 304 Or at 321 n 7); Katz, 389 US 347. Therefore, an intention to exclude the public by erecting barriers to entry, such as fences, or by posting signs is irrelevant under a Fourth Amendment analysis.

 

“[W]e reject the suggestion that steps taken to protect privacy establish that expectations of privacy in an open field are legitimate. It is true, of course, that petitioner * * * in order to conceal their criminal activities, planted the marihuana upon secluded land and erected fences and “No Trespassing” signs around the property. And it may be that because of such precautions, few members of the public stumbled upon the marihuana crops seized by the police. Neither of these suppositions demonstrates, however, that the expectation of privacy was legitimate in the sense required by the Fourth Amendment.”

 

Oliver v. United States, 466 US 170, 182, 104 S Ct 1735, 1743, 80 L Ed 2d 214 (1984). The government has the burden of proof to show that the search was not within the curtilage. United States v. Johnson, 256 F3d 895, 901 (9th Cir 2001).

 

4.   Vehicles

 

The Supreme Court has also restricted physical intrusions into vehicles. “It is beyond dispute that a vehicle is an ‘effect’ as that term is used in the Amendment.” Jones, 565 US at 405 (attaching GPS monitor to undercarriage of vehicle is a search because “[t]he Government physically occupied private property for the purpose of obtaining information.”) Thus, most physical intrusions into the protected area of the vehicle will be a search. On the other hand, “[a] person travelling in an automobile on public thoroughfares has no reasonable expectation of privacy in his movements from one place to another.” United States v. Knotts, 460 US 276, 281, 103 S Ct 1081, 1085, 75 L Ed 2d 55 (1983) (monitoring the location of a radio transmitter, which had been surreptitiously attached to an object and then placed in the car by a defendant, was a search under the Fourth Amendment if the transmitter was in a “private place” such as a home, but not a search if the transmitter was in a “public place,” including an automobile. An individual has a reasonable expectation of privacy in the movement and location of an object only within private places that are not open to public view). (Not followed on state law grounds by Campbell, 306 Or at 164 . Thus, under the Fourth Amendement, absent a physical invasion of a vehicle by a government actor, a person has a diminished expectation of privacy in their vehicle. “One has a lesser expectation of privacy in a motor vehicle because its function is transportation and it seldom serves as one’s residence or as the repository of personal effects. A car has little capacity for escaping public scrutiny. It travels public thoroughfares where both its occupants and its contents are in plain view.” United States v. Knotts, 460 US 276, 281, 103 S Ct 1081, 1085, 75 L Ed 2d 55 (1983).

 

B.  Visual Invasions of Privacy

 

Both the Oregon and United States Constitutions have a “plain view” exception for observations made from a lawful vantage point. “[A]n essential predicate to any valid warrantless seizure of incriminating evidence [is] that the officer did not violate the Fourth Amendment in arriving at the place from which the evidence could be plainly viewed * * * , the item be in plain view; its incriminating character must also be ‘immediately apparent.’” Horton v. California, 496 US 128, 136, 110 S Ct 2301, 2308, 110 L Ed 2d 112 (1990) (citations omitted).

 

“Thus a man’s home is, for most purposes, a place where he expects privacy, but objects, activities, or statements that he exposes to the ‘plain view’ ‘of outsiders are not protected’ because no intention to keep them to himself has been exhibited. On the other hand, conversations in the open would not be protected against being overheard, for the expectation of privacy under the circumstances would be unreasonable.”

 

Katz, 389 US 347. Under the Fourth Amendment there is also an exception for containers that “announce their contents.” “[T]o fall within the [single-purpose container] exception of [Sanders] a container must so clearly announce its contents, whether by its distinctive configuration, its transparency, or otherwise, that its contents are obvious to an observer.” This evaluation is made “from the objective viewpoint of a layperson.” United States v. Gust, 405 F3d 797, 800 (9th Cir 2005) (quoting Arkansas v. Sanders, 442 US 753, 764 n. 13, 99 S Ct 2586, 61 L Ed 2d 235 (1979)).

 

Papers are specifically protected under the Fourth Amendment as they are under Article I, section 9. Thus in order to search or read a person’s private papers the police must have a search warrant or a recognized exception to the warrant requirement. “One of the historical purposes of the Fourth Amendment was to bar the indiscriminate search/reading of personal papers under the authority of general warrants.” Crouch v. United States, 454 US 952, 956, 102 S Ct 491, 70 L Ed 2d 259, (1981) (citation omitted) (a search authorized by a warrant did not extend to the reading of personal papers not in plain view or included within the terms of that warrant).

 

“The full privacy protected by the Fourth Amendment is, however, reached when we come to books, pamphlets, papers, letters, documents, and other personal effects. Unless they are contraband or instruments of the crime, they may not be reached by any warrant nor may they be lawfully seized by the police who are in ‘hot pursuit.’ By reason of the Fourth Amendment the police may not rummage around among these personal effects, no matter how formally perfect their authority may appear to be.”

 

Warden, Md. Penitentiary v. Hayden, 387 US 294, 321, 87 S Ct 1642, 1657, 18 L Ed 2d 782 (1967). Likewise, the viewing of a person’s private video tape is a search. However, as is the case under the Oregon Constitution, if a private party seizes the tapes and views them before turning them over to the police, that person’s conduct destroys any expectation of privacy the owner may have. State v. Luman, 347 Or 487, 223 P3d 1041 (2009); Walter v. United States, 447 US 649, 657, 100 S Ct 2395, 2402, 65 L Ed 2d 410 (1980) (“[T]he Government may not exceed the scope of the private search unless it has the right to make an independent search.”).

 

Unlike in Oregon, the United States Supreme Court has held that garbage left exposed outside of a home’s curtilage is not protected under the Fourth Amendment because people do not have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the things they have discarded. Compare California v. Greenwood, 486 US 35, 40, 108 S Ct 1625, 1628-29, 100 L Ed 2d 30 (1988) (“It is common knowledge that plastic garbage bags left on or at the side of a public street are readily accessible to animals, children, scavengers, snoops, and other members of the public.”) with State v. Lien/Wilverding, 364 Or 750, 778, 441 P3d 185, 200 (2019) (“Defendants had privacy interests in their garbage as their bin sat at the curb for regular collection by the sanitation company * * * Because the police directed that private actor, Republic’s manager, to segregate and then to bring their private information in their garbage to the police for exposure in a search, the police bear responsibility for invading defendants’ privacy interests in their garbage.”).

 

C.   Tactile Invasions of Privacy

 

A “[p]hysically invasive inspection is simply more intrusive than purely visual inspection.” Bond v. United States, 529 US 334, 120 S Ct 1462, 1463, 146 L Ed 2d 365 (2000). (Law enforcement officer’s physical manipulation of opaque bag that passenger carried onto cross-country bus and placed in rack directly above his seat violated Fourth Amendment prohibition against unreasonable searches of effects; although bus passenger had expectation that his bag might be handled by bus employee or other passenger, he did not have expectation that employee or other passenger would feel bag in exploratory manner.) If an officer’s action exposes what would otherwise be hidden it is a search and no longer falls under the plain view exception. Arizona v. Hicks, 480 US 321, 107 S Ct 1149, 94 L Ed 2d 347 (1987) (“Officer’s actions, in moving stereo equipment in order to locate serial numbers and determine if equipment was stolen, constituted ‘search,’ notwithstanding that officer was lawfully present within apartment where equipment was located in plain view.”).

 

Terry v. Ohio, 392 US 1, 88 S Ct 1868, 20 L Ed 2d 889 (1968), makes it clear that a stop and frisk is a search and seizure within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. The narrow scope of the Terry exception only permits a frisk for weapons based on “a reasonable belief or suspicion directed at the person to be frisked....” Ybarra v. Illinois, 444 US 85, 94, 100 S Ct 338, 343, 62 L Ed 2d 238 (1979). “However, a frisk is not valid if it is a general exploratory search motivated out of a desire ‘to prevent the disappearance or destruction of evidence of crime.’” United States v. I.E.V., 705 F3d 430, 433 (9th Cir 2012) (quoting Terry v. Ohio, 392 US 1, 30). If the protective search goes beyond what is necessary to determine if the suspect is armed, it is no longer valid under Terry and its fruits will be suppressed. Sibron v. New York, 392 US 40, 65–66, 88 S Ct 1889, 1904, 20 L Ed 2d 917 (1968); Minnesota v. Dickerson, 508 US 366, 113 S Ct 2130, 124 L Ed 2d 334 (1993) (police officer overstepped lawful bounds marked by Terry when officer determined that lump in pocket of defendant’s jacket was contraband crack cocaine only after squeezing, sliding and otherwise manipulating the contents of the defendant’s pocket, a pocket which the officer already knew contained no weapon). “However, if an officer discovers, during an otherwise lawful pat-down search of the suspect’s outer clothing, ‘an object whose contour or mass makes its identity immediately apparent’ as contraband, its seizure is justified” under the “plain touch exception.” United States v. Maqueira, 70 F3d 121 (9th Cir 1995) (quoting Dickerson, 508 US 366).

 

D. Invasions of Privacy by Smell

 

There is a “plain smell exception” under the Fourth Amendment, but the officer must be in a lawful location otherwise it constitutes a search. United States v. Curran, 498 F2d 30, 33 (9th Cir 1974) (“[B]efore the officer could rely upon his smelling marijuana as probable cause, he would have to justify his presence at the place, i.e., the door of the house, where he detected the odor, just as he would have to justify his presence at the place from which he saw the contraband in order to rely on the doctrine of plain view.”). The rule also applies to odors emanating into public spaces involving a dog sniff because it only detects contraband and therefore does not violate any reasonable expectation of privacy. The Supreme Court treats a canine sniff by a well-trained narcotics-detection dog as “sui generis” because it “discloses only the presence or absence of narcotics, a contraband item” in which an individual does not retain a reasonable expectation of privacy. United States v. Place, 462 US 696, 103 S Ct 2637, 77 L Ed 2d 110 (1983) (canine inspection of luggage in an airport is not a “search”); Illinois v. Caballes, 543 US 405, 125 S Ct 834, 160 L Ed 2d 842 (2005) (canine inspection of an automobile during a lawful traffic stop, does not violate the “reasonable expectation of privacy”). Most importantly if the police invade a protected area and learn what they learned only by physically intruding on a person’s property to gather evidence it is a search regardless of whether an individual has a “reasonable expectation of privacy.” Jones, 565 US at 407 (a person’s “Fourth Amendment rights do not rise or fall with the Katz formulation” because the Katz reasonable-expectations test “has been added to, not substituted for” the traditional property-based understanding of the Fourth Amendment, and so is unnecessary to consider when the government gains evidence by physically intruding on constitutionally protected areas). An officer exceeds his implied license to enter the curtilage of a home to contact the residents by bringing a drug dog and therefore the entry constitutes a “search.” Jardines, 569 US at 10-13 (“The government’s use of trained police dogs to investigate the home and its immediate surroundings is a ‘search’ within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment.”).

 

E.   Invasions of Privacy by Listening

 

In public a person still maintains a reasonable expectation of privacy about what he says depending on the circumstances. “What a person seeks to preserve as private, even in an area accessible to the public, may be constitutionally protected under Fourth Amendment.” Katz v. United States, 389 US 347, 88 S Ct 507, 19 L Ed 2d 576 (1967) (“A person in a telephone booth may rely upon protection of Fourth Amendment” and “is entitled to assume that words he utters into mouthpiece will not be broadcast to the world.”). On the other hand, what a person knowingly exposes to the public, even in his own home, will not be protected under the Fourth Amendment. White, 401 US at 751-52. “At the very core stands the right of a man to retreat into his own home and there be free from unreasonable governmental intrusion * * * [and] [t]his Court has never held that a federal officer may without warrant and without consent physically entrench into a man’s office or home, there secretly observe or listen, and relate at the man’s subsequent criminal trial what was seen or heard.” Kyllo v. United States, 533 US 27, 31, 121 S Ct 2038, 2042, 150 L Ed 2d 94 (2001). Compare Silverman, 365 US at 511-12 (action of police officers in attaching electronic device, a so-called “spike mike” to heating duct of house used by defendant, and, in effect, turning duct into a gigantic microphone running throughout entire house, violated the Fourth Amendment) with United States v. Wahchumwah, 710 F3d 862, 868 (9th Cir 2013) (“[A]n undercover agent’s warrantless use of a concealed audio-video device in a home into which he has been invited by a suspect does not violate the Fourth Amendment.”).

 

F.   Technology

 

When a government actor commits a trespass (literally or figuratively) onto a protected area to utilize technology, it is relatively easy to conclude it is a “search” within the meaning of the Fourth Amendment. “Where * * * the Government uses a device that is not in general public use, to explore details of the home that would previously have been unknowable without physical intrusion, the surveillance is a “search” and is presumptively unreasonable without a warrant.” Kyllo v. United States, 533 US 27, 40, 121 S Ct 2038, 2046, 150 L Ed 2d 94 (2001) (use of thermal imaging device from public road to detect heat from a suspected in-home marijuana grow). The attachment of Global Positioning System (GPS) tracking device to vehicle, and subsequent use of that device to monitor vehicle’s movements on public streets, was search within meaning of Fourth Amendment. Jones, 565 US 400. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals has interpreted Jones as reaffirming the principle that “the home and its curtilage are sacrosanct” and that they are “areas expressly protected by the Fourth Amendment.” United States v. Duenas, 691 F3d 1070, 1080 (9th Cir 2012). “Accordingly, after Jones, there is no doubt that ‘[w]arrantless trespasses by the government into the home or its curtilage are Fourth Amendment searches.’” United States v. Broadhurst, 3:11-CR-00121-MO-1, 2012 WL 5985615 (D Or Nov. 28, 2012) (not reported) (quoting United States v. Perea–Rey, 680 F3d 1179, 1185 (9th Cir 2012) (citing United States v. Jones, 565 US at 406 n 3). In United States v. Broadhurst the District Court of Oregon held that the defendant did not have a reasonable expectation of privacy in the radio signals received by a Shadow device, which allows a user to locate a particular router or computer by displaying the access point or station device’s signal strength in real time to the user. However, the court determined that the detective’s trespass onto the defendant’s lawn to verify the signal strength coming from his home constituted an unlawful search under the Fourth Amendment. 2012 WL 5985615. Conversely, “the home and curtilage are not necessarily protected from inspection that involves no physical invasion,” and as such the police can use technology to gain an unusual but otherwise lawful vantage point. Florida v. Riley, 488 US 445, 449, 109 S Ct 693, 696, 102 L Ed 2d 835 (1989) (helicopter at 400 feet); Ciraolo, 476 US 207 (police officers’ warrantless aerial observation, from altitude of 1,000 feet, of fenced-in backyard within curtilage of home, during which plants readily discernible to naked eye as marijuana were observed, did not violate homeowner’s Fourth Amendment rights). An individual also has a lesser expectation of privacy in their work-related activities.

 

In the context of a personal cell phone, a defendant retains a reasonable expectation of the privacy of digital information on the phone. Riley v. California, 134 S Ct 2473 (2014); United States v. Davis, 787 F Supp 2d 1165, 1170 (D Or 2011) (“A person has a reasonable expectation of privacy in his or her personal cell phone, including call records and text messages.”); United States v. Lopez-Cruz, 730 F3d 803, 807 (9th Cir 2013) (“Possession and use of the phones suffices to make [a defendant’s] expectation of privacy objectively reasonable.”). However, a third-party relationship with a service provider may destroy any reasonable expectation of privacy a defendant has in records kept by the third party. See Davage v. City of Eugene, 2007 WL 2007979 (D Or July 6, 2007) (despite plaintiffs’ statement that the EWEB (Eugene Water and Electric Board) records were password protected, there was no privacy interest protecting such third-party records).
