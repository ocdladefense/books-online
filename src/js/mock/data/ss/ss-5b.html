I. Overview

A motion to controvert is a challenge to the good faith, truthfulness and/or accuracy of the affiant’s representations in the affidavit in support of a search warrant. It differs from a motion to suppress—in the context of a search pursuant to a search warrant—which accepts the content affidavit, but argues that evidence should nevertheless be suppressed. A motion to controvert, by contrast, pointedly argues that something about the affidavit reflects some degree of inaccuracy or dishonesty on the part of the affiant.

An affiant who is reckless with his presentation of the facts, who knowingly leaves out information that undermines probable cause, or who fails—even after obtaining the warrant but before its execution—to return to the magistrate upon learning new facts which undermine probable cause, can and should be challenged through a motion to controvert.

II. Statutory Basis

The controlling statute in this area is ORS 133.693, which provides:

“(1) Subject to the provisions of subsection (2) of this section, in any proceeding on a motion to suppress evidence, the moving party shall be entitled to contest, by cross-examination or offering evidence, the good faith, accuracy and truthfulness of the affiant with respect to the evidence presented to establish probable cause for search and seizure.

“(2) If the evidence sought to be suppressed was seized by authority of a search warrant, the moving party shall be allowed to contest the good faith, accuracy and truthfulness of the affiant as to the evidence presented before the issuing authority only upon supplementary motion, supported by affidavit, setting forth substantial basis for questioning such good faith, accuracy and truthfulness.

“(3) In any proceeding under subsection (2) of this section, the moving party shall have the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence that the evidence presented before the issuing authority was not offered in good faith, was not accurate and was not truthful.

“(4) When the motion to suppress challenges evidence seized as the result of a warrantless search, the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence the validity of the search is on the prosecution.

“(5) The court shall determine whether, under applicable law, any inaccuracy, untruthfulness or lack of good faith requires suppression.”

III. Procedure

A. The Affidavit and Motion

To challenge the good faith, truthfulness or accuracy of an affidavit, the defendant must file a motion to controvert and an affidavit. (A motion to suppress challenging a warrant does not require an affidavit, but a motion to controvert does.)

The affidavit must present a “substantial basis for questioning such good faith, accuracy and truthfulness” of the affiant. ORS 133.693(2). In other words, the defense affidavit must assert that the affiant (usually the officer or detective) either lied, was reckless with the truth, or omitted something from his affidavit.

Without an affidavit, or with an insufficient affidavit, the judge can and will deny the request for an evidentiary hearing, although anecdotal experience would suggest a majority of prosecutors and judges are unaware of this fact. But once a defendant has set forth a substantial basis for questioning the affiant’s good faith, accuracy, and/or truthfulness, he is entitled to present testimony to controvert information in the search warrant affidavit. State v. Modrell-Lydall, 128 Or App 372, 876 P2d 315 (1994).

A motion to controvert should (1) explicitly request an evidentiary hearing, (2) explicitly state that without those portions of the affidavit that are inaccurate, untruthful or—as a result of an omission—misleading, the warrant fails to establish probable cause either that evidence exists or that it will be found in a particular location, and (3) identify the evidence to be suppressed.

B. The Hearing

At the evidentiary hearing, the burden is on the defendant to demonstrate the inaccuracies and/or omissions. The standard is by a preponderance. Whether an affidavit for search warrant is true and accurate is a question of fact rather than of law. State v. Cole, 78 Or App 450, 717 P2d 221, rev allowed, 301 Or 445, 723 P2d 325, rev dismissed, 202 Or 297, 728 P2d 529 (1986).

Like any other evidentiary hearing, the inaccuracy of facts contained within the affidavit in support of a search warrant is a question of fact to be determined by the trial court. Thus, if a defendant contends that the facts recited were inaccurate and presents evidence contradicting them, the trial court may resolve the claim against him if it finds his witnesses incredible. As explained succinctly in State v. Mellinger, supra, defendant’s evidence may conflict with the affiant’s statements, but the trial court is not required to believe it. Furthermore, the appellate court is compelled to accept the facts as found by the trial court so long as they are supported by the record. Ball v. Gladden, 250 Or 485, 443 P2d 621 (1968).

On the other hand, the discovery of untruths—as opposed to mere inaccuracies—in the affidavit permits the trial court to distrust the remaining statements. See State v. Keeney, et al, 323 Or 309, 918 P2d 419 (1996), where the trial court found the officer’s affidavit to be untruthful, notably because of statements relating to the credibility of the informants. The trial court chose to suppress all of the evidence obtained as the result of the warrant, and the Supreme Court agreed.

C. The Trial Court’s Review of the Affidavit Following a Successful Evidentiary Hearing

Assuming the evidentiary hearing is successful, and the trial judge finds material omissions or inaccuracies, the judge should review the affidavit anew to determine if it still provides probable cause. The process is always one of subtraction, rather than addition. That is, even in those situations where key facts were known but omitted, those facts are not added to the affidavit but may be considered only to the extent they weaken the force of the other evidence in the affidavit. See State v. Goecks, 265 Or App 158, 333 P3d 1227 (2014) (“‘Although the court does not ‘correct’ an affidavit by adding evidence, we have held that ‘one way of subtracting from an affidavit is to use the additional information developed on the motion to controvert in order to weaken the force of the officer’s statements in the affidavit.’ State v. Pelster/Boyer, 172 Or App 596, 599, 21 P3d 106, rev den, 332 Or 632 (2001).”)

The difference between permissibly considering the omissions versus the prohibition against adding them to the affidavit might be a semantic one, but in practical effect, the judge can consider the omitted information that comes out of the hearing on the motion to controvert, but never against the defendant. Under no circumstances can the evidentiary hearing be used to supplement the affidavit in the state’s favor.

As noted in the chapter on motions to suppress, some case law suggests that deference has historically been given to the original magistrate, in terms of reasonable inferences, even though a determination of probable cause is a legal question that has only one correct answer. (See the prior chapter for a discussion on the continuing salience of “deference” in this context.) But once an affidavit has been controverted, no deference should be given to the issuing magistrate. See State v. Culley, 198 Or App 366, 108 P3d 1179 (2005). In Culley, the trial court properly conducted its probable cause review, without the ordinary deference to the decision of the issuing magistrate, once the affidavit of probable cause had been successfully controverted. The affidavit’s description of a named citizen informant was inaccurate, since it omitted the fact that the person was under the influence of intoxicants when providing information to police and may have had a motive to lie. The trial court, in testing the affidavit for probable cause “anew,” properly required more corroboration for the informant’s information than might otherwise be needed for a named citizen informant. Because such corroboration was “wholly lacking,” the trial court properly suppressed the evidence seized pursuant to the warrant.

Furthermore, as noted above, a judge who has found untruths—as opposed to mere inaccuracies—in the original affidavit has authority to distrust (i.e., disregard) the entirety of the affidavit. See Keeney, et al, 323 Or 309. An example of a mere inaccuracy would be continuous misspelling of the defendant’s name in an affidavit. State v. Derrah, 191 Or App 511, 84 P3d 1084 (2004). In Derrah, the trial court correctly struck the references to the defendant’s name, but the Court of Appeals found that enough information remained to establish probable cause.

IV. Grounds for Controverting

A. When Credibility of the Informant Might Reflect on the Credibility of the Affiant

It is well established that a motion to controvert is not allowed to challenge the statements of an informant (either named or unnamed), or any other witness whose statements are contained in the affidavit. ORS 133.693 allows a defendant to contest only the affiant’s statements. ORS 133.693(1). See State v. Hitt, 305 Or 458, 735 P2d 415 (1988).

See also State v. Esplin, 314 Or 296, 839 P2d 211 (1992) '''' (a defendant does not have the right, under either ORS 133.693 or under the warrant clause of the Oregon Constitution, to challenge the good faith, accuracy, or truthfulness of an unnamed informant whose statements were included in the affidavit supporting a search warrant).

Nevertheless, the informant’s dishonesty, lack of credibility or lack of personal knowledge can be offered in support of a motion to controvert if there is evidence that the affiant was aware of the informant’s dishonesty or inaccuracy and nevertheless failed to disclose such information in the affidavit or failed to corroborate the informant’s information. See Goecks, 265 Or App 158 (“To reiterate—the issue, as set forth in more detail below—concerns the ‘accuracy’ of the affiant in relating in his affidavit his knowledge of the relevant circumstances, not the ‘accuracy’ of the information provided to him by the informant.”).

In fact, one of the more frequent grounds for motions to controvert involves failure of the affiant to include information relevant to the informant’s honesty or accuracy. See '''' Culley, 198 Or App 366. The affidavit’s description of a named citizen informant was inaccurate, since it omitted the fact that the person was under the influence of intoxicants when providing information to police and may have had a motive to lie. The trial court, in testing the affidavit for probable cause “anew,” properly required more corroboration for the informant’s information than might otherwise be needed for a named citizen informant. Because such corroboration was “wholly lacking,” the trial court properly suppressed the evidence seized pursuant to the warrant.

Because the affiant did not supply information about a named informant’s criminal background that he knew at the time he applied for a warrant, the affidavit is reviewed anew in the light of the omitted facts. After inserting that information, the magistrate must determine, under the totality of the circumstances, whether the informant is reliable. State v. Strance, 118 Or App 645, 848 P2d 1226 (1993).

See also State v. Mellinger, 52 Or App 21, 26–27, 627 P2d 897 (1981). Statements in an affidavit in support of a motion to controvert may contest the good faith of the affiant by demonstrating that the affiant was not forthright with regard to an informant’s character or omitted relevant facts concerning the circumstances of the informant’s contact or the events.

A defendant who alleges that a self-disclosed confidential informant would testify that she related hearsay information to the affiant but that the affiant reported it as the informant’s first-hand information has set forth a substantial basis for questioning the affiant’s good faith, accuracy and truthfulness and should have been permitted to put on testimony to controvert the affidavit. Modrell-Lydall, 128 Or App 372.

B. Where to Look for Grounds to Controvert

If the defendant can identify the informant, sending an investigator to interview him or her may reveal that the affidavit reflects more than what the informant actually said, or it might have left out additional and material information that undermines the reliability of the affiant’s assertions.

Never ignore a person who is quoted in an affidavit but will not be testifying at trial. Perhaps because the affiant knows he or she will not be called as a witness for the trial, the affiant might not have been as careful with the witness’s statements as she should have been. Thus, an investigator should interview, for example, neighbors whose statements are only relevant to the search warrant but will not likely be witnesses at trial. If such an otherwise uninvolved witness denies making claims that the affiant attributed to her, she can be a very powerful witness in a motion to controvert.

Omissions are the most fruitful grounds for challenging an affidavit. It is simply contrary to human nature to include in an affidavit every harmful fact that might undermine the probable cause the affiant is seeking to demonstrate.

One way to uncover omissions is to develop a sense for what quality police work would normally entail. If the affidavit claims that the defendant resides at a particular address, but it makes no mention of the address listed in the DMV database, it is reasonable to suspect that the DMV database will list an address different than the one to be searched. No affiant in trying to verify a person’s address would ignore DMV or (if in Portland) the Portland Police Bureau Database or the name of the power subscriber at that address. See State v. Stockton, 120 Or App 111, 852 P2d 227 (1993) (“Here, the affiants knew that one of the verbal accounts from the utility company was incorrect. They also knew that the written records indicated that Howell was the only power subscriber. We conclude that it was not objectively reasonable for the affiants to believe that defendant was a power subscriber at Larson Drive without further verification. Particularly, in view of the lack of evidence linking defendant to the Larson property, the failure to verify this information constitutes bad faith.”).

When the affidavit cites other police reports relied on by the affiant, read those carefully, as the summary in the affidavit may leave out contradictory information.

If the affidavit mentions the defendant’s arrest history, does it also note which charges resulted in acquittal or dismissal?

What exactly is the payment arrangement with the informant? Named informants who are going to testify at trial may get paid to go to grand jury and trial, in addition to any payments they may have received for controlled buys. See State v. Payne, 150 Or App 469, 946 P2d 353 (1997). Omission of the benefits the informant is expecting to receive for their information could constitute bad faith.

C. A Change in Circumstance Between the Issuance and Execution of the Warrant

Information that undermines probable cause that is obtained after the warrant is signed but before it is executed requires the affiant to return to the magistrate with a new affidavit that contains the new information.

If the police—after they have obtained a search warrant but prior to its execution—obtain information that undermines the probable cause requirement, the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution obligates them to return to the magistrate for a new finding of probable cause. United States v. Marin-Buitrago, 734 F2d 889 (2d Cir 1984).

“Certain restrictions are placed on the execution of search warrants to ensure that probable cause, as well as the veracity of the information in the affidavit, exists when the warrant is executed.” Id. at 894; see also United States v. Jacobs, 986 F2d 1231, 1235 (8th Cir 1993) '''' (“Furthermore, we think the officers had a duty to provide the magistrate judge with any information which would undercut the warrant’s validity. The officers could not simply rest on a warrant they had already received when this information came to light.”).

In United States v. Bowling, 900 F2d 926 (6th Cir. 1990), the court observed that a line of cases interpreting Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 41(e), which requires the execution of a warrant within ten days, “evidences a strong concern that probable cause may dissipate between the time of a warrant’s issuance and its execution.” The court in Bowling went on to state that the law is clear that probable cause must exist at both points in time, i.e., when the warrant is issued and when it is executed. And, when new facts come to light after the warrant’s issuance, the officers must reappraise the issuing magistrate, and their failure to do so is relevant to a determination of whether they relied on the warrant in good faith.

See Washington v. Reichenbach, 153 Wash 2d 126, 101 P3d 80 (2004) '''' (where officer obtained search warrant on information that a person would be driving to Vancouver to buy drugs, but after the warrant was issued the person called the officer to say that the defendant had been unable to obtain the drugs, the officer’s failure to so inform the issuing magistrate rendered the warrant invalid at the time of its execution).

While there are a variety of circumstances where an affiant might learn more information before executing the search warrant, one of the most common involves the execution of the search warrant on a multi-resident dwelling. The affiant may believe, before arriving, that there are many people living at one residence, only discovering upon arrival that there are multiple separate units/residences at one location. The difference likely impacts whether a warrant sufficient for one unit provides probable cause for another. Before the police can search that second unit, they need to get a new search warrant.

Lastly, although affiants frequently rely on the same boilerplate for each affidavit as it relates to their training and experience, anecdotal experience suggests that some affiants have one affidavit for a drug dealer’s home (when the training and experience focuses on how often evidence is found at a suspect’s home) and another affidavit for a drug dealer’s stash house (when the training and experience focuses on how often drug dealers will keep drugs, money and firearms at any location other than their home). Obtain as many affidavits sworn to by that affiant as possible, and determine if he or she intentionally omitted certain parts of their training and experience because it contradicts the assertions applicable in the instant case.
