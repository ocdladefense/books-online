I. Searches for Officer Safety

A. Searches of a Person or Personal Property

“A police officer may take ‘reasonable steps’ to protect the officer or others if, ‘during the course of a lawful encounter with a citizen, the officer develops a reasonable suspicion, based upon specific and articulable facts, that the citizen might pose an immediate threat of serious physical injury to the officer or to others then present.’ State v. Bates, 304 Or 519, 524, 747 P2d 991 (1987) * * * [.] Such ‘reasonable steps’ may include '' alimited search of a citizen’s belongings.'' And, the permissible range of such a limited search turns on the nature of the threat in light of the specific and articulable facts known.”

[http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=State+v.+A.J.C.,+355+Or+552,+&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=14606489438088885617&scilh=0 State v. A.J.C., 355 Or 552, 559-60, 326 P3d 1195 (2014)] (emphasis added).

With respect to the search of a defendant’s person, the officer-safety exception generally permits a pat-down of the outer clothing; any search more thorough than that normally will require either an exceptional threat or probable cause (permitting a search incident to arrest). State v. Rudder, 347 Or 14, 24, 217 P3d 1064 (2009).

In Rudder, the officer was responding to a 4:00 a.m. burglar alarm, the defendant was walking away from the source of the alarm, wearing dark clothes, sweating profusely, and growing increasingly nervous during the course of the encounter; defendant’s pockets were visibly bulging, defendant furtively and selectively removed items when asked about the contents of his pockets, and the officer believed that defendant was hiding something and was concerned that it might have been a weapon. Id. at 24.

Initially, the officer tried to conduct a pat-down of defendant’s exterior clothing for weapons. However, when defendant twice turned away, he handcuffed defendant, opened his pocket, and visually inspected the pocket’s contents. Id.

The Supreme Court explained that the initial pat-down of the outer clothing was the type of limited search that it generally has approved when officer-safety concerns arise in the course of a lawful police stop. Id. “Although a patdown is an intrusion (indeed, it is a search), it normally is sufficient to identify those objects that are relevant to officer-safety concerns while leaving other information and objects relatively private.” Id.

By contrast, “opening and inspecting the contents of a suspect’s pocket—is a different matter * * * [;] intrusion into a suspect’s clothing requires something more—either probable '' cause or some greater justification than was present here.”Id.at 24-25.See also'' State v. Davenport, 272 Or App 725 (2015) (holding that removal of object officer thought was a bullet from the defendant’s pocket was not authorized by officer-safety exception without reasonable suspicion, based on specific and articulable facts, that defendant posed a serious threat of harm and that removal of the object from his pocket would lessen or eliminate that threat).

2024 Update

“[U]nder the officer-safety doctrine, the state bears a two-part burden of proof and persuasion to justify a warrantless search.” State v. Whitlock, 334 Or App 107, 109 (2024) (internal quotation marks and citation omitted). First, “the state must prove that an officer had subjective reasonable suspicion that the person searched posed an immediate threat of serious physical injury.” Id. (internal quotation marks and citation omitted). Second, “the state must prove that, under the totality of the circumstances, (1) the officer’s subjective safety concerns of an immediate threat of serious physical injury were objectively reasonable, and (2) the officer’s response to the safety concerns was, itself, objectively reasonable.” Id. (internal quotation marks and citation omitted). In Whitlock, the officer’s safety concerns were not objectively reasonable where the officer arrived at the scene of a car accident resulting from reckless or intoxicated driving, saw the defendant, who had left the scene and then returned, involved in a “heated verbal exchange” with some other people. Id. at 111. The defendant was “extremely emotionally labile,” appearing distraught about the circumstances surrounding the car accident and discord in his marriage. Id. at 112. He made “drastic” hand movements while talking to the officer, he had a bulge in his pocket, and he twice either put his hand in or touched that pocket. Id. at 112, 114-115.

The Court of Appeals emphasized the importance of viewing the situation holistically and after observing the body cam footage, concluded that although defendant was intoxicated and emotional, he was not angry or aggressive. Id. 110-12. His hand movements in context of the conversation were not threatening. Id. at 112.  There was no testimony about the size of the pocket bulge or any reason to think it was a weapon as opposed to a wallet, keys, or a phone. Id. 114-115. Further, the fact that the defendant had left the scene and then returned carried “almost no weight in the analysis,” because “the officer-safety doctrine does not exist to prevent people from fleeing or frustrating investigations.” Id. at 113. Finally, the Whitlock court emphasized that the officer testified that he “almost always” pats people down for weapons during DUII cases, thereby “undercutting a conclusion that he made a case-specific determination regarding the patdown in [the] defendant’s case.” Id. at 115.

In State v. Harmon, 329 Or App 639 (2023), the state failed to satisfy either part of the officer-safety test. The court held that the discovery of a gun wedged between the front seats of a stopped vehicle did not justify officer-safety search of the passenger’s backpack, which had been left in the car, after the vehicle’s occupants had been removed, because (1) the police did not identify any subjective threat associated with the backpack and (2) “A warrantless search to protect an officer’s safety ‘will be justified only when the area searched is still within the defendant’s control, so that the defendant would be able to obtain a weapon stashed in the area[.]’” Id. at 643 (quoting State v. Krause, 281 Or App 143, 146, 383 P3d 307 (2016)).

B. Searches of a Residence, i.e., “Protective Sweep”

Under Article I, section 9, there is no “protective sweep” doctrine that is distinct from the officer-safety doctrine. Thus, only if police can satisfy the Bates standard for an officer-safety search may police who are lawfully in a residence conduct a protective sweep. State v. Guggenmos, 350 Or 243, 253 P3d 1042 (2011) (holding that the trial court erred by denying motion to suppress because officer safety did not justify the protective sweep of defendant’s bedroom). Tips from unnamed informants who were not shown to be credible or reliable that drug sales had occurred in the house, that the occupants of house harbored “wanted people” in the house, and that a brown pickup parked outside house might contain drugs were insufficient to support a reasonable suspicion that any of the occupants of the house posed an imminent threat of serious physical injury to the officers, as required to justify a protective sweep of house. Id. at 256-57.

State v. Cocke, 334 Or 1, 45 P3d 109 (2002), considered the lawfulness, under Article I, section 9, of a protective sweep carried out by police following an arrest of a probationer in a large house that contained several private apartments. Id. at 3. ' One officer had heard about a month earlier that some occupants of the house had several firearms. Id. at 4.' Additionally, a month earlier neighbors complained that someone in the house had pointed a laser sight at them. Id. The court held, “The circumstances of this case do not approach the kind of immediacy that this court stated in Bates would be necessary to make a ‘protective’ search reasonable * * * We cannot justify a constitutional intrusion that is far more significant than the one in Bates on facts that are far less compelling.” Id. at 9.

II. Searches for School Safety — Student Searches

School officials can search a student on reasonable suspicion of an imminent threat to the safety of others at school.

When school officials perceive an immediate threat to student or staff safety at a school, they may take prompt and reasonable steps to remove that threat, including searching a student’s backpack for evidence of marijuana dealing. [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=State+ex+rel+Juv+Dept+of+Clackams+County+v.+M.A.D.,+348+Or+381&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=261293723463630385&scilh=0 State ex rel Juv Dept of Clackams County v. M.A.D., 348 Or 381, 392-93, 233 P3d 437 (2010)].

“As with an officer-safety search, when a school official develops a ‘reasonable suspicion,’ based on ‘specific and articulable facts,’ that a particular individual on school property either personally poses or is in the possession of some item that poses an ‘immediate threat’ to the safety of the student, the official, or others at the school [such as the presence of marijuana], the school official ‘must be allowed considerable latitude to take safety precautions.’”

Applying that test to the facts of M.A.D., one student told a vice principal that another student (youth) had attempted to sell him marijuana that morning near school grounds. Youth’s school records suggested a history of “drug issues,” and when the vice principal called youth’s mother, she thought that youth “probably was holding something.”

The record in this case demonstrates that the school official was aware of specific, articulable facts that would lead a reasonable person to suspect that youth was then in possession of illegal drugs. Moreover, he “reasonably could have concluded that youth’s possession and alleged attempt to sell those drugs earlier that morning created an immediate risk of harm to youth and to other students at the school.”

Those facts justified the school official’s act of reaching into youth’s jacket pocket and removing the bag that contained drugs and drug paraphernalia.

The Supreme Court applied the “school-safety exception to the warrant requirement” that it created in M.A.D. to a situation where school officials had a reasonable suspicion that a youth had brought a gun to school with the intent to shoot other students. [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=3240497469919498597&q=State+v.+A.J.C.,+355+Or+552,&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 State v. A.J.C., 355 Or 552, 564-65, 326 P3d 1195 (2014).]

In A.J.C., school officials seized a backpack from the youth, and although there was no immediate risk that the youth could access anything in the backpack, they searched it and found the gun. In those circumstances, the court held:

“A limited search of the parts of youth’s backpack that could contain the gun was therefore reasonable. At that point, verifying that a gun was in the backpack * * * would tend to negate the possibility that a firearm was located elsewhere on campus. Conversely, verifying that a weapon was not in the backpack would demonstrate that further investigation of other areas may be appropriate.”

Id. at 565.

In State v. Powell, 288 Or App 660, 406 P3d 1111 (2017) the court concluded that the school-safety exception does not permit an officer to conduct a warrantless search of a person who happens to be near a school during an incident that threatens the occupants of the school and who provokes only generalized suspicion in the officer.

III. Searches for Courthouse Safety

State v. Snow, 247 Or App 497, 504, 268 P3d 802 (2011), involved an administrative courthouse search policy that permitted security guards to search of an individual’s person and carried items. It required that any person entering the courthouse submit to a search of their person, but it failed to adequately limit the discretion of the security guard, and thus, was unreasonable under the state constitution.
