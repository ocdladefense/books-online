I. Introduction

Prior to the 1980s, administrative searches under the Oregon Constitution followed the Fourth Amendment. But with the Oregon Supreme Court’s decision in State v. Atkinson, 298 Or 1, 688 P2d 832 (1984), the analysis behind the Oregon administrative search exception has diverged from the Fourth Amendment exception. What follows is a description of the current state of the administrative search exception in Oregon, then a section detailing federal analysis.

Under both Article I, section 9, and the Fourth Amendment, administrative searches are those searches done to enforce laws by means other than criminal sanction. See, e.g., Weber v. Oakridge Sch. Dist. 76, 184 Or App 415, 434, 56 P3d 504, (2003), rev den, 335 Or 422 (2003) (stating that an “administrative search is one conducted for a purpose other than the enforcement of laws by means of criminal sanctions”); City of Los Angeles, Calif. v. Patel, 576 US 409, 420, 135 S Ct 2443, 192 L Ed 2d 435 (2015) (“the primary purpose of the [administrative] search[] is distinguishable from the general interest in crime control”).

II. Administrative Searches under Article I, Section 9

Article I, section 9, prevents unreasonable searches and seizures. Warrantless searches are per se unreasonable. Consequently, the state must prove that the administrative search was either conducted pursuant to a warrant or that the search fulfilled the requirements of the administrative search exception to the warrant requirement. That is, the state must show that the administrative search was tailored in scope and duration to fit the animating purpose behind the exception. State v. Arreola-Botello, 365 Or 695, 709, 451 P3d 939 (2019).

A. Warrantless Administrative Searches

To fall under the administrative search exception, the search must meet five requirements: 1) the search is not done for the purpose of criminal law enforcement, 2) “the search must be conducted pursuant to a policy that is authorized by a politically accountable lawmaking body,” 3) “the policy must limit the discretion of those conducting the search,” 4) the scope of the authorized search is “reasonable in relation to its purpose,” and 5) “the person performing the search must act within the bounds of the policy.” State v. Snow, 247 Or App 497, 504, 268 P3d 802 (2011).

1. The search or seizure is not conducted for criminal law enforcement purposes.

The search cannot be done with the intent of finding evidence of a crime. State v. Boyanovsky, 304 Or 101, 134, 743 P2d 711 (1987) (field sobriety checkpoints were not administrative and violated Article I, section 9, because they were designed to yield evidence of a crime without reasonable suspicion). That an administrative search might yield evidence of a crime does not make the search a criminal one. Weber v. Oakridge School Dist. 76, 184 Or App at 434. But if the authorizing statute explicitly requires the government agent to look for evidence of a violation of the law, the purpose is a criminal one. State v. Saunders, 103 Or App 488, 491, 799 P2d 159 (1990).

If the legislature calls an offense a crime then it is one. Brown v. Multnomah County Dist. Court, 280 Or 95, 102, 570 P2d 52 (1977). But just because the sanction for an administrative search is not labeled criminal does not mean that it avoids being one. Id. Several factors helps determine whether a sanction is criminal or civil:

• How has the offense been punished historically?

• Does the sanction lead to the punitive use of incarceration in contrast to civil detention like civil commitment or a detoxification hold? Fines may convert an administrative purpose into a criminal one. At minimum, fines are “strong evidence of the punitive significance” of the sanction. Id. at 105.

• What are the collateral consequences? Inconvenience is not sufficient to convert a regulatory deprivation into a criminal one. For instance, suspending or revoking a person’s license after their conviction for DUII is not punitive, but rather a regulatory decision that a “traffic offender should not continue to drive.” Id. at 105.

• Does the sanction carry social stigma? Brown, 280 Or at 106.

• Do arrest and detention occur prior to adjudication? Id.

Two cases provide examples of when an administrative search becomes a criminal one. For example, in Weber, students were subjected to random urinalysis testing if they wanted to participate in school sports. Id. at 417. But the policy set out noncriminal reasons for the search: to deter the use of alcohol and controlled substances, to “encourage participation in treatment programs, and to avoid injuries to student-athletes.” Id. at 434. Students who tested positive were not penalized academically or criminally, the results were not documented in their school records, and they were not intended to be disclosed to “criminal or juvenile authorities.” Id. at 434. Moreover, the policy explicitly stated that school authorities would not “initiate contact with law enforcement authorities concerning the results of the drug tests.” Id. As such, even though the search might have yielded evidence of a crime, because the purposes behind the policy were noncriminal, the testing was valid.

By contrast, unlike Weber, a search of a juvenile at school is likely a criminal search if conducted in the presence of a police officer, with the explicit purpose of recovering evidence of an illegal act, a violation of the law, or illegal items. State v. BAH, 245 Or App 203, 207, 263 P3d 1046 (2011) (so stating in the context of a search of youth at school).

But the presence of law enforcement alone does not necessarily make a search a criminal one, such as a search done primarily for safety purposes before placing a defendant in a secure waiting room in a police station. State v. Coleman, 196 Or App 125, 130, 100 P3d 1085 (2004), rev den, 338 Or 16 (2005) (upholding a policy that permitted officers to remove only those items from people being placed in a secure waiting room that presented officer safety concerns; the policy did not permit too much discretion because it applied to every person being placed in the secure waiting room).

The language of the authorizing statute can also point to whether a purportedly administrative search is done with a criminal purpose. A statute that authorizes routine regulatory inspections of premises may pass constitutional muster, but a statute that authorizes agents to search for evidence of a violation will convert the administrative search into a criminal one. In State v. Saunders, the defendant sold roe. 103 Or App 488, 491, 799 P2d 159 (1990). A fish and game officer believed that the defendant had been illegally harvesting roe and without a warrant opened a package of roe that was about to be airfreighted. Id. at 491. The state argued that the search was authorized under ORS 506.550[[#_ftn1|[1]]] as an administrative search to ensure protection of an important natural resource. Id.at 492. But because the statute explicitly authorized a search when an officer had probable cause to believe there had been a violation of the commercial fishing laws, the intent of the statute was to find evidence of a crime and thus the warrant requirement was not excused. Id. at 494.

Officers cannot justify a tow of a car if the record does not demonstrate that the statutory requirements were fulfilled and if one of the motives was to seize the car so that officers could then get a warrant to investigate the car to locate evidence of a crime. State v. Lambert, 263 Or App 683, 697, 328 P3d 824, modified on recons, 265 Or App 683 (2014). But if the officer’s suspicion played no part in his assessment to impound the car and the car is later inventories pursuant to a valid policy, the officer’s suspicion will not invalidate the impound. State v. Penney, 252 Or App 677, 685, 288 P3d 989 (2012).

2. The search is conducted pursuant to a policy that is authorized by a politically accountable lawmaking body.

“Proper authorization” may derive from a number of politically accountable bodies:

“Potential sources of proper authorization by politically accountable lawmakers would include, for example, a statute enacted by the Oregon Legislature or by the electorate through the initiative or referendum process, a regulation based on a statute, a city or county charter provision, a city or county ordinance, or a policy or procedure promulgated pursuant to authority.”

Weber, 184 Or App at 435 (internal quotation marks omitted).

The authorization need not be explicit, but can be implied. [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=15616378611269497368&q=Smith+v.+Washington+Cty.,+180+Or+App+505&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 Smith v. Washington Cty., 180 Or App 505, 516-17, 43 P3d 1171, 1178 (2002)], rev den, 334 Or 491 (2002). For example, a school board’s alcohol and drug policy is promulgated by a politically accountable body because state statutes authorize the creation of schools, those boards are accountable to voters, and additional statutes require those boards to adopt a “comprehensive alcohol and drug policy.” Weber, 184 Or App at 436. Similarly, a presiding judge is implicitly authorized to promulgate rules to ensure the safety of a courthouse as part of her administrative duties. See Smith, 180 Or App at 523 (finding that order promulgated by presiding judge was properly authorized pursuant to her administrative authority to provide for the safety of the courthouse). A county’s decision to establish jails or detox centers includes an implied grant of authority to control and inventory the personal property of people being held in those types of facilities. State v. Layman, 147 Or App 225, 935 P2d 1216 (1997); State v. Ketelson, 163 Or App 70, 986 P2d 1202 (1999).

But just because statutory authorization exists for a government agent to collect particular information does not mean that the agent is also authorized to search for that information. The statute must also be supplemented by a policy that implements the statute in a way that limits an officer’s discretion. In State v. Haney, 195 Or App 273, 97 P3d 1211 (2004), an officer entered the defendant’s car in order to find insurance and ownership information after the defendant was brought to a hospital following a single-vehicle traffic collision. Id. at 275. During the search, the officer located several prescription medication bottles. Id. The state argued that the search was justified under ORS 810.460, which requires officers at accident scenes to submit accident reports. Id. But the state adduced no evidence that the relevant police department promulgated a policy pursuant to that statute, and the statute itself provided no limitations on an officer’s discretion. As a result, the mere presence of an authorizing statute was not sufficient to establish a properly authorized policy or limitations on the officer’s discretion. Id.; see also State v. Lecarros, 187 Or App 105, 110, 66 P3d 543 (2003) (stating that although ORS 830.035(1) permits officers to stop boats to enforce the boating laws, because the state did not show that the statute was supplemented by a local policy that restricted an officer’s discretion to stop boats, seizing the defendant’s boat could not be justified under the administrative seizure exception).

For example, a sheriff’s deputy may enter premises to levy the property of a debtor against a judgment. But the sheriff’s entry is regulated by statute. For instance, if the debtor refuses entry to the sheriff, then the writ of execution, which is typically written out by a court clerk, does not justify the forcible entry onto the premises. See ORS 18.887(1) and ORS 206.030. Rather, forcible entry can only be made through a court order. Thus, if the officer forcibly enters premises where the debtor has a privacy interest, and the entry was made without a court order but only on the writ of execution, then the entry is not permitted under ORS 18.887(1), and thus is not justified by the administrative search exception. See State v. Mast, 250 Or App 605, 620 (2012) (holding that administrative search exception did not apply when deputies, with only a writ of execution and without consent, entered the private office of the defendant-debtor to levy his personal property against a judgment and opened the defendant’s safe, discovering mushrooms).

Similarly, citing Saunders, the Court of Appeals concluded that a DHS caseworker lacked statutory authority to make a warrantless entry into a residence to investigate child abuse because the authorizing statute, ORS 418B.200 (2005), permitted DHS to launch an investigation but did not specifically authorize entry into private property. State v. Weaver, 214 Or App 633, 168 P3d 273 (2007), rev den, 343 Or 692 (2007).

Furthermore, even when a statute authorizes an administrative search, it does not necessarily permit the seizure of discovered items. In State v. Anderson, a deputy fire marshal seized records that he believed were evidence that defendant was illegally selling fireworks. 101 Or App 594, 792 P2d 451 (1990). Because the pertinent OAR allowed the fire marshal to inspect the records but not seize them, and there existed no other exigency, the seizure of the records was not justifiable under the administrative seizure exception.

3. The policy must limit the discretion of those conducting the search.

Even if the state is able to show that the government agent was authorized to conduct an administrative search it must still adduce evidence that the policy describing the manner in which the search is conducted limits the agent’s discretion. Haney, 195 Or App 273.

First, the simplest way of attacking the discretion prong is to argue that a policy impermissibly allows officers to make discretionary choices when performing an administrative search. For examples of this approach, see the chapter on inventories. The second avenue of attack is to challenge the Court of Appeals jurisprudence on what constitutes permissible exercises of discretion. For instance, in State v. Cleland, the court upheld the inventory of a small container designed to hold small electronics under a policy that required the opening of any containers designed or likely to contain valuables. The court reasoned that an officer could reasonably believe that such a container was likely to contain valuables. State v. Cleland, 289 Or App 379, 410 P3d 385, rev den, 362 Or 699 (2017).

In a concurring opinion to Cleland, Judge James walked through the Court of Appeals’ inventory decisions and how it has departed from the requirements of Atkinson. See also State v. Barnett, 299 Or App 656, 633, 499 P3d 914 (2019), rev den, 366 Or 257 (2020) (James, J., concurring). Judge James’s concurrence provides a model for how to challenge the current state of the Court of Appeals’ inventory case law. Such challenges might not win the day in front of the trial court, but they are necessary for potential review in the Oregon Supreme Court.

The third way of attacking an administrative search under the discretion prong is by arguing that even though the policy might be valid, the officer who carried it out exceeded the policy and by doing so, departed from the purposes of the particular administrative search, thus rendering it unreasonable under Article I, section 9.

In certain instances, the policy might contain an invalid provision that does not apply to your case. In those cases, at least one Court of Appeals judge has suggested that the invalid portion should be severed and the remaining portions given effect. State v. Steele, 290 Or App 675, 685-93, 414 P3d 458 (2018) (DeVore, J., concurring). Judge Devore carefully distinguished two cases: State v. Nordloh, 208 Or App 309, 144 P3d 1013 (2006), and State v. Cherry, 262 Or App 612, 325 P3d 813 (2014), which the defendant had relied upon to support a non-severability doctrine. As of this writing, no other opinion has addressed the severability issues, but counsel may want to argue that because the policy has to be reasonable in all of its parts to be justified under the administrative search exception, the invalid portion invalidates the whole.

A search of a cigarette package located on defendant as he was going through courthouse security was not justified under the administrative search doctrine. Snow, 247 Or App at 505-06. The courthouse security policy authorized the screening of the general public for contraband and weapons prior to entry into the courthouse by using a metal detector. Id. at 501. The policy also permitted the examination of “brief cases, purses, packages, boxes, etc.” with x-ray equipment. Id. Here, the defendant set off the metal detector. Id. at 499. He removed a cigarette package and then went through the metal detector again. Id. at 500. Before he could reclaim the cigarette package, a security officer opened the package and discovered methamphetamine. Id. Because the policy granted “wide latitude to an officer to decide both who was to be searched and the scope of the search”—it did not specify who must submit to a search beyond the metal detector or how intrusive a search could be—the policy conferred too much discretion to the officers, violating Article I, section 9. Id. at 506.

4. The scope of the authorized search is reasonable in relation to its purpose.

The scope of the search must be reasonable to its intended fruits. State v. Weist, 302 Or 370, 377-78, 730 P2d 26 (1986). The Court of Appeals has long interpreted reasonableness in a manner that could be construed as being favorable to the state. See Weber, 184 Or App at 493 (“To be reasonable the search need not be the least restrictive means to accomplish an end.”). The “proper analysis involves first identifying the purpose or purposes of the policy and then determining whether the search is reasonable in relation to that purpose or purposes.” Id. at 437.

In a concurrence in Weber, Judge Schuman disagreed with the majority’s reasonableness analysis:

“Reasonableness, in the majority’s view, enters the analysis only in determining whether the search that the policy authorizes arguably promotes the accomplishment of the policy's objective and is limited in scope so as not to authorize a search that is unrelated to that objective; there is no requirement that the overall policy or the program itself be reasonable. 184 Or App at 437, 56 P3d at 517–518. I disagree. I maintain that a lawful administrative search must not only meet the three requirements developed in Atkinson and Nelson; it must also pass an overall reasonableness test implicit in those opinions.”

Id. at 444. Recently, the Oregon Supreme Court has indicated that it might be more aligned with Judge Schuman than with the Weber majority. First, in Arreola-Botello, 365 Or 695, the court concluded that extending the subject matter of a traffic stop without reasonable suspicion to suspect another crime or violation was not reasonable in light of the purposes of the traffic stop exception to Article I, section 9. Then, in State v. Fulmer, 366 Or 224, 233, 460 P3d 568 (2020), the court concluded that the inventory of a purse left in a car that was to be towed, before asking the driver if she wanted to take the purse, violated the purposes behind the inventory exception. That is, if an inventory is designed to safeguard valuables and property, there is no need for an inventory if the owner is present and can claim their property.

Accordingly, whether an administrative search is reasonable may no longer be restricted to whether the officer followed the policy but whether the policy itself is reasonable. Thus, there are several potential ways to challenge that the search was not reasonable in relation to the purpose behind the administrative search exception. First, a defendant could argue that the policy is inconsistent with the principles in Atkinson. Second, a defendant could argue that the policy is unreasonable in light of the legislative purposes that authorize the policy. And third, a defendant could argue that the search conflicts with “civilized conception[s] of privacy and liberty” and is thus unreasonable. Weber, 184 Or App at 445 (Schuman J., concurring).

5. The person performing the search must act within the bounds of the policy.

Only recently has the Court of Appeals began to address this point. See State v. Krumenaker, 306 Or App 9, 18, 472 P3d 760 (2020) (finding that the inventory was unconstitutional when officers were deciding to inventory based on the circumstances instead of the nondiscretionary terms of the inventory). Officers must scrupulously follow the relevant policy. If not, defendant can argue that the resulting inventory was not an inventory at all, but a search that does not fall under the administrative search exception.

B. Other Examples of Administrative Searches

1. Paternity Testing

DNA testing to establish paternity is a reasonable administrative search under Article I, section 9: 1) the purpose is noncriminal—to establish paternity and thus financial responsibility for a child; 2) the search follows a policy that was promulgated by a politically accountable body—the search is permitted by ORS 416.430; 3) the statute eliminates discretion because it requires that every person who denies paternity and seeks a hearing in circuit court provide DHS with a DNA sample. State ex rel. Dep’t of Justice & Div. of Child Support v. Spring, 201 Or App 367, 373, 130 P3d 1 (2005), rev den, 340 Or 483 (2006).

Spring is a potential example of how the court might have reasoned differently under Judge Schuman’s analysis in Weber. In Spring, '''' the search is viewed as reasonable in light of the statutory policy. Under Judge Schuman’s view, the question would be whether the entire statutory framework is reasonable.

2. Roadblocks

In State v. Tourtillot, the court upheld the constitutionality of a roadblock that stopped every car on a rural road. 389 Or 845, 858-59, 618 P2d 423 (1980). The purpose behind the roadblock was to ensure compliance with the state’s hunting, not criminal, laws. The roadblock was in operation during the first week of hunting season, and the officer had no discretion concerning which cars he stopped. But the same administrative search would likely not be reasonable in relation to its purpose had it taken place on I-5 because it would capture far more nonhunters. In Tourtillot, the state’s actions seem to follow reasonable time, place, and manner restrictions.

C. Administrative Warrants and Subpoenas

“[A]n administrative subpoena issued as part of a civil investigation will comply with Article I, section 9, as long as the subpoena is ‘relevant to a lawful investigatory purpose and no broader than the needs of the particular investigation.’” State v. Ghim, 360 Or 425, 439, 381 P3d 789 (2016).

Administrative subpoenas are statutory creations. Be sure to check the statute that authorizes the subpoena. For instance, who has the power to serve the subpoena, what are the requirements for its contents, and the nature of its scope, and who has the power to reissue the subpoena?

In State v. Ghim, the Oregon Supreme Court did not reach the question of whether subpoenas that required a bank to turn over financial records were a violation of Article I, section 9. 267 Or App at 438 n 3. Specifically, defendant did not argue that either the statutory exclusion provision in ORS 192.606(5) or Article I, section 9, prohibited the state from servicing a second set of subpoenas on a bank once the Department of Consumer Business Services (DCBS) had failed to serve the first set of subpoenas in compliance with ORS 192.596. Furthermore, the defendant did not argue that the second set of subpoenas violated Article I, section 9, because the prosecutor issued them under ORS 136.583 rather than DCBS reissuing them under ORS 59.315(1).

Notably, the court assumed without deciding that a person has a protected privacy interest in a record of their financial transactions kept by their bank. 360 Or 425, 438, 381 P3d 789 (2016). The court did not have to reach the issue because it concluded that even if such a privacy interest did exist, the state properly obtained the records through an administrative subpoena. Id. at 44. Thus, whether a person actually has a cognizable privacy interest in their financial records under Article I, section 9, is still an open question.

III. Administrative Searches under the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution

Like Article I, section 9, the touchstone for the Fourth Amendment is reasonableness. “In certain limited circumstances, the Government’s needs to discover such latent or hidden conditions or to prevent their development is sufficiently compelling to justify the intrusion on privacy entailed by conducting such searches without any measure of individualized suspicion.” Bd. of Educ. of Indept. Scho. Dist. No. 92 of Pottawatomie Cty. v. Earls, 536 US 822, 829, 122 S Ct 2259, 153 L Ed 2d 735 (2002) (internal quotation marks omitted). “Therefore, in the context of safety and administrative regulations, a search unsupported by probable cause may be reasonable when special needs, beyond the normal need for law enforcement, make the warrant and probable-cause requirement impracticable.” Id. (internal quotation marks omitted).

A. Camara v. Municipal Court and the Modified Probable Cause Standard for Administrative Warrants

In Camara v. Municipal Court, 387 US 523, 87 S Ct 1727, 18 L Ed 2d 930 (1967), the Court, for the first time, extended the Fourth Amendment’s coverage to administrative searches. Camara held that housing inspectors must obtain a search warrant if the occupant refuses consent. The warrant, however, need not be based on the traditional probable cause standard. As noted in a later decision,

“Probable cause in the criminal law sense is not required. For purposes of an administrative search such as this, probable cause justifying the issuance of a warrant may be based not only on specific evidence of an existing violation but also on a showing that ‘reasonable legislative or administrative standards for conducting an * * * inspection are satisfied with respect to a particular [establishment].”

[https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=11785638731606285522&q=Marshall+v.+Barlow%E2%80%99s,+Inc.,+436+US+307&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38 Marshall v. Barlow’s, Inc., 436 US 307, 320-21, 98 S Ct 1816, 56 L Ed 2d 305 (1978)] (invalidating loosely defined provisions of OSHA permitting warrantless workplace “safety” inspections).

In See v. City of Seattle, 387 US 541, 87 S Ct 1737, 18 L Ed 2d 943(1967), the Supreme Court applied Camara’s holding to commercial premises. Thus, probable cause to support an administrative warrant for a house inspection would not necessarily need to articulate a specific violation, but could be based on the length of time between inspections and that there is a need for periodic inspections to ensure the safety of the building. Camara, 387 US at 538.

Prior to the development of Oregon’s administrative search exception, Oregon courts applied the federal standard: “[T]he amount of evidence which amounts to probable cause will depend upon the nature of intrusion and its potential consequences. Where * * * the inspection will be without potential criminal consequences, issuance of a warrant will be justified without any showing of a history of wrongdoing so long as there are reasonable legislative or administrative standards.” Accident Prevention Div. v. Hogan, 37 Or App 251, 256-57, 586 P2d 1132 (1978). Such a standard ensures “that a decision to search private property is justified by a reasonable governmental interest.” Camera, 387 US at 539.

The reduced probable cause standard comes, in part, from the reduced privacy interest that a person has in a business where the premises to be inspected are subject to the plain view of other employees. State ex rel. Acc. Prevention Div, of Workmen’s Comp. Bd. v. Foster, 31 Or App 291, 296, 570 P2d 398 (1977).

An administrative warrant can also be based on specific knowledge that the premises contain a regulatory violation. In such a situation, the affiant need not demonstrate a serious violation, rather the “examples of wrongdoing will be disclosed by an inspection can be relatively slight and still justify issuance of a warrant.” In Accident Prevention Division v. Hogan, 37 Or App 251, 586 P2d 1132 (1978). In Hogan, probable cause existed for a trial court to issue an inspection warrant for a business when the business had a history of regulatory violations, employee injuries, a reluctance to cure the regulatory violations, and an unwillingness to accept voluntary inspections. Hogan, 37 Or App at 258.

But the authorizing statute cannot be so vague as to provide no guidance as to when and how inspections are to occur. For instance, a statute that allows an inspector to conduct an inspection at “reasonable times * * * within reasonable limits and in a reasonable manner” provides no guidance and does not meet the reasonable administrative standards test, and even if it did, such guidance is too “arbitrary” to pass muster under the Fourth Amendment. Foster, 31 Or App at 291.

Nor can the administrative search be a cover for a criminal search. By concealing the agency’s contact with the police or the genuine purpose of the search, the search is in bad faith and the exclusionary rule applies. '' United States v. Leon, 468 US 897, 923 (1984),citingFranks v. Delaware, 438 US 154 (1978);see alsoUnited States v. Boyce, 601 F. Supp. 947 (D. Minn. 1985) (“Once a reviewing court finds a search warrant affiant to be dishonest or reckless, suppression is appropriate underLeon...”).''

Lastly, because part of the justification of the relaxed Camara probable cause standard is that the administrative search is randomized, when a search is responsive to a specific event, then a higher standard might apply. For instance, in response to putting out a fire, exigency justifies the entry by firefighters. Michigan v. Tyler, 436 US 499, 511, 98 S Ct 1942, 56 L Ed 2d 486 (1978). Afterwards, additional entries to investigate the cause of the fire can be accomplished through a Camara administrative warrant, but if firefighters develop probable cause that arson occurred, they must go through the “traditional showing of probable cause” to search for evidence of a crime. Id.

B. Warrantless Searches of Closely Regulated Industries

Although the Fourth Amendment’s prohibitions against unreasonable searches applies to administrative inspections of private commercial property, what is considered unreasonable is different for commercial property than private residences. Donovan v. Dewey, 452 US 594, 598, 101 S Ct 2534, 69 L Ed 2d 262 (1981). That is, “a warrant may not be constitutionally required when Congress has reasonably determined that warrantless searches are necessary to further a regulatory scheme and that federal regulatory presence is sufficiently comprehensive and defined that the owner of commercial property cannot help but be aware that his property will be subject to periodic inspections undertaken for specific purposes.” Id. at 600.

Four factors assist in determining whether an industry is so regulated that an administrative search can be conducted without a warrant: 1) “the public interest to be protected in both cases is great,” 2) the industry is pervasively regulated by statute, 3) involves transient and disposable goods, rendering advanced notice of an inspection ineffective as a deterrent, and 4) because of the pervasive nature of the regulation, the person who operates a business in the industry has a lower expectation of privacy because they know the business will be subject to pervasive regulatory inspection.” [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=3655439439777707212&q=State+v.+Westside+Fish+Co.,+31+Or+App+299&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 State v. Westside Fish Co., 31 Or App 299, 302-03, 570 P2d 401 (1977)] (analyzing the factors in United States v. Biswell, 406 US 311, 92 S Ct 1593, 32 L Ed 2d 87 (1972)).

But when a regulatory regime “is so random, infrequent, or unpredictable that the owner, for all practical purposes, has no real expectation that his property will from time to time be inspected by government officials,” then the administrative search may be constitutionally objectionable. Id. at 599.

For instance, mines are an example of an industry that meets the pervasively regulated test: 1) the state has a strong interest in regulating a hazardous industry that is vital to commerce, 2) mines are pervasively regulated given their dangerous nature, 3) forewarning of an inspection would not serve the enforcement purpose of the regulatory regime because companies could hide violations, and 4) residual privacy is protected because the relevant act did not permit forcible entry—instead the inspector had to try to enjoin the company if barred from entry. Dewey, 452 US 594.

Other examples of closely regulated industries are liquor sales, Colonnade Catering Corp. v. United States, 397 US 72, 90 S Ct 774, 25 L.Ed.2d 60 (1970); firearms dealing, Biswell, 406 US at 311–312; and running an automobile junkyard, New York v. Burger, 482 US 691, 107 S Ct 2636, 96 L.Ed.2d 601 (1987).

Besides the searches of premises in heavily regulated industries, the Court has also upheld federally mandated drug testing for individual employees in such industries. See Skinner v. Railway Labor Executives’ Ass’n, 489 US 602, 109 S Ct 1402, 103 L Ed 2d 639 (1989) (upholding federally required breath testing and urine testing in private railway company because it is a heavily regulated industry and the government has a compelling government interest in the safe operation of trains); see also Nat'l Treasury Employees Union v. Von Raab, 489 US 656, 679, 109 S Ct 1384, 1398, 103 L Ed 2d 685 (1989) (same, but for customs agents).

The Court has extended the reduced expectation of privacy from the industry context to random drug testing in school athletics. “Somewhat like adults who choose to participate in a ‘closely regulated industry,’ students who voluntarily participate in school athletics have reason to expect intrusion upon normal rights and privileges, including privacy.” Vernonia Sch. Dist. 47J v. Acton, 515 US 646, 657, 115 S Ct 2386, 132 L ED 2d 564 (1996). But it has modified the test to determine if school drug testing is constitutional. First, the Court considers “the nature of the privacy interest allegedly compromised by the drug testing.” Bd. of Educ. of Indep. Sch. Dist. No. 92 of Pottawatomie Cty. v. Earls, 536 US 822, 829, 122 S Ct 2559, 153 L3d 2d 735 (2002). Second, the Court looks at the character of the intrusion imposed by the policy. Id. at 832. Third, the Court considers “the nature and immediacy of the government’s concerns and the efficacy of the policy in meeting them.” Id. at 835.

In Earls, the court concluded that students who participate in extracurricular activities have a limited privacy interest both as students and as students who participate in extracurricular activities that are subject to even more regulation. Id. at 832. The intrusion was also considered minimal. Id. at 833-34. A staff member waits outside of a closed bathroom stall for the urine sample, which is then placed into a mailing pouch. Id. at 832-33. Moreover, the results are kept confidential and separate from the student’s academic records and only released to staff who “need to know,” such as during off-campus trips. Id. at 833. The results are not turned over to law enforcement and have no academic consequences besides potentially being prevented from participating in extracurricular activities. Id. at 834. Lastly, the need to deter childhood drug use is sufficiently serious and immediate to justify the drug policy. Id. at 836.

The court has also concluded that because public employees have a reduced expectation of privacy in their workplace, an employer can search their offices, drawers, file cabinets so long as it is reasonable under all the circumstances. See O’Connor v. Ortega, 480 US 709, 725-26, 107 S Ct 1492, 94 L Ed 2d 714 (1987). (1987) (concluded that a public sector employer did not need a warrant to search the file cabinets, offices, desk drawers of a public employee so long as the search was for a “noninvestigatory, work-related purpose,” “work-related misconduct,” and was reasonable under all of the circumstances).

The Court has also upheld mandatory home visits by caseworkers for recipients of welfare benefits. In Wyman v. James, 400 US 309, 91 S Ct 381, 27 L Ed 408 (1971), the Court concluded that the Fourth Amendment did not prohibit visits by caseworkers because they could not force entry into the home and the only penalty that applied was the suspension of benefits, which was distinguishable from Camara because no criminal consequences flowed from the occupant’s refusal to allow the search. 400 US at 325.

Reasonable legislative or administrative guidelines must exist at the time the warrant is executed. State Accident Prevention Division of Worker’s Compensation Board v. Foster, 31 Or App 291, 570 P2d 398 (1977). The criteria may include “the passage of time, the nature of the building (e.g., a multifamily apartment house) or the condition of the entire area.” Camara, 387 US at 538.

The practitioner should first determine if the administrative warrant and resulting search complies with the requirements of the pertinent statute or ordinance. Certain inspection laws are more protective of privacy than the loose constitutional strictures of Camara. For example, Portland’s city housing code (§ 29.60.040) requires that the housing inspector first seek entry by consent before resorting to more intrusive methods. Failure to comply with such provisions provides an independent ground for suppression.

C. Administrative Subpoenas

Administrative agencies have broad authority to issue administrative subpoenas and summons as part of their regulatory function. The information obtained through these tools is increasingly being used in criminal prosecutions, particularly in the area of white-collar crime. Administrative Subpoenas Seek to Have Recipients Bring in Smoking Gun. 5 BNA Criminal Practice Manual: Current Reports No. 24 (Nov. 27, 1991) at 557, citing Pickholz, Horn and Simon, Administrative Investigators, BNA Corporate Practice Series: Guide to White Collar Crime at 67 (1987).

An administrative subpoena should be “sufficiently limited in scope, relevant in purpose, and specific in directive so that compliance will not be unreasonably burdensome. See v. City of Seattle, 387 US 541, 87 S Ct 1237, 18 L Ed 2d 943 (1967). The subpoena process must afford “an opportunity to obtain precompliance review before a neutral decisionmaker.” City of Los Angeles, Calif. v. Patel, 576 US 409, 420, 135 S Ct 2443, 192 L Ed 2d 435 (2015). The issuance of an administrative subpoena likely would meet this threshold because it provides the party being subpoenaed the opportunity to quash the subpoena and have their objections heard before a neutral magistrate. Id. at 422; see also [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=7224724735948036310&q=Donovan+v.+Lone+Street,+Inc.,+464+US+408&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 Donovan v. Lone Street, Inc., 464 US 408, 415, 104 S Ct 768, 78 L Ed 2d 567 (1984)] (“[A]lthough our cases make it clear that the Secretary of Labor may issue an administrative subpoena without a warrant, they nonetheless provide protection for a subpoenaed employer by allowing him to question the reasonableness of the subpoena, before suffering any penalties for refusing to comply with it, by raising objections to an action in district court.”).

In Patel, a Los Angeles ordinance required hotels to produce their guest registries for inspection upon request of a police officer or potentially be subject to up to six months jail or a fine of $1,000. Id. at 413. Such an ordinance was unconstitutional for three reasons. Fist, although the ordinance might have served a legitimate administrative interest—ensure recordkeeping compliance and thus deter criminals from using hotels to conduct illicit business—it did not provide any mechanism for precompliance review, such as permit time for the issuance of or contesting an administrative subpoena. Id. at 421.

Second, the hotel industry was not a “closely regulated industry,” subjecting it to higher scrutiny. Id. at 425-26. True, regulations applied to hoteliers, but those were general, widely applicable regulations more akin to minimum wage regulations. Id. at 425. If such regulation was enough to make an industry “closely regulated,” than all industry would fall under the scope of the exception. Id. Moreover, the historical practice of permitting public inspection of guest registries was not sufficient to create a regulated industry.

Third, even if hotels were closely regulated, the regulation still failed two other prongs of the closely regulated industry test: it was not necessary for the regulatory scheme and the inspection program did not have the requisite certainty and regularity. Id. at 426. Inspecting officers could guard registries to make sure they were not tampered with during precompliance review. Furthermore, the ordinance gave too much discretion to officers to determine when to inspect a hotel, and it provided no guidance under what circumstances the hotels were to be searched. Id. at 427.

D. The Investigation and Motions

1.   The investigator should speak to the employees in the administrative agency. Find out who initiated the inspection and why. Determine the standard procedures for initiating and conducting the inspections. Were they followed in this case or was there a deviation from those procedures? If there was a departure from normal practice, who ordered it and why? Who was the liaison with the police and what was the nature and extent of the contact? If police officers accompanied the inspector, is that the normal practice? Did more police than usual go along for the ride?

2.   File a discovery motion pursuant to ORS 135.825 requesting all documents regarding the inspection, the internal procedures followed, and any memoranda of the agency’s contact with police. Subpoena the inspector, the agency head or the records custodian duces tecum.

3.   Scrutinize the affidavit to discern if the inspector neglected to mention such things as the agency’s contact with the police on this case. Then file a motion to controvert the affidavit. Success on this motion will strip the magistrate’s decision of the deference it is otherwise accorded. State v. Harp, 299 Or 1, 10, 697 P2d 548 (1985).

4.   Subpoena every police officer who accompanied the inspector to the suppression hearing. Call them to testify one by one and the judge may realize that four detectives from the Drug and Vice Unit seems a bit unusual for a routine housing code inspection.

5.   In Nelson v. Lane County, 304 Or 97, 104, 743 P2d 692 (1987), the court stated: “We do not mean to suggest that if while conducting a legally authorized and properly administered administrative inspection, officers came across evidence of another crime in plain view, that such evidence could not be used in a criminal prosecution.” 304 Or at 104 n 5 (emphasis added). Accordingly, if incriminating evidence is found in plain view, argue that there was nothing inadvertent about the discovery. In fact, because of the collusion, the police and the inspector were not lawfully on the premises in the first place.

IV. Searches of Probationers

Searches of probationers are their own special species of search, which blends administrative and criminal elements. “Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution, and ORS 137.540 do not authorize probation officers to exploit their supervisory authority over probationers unlawfully.” State v. Guzman, 164 Or App 90, 103, 990 P3d 370 (1999), rev den, 331 Or 191 (2000).

A probationer retains all of his civil liberties except those taken away as a condition of probation. Guzman, 164 Or App at 96. ORS 137.540(1) lists probation conditions that are automatically imposed on a probationer “unless specifically deleted by the court.” Where the legislature has chosen to set out specific probation conditions, those conditions are “statutory restrictions to which a court must conform[.]” State v. Schwab, 95 Or App 593, 597, 771 P2d 277 (1989). Because a probation officer’s authority flows from a court’s ability to impose a sentence, a probation officer cannot exercise authority that the sentencing court never had. See Rivera-Waddle, 279 Or App at 279 (stating that a probation office’s authority is derivative of a court’s ability to impose sentence). As such, neither a trial court nor a probation officer can impose further burdens on a defendant’s conduct, if such conduct is already provided for in the general conditions of probation.

As relevant here, two such conditions are that a probationer shall:

“(h) Permit the parole and probation officer to visit the probationer or the probationer’s work site or residence and to conduct a walk-through of the common areas and of the rooms in the residence occupied by or under the control of the probationer.

“(i) Consent to the search of person, vehicle or premises upon the request of a representative of the supervising officer if the supervising officer has reasonable ground to believe that evidence of a violation will be found * * * ”

ORS 137.540(1).

ORS 137.540(1)(h), the walk-through condition, does not permit probation officers to enter private areas of a residence. Guzman, 164 Or App at 96-97 (“Although a home visit reasonably could extend to the common areas of a probationer’s residence where visitors are customarily allowed, the authority to conduct a home visit does not carry with it the authority to inspect private areas of the residence.”).

By its terms, ORS 137.540(1)(i) only permits a representative to request consent to search when “the supervising officer has reasonable grounds to believe that evidence of a violation will be found.” Reasonable grounds “means a quantum of information that is greater than that which would justify a ‘stop,’ but less than that required for ‘probable cause.’” State v. Gulley, 324 Or 57, 65, 921 P2d 396 (1996); cf. Griffin v. Wisconsin, 483 US 868, 107 S Ct 3164, 97 L Ed 2d 709 (1987) (concluding that warrantless search of a probationer’s home by probation officers under a state law requiring reasonable grounds to suspect the presence of contraband did not violate the Fourth Amendment).

ORS 137.545(2)[[#_ftn2|[2]]] and ORS 144.350[[#_ftn3|[3]]] authorize arrests when a probation officer has reasonable grounds to believe that a probationer violated the conditions of probation. “[T]he authority to arrest a probationer for violation of a probation condition implie[s] the authority to stop persons reasonably suspected of violating that probation condition.” State v. Hiner, 240 Or App 175, 180, 246 P3d 35 (2010). But that authority does not permit the arresting officer to search the probationer and the surrounding premises. See State v. Meier, 145 Or App 179, 186, 929 P2d 1052 (1996) (cannot search probationer incident to arrest when arrest is justified on reasonable grounds standard); cf. State v. Brown, 110 Or App 604, 611, 825 P2d 282 (1992) (consent condition did not permit search of vehicle that lacked nexus to premises containing evidence of probation violation). Consequently, knowledge of the typical general conditions of probation may provide reasonable suspicion to stop a probationer and investigate the suspected violation, but absent authorization under ORS 137.540(1)(i) or some other exception to the warrant requirement, a police officer? cannot search the probationer.

For instance, in State v. Hiner, a deputy had reasonable suspicion to stop the defendant based in part on the mistaken belief that the defendant had a condition of probation that prohibited the consumption of alcohol. 240 Or App at 177. The deputy detected the odor of alcohol on the defendant’s breath, the defendant lied about being on probation, and after being confronted with the lie the defendant told the deputy, mistakenly, that a condition of his probation prohibited alcohol consumption. Id. at 178-79. The deputy contacted the defendant’s supervising officer who authorized the deputy to request consent to search and to detain the defendant if he refused. Id. The resulting consent search located contraband. Id.

Despite the mistaken belief that a probation condition applied when it did not, this court held that:

“The facts that the deputy perceived were that defendant was on probation, was prohibited from consuming alcohol (later confirmed by defendant), and had consumed alcohol. Those perceived facts gave rise to a reasonable (though ultimately mistaken) belief that defendant had violated his probation. Defendant’s guarded movements and his false denial that he was on probation likewise contributed to the deputy’s belief that defendant had violated his probation.”

Id. at 181. Accordingly, the deputy had reasonable suspicion to stop the defendant and contact the defendant’s probation officer to seek authorization to request consent to search the defendant for evidence of a probation violation. Id.

The Hiner court relied on State v. Ingman for the proposition that an officer’s mistaken belief, so long as it is reasonable under the circumstances, may furnish a valid basis to initiate a stop. 127 Or App 27, 870 P2d 361, rev den, 319 Or 81 (1994). In Ingman, an officer mistakenly believed that the defendant unlawfully possessed a firearm after a dispatcher told the officer, mistakenly, that the defendant was a felon. Id. at 31. Because there was nothing in the record to cause the officer to doubt the dispatcher’s information, the officer’s belief that the defendant was committing a crime was reasonable. Id.

Thus, in both Hiner '' andIngman'', an important part of determining whether the officers’ actions were reasonable was that both sought to verify what legal restrictions applied to the defendants by checking with sources that the officers could reasonably assume would provide correct information—a probation officer about her probationer and a dispatcher about a person’s criminal record.

If verifying the applicable conditions of probation is important for developing reasonable suspicion, then it is critical in developing reasonable grounds for a violation. A probation officer’s authority is “derivative” and “cannot be greater than that of the court which gave the probationer into his charge.” Stephens, 47 Or App 305, 312, 614 P2d 1180 (1980). Because a sentencing court retains the authority to “delete” general conditions of probation, the circumstances must give the probation officer reasonable grounds to believe that he is going to find evidence that a person violated a condition of probation specific to the probationer. Cf. State v. Maciel-Figueroa, 361 Or 163, 165, 389 P3d 1121 (2017) (stating that even during a stop an officer must still be able to “point to specific and articulable facts that give rise to a reasonable inference that the defendant committed or was about to commit a specific crime or type of crime”).

“In determining whether a probationer who was subject to a condition of probation that required him to submit to a search voluntarily consented to a search, [this court] consider[s] whether the probationer was effectively denied a reasonable opportunity to refuse the search or whether the environment was sufficiently coercive to preclude him from doing so.”

State v. Brock, 254 Or App 273, 277, 295 P3d 89 (2012). Such coercion can occur when a probation officer trades on his supervisory authority to gain consent for which he is otherwise not entitled to ask. Guzman, 164 Or App at 100.

In Guzman, the defendant’s probation officer told him that she wanted to do a home visit to ensure that the defendant was complying with the conditions of his probation. Id. at 93. The defendant agreed, stating, “Gee, I was wondering when you guys were going to come and visit me at my house.” Id. (internal quotation marks omitted). The interaction between the defendant and the probation officer was “‘[e]xtremely friendly.” Id. at 94. The defendant told the probation officer that the home visit would be “[n]o problem” and told her “[s]everal times” that it was okay for her to go into the house and look around. Id. During the search the probation officer found contraband. Id.

At a subsequent suppression hearing “[t]he trial court ruled that [the] defendant knew that if he did not consent to [his probation officer]’s activities, he risked probable arrest, but that [the] defendant voluntarily consented to the search of his residence.” Id. at 95.

After finding that ORS 137.540 did not justify the search of the defendant’s home, this court concluded that the defendant did not voluntarily consent because the probation officer had unlawfully traded on her supervisory authority to gain consent for a search for which she had no authority to request. Id. at 102-03. This court analogized the situation to one where

“a law enforcement officer stops an individual on the street, asks for permission to search and also indicates that he or she can obtain a warrant when the officer has no actual grounds to obtain a warrant. The representation is that the search will occur whether the consent is given or not. Under those circumstances, the officer is using the force of the law illegally to overcome the individual’s will and to coerce consent to search.”

Id. at 102. As such, the defendant’s consent was not voluntary. Id.

By contrast, in Brock, the defendant voluntarily consented to a search of his residence after admitting to his probation officer that he stored marijuana there. 254 Or App ' at 275. The probation officer thus had lawful grounds to request consent to search. Id. at 278. The possibility of detention if the defendant did not give the probation officer consent to search did not invalidate that consent. Id. at 279-81. The encounter was otherwise calm, the defendant had submitted to walk-throughs in the past, and he was familiar with the conditions of his probation. Id. at' 280 '' That is, his probation officer did not threaten to do anything that she was not legally empowered to do.Id.;see also'' State v. Davis, 133 Or App 467, 475-76, 891 P2d 1373 (1995) (finding that the defendant nonverbally consented to a search when he led his probation officer to marijuana plants during a home visit, knew that such possession would give his probation officer reasonable grounds to request consent to search, and the defendant later did refuse consent to a further search).

Thus, unlike in Guzman, the probation officers in Brock and Davis did nothing more than what the law empowered them to do. Cf. State v. Barber, 279 Or App 84, 96, 379 P3d 651 (2016) (noting that consent following a threat by an officer to do what the law empowers the officer to do is not objectionable).

Practice Tip

In Hiner, it appears that defendant took too narrow a view of the unlawful conduct by focusing exclusively on the deputy’s actions.

In that case, as noted above, a deputy relayed his observations of the defendant’s intoxication to the defendant’s probation officer who told the deputy to advise the defendant that his probation conditions required him to consent to a search and that his refusal to consent to a search could result in his arrest. The deputy did so, and the defendant consented to a search, which yielded contraband that led to the convictions at issue. Hiner, 240 Or App at 178.

While it may have been reasonable for the deputy to mistakenly believe that defendant was violating a probation condition prohibiting alcohol use after both defendant and his probation officer had acknowledged as much, no argument was made that the probation officer’s error undermined the voluntariness of defendant’s consent.

That is, ORS 137.540(1)(i) only permits a representative of the supervising officer to request consent to search when “the supervising officer has reasonable grounds to believe that evidence of a violation will be found.” The supervising officer did not have reasonable grounds to believe that defendant was in violation of his probation because her authority was limited to enforcing defendant’s actual conditions of probation. Thus, the supervising officer did not have authority to direct the deputy to request consent to search. Guzman, 164 Or App at 99. Absent “reasonable grounds” to request the search, it was not accurate to inform defendant that he could be arrested for refusing to consent to the requested search. See ORS 144.350 (authorizing arrest only where the supervising officer has “reasonable grounds to believe that the person has violated a condition of probation”).

Thus, the probation officer’s statement to the defendant relayed through the officer that he was required to consent to a search and that he could be arrested for refusing was an erroneous statement, which was every bit at coercive as the probation officer’s conduct in Guzman, 164 Or App at 102. There, the court held that that the defendant did not voluntarily consent because the probation officer had unlawfully traded on her supervisory authority to gain consent for a search for which she had no authority to request. Id. at 102-03. Had the defendant made that same argument in Hiner, he should have prevailed.
