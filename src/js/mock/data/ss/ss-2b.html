I. Stop-level Seizures of Drivers (Traffic Stops)

A. Probable Cause of a Non-Criminal Traffic Violation Can Justify a Traffic Stop

To stop a motorist for a non-criminal traffic violation, an officer must have probable cause to believe that the person has committed a violation. State v. Stookey, 255 Or App 489, 491, 297 P3d 548 (2013) (citing State v. Matthews, 320 Or 398, 402, 884 P2d 1224 (1994).

2024 Update

In the absence of facts indicating otherwise, it is objectively reasonable to believe that the driver of a vehicle is the owner of the vehicle. State v. Sullivan, 322 Or App 563 (November 2, 2022) (An officer had probable cause to justify a traffic stop where a license-plate check revealed that the car in front of the officer was unregistered, because “both critical elements of ORS 803.300—that the person own the vehicle and that the vehicle be unregistered—were satisfied by an objective belief that defendant was the owner of the vehicle and had committed a traffic violation.”).

2024 Update

A strong argument can be made that when a traffic violation contains a mental state, such as “knowingly,” the officer must perceive facts that would establish that the driver had the requisite mental state.  State v. Mendell, 333 Or App 342, 347-49 (2024) (Jacquot, J. concurring).  In the Mendell concurrence, Judge Jacquot noted some tension in the caselaw.  In State v. Lipka, 314 Or App 154, 164, 498 P3d 811, rev. dismissed, 368 Or 703, (2021), the court first recited the well-settled principle that “[W]hen the facts an officer perceives do not meet the essential elements of the violation, probable cause is lacking.” Id. at 165.  However, the court added a concerning caveat:

“[T]o support probable cause, an officer need only perceive facts sufficient to establish the essential elements of a statutory violation, not necessarily facts with respect to every element, attendant circumstance, or culpable mental state required to ultimately convict a person of the crime.”

Id. (emphasis added).

Judge Jacquot criticized the caveat in Lipka, as unsupportable for three reasons: (1) there is no authority to categorize the mental-state element as anything other than an “essential element”; (2) In Lipka, the court assumed without deciding that probable cause existed, so the caveat was dicta; and (3) The case cited in support of the proposition, State v. Boatright, 222 Or App 406, 193 P3d 78 (2008), is inapposite because there the court concluded that the officer had probable cause that the defendant was displaying an illegally altered plate and that the defendant acted “knowingly.”  Mendell, 333 Or App at 348-49.

For those reasons, attorneys should preserve arguments that the probable cause requirement to stop a vehicle for a traffic violation extends to the mental-state element of the offense.

1. What does probable cause mean in the context of a traffic stop?

Probable cause of a traffic infraction exists “if, and only if, the facts as the officer perceived them actually satisfy the elements of a traffic infraction.” State v. Elmore, 241 Or App 419, 425, 250 P3d 439 (2011) (citing State v. Tiffin, 202 Or App 199, 203, 121 P3d 9 (2005)); see also State v. Baker/Jay, 232 Or App 112, 132, 221 P3d 749 (2009) (“[I]f the facts that the officer believed she observed, if true, would have constituted a traffic violation, then the officer’s belief that a traffic violation occurred was objectively reasonable. In contrast, if the facts that the officer believed she observed, if true, would not constitute a traffic violation, the officer’s belief that a traffic violation occurred was not objectively reasonable.”).

The probable cause standard is fundamentally different in the context of a traffic violation than in the criminal context. That the facts observed by the officer establish that it is more likely than not that the driver committed an infraction is insufficient—the facts observed must conclusively establish a violation. Moreover, the requirement that the officer personally perceive the facts constituting the violation suggests that it would be unlawful for an officer to stop a driver based merely on an informant’s report of a traffic violation.

2. What is a traffic violation?

“‘Traffic violation’ means a traffic offense that is designated as a traffic violation in the statute defining the offense, or any other offense defined in the Oregon Vehicle Code that is punishable by a fine but that is not punishable by a term of imprisonment.” ORS 801.557; State v. Jimenez, 263 Or App 150, 363 P3d 1222 (2014) (the state agreed that “although defendant was a pedestrian when [officer] encountered him, the encounter was a traffic stop because its purpose was to cite defendant for a violation of the traffic code”). See State v. Klein, 234 Or 523, 228 P3d 714 (2010) (treating bicyclist’s violation of a traffic rule as a traffic violation).

3. Can probable cause of a violation dissipate?

In State v. Farley, 308 Or 91, 93, 775 P2d 835 (1989), an officer stopped defendant because his vehicle had no visible license plates, an apparent violation of ORS 803.540. While approaching the defendant’s vehicle, however, the officer noticed a valid temporary vehicle permit posted on the windshield, allowing the vehicle to be operated without license plates. ORS 803.540(2)(a). “Upon seeing the temporary permit, the justification of any investigation was vitiated. Plain and simple, the officer had no statutory authority to proceed further.” Id. at 94.

Similarly, the authority to continue a properly initiated traffic stop dissipates upon the observation of facts that divest the officer of his subjective belief that the driver had more likely than not committed a violation. State v. Pettersen, 256 Or App 385, 389, 300 P3d 277 (2013) (although DMV records indicated that the vehicle’s registration had expired, upon observing the facially valid registration tags, the officer was required to cease his investigation and terminate the stop because he no longer had subjective probable cause).

4. Can police make pretext stops?

Oregon law does not prohibit pretext stops. State v. Carter/Dawson, 287 Or 503, 485, 600 P2d 873 (1979) (“[O]fficer’s motives for an otherwise justifiable traffic stop are * * * not relevant to the question of its validity.”).

However, under State v. Arreola-Botello, 365 Or 695, 714, 451 P3d 939 (2019), an officer who stops a driver for a traffic infraction must focus their actions on investigating the infraction, ascertaining the identity of the driver, and issuing a traffic citation. Officers cannot extend the duration or subject-matter scope of a traffic stop without independent constitutional justification. In other words, if an officer does not have reasonable suspicion of some other crime, the officer cannot use a pretext stop to conduct a criminal investigation or take other investigative actions that are unrelated to the purpose of the stop.

As a result, an officer can no longer perform a pretext stop and then conduct a criminal investigation while processing the stop. For example, an officer who has a hunch that a driver is carrying drugs can still perform a pretext stop, but the officer cannot ask to search for drugs unless the officer has reasonable suspicion of drug possession, or develops reasonable suspicion while expeditiously processing the stop. Conversely, an officer who performs a pretext stop and then immediately develops reasonable suspicion of DUII upon speaking with the driver can then extend the stop to investigate the DUII.

Practice Tip

Force the state to prove that the facts observed by the officer actually satisfy the elements of a particular traffic offense.

5. What is the extent of an officer’s authority during a traffic stop?

The extent of an officer’s authority during a traffic stop is governed by State v. Rodgers/Kirkeby, 347 Or 610, 227 P3d 695 (2010); Jimenez, 263 Or App 150; and ORS 810.410; see Chapter 2C on extending traffic stops).

B. Common Factual Observations That May Not Be Traffic Violations

1. Cracked Windshields

State v. Stookey, 255 Or App 489, 499, 297 P3d 548 (2013) (“It is not enough that defendant’s cracked windshield caused some minimally higher risk of harm by creating the possibility of interference with defendant’s vision and, thus, negligibly increasing the risk of a collision. Rather, the interference had to be such that it exposed someone to a danger of probable harm or loss.” “To violate ORS 815.020, the condition of the vehicle must pose more than a possible risk of danger. Rather, for the statute’s definition to be commensurate with its classification, that danger must be probable.”)

State v. Anderson, 259 Or App 448, 455, 314 P3d 335 (2013) (“It might have been objectively reasonable for the trooper to have believed that the [spider web] crack minimally weakened the windshield, but it would not have been objectively reasonable for him to have believed that the crack so significantly weakened the windshield that it made it likely to shatter, thus bringing the vehicle within the ambit of ORS 815.020(1)(a).).”

State v. Elmore, 241 Or App 419, 427, 250 P3d 439 (2011) (“[W]e readily conclude that a crack is not a ‘material’ [that impairs the ability to see out of a vehicle] * * * Accordingly, because the facts, as the deputy actually perceived them, did not satisfy the elements of ORS 815.220(2), he lacked objective probable cause to stop defendant.”).

2. Failure to Drive Within a Single Lane

ORS 811.370: To stop a driver for failure to drive within a single lane, there must be “at least two clearly marked lanes for traffic” and the officer must observe the driver fail to “operate the vehicle as nearly as practicable entirely within a single lane” (emphasis added).

State v. McBroom, 179 Or App 120, 125 n 3, 39 P3d 226, rev den, 334 Or 397 (2002): “[D]efendant drove for more than 300 feet on the center line for no apparent reason,” and the court noted: “This is not a case in which defendant’s car’s tires touched the center line only briefly. We accordingly need not decide whether that act, standing alone, would give an officer probable cause to believe that a driver had failed to operate his or her car ‘as nearly as practicable entirely within a single lane.’”

State v. Vanlom, 232 Or App 492, 222 P3d 49 (2009) (noting, “This case, however, does not involve a single incident of defendant briefly driving onto a lane line. Defendant drove onto the lane lines four times over a relatively short distance”).

2024 Update

“[M]omentary and minor deviation over a lane line” does not constitute failure to maintain a lane under ORS 811.370. State v. Little, 326 Or App 788, (July 6, 2023) (police lacked probable cause to pull the defendant over where the defendant’s tire moved over the fog line by the width of a tire, approximately six inches, and stayed there for 1.5 seconds).

3. Failure to Signal Lane Change Within 100 Feet

2024 Update

Failing to signal when a single lane splits into two does not constitute failure to signal before changing lanes under ORS 811.375. State v. Redding, 325 Or App 115 (April 5, 2023) (police lacked probable cause to pull the defendant over where she maneuvered to the right-most side of the road as the road began to widen but before lane markers indicated the presence of a new, additional lane).

4. Obscured License Plate

2024 Update

ORS 803.550 prohibits “knowingly” displaying an “altered, modified, covered or obscured” license plate.  In State v. Mendell, 333 Or App 342 (2024), Judge Jacquot wrote a concurrence, arguing that, contrary to prior dicta, an officer would need to observe facts sufficient to prove that the defendant knew that her license plate was altered, modified, covered, or obscured, and that given the unusual circumstances at issue, the evidence did not suffice to prove that the defendant had the requisite knowledge.  Id. at 349.

C. Common Factual Observations That May Suffice to Establish Traffic Violations

1. Failure to signal within 100 feet prior to turning or changing lanes

ORS 811.335, and ORS 811.375 respectively.

State v. Kelly, 227 Or App 553, 556-57, 206 P3d 1098 (2009) (strictly construing the requirement to “signal continuously” for a distance “not less than the last 100 feet traveled[,]” regardless of how long a person sits at a stop sign with her blinker activated before turning).

State v. Chilson, 219 Or App 136, 138, 182 P3d 241 (2008) (strictly construing statute where driver entered the street less than 100 feet from the intersection at which she turned).

2. Tinted Windows

State v. Baker, 232 Or App 112, 132-33, 221 P3d 749 (2009) (“[W]indows were very darkly tinted, causing [officer] to believe that the tinting exceeded the amount permitted by law * * * That the windows were tinted to the maximum allowed by law, further demonstrate[es] that [the officer’s] belief, although mistaken, was nevertheless reasonable.”).

State v. De La Rosa, 228 Or App 666, 208 P3d 1012 (2009) (holding that the officer’s belief was objectively reasonable where he described the window tint as “extremely dark,” and was unable to “make out the silhouettes of anyone inside” the car).

D. Reasonable Suspicion of a Crime Can Justify a Traffic Stop

In State v. Ricks, 166 Or App 436, 441, 998 P2d 234 (2000), the court clarified that “reasonable suspicion” of a crime—not “probable cause” is all that is required to stop a vehicle to investigate whether the person has committed a crime.

1. Informants’ Reports

When an officer’s reasonable suspicion is based solely on the report of a citizen-informant, the informant’s report must bear some indicia of reliability. State v. Hames, 223 Or App 624, 628, 196 P3d 88 (2008). Three factors help the court assess the reliability of the tip: (1) whether the informant subjects himself to possible liability if the report is false; (2) whether the report is based on the informant’s own personal observations; and (3) whether the officer’s observations corroborate the information provided by the informant. State v. Goss, 219 Or App 645, 651, 184 P3d 1155, rev den, 345 Or 94 (2008). “The officer may corroborate the tip either by observing the illegal activity or by finding the person, the vehicle, and the location substantially as described by the informant.” State v. Bybee, 131 Or App 492, 497, 884 P2d 906 (1994).]

2. DUII

“Because members of the general public have a common knowledge about whether a person is under the influence of alcohol, a statement from a named informant, based on his own personal observations, that a person is a ‘drunken driver’ is sufficient to establish reasonable suspicion.’” State v. Bybee, 131 Or App 492, 497, 884 P2d 906 (1994); see also State v. Goss, 219 Or App 645, 651 n 3, 184 P3d 1155 (2008) (same); State v. Perrin, 143 Or App 123, 128 n 3, 923 P2d 1249 (1996) (same).

E. Out-of-State Law Enforcement Officers

An out-of-state officer can stop a defendant in Oregon so long as an Oregon officer could have performed the same stop without violating Article I, section 9, and the extrajurisdictional aspect of the stop was reasonable. State v. Keller, 361 Or 566, 580, 396 P3d 917 (2017).

In Keller, a Washington State Trooper observed defendant commit traffic violations in Washington, just north of the Interstate Bridge. The trooper activated his emergency lights to initiate the stop while he and defendant were both still in Washington. The trooper stopped defendant at the earliest opportunity, just across the bridge in Oregon. There is no statutory authority for an out-of-state police officer to stop a person in Oregon to enforce an out-of-state violation. Nor would such conduct have been tolerated at common law. However, the court concluded that under the totality of the circumstances—stopping the defendant as soon as practicable and then immediately contacting Oregon authorities—made the extraterritorial aspect of the stop reasonable.
