I. Introduction

This chapter focuses on the last—and often most important—part of the Article I, section 9, analysis—the remedy. The purpose of raising an Article I, section 9, violation in the context of defending a criminal case is ultimately to obtain a court order suppressing whatever evidence the state obtained as a result of its unlawful search or seizure. This chapter focuses on the rules that govern when and whether evidence should be suppressed and the three primary exceptions to the exclusionary rule (attenuation, independent source, and inevitable discovery) that the state may argue saves their evidence from suppression.

II. Fruit of the Poisonous Tree: The Origins of the Exclusionary Rule

More than a century ago, the United States Supreme Court eloquently explained the policy rationale underlying the federal rule that requires suppression of all evidence derived from unlawful searches or seizures:

“If letters and private documents can [illegally] be seized and held and used in evidence against a citizen accused of an offense, the protection of the Fourth Amendment declaring his right to be secure against such searches and seizures is of no value, and, so far as those thus placed are concerned, might as well be stricken from the Constitution. The efforts of the courts and their officials to bring the guilty to punishment, praiseworthy as they are, are not to be aided by the sacrifice of those great principles established by years of endeavour and suffering which have resulted in their embodiment in the fundamental law of the land.”

Weeks v. United States, 232 US 383, 393, 34 S Ct 341, 58 L Ed 652 (1914) (emphasis added).

The Supreme Court has since made clear that deterring the government from engaging in unreasonable searches and seizures is the primary (if not exclusive) purpose of the federal exclusionary rule. Elkins v. United States, 364 US 206, 80 S Ct 1437, 4 L Ed 2d 1669 (1960) (“The rule is calculated to prevent, not to repair. Its purpose is to deter—to compel respect for the constitutional guaranty in the only effectively available way—by removing the incentive to disregard it.”).

The most famous and influential application of the poisonous-tree rule is found in Wong Sun v. United States, 371 US 471, 83 S Ct 407, 9 L Ed 2d 441 (1963). That case, more than any other, has defined the scope of the rule, both under the Fourth Amendment and Article I, section 9. See State v. Unger, 356 Or 59, 72, 333 P3d 1009 (2014) (“Since at least [State v. Kennedy [290 Or 493, 624 P2d 98 (1981)]], this court has referred to and used the exploitation analysis announced in Wong Sun in the context of determining whether evidence obtained through voluntary consent searches should be suppressed.”).

Wong Sun explains that both direct and indirect evidence are suppressible as fruit of the poisonous tree. Wong Sun v. United States, 371 US 471, 484-85, 83 S Ct 407, 9 L Ed 2d 441 (1963) (“The exclusionary prohibition extends as well to the indirect as the direct products of such invasions.”).

Direct (or primary) evidence is anything that police learn or obtain as a direct, immediate result of their illegal conduct (e.g., physical evidence obtained during an illegal search, incriminating statements overheard during an illegal wiretap, observations made during an unlawful stop):

“The exclusionary rule has traditionally barred from trial physical, tangible materials obtained either during or as a direct result of an unlawful invasion * * * [T]he Fourth Amendment may protect against the overhearing of verbal statements as well as against the more traditional seizure of ‘papers and effects.’ Similarly, testimony as to matters observed during an unlawful invasion has been excluded in order to enforce the basic constitutional policies.”

Wong Sun, 371 US at 485 (internal citations omitted).

Indirect or derivative evidence is evidence obtained by exploiting some aspect of the illegal search or seizure to generate evidence that was not immediately apparent because of the illegality. This usually occurs in one of two ways: either (1) police gain information that influences the course of their investigation, resulting in the discovery of incriminating evidence, or (2) the officers’ illegal conduct influences the defendant to give consent, make statements, or take action that is, or results in the discovery of, incriminating evidence.

Wong Sun cited Silverthorne Lumber Co. v. United States, 251 US 385, 40 S Ct 182, 64 L Ed 319 '' (1920), for the proposition that “[t]he exclusionary prohibition extends as well to the indirect as the direct products of such invasions.”Wong Sun'', 371 US at 484-85.

In Silverthorne, the government illegally searched the defendant’s business and seized certain documents. It later returned those documents and then, using the knowledge gained from those documents, sought and obtained a subpoena ordering the defendant to produce the same documents. The government acknowledged the illegal seizure, but sought “to maintain its right to avail itself of the knowledge obtained by that means which otherwise it would not have had.” Silverthorne Lumber Co. v. United States, 251 US
at 391.

The government argued that the exclusionary rule applied only to physical seizures and merely prohibited it from possessing and presenting the original documents to a court or grand jury, “but not any [other] advantages that the Government can gain…by doing the forbidden act.” Id.

In rejecting the government’s proposition, Justice Holmes explained that the Fourth Amendment applies both to illegally seized evidence (primary or direct evidence) and to evidence that the government subsequently develops or generates (derivative evidence) as a result of the illegal search or seizure:

“The essence of a provision forbidding the acquisition of evidence in a certain way is that not merely evidence so acquired shall not be used before the court, but that it shall not be used at all.”

Silverthorne, 251 US at 392.

Returning to the facts of Wong Sun: federal agents in San Francisco arrested a man who told them that he had bought an ounce of heroin from Mr. Toy, the proprietor of a laundry. The agents located Mr. Toy’s business, and one agent rang the bell while five or six other officers hid out of sight. After initially inquiring about laundry, the agent informed Mr. Toy that he was a federal narcotics officer. Toy slammed the door and ran back toward his living quarters. The agents broke down the door and arrested Mr. Toy at gunpoint. Mr. Toy told the officers that Johnny Yee on Eleventh Avenue had been selling drugs.

The agents found Mr. Yee’s place and obtained an ounce of heroin from him. Mr. Yee told the agents that he got the heroin from Mr. Toy and Wong Sun. The agents found Wong Sun’s apartment, entered without a warrant, and arrested him.

Mr. Toy and Wong Sun were arraigned in federal court and released on their own recognizance. Within the next few days, both men voluntarily and separately returned to the station. They were each advised of the right to withhold information which might be used against them, and that they were entitled to the advice of counsel. Both men made incriminating statements.

Mr. Toy sought to suppress:

(1) the statements he made at the time of his arrest in his bedroom

(2) the heroin surrendered to the agents by Johnny Yee

(3) his post-arraignment statements and

(4) Wong Sun’s similar post-arraignment statements.

The government conceded that the statements Mr. Toy made in the bedroom should be “excluded if they are held to be ‘fruits’ of the agents’ unlawful action.” Id. at 416. However, it argued that Mr. Toy’s statements were not fruits because they resulted from his own “intervening independent act of a free will.” Id. The Court rejected the claim, stating that, under the circumstances in which the statements were made, “it is unreasonable to infer that Mr. Toy’s response was sufficiently an act of free will to purge the primary taint of the unlawful invasion.” Id. at 486 (footnote omitted). The Court explained:

“[V]erbal evidence which derives so immediately from an unlawful entry and an unauthorized arrest as the officers’ action in the present case is no less the ‘fruit’ of official illegality than the more common tangible fruits of the unwarranted intrusion.”

Id. at 485. The Supreme Court held that the statements made during the defendant’s initial seizure were “fruit,” and, because those statements led the agents to find and seize heroin in another location, the heroin was additional derivative fruit. The Court noted:

“[T]his is not the case envisioned by this Court where the exclusionary rule has no application because the Government learned of the evidence from an independent source, nor is this a case in which the connection between the lawless conduct of the police and the discovery of the challenged evidence has become so attenuated as to dissipate the taint.”

Id. at 488 (internal citations and quotation marks omitted). The Court explained the scope of the fruit-of-the-poisonous-tree doctrine:

“We need not hold that all evidence is ‘fruit of the poisonous tree’ simply because it would not have come to light but for the illegal actions of the police. Rather, the more apt question in such a case is whether, granting establishment of the primary illegality, the evidence to which instant objection is made has been come at by exploitation of that illegality or instead by means sufficiently distinguishable to be purged of the primary taint.”

Id. (Internal citation and quotation marks omitted). Because the illegally obtained statements led the police to find the heroin, the heroin too derived from the primary illegality. To allow the government to introduce the heroin would reward its unlawful action. Consequently, the Court suppressed the drugs as tainted fruit of the poisonous tree.

The Court found it unnecessary to decide whether either Mr. Toy’s confession or Wong Sun’s confession were fruits of the poisonous tree because they were deemed inadmissible on other grounds. Id. at 488.

In the case against Wong Sun, the only evidence that came to light after the agents violated his rights by entering his apartment illegally was the statement that he gave several days later after he had been released on his own recognizance:

“On the evidence that Wong Sun had been released on his own recognizance after a lawful arraignment, and had returned voluntarily several days later to make the statement, we hold that the connection between the arrest and the statement had ‘become so attenuated as to dissipate the taint.’”

Id. at 485 (quoting Nardone v. United States, 308 US 338, 341, 60 S Ct 266, 84 L Ed 307 (1939)).

III. Locally Sourced Fruit: The Exclusionary Rule Under Article I, Section 9, of the Oregon Constitution

Oregon’s exclusionary rule operates the same way as the Fourth Amendment rule: evidence gained directly or indirectly through illegal police conduct must be suppressed. But, as we know, Oregon flies with her own wings, and the Oregon exclusionary rule is based on a different rationale than the federal exclusionary rule. Oregon’s rule is not intended to deter unlawful police conduct. Rather, Oregon’s rule is meant to protect the liberty of the people against unreasonable intrusion by the state.

In State v. Davis, 295 Or 227, 231-37, 666 P2d 802 (1983), the Oregon Supreme Court explained the state-specific principles underlying Article I, section 9’s, exclusionary rule. The court recognized that the rationale in Weeks v. United States and other pre-Weeks cases was that exclusion was necessary to effectuate the Fourth Amendment’s protections because to do otherwise would “emasculate the constitutional guaranty, and deprive it of all beneficial force or effect in preventing unreasonable searches and seizures.” Id. at 232-33 (internal citations and quotation marks omitted).

The court then reviewed its own cases and concluded that the exclusionary rule under Article I, section 9, is remedial and personal. Unlike the Fourth Amendment rule, the Article I, section 9, rule’s function is not to deter the state, but rather to vindicate the individual’s constitutional rights and to restore the individual to the position that they would have been in had the state acted lawfully:

“The object of denying the government the fruits of its transgression against the person whose rights it has invaded is…to preserve that person’s rights to the same extent as if the government’s officers had stayed within the law.”

Davis, 295 Or at 233-34.

“[T]he deterrent effect on future practices against others, though a desired consequence, is not the constitutional basis for respecting the rights of a defendant against whom the state proposes to use evidence already seized. In demanding a trial without such evidence, the defendant invokes rights personal to himself.”

Id. at 235.

“[Since 1922 the Oregon Supreme Court] has maintained the principle that those rules of law designed to protect citizens against unauthorized or illegal searches or seizures of their persons, property, or private effects are to be given effect by denying the state the use of evidence secured in violation of those rules against the persons whose rights were violated, or, in effect, by restoring the parties to their position as if the state’s officers had remained within the limits of their authority.”

Id. at 237. Thus, evidence that the government obtains in violation of a person’s Article I, section 9, rights is inadmissible against that person. The court must suppress the evidence to vindicate that person’s constitutional rights. The rule is meant to restore individuals to the positions that they would have held if “the government’s officers had stayed within the law.” Davis, 295 Or at 234.

If the illegal police conduct puts the person in a worse position than if the governmental officers had acted within the bounds of the law with respect to its discovery of evidence, that evidence may be subject to suppression to remedy the wrong. '''' Unger, 356 Or at 74.

IV. Exceptions to the Exclusionary Rule: The Doctrines of Inevitable Discovery, Independent Source, and Attenuation

If the state can prove that the police did not exploit their prior illegal conduct to get the disputed evidence, the evidence will likely not be suppressed. “Under an exploitation analysis, the state has the burden to prove that the evidence discovered was independent of, or only tenuously related to, the unlawful police conduct.” State v. Cowdrey, 290 Or App 415, 426, 416 P3d 314 (2018). If the state meets that burden, the evidence will probably be admitted.

Even if the state fails to prove that a warrantless search or seizure was lawful, the state may be able to defeat suppression, by proving that “‘(1) the police inevitably would have obtained the evidence through lawful procedures; (2) the police obtained the evidence independently of the illegal conduct; or, as relevant [in every consent case], (3) the illegal conduct was “independent of, or only tenuously related to” the disputed evidence.’” Unger, 356 Or at 64 (quoting State v. Hall, 339 Or 7, 25, 35, 115 P3d 908 (2005)).

In each of the those three scenarios, the admission of the challenged evidence does not offend Article I, section 9, because the defendant has not been disadvantaged as a result of the unlawful police conduct, or, put another way, because the defendant has not been placed in a worse position than if the officers had remained within the bounds of the law.

To clarify, despite a prior violation of Article I, section 9, the state may demonstrate a disconnection between the illegality and the state’s possession of the evidence under any of three doctrines: inevitable discovery, independent source, or attenuation. Suppression of evidence in any of those circumstances is not required to vindicate the defendant’s rights under Article I, section 9, because the evidence sought to be suppressed was not a product of a violation of the defendant’s rights.

Practice Tip

Don’t forget that—as with other warrantless-search challenges—the state bears the burden of proof with respect to these three doctrines. It is not the defendant’s job to disprove their application. ORS 133.693(4); see, e.g., '' State v. Where a warrant search is involved, the initial burden to establish a police illegality lies with the defendant, ORS 133.693(3), but once the illegality is established, the state must prove that the police did not exploit their unlawful conduct to gain either evidence or the defendant’s consent.SeeState v. At the time of publication, the Supreme Court has granted review in DeJong, and it would not be surprising for the court to hold that where a warrant is obtained after a potentially illegal seizure, the state bears the burden to establish the lawfulness of the initial seizure and that the warrant was not obtained by way of exploitation of that illegality.

The “inevitable discovery” doctrine applies when the state establishes by a preponderance of the evidence that existing proper and predictable police procedures would have produced the contested evidence even if there had been no illegality. State v. Miller, 300 Or 203, 225-26, 709 P2d 225 (1985) (explaining principle). See, e.g., Chapter 4 (if the state can prove that a valid inventory conducted pursuant to a valid inventory policy would have led to the discovery of the evidence, the state may avoid suppression).

The “independent source” doctrine applies when the state establishes that something other than and separate from the police illegality in fact produced the evidence. For example, assume that the police conduct an unlawful search of a vehicle. Then, later in the investigation, they obtain a warrant to search related premises. Evidence discovered pursuant to the warrant is admissible only if the state can establish that the warrant was issued solely based on information that police properly possessed and not on information that they gained during the illegal vehicle search. See State v. Johnson, 335 Or 511, 73 P3d 282 (2003) (the state argued that the evidence should not be suppressed because “after the initial seizure, [the evidence was] reseized pursuant to a lawful warrant that was entirely independent of, and was not obtained by exploitation of, the previous illegality”).

Practice Tip

Independent source and inevitable discovery are conceptually similar doctrines that litigators sometimes conflate. Each doctrine requires the state to establish a source other than the police illegality for the disputed evidence. Bear in mind, though, that the doctrine of inevitable discovery does not '' require the state to prove that it actually'' gained the evidence in some lawful way, only that it would have done so in the ordinary course of the investigation. By contrast, the independent source doctrine requires proof that the evidence was actually gained lawfully, not just that it would have been.

The “attenuation” doctrine applies when the state proves that a significant intervening event (or events) took place between the police illegality and the discovery of the evidence, or when the state proves that the illegal police conduct did not affect or had only a tenuous connection to the evidence discovered such that it cannot be said that agents of the state “exploited” their illegal conduct to obtain the evidence. Commonly, it is a person’s consent to search that constitutes attenuation, but other factors may also
attenuate.

State v. Riley, 288 Or App 264, 406 P3d 81 (2017), rev den, 362 Or 861 (2018), illustrates how attenuation works. In that case, the court assumed without deciding that the defendant was unlawfully seized when police surrounded an apartment where the defendant and his codefendants were hidden and ordered a codefendant to come out. The Court of Appeals assumed without deciding that the defendant was unlawfully seized but held that the evidence gained while the defendant was holed up in the apartment and after he was arrested was not gained as the result of any unlawful seizure of the defendant. First, the purported seizure occurred at least two hours before the police obtained any of the evidence, and was thus temporally distant. Second, the residents of the apartment provided information to a relative who then provided it to the police, a disclosure unrelated to any seizure. Further, the residents’ consent to a search of the premises was also unrelated to the earlier seizure—the evidence showed that the residents allowed the search because they were concerned about their own safety. In sum, the police did not trade on any aspect of the earlier unlawful seizure to gain the challenged evidence.

2024 Update

State must show that the defendant would have remained at the scene regardless of illegal police conduct to justify search of the defendant under inevitable discovery doctrine. State v. Alonso-Vasquez, 315 Or App 208, 212, 501 P3d 20 (2021).

The predictable legal consequences of an unlawful arrest can establish the exploitive link to defeat the state’s attenuation argument. State v. Pitz, 317 Or App 8, 17, 504 P3d 685 (2022). In Pitz, the court reasoned that once the defendant was arrested her only available alternatives besides consenting to the search were abandoning her closed container or bringing it with her to the jail, “where it would be inevitably seized and searched as part of the booking process.” The defendant's lack of a meaningful choice to avoid the search of her possessions reduced any motivation she may have had to withhold consent (apparently whether or not she was actually aware of the inventory procedure/exception) and thus her consent was a product of her unlawful arrest.

V. Consent: A Special Kind of Attenuation

The most common type of attenuation that arises in search and seizure cases is consent. Our clients frequently give consent when police ask to search their persons, cars, and premises.

•       If no prior illegal search or seizure has occurred, and a person voluntarily consents to a search, evidence discovered in the consent search is not subject to suppression because no violation of the person’s constitutional rights has taken place.

•       If a person voluntarily consents to a search after a police illegality, evidence discovered in the search may be admissible despite the illegality if the state establishes that the police did not exploit the illegality to gain the person’s consent.

•       If a person’s consent to search is not voluntary, suppression is required regardless of whether a prior illegality occurred or, if a prior illegality did occur, whether the police exploited the illegality to gain consent.

In 2014, the Oregon Supreme court decided a trio of cases that changed the landscape with respect to suppression of evidence gained in consent searches: Unger, 356 Or 59; State v. Lorenzo, 356 Or 134, 335 P3d 821 (2014), and State v. Musser, 356 Or 148, 335 P3d 814 (2014). Before, under Hall, 339 Or at 25, 34-35, if a defendant established a “minimal factual nexus” between the police illegality and the evidence sought to be suppressed, the evidence would be suppressed unless the state could show that (in the consent context) the consent was independent of or only tenuously related to the unlawful police conduct. In the three 2014 cases, the court “clarified” Hall, disavowing the “minimal factual nexus” part of Hall’s test and holding instead that when a defendant establishes an illegal stop and asserts that their consent was invalid, the state must demonstrate that (1) the consent was voluntary and (2) the consent was not the product of police exploitation of the illegality. Anyone litigating an exploitation/consent-search suppression issue must start with Unger, Lorenzo, and Musser.

A. State v. Unger, 356 Or 59 (2014): The Basics

Three issues arise in every case where the state asserts that the defendant consented to a search. Unger, 356 Or at 76, 86. “[T]he inquiry into whether evidence obtained pursuant to a consent search must be suppressed involves three overlapping issues: (1) whether the initial stop or search was lawful; (2) whether the defendant’s consent to the subsequent search was voluntary; and (3) assuming that the initial stop or search was unlawful and the consent to the subsequent search was voluntary, whether the police exploited the illegality to obtain the disputed evidence.” Unger, 356 Or at 76, 86.

Where the state attempts to prove attenuation (i.e., lack of exploitation) on the grounds that the defendant’s voluntary consent was a sufficient intervening factor to break the chain of causation between the police illegality and the obtaining of the evidence, it should fail where “police sought the defendant’s consent solely as the result of knowledge of inculpatory evidence obtained from unlawful police conduct” or “because the unlawful police conduct, even if not overcoming the defendant’s free will, significantly affected the defendant’s decision to consent.” Unger, 356 Or at 70-71.

The legal determination of whether a grant of “consent has so attenuated the connection between the prior illegal conduct and the evidence obtained in the consent search” involves an examination of the “illegal conduct that comprised the stop or search, the character of the consent, and the causal relationship between the two.” Unger, 356 Or at 78.

The court has held that an “unprompted grant of consent in Kennedy and a similar volunteering of consent in Rodriguez were intervening circumstances that indicated that there was, at most, a tenuous causal connection between the consent and the prior illegal police conduct.” Unger, 356 Or at 78.

In Unger, the court pushed that line further still, stating that even “voluntary consent to search that is prompted by an officer’s request can be sufficient to demonstrate that the consent is unrelated or only tenuously related to the prior illegal police conduct.” Unger, 356 Or at 79 (emphasis added). The determination of whether “voluntary consent is sufficient—or whether the police exploited their illegal conduct to obtain consent—will depend on the totality of the circumstances.” Id.

Practice Tip

What feels like voluntary consent to our clients and what constitutes voluntary consent as a matter of law may be different. Even if your client tells you that she agreed to a search, be sure to examine police reports and client and witness statements with an eye to determining true voluntariness. Truly voluntary consent is that given by an act of free will, not in response to implied or express coercion. And mere acquiescence to police authority does not constitute voluntary consent. See State v. Stanley, 287 Or App 399, 406-07, 404 P3d 1100 (2017) (helpful roundup of voluntariness cases; holding that consent was not voluntary where officer told the defendant that he was going inside the defendant’s house, and the defendant said, “Go ahead”).

Although “Hall correctly stated that the exploitation inquiry involved consideration of the ‘totality of the circumstances,’ that decision’s focus on temporal proximity and intervening and mitigating circumstances was too narrow because, at least by implication, it excluded other relevant considerations[:] The nature, extent, and severity of police misconduct…[and] the purpose and flagrancy of that misconduct[.]” Unger, 356 Or at 64.

By all appearances, the Unger court “adher[ed]” to the portion of Hall’s exploitation analysis that would require suppression in the two scenarios that follow:

“‘A causal connection requiring suppression may exist because [1] the police sought the defendant’s consent solely as the result of knowledge of inculpatory evidence obtained from unlawful police conduct. A causal connection requiring suppression also may exist because [2] the unlawful police conduct, even if not overcoming the defendant’s free will, significantly affected the defendant’s decision to consent.’”

Unger, 356 Or at 76 (quoting Hall, 339 Or at 35). The court’s express adherence to those principles can be found in its summary of the exploitation test it announced:

“The overarching inquiry is whether the evidence that the state seeks to introduce must be suppressed because that evidence was obtained in violation of the defendant’s constitutional rights * * * [W]here an illegal stop [or search] preceded a consent to search…that inquiry has two prongs. First, the court must assess whether the consent was voluntary. If the consent to search was not voluntary, then the evidence must be suppressed, because only a voluntary consent to search provides an exception in this context to the warrant requirement of Article I, section 9.

“Second, even if the consent is voluntary, the court must address whether the police exploited their prior illegal conduct to obtain the evidence. Exploitation may be found if, for example, the police illegally stop a vehicle, allowing them to view contraband that otherwise would not have been visible, and then request the driver’s consent to search the vehicle as a result of what they saw. In that example, there may be a direct causal connection between the prior illegal stop and the consent because the request for consent itself (and the evidence gathered) resulted from police knowledge of the presence of that evidence, which they had only because they had observed it during the illegal stop. See Hall, 339 Or at 35. (‘A causal connection requiring suppression may exist because the police sought the defendant’s consent solely as the result of knowledge of inculpatory evidence obtained from unlawful police conduct.’). We articulated those principles in Hall and other cases, and we adhere to them.

“Hall also held that evidence may be subject to suppression if the police obtained the consent to search through less direct exploitation of their illegal conduct. Id. We adhere to that principle as well. As discussed previously, Hall stated that the exploitation analysis required consideration of the totality of the circumstances to determine whether the state had carried its burden of proving that the consent was independent of, or only tenuously related to, the unlawful police conduct * * * In this opinion, we have identified additional considerations that are relevant to that inquiry[.] … We have explained that the nature, extent, and severity of the constitutional violation are relevant, as are the purpose and flagrancy of the misconduct.”

Unger, 356 Or at 85-86 (emphasis added). Unger directs courts to consider the totality of the circumstances to determine whether the illegal stop impermissibly affected the defendant’s voluntary consent, with express attention to the following nonexhaustive list of considerations:

(1) The “nature, extent, and severity” of the constitutional violation.

(2) The temporal proximity of the consent to the illegality.

(3) Whether warnings were administered prior to consent.

(4) The presence of significant intervening events.

(5) The “purpose” of the constitutional violation.

(6) The “flagrancy” of the constitutional violation.

Id. at 86.

B. An Unger Reminder: No Fishing Expeditions Allowed

“When police observe contraband because they have unlawfully stopped someone or unlawfully entered a home—and then ask for consent to search, their ‘purpose’ is more likely to seize the contraband that they already have seen as a result of their misconduct. In those circumstances, the police have ‘taken advantage of’ or ‘exploited’ their unlawful conduct to the defendant’s detriment, and that tainted ‘purpose’ suggests that the defendant’s consent, even if voluntary, also may be tainted. So, too, may be a consent that follows a random stop or seizure that lacks probable cause or reasonable suspicion that a crime has been committed and that is nothing more than a fishing expedition for incriminating evidence.”

Unger, 356 Or at 91 (emphasis added).

C. Consent During an Unlawful Stop Is Still Presumed Invalid under Unger and Its Progeny

“[The dissenters] suggest that we have modified the Hall analysis to remove the presumption that a consent search following unlawful police conduct is ‘tainted’ or is invalid. On the contrary…we adhere to Hall in requiring the state to prove that the consent was independent of, or only tenuously related to, the illegal police conduct. 356 Or at 74–75…; Hall, 339 Or at 35…” Unger, 356 Or at 84.

VI. Applications

A. State v. Unger, 356 Or 59 (2014) (Held: Admissible)

In Unger, police responded to a report that children in the defendant’s residence were being exposed to guns and drugs. The police knocked on the front door and got no answer. Police then trespassed into the defendant’s backyard and knocked on the sliding glass door that led directly into the defendant’s bedroom. The police awakened the defendant, stated their business, and requested consent to look around. The defendant consented and gave the officers a tour of the house. The police found a baggie that appeared to have drug residue on it.

The court observed that the officers did not find any “inculpatory evidence as a result of their unlawful conduct [the trespass into the defendant’s backyard] and therefore sought consent to search. Rather, the unlawful conduct simply put the detectives in a place where they could initiate contact with the occupants of the house.” Unger, 356 Or at 87-88 (emphasis added, citation omitted).

The temporal proximity of the trespass to the discovery of evidence weighed in defendant’s favor, and the state did not identify any intervening or mitigating circumstances, such as providing Miranda warnings or admonitions to defendant that he could refuse to consent to a search. Id. at 90.

With respect to the flagrancy and purpose of the unlawful police conduct, the court noted that “[t]he detectives’ conduct did not rise to the level of an unlawful arrest or stop * * * Within the universe of possible unlawful police activity, the trespass here was limited in ‘extent, nature, and severity.’” Id. at 89.

“[The officers’] purpose—both in knocking on the front door and later on the sliding glass door—was to contact the homeowner to ask for permission to search the house, not to search for incriminating evidence near the back door… Moreover, there is no indication that the purpose of going to the back door was that defendant would be more likely to consent at the back door, rather than the front door.

“In contrast, when police observe contraband because they have unlawfully stopped someone or unlawfully entered a home—and then ask for consent to search, their ‘purpose’ is more likely to be to seize the contraband that they already have seen as a result of their misconduct. In those circumstances, the police have ‘taken advantage of’ or ‘exploited’ their unlawful conduct to the defendant’s detriment, and that tainted ‘purpose’ suggests that the defendant’s consent, even if voluntary, also may be tainted. So, too, may be a consent that follows a random stop or seizure that lacks probable cause or reasonable suspicion that a crime has been committed and that is nothing more than a fishing expedition for incriminating evidence. This case presents no one of those scenarios.

“Moreover, the detectives’ conduct in walking around defendant’s house to knock on his door was not flagrant or egregious. The detectives followed a path around the side of the house to the back door, which defendant could have chosen not to open * * * In short, nothing about the limited nature of the unlawful conduct, or the purpose or flagrancy of the conduct, suggests that it caused defendant to consent to the search.”

Id. at 91-92 (citations omitted).

On balance, the court determined that the state “met its burden of showing that, under the totality of the circumstances, the detectives in this case did not exploit their unlawful entry into defendant’s backyard to obtain his consent to enter the house or to obtain his consent to show the detectives around his house.” Id. at 92.

B. State v. Lorenzo, 356 Or 134, 335 P3d 821 (2014) (Held: Admissible)

The officer in Lorenzo was concerned for the defendant’s safety. He knocked on the front door of the defendant’s apartment and called the defendant’s name, asking if the defendant was okay. The officer then reached inside the front door and knocked on the defendant’s bedroom door, which he was able to do without stepping into the apartment. That trespass was the illegal police conduct at issue in the case. After knocking on the defendant’s bedroom door, the officer waited outside the apartment. The defendant came to an area visible from the front door and, when the officer asked if he could come inside and “talk,” consented to the officer’s entry. When the officer entered the apartment the second time, he smelled marijuana and asked the defendant for ID. When the defendant went to his bedroom to get the ID, the officer saw a bag containing what appeared to be marijuana on the bedroom floor and asked the defendant if he could search the bedroom. The defendant then consented to a search, which turned up incriminating evidence. Applying Unger, the court held that evidence found pursuant to the consent search was admissible.

The court found there was close temporal proximity between the illegality and the consent and no intervening or mitigating circumstances, but the other considerations cut in the opposite direction. Id. at 143-44. In particular, the search was limited in extent and severity and was not an effort to direct or control the defendant. Id. at 144. Moreover, the purpose of the search was to check on the defendant’s welfare and not to conduct an investigation. Id. at 145. As to flagrancy, the court acknowledged that the first entry into the defendant’s apartment was unlawful, but explained that the officer’s conduct was restrained. Id. at 145-46. Based on all those considerations, the court concluded that the state had met its burden of showing that the police did not exploit the unlawful conduct to obtain the defendant’s consent.

C. State v. Musser, 356 Or 148, 335 P3d 814 (2014) (Held: Suppressed)

A police officer saw the defendant on a walkway behind a shopping center that was located in a high crime area. Most of the businesses were closed. The officer directed the defendant and her companion to come speak to him, in order “to make sure they were not doing anything wrong.” When the defendant continued walking, the officer stated in a “more direct, firm tone,” “Hey, come back here. I need to talk to you.” The defendant complied. Musser, 356 Or at 151.

The officer asked for identification. While defendant was looking through her purse for identification, the officer saw two Crown Royal pouches. The defendant was nervous and fidgety, which led the officer to suspect that the defendant had recently used meth. Because of his general suspicion that she had drugs in her purse, he asked for consent to search the pouches. The defendant consented, and the officer found drugs. Id. at 151.

The officer lacked reasonable suspicion that the defendant had committed a crime when he stopped her; the officer’s conduct therefore constituted an unconstitutional stop. Further, the officer’s order “clearly indicated to defendant that she had no choice but to respond to the order, bringing her significantly under the control of the police.” Id. at 157. The stop lasted about an hour. Id. at 156.

Notably, the court explained, “the stop here was a more severe violation of defendant’s rights than the violation in Unger…or the similar conduct in Lorenzo.” Id.

The court reaffirmed the principle that exploitation may exist if the police seek the defendant’s consent solely as a result of knowledge of inculpatory evidence obtained from their unlawful conduct, recognizing that “the officer pursued several lines of inquiry ‘spurred by his observations of the contents of defendant’s purse during the unlawful seizure.’” Id. at 158.

The causal chain was plain: “[T]here was not simply ‘but for’ causation—unlawful police conduct and then a request for consent. Instead, the unlawful conduct led to the request for identification, which led to observation of pouches that the officer believed contained drugs, which led to the request to search and the evidence ultimately obtained. That evidence was the product of the initial unlawful stop.” Id. at 158.

Additionally, and independently, the officer’s purpose was “basically [making] sure they were not doing anything wrong: Breaking the law, damaging any property.” The court strongly disapproved of the officer’s conduct and purpose, stating:

“[P]olice are not authorized to detain and question citizens merely to ‘make sure they [are] not doing anything wrong.’ The purpose of the unlawful stop here—in contrast to the ‘knock-and-talk’ in Unger, which was precipitated by information about the presence of drugs, guns, and children in a particular house—apparently was a ‘shot in the dark’ to check for criminal activity…The initial and developing purpose of the police misconduct in continuing to detain defendant while inquiring about various possible crimes shows the state taking advantage of that misconduct in a way that likely had an effect on defendant’s decision to consent.”

Id. at 159 (emphasis added). As emphasized above, one theory of exploitation is that the police illegality affected the defendant’s decision to consent. In Musser, the court held that an officer’s apparent purpose (investigating drug activity without reasonable suspicion) “likely [has] an effect on the defendant’s decision to consent.” Id.

VII. Theories of Exploitation

Evaluating exploitation involves two separate inquiries:

(1) From the Officers’ Perspective: What effect did the illegality have on the officer’s actions?

Did the police illegality lead the police to observations or information that they would not have, absent the illegality? Did those observations prompt the police to seek consent, a warrant, or to take any other action that exposed the evidence at issue? This prong focuses on how the police used or exploited the information they gained as a result of unlawful conduct; it applies even if the defendant is entirely unaware of the prior illegality. It also applies regardless of whether the case involves a consent search or not.

(2) From the Defendant’s Perspective: What effect did the illegality have on the defendant’s actions?

This inquiry entails three distinct sub-questions:

·      Where consent is at issue, did the police illegality render the defendant’s consent
involuntary?

·      Where consent is at issue, did the police illegality affect or influence the defendant’s
decision to consent?

·      Even if consent is not at issue, did the police illegality otherwise affect the defendant’s conduct, resulting in incriminating evidence?

Practice Tip

Remember that those first two sub-questions require distinct '' analysis. Litigants sometimes conflate them or (especially if they’re prosecutors) tend to think that if consent to search is voluntary, the products of the search must be admissible. But even if a defendant’s consent is voluntary (i.e.'', the product of the person’s free choice, not mere acquiescence or the product of police coercion), the consent may still be a product of the police illegality.

Even if the police did not gain any direct evidence as a result of their illegal search or seizure (see, e.g., Unger and Lorenzo), the illegal search or seizure may nevertheless affect the defendant’s decision to make statements, give consent to search, or take incriminating actions. To determine whether the police contact has influenced the defendant’s decision-making, courts must use the Unger factors and look to the temporal proximity between the illegality and the consent or other conduct, the presence of intervening mitigating factors, the severity of the police conduct, and its purpose and flagrancy.

VIII. The Unger Games: Application of Unger, Musser, and Lorenzo

A. Exploitation from the Officer’s Perspective

What effect did the illegality have on the officer’s actions? Did the illegality lead the police to observations or information they would not have absent the illegality? Did those observations prompt the police to seek consent, a warrant, or to take any other action that exposed the evidence at issue?

State v. O’Dell, 291 Or App 359, 421 P3d 417 (2018). During a traffic stop, police asked the defendant, who seemed nervous and had a folding knife in his pocket, to get out of the car for officer safety purposes. During the ensuing pat down, the officer felt a pill bottle in the defendant’s pocket. The defendant consented to the removal of the pill bottle from his pocket and the search of the bottle. He then consented to a search of his car and phone. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that the defendant was unlawfully seized when the police ordered him out of the car, and that his consent, though voluntary, was a product of the illegal seizure. Id. at 366. In particular, the court noted that “the officer was able to leverage what he discovered in the unjustified seizure to shift the interaction from one concerned with officer safety to one aimed at finding evidence of [drug crimes] by asking questions and seeking consent to search [the defendant’s belongings.]” Id. (emphasis added). ''''

State v. Jackson, 268 Or App 139, 342 P3d 119 (2014). A state trooper approached the defendant in a service station mini-mart and told the defendant that he had failed to properly signal a turn. The trooper asked the defendant a number of questions about what he was doing, including questions about marijuana. During the conversation, the trooper, who was a DRE, observed signs of marijuana consumption in the defendant. The defendant eventually consented to a search of his car, and the trooper found incriminating evidence. The Court of Appeals held that the defendant was stopped when the trooper “unambiguously told” the defendant that he was under investigation for a traffic offense. The court held that the stop was unlawful because it was not supported by probable cause. Citing Musser, the court explained that the trooper relied on information garnered during that unlawful stop—the signs of marijuana use that the defendant manifested—to change the investigation from a traffic stop into a criminal investigation in which the trooper asked for consent to search the car. Thus, the defendant’s consent derived from the unlawful traffic stop, and the fruits of the search had to be suppressed.

B. Exploitation from the Defendant’s Perspective

What effect did the illegality have on the defendant’s decision to give consent? Did the police illegality render the defendant’s consent involuntary? Did the police illegality affect or influence the defendant’s decision to consent?

Even if the police did not gain incriminating information as a direct result of their illegal search or seizure, the illegal search or seizure may nevertheless affect the defendant’s decision to make statements, give consent, or take incriminating actions. To determine whether the police contact has influenced the defendant’s decision making, courts must look to the temporal proximity between the illegality and the consent or other conduct, the presence of intervening mitigating factors, the severity of the police conduct, and the purpose and flagrancy of the police conduct.

State v. Stevens, 364 Or 91, 430 P3d 1059 (2018). The defendant—a passenger in a car that was stopped for having a headlight out—was seized without reasonable suspicion of criminal activity when the officer told her that she could be in trouble with her PO if she lied to the officer about the identity of another person in the car. As the defendant began to walk away from the scene, the officer asked to search her backpack and she consented. That consent was a product of the illegal seizure. The consent was close in time to the illegal seizure, and the request occurred while the officers were engaged in searching the other passenger’s belongings, reinforcing the notion that the search of the defendant’s backpack was related to the investigation that was underway. Her consent was therefore neither unrelated nor only tenuously related to the illegal seizure.

State v. Rhyne, 290 Or App 827, 417 P3d 422, rev den 363 Or 481 (2018). In a fact- and law-intensive decision, the Court of Appeals affirmed the denial of the defendant’s motion to suppress, concluding that the stop at issue was not unlawfully extended in length or scope, and that the unlawful seizure of the defendant’s clip knife did not cause the defendant’s consent to search or his incriminating statements. Rather, the illegality simply coincided with a lawful police investigation. The lawful investigation produced the consent and the incriminating statements, not the unlawful seizure of the defendant’s property.

State v. Pichardo, 360 Or 754, 388 P3d 320 (2017). Three patrol cars stopped the defendant’s car—based on reasonable suspicion that the defendant was helping another person evade the police—and blocked the car in. Officers removed the defendant’s companion from the car at gunpoint and ordered the defendant to get out of the car. Immediately after asking for ID, an officer asked if he could search the defendant for drugs. The defendant consented. The Supreme Court held that the stop was initially lawful but that the request for consent to search was not reasonably related to the purpose of the stop and therefore unlawfully extended the stop. Further, the court held that given the extreme nature of the officers’ conduct and the fact that the officer’s question about drugs was not related to the stop, the defendant’s voluntary consent was not enough to attenuate the taint of the illegal stop.

State v. Kelly, 274 Or App 363, 360 P3d 691 (2015). A DHS worker and sheriff’s deputies went to the defendant’s house, saw a garage door open 8–18 inches from the ground, and heard voices inside the garage. A deputy asked the people inside to open the door and, when defendant could be heard saying, “hide that,” a deputy opened the garage door. When the deputies asked for permission to enter first the garage and then the house, the defendant allowed them entry. Drug paraphernalia was visible in the garage, and the defendant consented to a search of the garage. The Court of Appeals held that, in the totality of the circumstances, the “intrusiveness, purpose, and flagrancy of the unlawful search” [the opening of the garage door] were decisive in showing that the state did not meet its burden to prove that the defendant’s consent was not a product of the illegality.

State v. Kuschnick, 269 Or App 198, 344 P3d 480 (2015). Kuschnick '' is one of the Court of Appeals’ first exploitation decisions afterUnger. The decision contains a useful summary and thorough application of theUngerframework. 269 Or App at 210-17. The defendant was pulled over for a traffic violation, and a criminal investigation of both the defendant (driving while suspended) and the passenger (identity theft) followed. The ID theft investigation dead-ended. The officers told the defendant that he would be cited and that the passenger could drive the car away from the scene. But the seizure did not end. At that point, the officers unlawfully extended the stop by directing the defendant and the passenger to change places and requesting that each submit to a pat-down. Defendant consented, the officers found brass knuckles and arrested him for felon in possession of a restricted weapon. A search of the car followed, and ultimately the police found drug evidence. The Court of Appeals examined the totality of the circumstances carefully and held that the evidence should have been suppressed. In particular, the court relied on the fact that, “like the police conduct inMusser, the officers’ actions reasonably conveyed to defendant that he ‘had no choice but to respond to the [officers’] order[s], bringing [him] significantly under the control of the police.’” Kuschnick, 269 Or App at 214 (citingMusser, 356 Or at 157). The officers escalated their control over the defendant during the encounter and “traded on the illegality to gain his consent” to search.Id.'' at 216.

2024 Update

The predictable legal consequences of an unlawful arrest can establish the exploitive link to defeat the state’s attenuation argument. State v. Pitz, 317 Or App 8, 17, 504 P3d 685 (2022). In Pitz, the court reasoned that once the defendant was arrested her only available alternatives besides consenting to the search were abandoning her closed container or bringing it with her to the jail, “where it would be inevitably seized and searched as part of the booking process.” The defendant's lack of a meaningful choice to avoid the search of her possessions reduced any motivation she may have had to withhold consent (apparently whether or not she was actually aware of the inventory procedure/exception) and thus her consent was a product of her unlawful arrest.

IX. Non-Consent Attenuation Situations

A. An Outstanding Arrest Warrant Does Not Constitute Per Se Attenuation

1. Article I, Section 9

Under Article 1, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution, courts must apply the five-factor exploitation analysis under Unger to determine whether the discovery of an outstanding warrant attenuates the taint of prior unlawful police conduct. State v. Benning, 273 Or App 183, 359 P3d 357 (2015), and State v. Batistia, 276 Or App 634 (2016).

In Jones, after stopping the defendant without reasonable suspicion, police ran a records check, found an outstanding arrest warrant, arrested him, and discovered methamphetamine. Applying Benning, the court explained that the temporal proximity was “right at the same time.” There were no mitigating circumstances. The arrest warrant was not an intervening circumstance because it was a direct consequence of the unlawful detention, which was investigatory in nature. Finally, “purpose and flagrancy” focuses on whether the stop had an investigatory objective. Here, the officer saw something unusual and took a “shot in the dark to see what might turn up.” Thus, the factors weighed in favor of suppression. State v Jones, 275 Or App 771 (2015).

2. Fourth Amendment

State v. Bailey, 356 Or 486 (2014) (discovering a warrant for a defendant’s arrest during an unlawful stop no longer constitutes per se attenuation).

The Oregon Supreme Court held that under the Fourth Amendment, the discovery and execution of a valid arrest warrant following an unlawful search or seizure do not always break the causal connection between an initial illegality and the discovery of evidence during a search incident to the arrest. Instead, the causal connection should be tested according to the three-factor test established by the United States Supreme Court in Brown v. Illinois, 422 US 590, 95 S Ct 2254, 45 L Ed 2d 416 (1975).

To deter police officers from conducting unreasonable searches and seizures, the federal exclusionary rule generally prohibits using evidence against a defendant at trial when the evidence was discovered as the result of an unreasonable search or seizure subject to certain exceptions, including the attenuation. The attenuation exception considers whether the causal chain between the discovery of the evidence and the unreasonable search or seizure is sufficiently attenuated to allow the evidence to be used at trial.

In this case, police officers unlawfully detained defendant when he was a passenger in a stopped car. During that unlawful detention, the officers ascertained defendant’s identity and ran a warrant check, which revealed an outstanding arrest warrant. The officers arrested defendant and, during a search incident to arrest, discovered that he was in possession of illegal drugs.

Defendant moved to suppress the evidence, contending that it resulted from an unlawful seizure and subject to the federal exclusionary rule. The circuit court and Court of Appeals rejected defendant’s argument, relying on State v. Dempster, 248 Or 404, 434 P2d 746 (1967), in which this court adopted a per se rule under the Fourth Amendment: the discovery and execution of a valid arrest warrant necessarily break the connection between any preceding unlawful police conduct and a search incident to the arrest on the warrant.

In a unanimous opinion, the Oregon Supreme Court concluded that Dempster’s per se rule is inconsistent with the United States Supreme Court’s subsequent development of the Fourth Amendment. Notably, eight years after Dempster, the United States Supreme Court decided Brown v. Illinois, which rejected a similar per se rule in favor of a three-factor test: (1) the temporal proximity between unlawful police conduct and the discovery of challenged evidence; (2)
the presence of intervening circumstances; and (3) the purpose and flagrancy of the official misconduct.  

The Oregon Supreme Court determined that Brown’s three-factor test should have been applied in this case as well. The court applied that three-factor test to the facts found by the trial court and concluded that the trial court erred in denying the defendant’s motion to suppress.

3. United States Supreme Court

Utah v. Strieff, 579 US ___, 136 S Ct 2056, 195 L Ed 2d 400 (2016). Under the Fourth Amendment, evidence discovered during an illegal stop may be admissible when the defendant is searched after officers learn the defendant has an outstanding arrest warrant. The officer discovered drugs on the defendant’s person only minutes after the illegal stop, which favored suppression. But once the officer discovered the warrant, he had an obligation to arrest defendant. It was then lawful to search the defendant incident to his arrest on the warrant. The officer was at most negligent in stopping defendant, and the stop did not reflect flagrantly unlawful police misconduct.


 From Justice Sotomayor’s ringing dissent:

“[T]his case tells everyone, white and black, guilty and innocent, that an officer can verify your legal status at any time. It says that your body is subject to invasion while courts excuse the violation of your rights. It implies that you are not a citizen of a democracy but the subject of a carceral state, just waiting to be cataloged.

“We must not pretend that the countless people who are routinely targeted by police are ‘isolated.’ They are the canaries in the coal mine whose deaths, civil and literal, warn us that no one can breathe in this atmosphere. See L. Guinier & G. Torres, The Miner’s Canary 274–283 (2002). They are the ones who recognize that unlawful police stops corrode all our civil liberties and threaten all our lives. Until their voices matter too, our justice system will continue to be anything but.”

Id. at ___, 136 S Ct at 2071.

B. Commission of a New Crime During the Illegal Seizure Likely Attenuates the Discovery of Evidence from the Prior Illegality

Applying the Unger factors, the Oregon Supreme Court held that the defendant’s choice to commit a new crime (by giving a false name to an officer) was unrelated, other than in a “but for” sense, to the officer’s unlawful stop. Therefore, the defendant’s statement misidentifying himself was sufficiently attenuated from the police illegality and thus admissible. State v. Suppah, 358 Or 565 (2016).

C. Subsequent Search Warrant Does Not Constitute Per Se Attenuation

2024 Update

Under Article I, section 9, when a defendant seeks to suppress evidence discovered pursuant to a warrant search that was conducted after the police had subjected the defendant to an unlawful search or seizure, the defendant bears an initial “minimal” burden to show a factual nexus between the illegal seizure and the subsequent warrant search. State v. DeJong, 368 Or 640, 642, 497 P3d 710 (2021).

That burden can be satisfied if information obtained as a result of the illegality was included in

the warrant affidavit—such as the identity of the defendant, the items sought, the relationship

between the defendant and the items sought, or the locations in which they might be found. Id. at 654-55.

The burden then shifts to the state to prove that the evidence was untainted by the prior illegality. Id. 656.

Moreover under, State v. Turay, 371 Or 128 (2023) (June 16, 2023), when even a minor part of the warrant is defective; e.g., if there is one insufficiently particular command within a warrant and the defendant establishes a minimal factual nexus between that part of the warrant and the subsequently discovered evidence, it no longer suffices to excise that command from the warrant and simply re-review for probable cause. Now, after Turay, the bad command taints the entire warrant unless and until the state proves the applicability of inevitable discovery, attenuation, or independent source.

2024 Update

State v. De Witt Simons, 329 Or App 506 (2023)

The trial court erred when it excised constitutionally tainted information from warrant affidavit and rereviewed for probable cause, overruling State v. Binner, 128 Or App 639, 646 (1994); State v. Gardner, 263 Or App 309 (2014); State v. Hitesman/Page, 113 Or App  356, 359 (1992).  Id. at 523.

Under DeJong, excision is no longer a sufficient remedy; the whole warrant is presumptively tainted.  Once a defendant establishes a “minimal factual nexus,” the state may not use any evidence discovered pursuant to the warrant, unless the state establishes that the item of evidence is “untainted” by the preceding illegality by proving independent source, inevitable discovery, or attenuation.  Id. at 523-24.

X. Inevitable Discovery

The inevitable discovery doctrine allows the state to purge the taint of illegally seized evidence by demonstrating that it would have been discovered, absent the illegality, by proper and predictable police procedures. State v. Miller, 300 Or 203, 225-26, 709 P2d 225 (1985), cert den, 475 US 1141 (1986). The state has the burden prove the requirements of inevitable discovery. State v. Hall, 339 Or 7, 25, 115 P3d 908 (2005). Additionally, in order for the state to satisfy its burden, “the state [is] required to establish that, as a factual matter, [the] items would still—notwithstanding [the detective’s] unlawful conduct—have been available for seizure [when the warrant issued].” State v. Lovaina-Burmudez, 257 Or App 1, 14, 303 P3d 900, rev den, 354 Or 148 (2013) (holding that [emphasis in original]). See also Chapter 4 (often the state will attempt to rely on the inventory exception in an effort to establish that the evidence would have inevitably been discovered).

State v. Johnson, 177 Or App 244, 35 P3d 1024 (2001) (Johnson I), and State v. Johnson, 335 Or 511, 73 P3d 282 (2003) (Johnson II), are especially instructive.

The two Johnson cases arose from the same aggravated murder prosecution. Police arrested the defendant on an unrelated charge and noticed that his work boots were “consistent” with a tread print at the murder scene. 335 Or at 514. The officers seized the defendant’s boots without a warrant and then processed them as evidence of the homicide. Id. The defendant moved to suppress the boots because they had been unlawfully seized. Id. The state responded by arguing, in part, that regardless of the lawfulness of the initial seizure, the evidence would inevitably have been discovered pursuant to a lawful inventory. Id. The trial court granted the motion to suppress. Id. at 515.

After losing the motion to suppress, the state pursued two divergent courses. First, the state appealed the trial court’s suppression order, culminating in Johnson I. Second, and concurrently, the state applied for a search warrant authorizing the seizure of the boots, which were still retained in the police department’s evidence locker, which culminated in Johnson II.

In Johnson I, the state advanced an inevitable discovery argument, based on testimony presented during the suppression hearing, that if the defendant’s property had not been taken as evidence, “‘it would have gone with the defendant to the jail and been seized by the jail staff and placed in their inventory of property.’” 177 Or App at 250. The court concluded that that testimony was not sufficient to meet the requisites of inevitably discovery:

“At best, that testimony establishes only that the evidence would have been taken into inventory at the jail, not that the police would inevitably have seized the evidence and held it until trial. The testimony does not address how long the clothing would have been kept in the jail, whether it would have been released to a relative or friend of defendant’s if he had so requested, or any steps that the police would have had to take to obtain a transfer of items in the jail’s possession. These are all things that defendant could have done with the clothes, because they remained his property while he was in custody.”

Id. at 250. The court further rejected an alternative inevitable discovery argument by the state, to the effect that the state could have successfully sought and obtained a warrant authorizing the seizure of the disputed items: ‘I had the affidavit [in support of a search warrant application], and I could have perfectly easily put that information in the affidavit and seized it.’ However, in Miller, 300 Or at 226, the Supreme Court emphasized that for the inevitable discovery doctrine to be applicable it must be shown “that the evidence would have been discovered absent the illegality by proper and predictable investigatory procedures,” and that “[i]t is not enough to show that the evidence ‘might’ or ‘could have been’ otherwise obtained. The officer’s belief that he could have obtained a warrant was not sufficiently certain to satisfy the inevitable discovery doctrine.” Id. at 252–53, Consequently, the trial court correctly suppressed the evidence.

While the state was pursuing its inevitable discovery arguments in the Court of Appeals, culminating in Johnson I, it had also “appli[ed] for a search warrant authorizing a new seizure of the [the boots].” Johnson II, 335 Or at 515. Following the issuance of that warrant, the defendant again moved to suppress, asserting, in part, that “the police had held the property in their own evidence room [and] had denied [the] defendant’s request for return of the property.” Id. The trial court found that the defendant had, in fact, requested that the items be returned through his girlfriend. Id. at 517.

The state presented evidence in support of its assertion that if the items had been turned over to the girlfriend, the police would have been able to recover those items from her. Id. at 517–18. The trial court was not persuaded.

The state then appealed to the Oregon Supreme Court. The Supreme Court first explained that, despite the issuance of the warrant, in circumstances where the disputed evidence had initially been seized unlawfully, the presumption that evidence seized pursuant to a warrant was lawfully obtained did not apply, and upon an initial defense showing of a minimal factual nexus (i.e., a but-for relationship) between the illegality and the later discovered evidence, “the burden of proof fairly may be shifted to the government to show that the evidence is not tainted by the misconduct.” Id. at 521. The Supreme Court, while acknowledging some evidentiary support for the state’s position, rejected it as unavailing under the controlling standard of review and affirmed the order of suppression.

In order to show “inevitable discovery,” the state must do more than show that it had sufficient evidence to obtain a warrant; rather, the state is generally required to show that the state was actually engaged in the process of obtaining a warrant at the time, as opposed to merely demonstrating that the procedure was available. See Nix v. Williams, 467 US 431, 104 S Ct 2501, 81 L Ed 2d 377 (1984) (explaining that the inevitable search had already begun and then demonstrating that it would have uncovered the body).

Moreover, “In the case of a warrantless entry into premises, [the court must consider] the possibility that, if police had not made the illegal entry into the premises, evidence might have been disposed of or hidden.” Miller, 300 Or at 227.

Editor’s Note: In State v. DeJong, 305 Or App 325, 338, 469 P3d 253 (2020), rev allowed, 367 Or 496 (2021), the Court of Appeals relied on Johnson II to affirm a trial court order denying suppression on the ground that the defendant had failed to satisfy her initial burden to prove a minimal factual nexus. At the time of publishing, the Supreme Court has allowed review and will likely overrule that aspect of Johnson II, as it is a relic from an earlier era that imposed a burden on the defendant to establish a minimal factual nexus in all cases in which the defense sought suppression of evidence following an unlawful search or seizure. That general rule was rejected in Unger, after evidence discovered following an unlawful search or seizure is presumed tainted, and the state alone has the burden to establish attenuation, independent source, or inevitable discovery. The subsequent issuance of a warrant speaks only to the lawfulness of a later search or seizure, not whether the warrant or the evidence discovered pursuant to the warrant were connected to a prior illegality. Thus, it should not alter the presumption that evidence discovered after an illegality is tainted unless the state proves otherwise. The court in Yaeger, however, appears to have gone on to reapply—without acknowledging it—the same standard from Gardener that it just announced had been overruled by DeJong. In essence, it excised the illegally obtained evidence, considered the remainder of the evidence against defendant, and determined that the police would have sought and the court would have granted the warrant based on that information. A petition for review challenging that aspect of the court’s analysis is currently pending before the Supreme Court at the time of this writing.


 Attenuation Checklist

1.   Was the stop: (a) an investigatory stop supported by reasonable suspicion, or (b) a traffic stop supported by probable cause?

•       If yes, the state has the burden to prove that the consent was voluntary. If the consent was voluntary, the evidence is admissible. If the consent was not voluntary, the evidence must be suppressed.

2.   What if: (a) the stop was not supported by reasonable suspicion, or (b) the stop was an unlawfully extended or expanded traffic stop, or a traffic stop not supported by probable cause of a traffic violation?

If the stop was not supported by reasonable suspicion, or if the stop was an unsupported or unlawfully extended or expanded traffic stop, then the stop is unlawful. When the stop is unlawful, the state has the burden to prove:

•       any consent to search the person gave was not the product of the illegal stop, and

•       any evidence discovered during or after the illegality was not the product of the stop (regardless of whether the person consented or not).

The state can only demonstrate that consent was not the product of the illegality by demonstrating:

•       the consent was voluntary, and

•       the illegal stop did not lead directly to the officer’s request for consent or affect the person’s decision to consent.

3.   When does an illegal stop lead directly to the consent request and therefore invalidate the consent?

An illegal stop leads directly to and invalidates consent when the police see incriminating evidence as a result of the illegal stop and request consent to search based on what they saw. Unger, 356 Or at 86. For example, say that during a traffic stop unsupported by probable case, the officer sees drug paraphernalia on the passenger floorboard and asks for consent to search based on the drug paraphernalia. Both the paraphernalia and any evidence turned up in the search should be suppressed.

4.   What happens when the illegal stop leads indirectly to consent? That is, what happens when the police (1) conduct an illegal stop, (2) do not directly obtain incriminating evidence, but (3) obtain voluntary consent to search that produces incriminating evidence?

This situation implicates the factors described in Unger. In determining exploitation, courts must consider the totality of the circumstances to determine whether the illegal stop impermissibly affected the defendant’s voluntary consent, paying attention to the following non-exhaustive list of considerations:

(1) The “nature, extent, and severity” of the constitutional violation.

(2) The temporal proximity of the consent to the illegality.

(3) Whether warnings were administered prior to consent.

(4) The presence of significant intervening events.

(5) The “purpose” of the constitutional violation.

(6) The “flagrancy” of the constitutional violation.
