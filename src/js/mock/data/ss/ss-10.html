Search and Seizure Manual

__TOC__

Although Miranda issues are not technically search and seizure issues, they often arise together and involve similar suppression analyses. For that reason, it is imperative that defense counsel be able to spot and litigate any potential Miranda issues that arise during or following a client’s seizure. This chapter explains (1) the circumstances under which the police have an obligation to advise a person of their Miranda rights, (2) ways in which police can violate those rights even after giving the advisement, and (3) how to obtain suppression of evidence derived from such a violation.

I. The Basics

Article I, section 12, of the Oregon Constitution provides that “[n]o person shall be…compelled in any criminal prosecution to testify against himself.” To protect against compelled self-incrimination, police officers “must provide Miranda warnings to a suspect before interrogating that suspect if the suspect is either in ‘full custody’ or in ‘compelling circumstances.’” State v. Alapai, 308 Or App 309, 313, ___P3d___ (2020) (quoting State v. Courville, 276 Or App 672, 677, 368 P3d 838 (2016)).

The warnings must convey to the person that “he or she has a right to remain silent and to consult with counsel and that any statements that the person makes may be used against the person in a criminal prosecution.” State v. Vondehn, 348 Or 462, 474, 236 P3d 691 (2010).

Miranda warnings are not limited to police officers; probation officers must also give such warnings when asking questions during a custodial interrogation. [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=11124495047966059584&q=State+v.+M.S.S.K.,+289+Or+App+450,+&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 State v. M.S.S.K., 289 Or App 450, 410 P3d 393 (2017)] (concluding that probation officer should have given Miranda warnings to youth who was in holding). Once a defendant invokes their right to remain silent and to counsel, police interrogation must immediately end. State v. Boyd, 360 Or 302, 318, 380 P3d 941 (2016).

Additionally, “The state has the burden to prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that any statements by a defendant were voluntarily made.” State v. Cazarez-Hernandez, 280 Or App 312, 314, 381 P3d 969 (2016).

II. Compelling Circumstances

When looking at whether a noncustodial situation is nonetheless compelling, thus requiring Miranda warnings, the court should look at the totality of the circumstances, including: (1) the location of the encounter, (2) the length of the encounter, (3) the amount of pressure exerted on the defendant, and (4) the defendant’s ability to terminate the encounter. State v. Grimm, 290 Or App 173, 414 P3d 435 (2018). These are not an exhaustive list of factors for the court to consider but should serve as guideposts when looking at whether “officers created the sort of police-dominated atmosphere that Miranda warnings were intended to counteract.” Id. at 178.

A. Factors to Consider in Evaluating Whether Defendant Is in Compelling Circumstances

1. Character of the Questions

“Pressure can also come in the form of the specific questions that officers ask or accusations that they make.” State v. Phillips, 302 Or App 618, 626, 459 P3d 909 (2020). “Circumstances do not become compelling simply because a suspect knows that officers have information that he or she may have committed a crime, or because officers suggest that possibility.” Id. at 627. However, the encounter becomes more compelling when officers use that information to explicitly contradict a suspect’s assertions of innocence or engage in police practices that are predicated on assumptions of a suspect’s guilt. Id. Indeed, pressure that escalates over the course of an interview such as “repeatedly confronting a suspect with [the officer’s] knowledge that [the suspect] is lying,” will increase the coercive, and thus compelling nature of the encounter. Id. at 628.

2. Length of Encounter

A two-hour encounter with police was not, by itself, sufficient to make the circumstances compelling. Id. at 625 (2020).

3. Location of Encounter

Typically inviting officers into one’s home is going to “Significantly reduce the likelihood that the circumstances were inherently compelling.” Id. at 623 (2020).

4. Prior Police Activity

A confrontation between a third party and police officers outside the presence of defendant is not likely to put the defendant into compelling circumstances. Id. at 624 (2020).

5. Officers’ Demeanor

An officer’s calm and conversational tone is likely to reduce how compelling an encounter is, while using an aggressive tone, raising their voice, or threatening defendant is likely to make the encounter more compelling. Id. at 626 (2020).

6. Threat to Get a Warrant or to Arrest

Providing a person a choice between cooperating or arresting them places that person in compelling circumstances. State v. Esquivel, 288 Or App 755, 407 P3d 879 (2017).

7. Physical Restraint

Typically, placing a defendant in handcuffs places that person in compelling circumstances. State v. Satchell, 209 Or App 809, 810-11, 150 P3d 4 (2006).

Defendant was in compelling circumstances when officers went “hands on” with defendant during a DUII investigation to prevent defendant from confronting a witness. State v. Beeson, 307 Or App 808, 819, 469 P3d 576 (2020).

2024 Update

As mentioned, whether the circumstances surrounding a defendant’s interrogation are compelling depends on the totality of the circumstances, including, but not limited to, the length of the interrogation, the location of the interrogation, the defendant’s ability to terminate the interrogation, and the pressure exerted on the defendant.  Applying those factors, in State v. Reed, 371 Or 478 (2023), the Oregon Supreme Court held that the defendant was in “compelling circumstances” because she was meeting with her probation officer in a secured building, when two police officers interrupted the meeting and asked both her and her probation officer to stay while they accused her of drug crimes.  Id. 480-81.  The court emphasized that although the duration of the encounter was short, the other factors weighed in favor of the defendant.  Id. 488-89.  First, the interrogation occurred in a “police dominated atmosphere”— a small room in a secured building that served as the place of employment for law enforcement officers.  Id. at 489.  Second, the defendant was not free to leave, she was in the middle of a mandatory meeting with her probation officer, she could not walk through the probation department without an escort, the officers told the probation officer not to excuse her from the meeting, and one officer stood in the doorway.  Id.  Third, the pressure exerted on the defendant was significant as the officers implied that the questioning would be of interest to the probation officer and they asked questions that “assumed her guilt” and accused her of crimes.  Id. at 489-90.  Given that the defendant was subject to probation conditions that required her to obey all laws, answer all reasonable inquiries by her probation officer, and consent to searches at her probation officer’s request if he had reasonable grounds to believe that evidence of a probation violation would be found, the “defendant was in state-created circumstances that carried a significant risk of undermining her ability or willingness to exercise her constitutional rights.”  Id. at 490.

Although it did not explicitly discuss those cases, Reed undermined a line of Court of Appeals decisions suggesting that certain law enforcement buildings (such as probation offices and other police buildings) were not particularly “compelling” under the first Roble-Baker factor.  See, e.g., State v. Revette, 318 Or App 749, 759 (2022) (reasoning that an interview in an unfamiliar police-controlled office building “does not weigh heavily” in favor of compelling circumstances).  Reed emphasized that police-controlled buildings like probation offices contribute heavily toward a finding of compelling circumstances.

B. Cases Where Defendant Was in Compelling Circumstances

In Esquivel, 288 Or App 755, an interview done at defendant’s door was compelling where officers gave the defendant the option of either speaking to them then and getting a citation or having the police get a warrant and then arresting her. Given that choice, even though the interview was done at defendant’s home and only lasted for fifteen minutes, a reasonable person would have felt compelled to answer the officer’s questions.

2024 Update

State v. Reed, 371 Or 478 (2023) (holding that the circumstances were compelling when both legal and practical barriers constrained the defendant’s ability to leave or exercise her constitutional rights: the defendant was in a police-secured building and could not walk around the interior offices unattended, she was participating in a mandatory meeting with her probation officer and subject to probationary conditions that exposed her to liability if she refused to cooperate, and two police officers asked guilt-presumptive questions about her involvement in drug crimes).

C. Cases Where Defendant Was Not in Compelling Circumstances

In the absence of compelling circumstances, even when a person invokes their right to silence and their right to an attorney, subsequent questioning by the police do not render the defendant’s statements involuntary. State v. Anderson, 285 Or App 355, 396 P3d 984 (2017) (holding that because the defendant was not in custody and the circumstances were not compelling, officers could continue to ask questions even after the defendant handed them a card invoking her right to silence and her right to an attorney).

Traffic stops will not generally require Miranda warnings at the onset if the stop is not occurring in a place of confinement, is not overly long for the type of stop, and the officer is not confronting the defendant with circumstantial or testimonial evidence that would alert a defendant that he is under arrest. In State v. Nelson, 285 Or App 345, 397 P3d 536 (2017), Miranda warnings were not required when defendant gave incriminating statements after being stopped in his driveway for a taillight infraction that morphed into a DUII investigation. The officer told the defendant that he was not free to leave but exercised no more restraint than in a routine traffic stop. The stop was not overly long. And when asked if he was arresting the defendant, the officer replied that he had to complete his investigation and thus did not notify defendant that he was under arrest.

Defendant was not in compelling circumstances when officers came to her home and told her that they had information that she was given a firearm from a person arrested earlier in the day in the same trailer park where defendant lived. Philips, 302 Or App at 631. The court evaluated the following circumstances: 1) officers encountered defendant outside of her home; ' 2) the officers did not request to enter defendant’s home, the defendant invited them in; 3) the officer did not immediately accept the defendant’s invitation to enter; 4) once inside, the officer and the defendant continued to engage in “comfortable” conversation and the officer’s request that the defendant be honest with him did not make the circumstances compelling; 5) once other officers arrived, they only commenced a search at the invitation of the defendant; and 6) after the discovery of methamphetamine during the search the further nonconfrontational inquiry of whether there were additional drugs did not make the circumstances compelling. Id. at 632. Because the overall tenor of the encounter was non-confrontational—the defendant appeared to want to cooperate—a reasonable person in the defendant’s position would not feel compelled to respond to the officers’ questions. Id. at 632.'

By contrast, in Grimm, the trial court erred when it found that defendant was not in compelling circumstances when: 1) the questioning was done at a police station, although defendant was there voluntarily, 2) the interview was an hour and a half long, which in itself was not unduly compelling, 3) the officer’s continued questioning escalated the pressure on defendant to continue to talk at every stage until the officer asked the defendant “tell me what really happened,” and 4) although the defendant was informed three times that he was free to go, there was nothing on the record about when that advisement was given and whether they were close in time to when the questioning “intensified and became coercive.” Grimm, 290 Or App at 185. The court noted that the questions “went beyond the mere brief and noncoercive use of incriminating evidence * * *and instead were calculated to contradict defendant’s repeated assertions of innocence and pressure him to continue talking.” As such, defendant’s incriminating statements should have been suppressed. Id. at 186.

III. Equivocal Invocations

When a person unequivocally invokes the right to counsel, the interrogation must immediately cease. But if a person invokes equivocally, then “officers may ask clarifying questions, but those questions must be aimed at clarifying whether the person intended to invoke that specific right.” State v. Dodge, 297 Or App 30, 33, 441 P3d 599 (2019). An equivocal invocation is a “statement or request [that] is subject to more than one reasonable interpretation, one of which is that he or she is invoking the right to counsel.” Id. at 40 (internal quotation marks omitted). That is, a

“statement that appears tenuous or equivocal in isolation may be a sufficient request for counsel when evaluated in the context of all of the circumstances. The question is whether a reasonable officer would have understood at least one plausible meaning of the suspect’s statements or question to be that defendant was invoking the right to counsel.”

Id. (internal quotation marks and citations omitted).

“When confronted with an equivocal invocation, the only permissible question that officers may ask must be a neutral question that clarifies whether the person is invoking their right to counsel. Dodge, 297 Or App at 41. Clarification of any other right is insufficient. Id. at 42. Merely asking if a person wants to keep talking is not sufficient where it “deflect[s] that invocation and risk[s] suggesting to the suspect either that he does not have such a right or that the right will not be honored.” Id.

Note that the duty to clarify an unequivocal invocation does not exist under the Fifth Amendment, but inheres in Article I, section 12. State v. Sanelle, 287 Or App 611, 404 P3d 611 (2017).

A. Unequivocal Invocations

The statement, “[i]t’s not something I want to talk about,” was an unequivocal invocation of defendant’s right to remain silent. State v. Nichols, 361 Or 101, 390 P3d 1001 (2017). Although the statement alone might be ambiguous, because it immediately followed an officer’s question—“do you know about the circumstances of [the decedent’s death]?—it indicated a desires to not talk about any of those circumstances and officers should have terminated their questioning. Nichols, 361 Or 101.

After a defendant stated to officers while in custody, “I appreciate the hospitality here, fellas, but I think I’m going to get a lawyer,” officers made statements designed to provoke additional responses from the defendant. Indeed, the defendant eventually made incriminating statements. Because defendant’s statements showed that he was reluctant to continue without a lawyer, continuing to question him was improper additional interrogation. State v. Wirkkala, 290 Or App 263, 414 P3d 421 (2018).

The statement, “shut your fucking mouth and quit talking to me,” was an unequivocal invocation of the right to silence, and officer’s questioning of the defendant a mere ten minutes later required suppression. State v. Schrepfer, 288 Or App 429, 406 P3d 1098 (2017).

2024 Update

During a custodial interrogation, when asked if he knew his SID number, the defendant said, “I’m not giving you no more information,” “I don’t want to talk to you any more,” and when officers attempted to clarify, defendant said, “I don’t want to talk about anything. Just lock me in the room over there.” The court held that those statements were unequivocal invocations of the defendant’s right to silence. State v. B. Equivocal Invocations

The defendant’s statement, “Sounds like I need a lawyer,” was at least an equivocal invocation that a reasonable officer would understand as asserting a desire for an attorney. Dodge, 297 Or App at 40.

But the officer did not ask clarifying questions, he merely said, “Okay,” and then, after defendant stated “that never happened,” the officer asked, “It goes on from there,” before asking whether the defendant wanted to keep talking. Id. at 41. By not clarifying that the defendant had the right to an attorney, the officer’s statement deflected the topic from the invocation and risked suggesting that the defendant did not have a right to counsel or that it would not be honored. Id. at 42. Consequently, defendant’s post-invocation statements had to be suppressed.

In State v. Rose, 296 Or App 99, 437 P3d 1144 (2019), following the Supreme Court’s decision in State v. Nichols, the Court of Appeals reversed its prior decision in 278 Or App 551 and held that the statement, “I don’t have nothing to say,” was at least an equivocal invocation that required an officer to make further inquiries about whether defendant was invoking. 296 Or App at 108. There, following an hour-long interrogation where the defendant denied being in possession of sexually explicit photographs of children, the statement, “I don’t have nothing to say,” when presented with the photos could have been a register of shock or inability to form a response, but because a reasonable officer could also determine that it was an invocation, further inquiry about invocation was required. Consequently, the statements that were made after the equivocal invocation needed to be suppressed.

The statement, “[w]ell can I, I, I really don’t want say too much I would rather have my lawyer with me but,” was at minimum an equivocal invocation of his right to silence. State v. Hickman, 289 Or App 602, 410 P3d 1102 (2017). The officer interrupted defendant and stated that that was “completely [his] right,” but then continued after six seconds of silence by saying “[a]nd if that’s the way you wanna go with it then that’s the way we play it. We came here to try to get your side of it, though, because we believe that there’s more to it.” The officer’s mere recitation of a defendant’s rights is not sufficient to resolve whether there was an invocation. Id. Furthermore, the officer’s continued interrogation six seconds afterward that led to defendant making incriminating statements was not a change in circumstance or a break in time sufficient to indicate a voluntary waiver. Id. at 608.

When a defendant asks, “Where’s the lawyer?” the officers have a duty to inquire as to whether the defendant is asserting his right to counsel. Sanelle, 287 Or App 611. In Sanelle there was no intervening action between the defendant’s question and the officers’ continued interrogation. The officers told the defendant that he would be appointed an attorney in two days. The officers then repeated whether defendant understood his rights and whether he would continue to talk to them. The officers’ responses clarified that the defendant had a right to a court-appointed attorney, but not that he had the right to have an attorney be present to assist him in the interview. Nor was it sufficient to ask the defendant if he was willing to talk to them without also informing him that he could have an attorney present. Id. at 628.

C. No Invocation At All

The defendant must evince a present desire to have counsel. The statement, “Do I need one?” in response to officers going through the Miranda warnings was not an equivocal invocation for counsel because it did not express a “present desire” to invoke his right to counsel. State v. Roberts, 291 Or App 124, 418 P3d 41 (2018).

In State v. Castillo, 295 Or App 121, 433 P3d 467 (2018), during an interview concerning how a three-month-old baby sustained physical injuries, the defendant stated “I’m tired of these interviews. I want to be with my family.” Id. at 129. In context, the statement was not an invocation of the right to remain silent, but the defendant’s statement to the officers to show that their interpretation of the defendant’s demeanor was incorrect. Immediately after those statements were made, the defendant told the detectives to “bring in a lie detector test,” which demonstrated the defendant’s intent to continue with the interview and not invoke his right to silence. Id. at 131 (ultimately the case was reversed and remanded for the trial court to make factual findings with respect to whether the state had met its burden in proving that the defendant’s waiver of his right against self-incrimination was voluntary).

IV. Interrogation

For the purposes of Article I, section 12, “interrogation refers to statements or questions, other than questions normally attendant to arrest and custody, that are reasonably likely to elicit an incriminating response.” State v. Swan, 363 Or 121, 124, 420 P3d 9 (2018) (internal quotation marks omitted). That is, following an invocation, police can ask “routine booking questions,” as well as direct questions such as if the defendant would like a cup of water, but cannot ask questions that are “likely to elicit some type of incriminating response.” State v. Boyd, 360 Or 302, 380 P3d 941 (2016).

A. Questions Likely to Elicit Incriminating Information

In Boyd, the defendant asked a detective why he had been detained. The detective, who had been present during an earlier conversation when the defendant reacted with disbelief and agitation after being told that his girlfriend was dead, asked if the defendant remembered the prior conversation about his girlfriend’s death. Based on that prompt, the defendant asked to speak to the other detective and made incriminating statements. Because the defendant’s memory was at issue and the detective knew that the defendant became agitated when discussing the death of his girlfriend, the detective should have known that when he inquired about what defendant recalled from the previous conversation, it was likely to lead to incriminating responses. Boyd, 360 Or at 322.

In State v. Schmidtke, 290 Or App 880, 417 P3d 563 (2018), officers were investigating a series of vehicle break-ins. An officer encountered defendant and told him he was being detained and placed him in handcuffs. After defendant was in handcuffs, rather than providing Miranda warnings, the officer told him that the officer was investigating thefts in the area, the defendant’s parole officer wanted to speak to him, and that the defendant was on video in the area of the crime and putting purportedly stolen items in a storage facility. The defendant then made incriminating statements.

The officer’s first statement—that he was detaining the defendant and investigating theft—was routine, informing the defendant that he was detained and the criminal activity that the officer was investigating. But the officer’s second statement—that defendant was spotted on video in the area where the offense occurred—was a statement that went beyond merely stating what the defendant was being charged with. Rather, the officer used the statement to “let the defendant know how and why he had been connected with the crime,” a reasonable officer in that situation would have known that it was likely to elicit an incriminating response. Schmidtke, 290 Or App at 888.

A question about whether a person has “any more” of a controlled substance when that person is in handcuffs is not a question that is “normally attendant to arrest and custody,” and must be preceeded by Miranda warnings because it elicits exactly the type of incriminating information that Miranda seeks to protect against. State v. Lanier, 290 Or App 8, 413 P3d 1020 (2018).

2024 Update

In State v. Gold, 329 Or App 479 (2023), the defendant was on probation for sex crimes and subject to conditions that prohibited him from possessing any device with access to the internet, and he was required to consent to a search upon his probation officer’s reasonable belief that evidence of a probation violation would be found.  Id. at 480.  Police and the defendant’s probation officers were monitoring the defendant’s email addresses and learned that he had been accessing the internet through a Kindle Fire.  Id. at 481.  The officers initially sought the defendant’s consent to search for the device, but he declined, so they arrested him for violating the search-permission condition of his probation.  Id. at 482-84.  The defendant invoked his right to counsel, and while he was waiting to be transported to jail, one of the officers laid out their evidence against him for his mother and explained what would likely happen to her house if they applied for and obtained a warrant.  Id. at 483-86. Upon observing the distress this was causing his mother, the defendant told the police where the Kindle was hidden.  Id. at 488-89.

The court held, “we think the pressure law enforcement put on defendant through its presentation of the evidence against defendant was likely to elicit the incriminating response that it did from defendant—viz., the location where he had hidden the Kindle Fire,” which made it interrogation under Art I, sec 12.  Id. at 495.  Importantly, the court distinguished this case from State v. Hatfield, 246 Or App 736 (2011), where the court held that a request for consent to search is not “interrogation” under Article I, section 12, because giving “consent to search is not an incriminating statement[.]”  Id. at 492.  According to the Gold court, even though the officer’s conduct was aimed at getting the defendant’s consent to search for the Kindle, it “was not a mere request for consent to search.”  Id. at 492 n 7.  Rather, law enforcement conveyed to the defendant that they wanted him to disclose the “location of the Kindle Fire.”  Id. at 495. 

B. Questions Unlikely to Elicit Incriminating Information

As previously mentioned, an officer can ask “booking questions” even after a person has invoked their right to counsel. In State v. Fink, 285 Or App 302, 395 P3d 934 (2017), the defendant was arrested for DUII. She was read Miranda warnings and indicated that she understood them. The defendant asserted her right to silence, but the officer continued to ask biographical questions—eye color and the defendant’s apartment number. The officer then asked the defendant if she wanted the temperature in the patrol car turned town. The defendant responded with incriminating statements. Because the questions the officer asked after the defendant’s invocation but prior to her statements were routine booking questions, given close-in-time to the Miranda warnings, defendant understood the purpose of Miranda, the officer was polite and professional, the questions were not likely to elicit incriminating information.

Booking questions can extend to questions about a person’s medical condition, even if those questions might lead to an incriminating response, so long as the question is not designed to elicit such a response—that is, the officer cannot intend to extract incriminating information; thus, the question must not evince an investigatory purpose. State v. Montiel-Delvalle, 304 Or App 699, 713, 468 P3d 995 (2020). In that case, the arresting officer asked the defendant standard medical questions followed by more specific questions about medical aid once the defendant told him that he was hurt. The officer’s questions relating to “why” the defendant was injured were, in context, not designed to illicit incriminating information but to provide medical care while the defendant was in custody.

Practice Tip

Some courts, including the Oregon Court of Appeals, have held that asking a defendant to perform field sobriety tests and to take a breath test do not amount to interrogation, because each question is “normally attendant to arrest and custody.” See Swan, 363 Or at 124-25 (collecting Oregon cases); South Dakota v. Neville, 459 US 553, 564 n 15, 103 S Ct 916, 74 L Ed 2d 748 (1983); Pennsylvania v. Muniz, 496 US 582, 110 S Ct 2638, 110 L Ed 2d 528 (1990). In Muniz, for example, the Court held that the Fifth Amendment did not preclude officers from asking the defendant to perform field sobriety tests, because the questions and instructions “were necessarily ‘attendant to’” the state’s DUII intake procedure. 496 US at 604-05.

However, that reasoning should not survive scrutiny under the Oregon Constitution, which has long held that neither the legislature nor the police can circumvent the constitution by creating rules or procedures designed to generate evidence for use in a criminal prosecution. State v. Connally, 339 Or 583, 587, 125 P3d 1254 (2005); State v. Soriano, 68 Or App 642, 662, 684 P2d 1220 (1984), aff’d and opinion adopted, 298 Or 392 (1984).

Thus, holding that it is not interrogation to ask a defendant to perform field sobriety tests or take a breath test because it is attendant to a standardized process for evidence gathering in DUII cases would be contrary to those principles and effectively create a DUII exception to Article I, section 12.

Moreover, advocates should continue to press the issue, given that a “no” response may be admissible as evidence of a guilt if the request could “reasonably be understood only as a request to provide physical cooperation and not as a request for constitutionally-significant consent to search.” State v. Banks, 364 Or 332, 342, 434 P3d 361 (2019). Upon meeting that burden, the state could presumably still ask a jury “to infer from the fact of the defendant’s refusal that he or she is saying, ‘I refuse to perform field sobriety tests because I believe I will fail them.’”

Additionally, a “yes” response discloses the defendant’s state of mind by revealing his or her judgment about which form of evidence—a refusal or the test results—will be the least damaging at his or her ensuing DUII trial. 

Lastly, a request to take field sobriety tests is interrogation, notwithstanding that field sobriety tests primarily involve the production of physical evidence. '''' First, field sobriety tests also require testimonial responses in terms of demonstrating an ability to follow instructions, display memory and comprehension, and communicate those abilities to the officer through specific actions. Second, Article I, section 12, directly prohibits the state from requiring a defendant to producephysical evidenceas well as testimonial evidence, including evidence of his performance on the field sobriety tests and the breath-test evidence. Indeed, inSorianothe Supreme Court, through the adoption of the Court of Appeals’ decision, “also agreed that the word ‘testify’ is not a limit on the protections that Article I, section 12, affords: ‘We see no reason to construe the Oregon Constitution to give protection from testifying but not from furnishing evidence.’” Vondehn, 348 Or at 469 (quotingSoriano'', 68 Or App at 646-57 n 4). 

V. Suppressing Evidence Obtained from a Prior Miranda Violation

When there is an Article I, section 12, violation, the defendant’s testimonial statements must obviously be suppressed. But so too must nontestimonial statements (like consent to search) and—critically—physical evidence subsequently discovered (like blood-alcohol content, controlled substances, etc.).

In Swan, 363 Or 121, the defendant was arrested for DUII and advised of his Miranda rights. Swan, 363 Or at 125. The defendant invoked his right to counsel. Id. at 126. The officer transported the defendant to the police station and provided the defendant with an opportunity to consult with counsel. Id. The officer took the defendant to the Intoxilyzer room and, during the 15-minute observation period, asked the defendant 28 DUII interview questions. Id. The officer read the defendant the implied consent warnings and asked the defendant to consent to a breath test. Id. 126-27. The defendant again invoked his right to counsel, and the officer said he had already had that opportunity. Id. at 127. The defendant consented to the breath test and blew a 0.18. Id.

At trial, the state conceded that the officer had violated the defendant’s Article I, section 12, right to counsel by continuing to interrogate the defendant with the DUII interview questions after defendant had unequivocally invoked his right to counsel. Id. at 127-28. The state argued that it simply would not use those statements at trial. Id. The defense responded that those statements and any evidence that derived from that violation must be suppressed. The trial court denied the motion, and the Court of Appeals affirmed.

The Oregon Supreme Court agreed with the defendant and reversed. The state was right to concede that the police violated the defendant’s Article I, section 12, rights. When a defendant is in custody and invokes his right to counsel, police must cease all interrogation or provide a defendant with an attorney—not just an opportunity to consult with counsel. (Under the case law, a reasonable opportunity to consult counsel satisfies a defendant’s Article I, section 11, right to counsel in DUII cases, but not Article,
section 12.)

The court applied the five-factor test from State v. Jarnagin, 351 Or App 703, 277 P3d 535 (2012), to address whether the defendant’s decision to consent to a breath test and the results of his blood test derived from the earlier constitutional violation. Those factors are:

“(1) the nature of the violation; (2) the amount of time between the violation and any later statements; (3) whether the suspect remained in custody before making any later statements; (4) subsequent events that may have dissipated the taint of the earlier violation; and (5) the use that the state has made of the unwarned statements.”

Before diving into the court’s application of the factors, it is important to remember the state bears both the burden of production and persuasion to show that the defendant’s decision to take the breath test was not the product of the Article I, section 12, '' violation.Id.'' at 133.

The court analyzed the factors out of order, starting with the second and third factors—the amount of time between the violation and the defendant’s consent to take the breath test and whether he remained in custody before consenting. Those factors weighed in favor of suppression because no time elapsed between the questioning done in violation of the defendant’s right to counsel and the officer asking the defendant to consent to a breath test. Id. at 132-33. “The latter followed immediately on the former.” Id. at 132. Additionally, the defendant remained in custody throughout that time. Therefore, “there was no break in time, place, or custody between the officer’s repeated Article I, section 12, violation and defendant’s decision to take the breath test that might have attenuated the effect of the violation.” Instead, this was a case where the defendant’s decision to take the breath test and the prior illegality “blended into a continuum.” Id.

Turning to the fifth factor, the court focused heavily on the fact that the state had failed to meet its burden. The record did not include the 28 questions that the officer had asked, and “more importantly,” the record did not include the defendant’s answers to those questions. “Without that information, there is no way of knowing whether the answers that the officer elicited from defendant in violation of Article I, section 12, affected defendant’s decision to consent.” Id.[[#_ftn1|[1]]]

Still analyzing the fifth factor, the court rejected the state’s argument that the officer had probable cause to arrest the defendant for DUII, so he would have asked the defendant for consent to take the breath test regardless of the earlier violation. The court explained that even if it did not affect the officer’s decision to ask for consent, the question is, did the violation affect the defendant’s decision to take the test? Without knowing the 28 DUII interview questions and the defendant’s responses, the court “had no way of determining that defendant’s decision did not derive from the immediately preceding violation of his Article I, section 12, right to counsel.” Id. at 133.

Next, the court analyzed the first factor—the nature of the violation. The court determined that the violation in Swan was different than violations in other cases where the officers had merely failed to recognize that borderline circumstances were compelling and that Miranda was required. In Swan, the court explained that the officer had just told the defendant he had a right to have an attorney with him during questioning, and, after advising him of that right, the defendant expressly invoked counsel’s assistance in responding to police questioning. Nevertheless, outside the presence of an attorney, the officer asked the defendant 28 questions. That was a repeated and flagrant violation.

The court acknowledged that the officer had allowed the defendant the opportunity to consult with a lawyer before questioning him and that had mitigated the effect of the violation. But ultimately the officer’s repeated disregard for the defendant’s expressed right to counsel placed the defendant in a more disadvantaged position, making it easier and more likely for the officer to exploit that illegality to obtain consent. Id. at 134.

The final factor analyzed, factor four, was whether there were any subsequent events that dissipated the taint of the earlier violation. The court concluded there were none. The court rejected the state’s argument that he had an opportunity to consult with counsel because that opportunity occurred prior to the violation. Moreover, the defendant had requested counsel again after the officer had requested consent to take the breath test, and the officer denied his request. “Far from dissipating the taint of the preceding Article I, section 12, violation, the officer’s subsequent denial reinforced it.” Id. at 135.

The court also rejected the state’s argument that the implied consent warnings dissipated the taint of the violation. The court first noted that the state had a record problem because it had not offered the report into evidence. The court then analyzed the state’s argument based on the officer’s testimony that he had warned the defendant of a few of the specific consequences, including that refusal would be used against the defendant in court. Id. The court explained that the implied consent warnings are designed to tilt a defendant’s decision toward taking the test, and there was nothing in the record to show that the officer had “reminded the defendant that he was free not to take the breath test.” Id. (emphasis added).

Thus, the court concluded that the state failed to prove that the defendant’s decision to take the breath test did not derive from the earlier and repeated violations of the defendant’s Article I, section 12, right to counsel.[[#_ftn2|[2]]] Consequently, the trial court erred by denying defendant’s motion to suppress the results of the breath test. See also State v. Taylor, 296 Or App 278, 438 P3d 419 (2019) (applying the five-factor test and holding that the defendant’s breath test results derived from a prior Miranda violation and should have been suppressed).

In State v. Joaquin the statement, “Can we wait for an attorney to be here,” was at minimum an equivocal invocation of counsel; detectives should only have confirmed whether he did or did not want an attorney. 307 Or App 314, 322 (2020). Repeating a defendant’s rights merely to keep the defendant talking is not sufficient. Id. The defendant subsequently confessed to killing his girlfriend and consented to the search of his apartment, having photographs taken of him, an inspection of the contents of his phone, and the taking of DNA swabs.

The defendant’s consent was obtained late in the interrogation when defendant had already made inculpatory statements regarding the crime, his phone, marks on his body, fingernail scrapings, and DNA. And “[t]here was no break in time, place, or custody between the violation of defendant’s right to counsel and his decision to consent.” Id. at 326. Thus, like Swan, that physical evidence was derivative of the Miranda violation and had to be suppressed.

In State v. Heise-Fay, 274 Or App 196, 212, 360 P3d 615 (2015) the police also used the defendant’s unlawfully obtained statements in obtaining the defendant’s consent to search. 274 Or App at 210. There, the police had placed the defendant in compelling circumstances when they surrounded her home in search of a felon and convinced her to cooperate in order to avoid going to jail. Id. at 198-99. The officer seized on that unlawful interaction later in the encounter to obtain her consent to search the home. He assured her that he had no intention of taking her to jail. He asked her if she was going to continue to be “honest and cooperative” and if she would consent to a search of her residence. Id. at 210. The defendant said she would. Id. The court explained that the officer’s “coercive approach from earlier in the encounter would have suggested to a reasonable person that he would not take defendant to jail if she was honest and cooperative—implying that her cooperation included consenting to the search of the residence.” Id. As such, the earlier, coercive encounter tainted the defendant’s decision to consent to search. Id. And as explained above, the state presented no evidence of attenuation. Id.

2024 Update

In State v. Williams, 320 Or App 705, 514 P3d 501 (2022), the defendant was arrested for failing to register as a sex offender, and officers sought a DNA sample from the defendant to connect him to a cold-case rape kit from 2011. Officers questioned the defendant in custody without Miranda for nine minutes, with a focus on his failure to register. When asked if he knew his SID, the defendant said, “I’m not giving you no more information,” and “I don’t want to talk to you anymore.” Id. at 712. When officers attempted to clarify, defendant said, “I don’t want to talk about anything. Just lock me in the room over there.” Id. After some back and forth – where the officers tried to question the defendant about the 2011 case, he again invoked, and the officers tried to persuade him to speak and took him to a holding cell for a few minutes, the defendant agreed to give DNA samples.

The court held that the DNA samples “derived from” the Miranda violation and should have been suppressed. Under the Jarnagin factors, the nature of the violation was flagrant, the four-minute period in the holding cell did not meaningfully attenuate his consent, the defendant remained continually in custody, and although defendant consented, that consent did not dissipate the taint because the detective’s responses to the defendant’s questions about the DNA sample were misleading. Id. at 715-22.

VI. Derivative Evidence Obtained after Belated Miranda Warnings

If police initially fail to inform a defendant of their Miranda rights but later correct themselves and deliver the warnings, any “post-Miranda statements are only admissible if the state establishes that the police ‘effectively and accurately informed the defendant of his or her Article I, section 12, rights.’” State v. Edgar, 297 Or App 193, 198, 441 P3d 234 (2019) (quoting State v. Heise-Fay, 274 Or App 196, 211, 360 P3d 615 (2015). By questioning first and warning later, the demonstration of police authority and violation of the defendant’s constitutional rights “communicate to a defendant that he or she has no real choice to remain silent.” Edgar, 297 Or App at 198. Consequently, a trial court cannot assume that the recitation of the Miranda warnings served “the intended informative function.” Vondehn, 348 Or at 481.

To determine whether belated warnings are effective, the court should examine “all relevant circumstances” with reference to the Jarnagin factors. State v. Alapai, 308 Or App 309, 315, P3d (2020)

When the warned and unwarned questioning blend into “one continuum,” the belated warnings are unlikely to purge the taint of the unwarned questioning; conversely, when the warned questioning represents a “markedly different experience from the unwarned questioning,” they are more likely to have attenuated the taint. Edgar, 297 Or App at 199-200 (internal quotations marks and citations omitted).

A. Belated Warnings Effective

In Alapai, 308 Or App 309, the trial court suppressed unwarned statements made to an officer who was investigating the defendant for hit-and-run. But once a second officer arrived and told the defendant that he did not know what the defendant and the first officer spoke about, the defendant, unprompted, stated that he had run from the scene of an accident. The second officer, who did not know that the first officer had not given the defendant the Miranda warnings, gave the warnings. After the defendant stated that he understood his rights, the officer questioned him about his alcohol consumption and then asked him to submit to field sobriety tests. The defendant failed those tests. Because the prior violation took place in front of the defendant’s home, in a “barely custodial” setting, and the first officer suspended his questioning of the defendant while waiting for the second officer, whose arrival terminated the small talk between the defendant and the first officer, these facts combined with the second officer’s statement that he did not know what had been discussed prior to his arrival, attenuated the taint of the defendant’s prior unwarned statement.

In State v. Beeson, 307 Or App 808, 827, 479 P3d 576 (2020), the court affirmed the trial court’s denial of a motion to suppress a post-Miranda-violation breath test. There, the nature of the violation was not particularly flagrant, the officers failed to Mirandize the defendant after they had restrained him during a DUII investigation when he suddenly moved to confront a witness who had unexpectedly arrived at the scene (at which point he should have been advised of his Miranda rights). Id. at 824. After the defendant calmed down, he proceeded to perform field sobriety tests (FSTs) that he had previously agreed to perform at the outset of the stop. After the FSTs, the defendant was arrested for DUII. The officer began to question the defendant, who responded that he had nothing to say. Another officer advised the defendant of his Miranda rights and confirmed that he understood them. The defendant was taken to jail where he consented to a breath test about 35-40 minutes after the initial Miranda violation.

On reconsideration, the court concluded that two factors purged the taint of the initial violation: (1) an officer read the defendant his Miranda warnings prior to the breath test, and (2) the defendant was transported to another location, the police station. Id. at 825 (on reconsideration modified and adhered to as modified). The court also did not regard the Miranda violation as particularly flagrant or egregious because it was a quintessential example of a change in circumstances that an officer could reasonably fail to recognize necessitated Miranda warnings.

Finally, the court concluded that the state did not make use of any unwarned statements. In reaching that conclusion, the court treated the Miranda violation as occurring after the defendant had already agreed to perform the FSTs. Id. at 826. For those reasons, the court held that the defendant’s consent to the breath test was attenuated from the Miranda violation.

B. Belated Warnings Ineffective

In State v. Mast, 301 Or App 809, 459 P3d 938 (2020), belated Miranda warnings were not sufficient to purge the taint from the defendant’s statements, sobriety tests, or breath test. There, officers located a crashed truck, which they determined was the defendant’s. Upon meeting officers at the crash site, the defendant appeared intoxicated. An officer confronted him with his belief that he had crashed the truck while intoxicated. At that point, the defendant should have been—but was not—advised of his Miranda rights. The defendant admitted to driving while intoxicated. A second officer took over the investigation and read the defendant the Miranda warnings before administering field sobriety tests and transporting the defendant to the jail for a breath test. The belated Miranda warnings did not attenuate the defendant’s unwarned statements from the warned statements. The statements that the defendant made to each officer were largely the same. Questioning by the second officer was not a substantial break because the second officer was present throughout the encounter. Moreover, the second officer had referenced the defendant’s unwarned statements, further erasing any break between the two officers’ conduct. The two rounds of interrogation took place at the same time and place. Finally, the officer did not tell the defendant that his unwarned statements could not be used against him in a future prosecution, which although not required would have given defendant a more “meaningful” choice to waive his Article I, section 12, rights.

The court also held that even though the field sobriety tests and breath tests were done at different places and times, because they were the result of a continuous encounter those factors contributed little. As such, despite the belated Miranda '' warnings the results of the field sobriety tests and breath test also had to be suppressed.See alsoEdgar, 297 Or App at 203  (holding that because there was no significant change in circumstances between the warned and unwarned statements, the belatedMiranda'' warnings did not purge the taint of the unwarned statements).

C. Defendant Initiates Further Conversation After Invoking (Voluntary Waiver)

Only the defendant can waive Miranda protections once he has invoked those rights. State v. Gillespie, 295 Or App 702, 711, 436 P3d 65 (2019). “To demonstrate that a suspect has waived a prior invocation of the right to counsel, the state must show not only that the suspect initiated [further conversation] with the police in a way that ‘evinced a willingness and a desire for a generalized discussion about the investigation’ but also that the suspect’s waiver was knowing and voluntary under the totality of the circumstances.” State v. Fink, 285 Or App 302, 312, 395 P3d 934 (2017) (quoting State v. Meade, 327 Or 335, 341, 963 P2d 656 (1998) (“[W]e hold that defendant, without prompting from the police, initiated further conversation that evinced a willingness and a desire for a generalized discussion about the investigation. Defendant’s physical gestures cut off further questions by the officers. Having asserted control over the conversation, he then chose to reopen the topic of the investigation.”)).

Once a court makes that determination, it must then decide whether the defendant’s waiver was voluntary, knowing, and intelligent and, thus, valid. That test, under both the state and federal constitutions, “is whether, under the totality of the circumstances, it is apparent that the ‘defendant’s will was not overborne and his capacity for self-determination was not critically impaired.’” Gillespie, 295 Or App at 711-12 (quoting State v. Acremant, 338 Or 302, 324, 108 P3d 1139 (2005)).

In State v. Gillespie, the court held that the defendant’s post-invocation waiver was voluntary and that his subsequent incriminating statements were admissible. There, two detectives intended to interview the defendant concerning a fatal stabbing. The defendant was being held in the Clark County Jail in Washington. After the detectives explained the defendant’s rights, the defendant asked if they could talk with the video recorder off. The detectives declined to do so, and the defendant invoked. The detectives then asked the defendant if he had an attorney. The defendant told them he did not. The detectives told the defendant he would be charged with murder and extradited back to Oregon and that they could not proceed to talk to him with the video off because he had just invoked. The detectives then told the defendant that the interrogation was over and that they would leave. Before they could do so, however, the defendant began asking questions about extradition, whether he faced federal charges, and whether his girlfriend knew about the charges. The detectives reiterated that they could not talk further because he had invoked. The defendant told them that he did not want his girlfriend involved and he turned toward the camera and said: “I do not want an attorney any longer. I am willing to talk to you guys.” The detectives told the defendant that he could reinvoke at any time. The defendant then made incriminatory statements.

The defendant voluntarily waived his Miranda rights. The record reflects that the defendant was aware of the nature of his rights and the detectives did not induce or overbear defendant’s ability to remain silent. The detectives: (1) refused to turn off the recording device in light of the defendant’s invocation; (2) acknowledged the defendant’s invocation immediately when it was made; (3) tried to terminate the encounter; (4) explained to the defendant that his invocation required them to terminate the encounter; and (5) made a “clear and explicit explanation to defendant that he needed to be very clear that he no longer wanted an attorney if he wanted to continue the interview” and (6) informed him that he continued to have the power to reinvoke his right to counsel. Gillispie, 295 Or App at 716. As such, the incriminating statements were not the product of an unlawful continued interrogation after the defendant had invoked.

VII. Defendant’s Post-Miranda Statements Must Be Knowing and Voluntary

“The provision of Miranda warnings is not a guarantee that statements made after the warnings are voluntary.” State v. Jackson, 364 Or 1, 21, 430 P3d 1067, 1078 (2018). By statute, “A confession or admission of a defendant, whether in the course of judicial proceedings or otherwise, cannot be given in evidence against the defendant when it was made under the influence of fear produced by threats.” ORS 136.425(1). The state must prove that in making a statement, “defendant’s free will was not overborne and his capacity for self-determination was not critically impaired, and that he made his statements without inducement from fear or promises.” Id. at 22. The defendant must also have the requisite comprehension to knowingly and intelligently waive his Miranda rights. State v. Norgren, 287 Or App 165, 170, 401 P3d 1275 (2017), rev dismissed, 363 Or 40 (2018) (holding that it was error for trial court to allow incriminating statements made after Miranda warnings were given, but where the defendant was clearly having a break from reality by running around in the woods, naked, and believing that he was a sasquatch). Statements made in that condition were not knowing or intelligent. Id. The state bears a “heavy” burden to prove that under the totality of the circumstances defendant’s waiver was knowingly, voluntarily, and intelligently made. State v. Ward, 367 Or 188, 200, 367 P3d 420 (2020). To overcome the presumption that statements post-Miranda were not voluntary, the state must offer “clear evidence” that, at the time of the later confession, “the delusion hopes or fears, under the influence of which the original confession was obtained, were entirely dispelled.” Jackson, 364 Or at 33.

The passing of time can ease the state’s burden to show that a defendant voluntarily waived his Miranda rights following the provision of the warnings. Id. at 204. But any value that accrues to the passage of time is diluted if a defendant is held in jail without a lawyer or a significant amount of time passes between the warnings and the questioning. Id. at 205. The state should also elicit evidence of what the defendant said before the warnings were given to determine whether any initial Miranda violation impacted the defendant’s later statements. Id. at 206.

Jackson established a framework to determine whether the state had met its burden to prove voluntariness. First, it “is helpful to begin with the issue of whether the officers who interrogated the defendant induced him to make admissions by influence of hope or fear.” Id. at 22. If the answer is “yes,” then “the next question is whether the state has demonstrated that, in view of the totality of circumstances, the defendant’s admissions were nonetheless the product of the defendant's free will. State v. Vasquez-Santiago, 301 Or App 90, 107, 456 P3d 270, 280 (2019) (citing Jackson, 301 Or App at 27-28). “The ‘totality of the circumstances’ subject to examination includes the individual characteristics and circumstances of the defendant, in addition to the circumstances of the interrogation.” Id. (quoting Jackson, 301 Or App at 28).

A. Defendant’s Post-Miranda Statements Were Not Knowing and Voluntary

In Jackson, the defendant was accused of multiple murders. During a multi-day custodial interrogation, the defendant made inculpatory statements following the giving of the Miranda warnings. The trial court suppressed two sets of statements and the state filed an interlocutory appeal. For the first set of statements, the state induced the defendant into telling them what they wanted to hear by isolating the defendant—who suffered from severe disabilities—from his care provider, removed his cell phone, did not permit him to make any phone calls despite his request to do so, and continued to question him “for a significant length of time and told him that they would continue to do so until they were ‘working together.’” Id. at 31-32. Only after that would the detectives permit the defendant to speak with his family. Id. Moreover, they told the defendant that if he confessed, it might eliminate him as a suspect from other crimes. But if he did not confess, then the detectives would pursue the maximum possible sentence. Under the totality of the circumstances, the defendant’s statement following those inducements was not voluntary.

With respect to the second statement, which was made on the second day of interrogation after the defendant had been re-Mirandized when the defendant was allowed to call his sister from the interrogation room, the call to his family member did not alleviate the prior inducement, it was “part of the inducement of the earlier admissions, and, ultimately, was carried out in the interrogation room, with the interrogating detectives placing, listening in on, and participating in the call.” Id. at 35. The defendant had not been released from custody between the first and second statements. No time separated the interrogation from the defendant’s telephone conversation. Thus, the improper coercion that had led to the defendant’s compelled admissions during the interrogation were not dispelled by the time of the telephone call and the trial court was correct to suppress those statements. Id.

In Ward, 367 Or 188, the defendant was arrested in Union County for a murder that took place in Washington County. The defendant was read the Miranda '' warnings when he was initially arrested and when he was brought to the Union County Jail. Despite the defendant telling the investigating officers that he was not going to speak with them, the officers rereadMiranda'' and then continued to interrogate the defendant.

After being held in the Union County Jail for four days, the defendant was transported to the Washington County Jail. Before departing the defendant was read the Miranda warnings, but made no statements on the four- to five-hour drive to Washington County. When the defendant arrived in Washington County, he was taken to a soft interview room. At that point, without rereading the Miranda warnings or asking the defendant if he wanted to waive his Miranda rights, the detectives began asking the defendant about the murder. The conversation appeared relaxed and the defendant made incriminating statements. The trial court ruled that the first interrogation had violated the defendant’s rights by asking questions after an unequivocal invocation. However, it admitted statements made after the second interview in Washington County.

The Court of Appeals held that state did not carry its burden to prove that the statements made in the second interview were voluntarily made. First, “any value that the passage of time might normally provide was diluted in this case by the fact that defendant was held in jail during that entire time without the benefit of advice from counsel.” Id. at 205. Thus, being held in custody for four days in between the first and second interrogation did not “minimize the harm from the earlier violation.” Id. Second, any value from the renewed Miranda warnings was undermined because those warnings were given at the beginning of a five-hour trip to Washington County. Third, the state impaired its ability to prove that the results of the first interrogation did not impact the defendant’s decision to make statements in the second interview by only playing a recording of the interview following the defendant’s invocation. Id. at 206. Consequently, there was no way to know whether the statements that the defendant made or the questions that officer posed during the first interview affected the defendant’s decision to participate in the second interview. Id. at 206-07. Taking all these circumstances together, the state failed to prove that defendant knowingly, intelligently, and voluntarily waived his Article I, section 12, rights. Id. at 208.

Vasquez-Santiago, 301 Or App 90, applied the Jackson framework to an interrogation of a defendant with an IQ of 53 who was accused of murder. 301 Or App at 92-105. There, detectives improperly induced a confession by implicitly, if not explicitly, stating that the defendant’s infant son and other family members were detained by police and were suffering and that only the defendant’s confession would secure their release. Id. at 118. Those interrogation techniques played on a parent’s instinct to care for their child. Id. As such, the state did not prove that the techniques did not “critically impair” the defendant’s free will by inducing a statement out of the defendant’s fear for his family and an implicit promise that the detectives could do something about freeing them. Id.

The state failed to carry its heavy burden to show that the defendant knowingly, intelligently and voluntarily waived his right to counsel after he had invoked his right not to speak without an attorney present. State v. Dean, 309 Or App 249, 261 (2021). There, the defendant was arrested and questioned by detectives about several robberies. After invoking, a detective continued to explain to the defendant the incriminating evidence against him. Id. at 258-59. The defendant sought to interrupt the detective several times but was stopped by the detective who continued to lay out the evidence after explaining to the defendant, multiple times, that he would not accept any of the defendant’s statements because the defendant had invoked his right to counsel. Id. at 259. After an hour break, the defendant signaled the detectives that he wanted to talk and gave incriminating statements. Id. at 254. The state did not meet its burden for several reasons. First, the detective continued to explain the evidence even though it was provoking statements from the defendant while simultaneously preventing the defendant from responding. Id. at 263. Second, the detective “primed” the defendant to respond to him by laying out the evidence and then leaving the defendant in a cell for an hour to consider the evidence against him. Id. at 263. Third, the “break” did little to attenuate the violation when the defendant was in contact with the same detectives throughout the break. Id. at 264. Fourth, simply restating the defendant’s rights did not further clarify them when the detective had violated those very same rights prior. Id. at 265.

In State v. Simmons, 302 Or App 133, 138 (2020), a detective improperly induced the defendant into making statements by telling the defendant that “if the defendant was willing to talk with him that maybe something could be done. Maybe he could talk with the DA’s Office, maybe he’d dismiss the charges.” Those statements communicated to defendant that there would be a benefit in continuing to talk to the detective. That is, the detective’s statements gave the defendant hope that by continuing to talk, defendant would receive a more lenient result. Because the detective’s statements gave the defendant hope for a more lenient result, they induced statements contrary to the Miranda warnings and should have been suppressed. See also State v. Belle, 281 Or App 208, 211, 216 (2016) (holding a detective’s promise to a suspect, who was a member of the National Guard, that a matter could be handled at the state level and not under the military code, was the type of promise of a benefit prohibited by ORS 136.425(1)).

B. Defendant’s Post-Miranda Statements Were Knowing and Voluntary

The defendant’s statements were not induced or overborne when made during a five-hour-long interview: the defendant was given regular breaks at his request, the officers did not accompany the defendant on his breaks except at his request, the defendant drove himself to the police station, Miranda warnings were read to the defendant, the defendant chose when to answer questions and when not to, the tone of the interview was direct but quiet and “decent,” and even though an officer made a misrepresentation, it was not a misrepresentation that suggested to the defendant that it would be beneficial for him to do or say a particular thing. State v. Pryor, 309 Or App 12, 21-25, ___P3d___ (2021). Furthermore, although the investigating detective made comments about how the defendant would be perceived as a monster if he did not confess, the detective did not offer anything in exchange for the defendant’s confession. Id. at 20.

In State v. Chavez-Meza, 301 Or App 373, 456 P3d 322 (2019), the state proved that defendant’s statements were voluntary and not overborne when the interviewing detectives, although minimizing the defendant’s conduct, did not promise the defendant that he would not be prosecuted, there was no implied promise that if the defendant did not confess that his wife would be alerted to the allegations of sexually abusing a child. Moreover, when the defendant ultimately did provide incriminating statements, it was during a fact-intensive part of the interview, long after Miranda warnings were given, and not when the officers were exerting the most pressure, and it appeared that the defendant made the statements to unburden himself. Id. at 392.

In State v. Bush, 291 Or App 407, 421 P3d 403 (2018), the defendant’s waiver was knowing despite the fact that he was visibly under the influence of a controlled substance. The interviewing officer initially provided the Miranda warnings, the defendant asked appropriate questions and sought clarification for words he did not understand, and he appeared to become more coherent overtime, including when he ultimately waived his right to remain silent two and a half hours after he was arrested. Consequently, the defendant’s intoxication did not preclude a knowing waiver.

VI. Harmlessness

Permitting the state to introduce an unmirandized statement can be harmless on appeal under Article I, section 12, of the Oregon Constitution where the statement is cumulative of other uncontested evidence. State v. Forshee, 300 Or App 739, 758 (2019). In Forshee, a jury found defendant guilty of murder. Id. The defendant stipulated that he had shot and killed the victim, but argued that he had done so under the influence of an extreme emotional disturbance (EED). The question was whether the trial court erred by permitting the state to introduce the defendant’s statement, “I shot the guy,” after he was ordered to the ground by a police officer and before being read Miranda warnings. Id. at 757. The Court of Appeal did not address whether Miranda was required because it found that if there was error, it was harmless. Id. at 765-66. The court concluded that whatever relevance the statement had to undermining his EED defense was merely cumulative of other properly admitted evidence, such as his planning leading up to the shooting (scheduling a cab to take him to the scene of the shooting and getting a neighbor to look after his dogs), the defendant’s correction of the 911 caller misidentifying the building that the shooting had occurred in, other unchallenged statements made to police indicating that the defendant had as much as he could take with the victim, and expert testimony that the defendant’s calm demeanor and planning were not signs of EED. Thus, any error was harmless because the challenged statement did not add much if anything to the other evidence, which showed the defendant’s planning and ability to respond appropriately even after the shooting. Id. at 765-66.
