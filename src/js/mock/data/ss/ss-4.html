I. Introduction

Most Oregon counties, cities, and towns have enacted policies that allow law enforcement agencies to inventory for safekeeping any property that the agency impounds or otherwise takes into administrative custody. In practice, the inventory arises most commonly in connection with law enforcement impound of a client’s car or the storage of a client’s belongings when the client is booked into jail. The purpose of the inventory is not to investigate possible criminal activity, but to protect property and to protect law enforcement from claims that property in their custody was mishandled or stolen.

Police frequently find contraband during inventories. The Oregon Supreme Court has held that admission of evidence discovered during a properly authorized and conducted non-investigatory inventory of property does not violate Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution. State v. Atkinson, 298 Or 1, 4-6, 688 P2d 832 (1984); State v. Keller, 265 Or 622, 629, 510 P2d 568 (1973). When “responsible policy makers” promulgate an inventory policy for protective reasons, “such a policy is not inherently ‘unreasonable’ within the meaning of Article I, section 9.” Atkinson, 298 Or at 8. The properly authorized and conducted inventory is therefore a reasonable intrusion. Id.

This is true whether the inventory is of an impounded automobile, Atkinson 298 Or 1, impounded personal property of a person in police custody, State v. Perry, 298 Or 21, 688 P2d 827 (1984), or property in any type of “administrative”—i.e., non-investigative—custody. Atkinson, 298 Or at 8.

The government may inventory personal property to safeguard the property it takes into administrative custody and to insulate itself against claims that it has mishandled or misappropriated it. Atkinson, 298 Or at 8. In that context, “there is no search for predictable ‘things to be seized’ that could be ‘particularly described,’ as the warrant requirement of Article I, section 9, contemplates.” Atkinson, 298 Or at 8 (emphasis added). Such limited, purely administrative cataloging of a person’s property for safekeeping is reasonable and therefore does not offend Article I, section 9, so long as it complies with Atkinson’s requirements.


Practice Tip

Notwithstanding Atkinson and its progeny, counsel should consider challenging the admission of evidence discovered during any inventory, even if the police did everything “right” under Atkinson. It makes practical sense that the police are authorized to impound and inventory property pursuant to certain policies and procedures, but beyond merely stating that such procedures are reasonable, no Oregon case satisfactorily addresses why the use of evidence discovered during an inventory of impounded property against a defendant at trial is “reasonable” under Article I, section 9. As the Supreme Court noted in a different but related context in State v. Bridewell, 306 Or 231, 239, 759 P2d 1054 (1988), the mere fact that police conduct may comport with law enforcement policy or societally-accepted police function “does not ensure [that conduct’s] compliance with Article I, section 9. Any intrusion of state power upon a constitutionally protected interest, be it for civil or criminal investigative purposes, must comply with constitutional standards.”

II. Attacking the Inventory: The Atkinson Criteria

State v. Atkinson, 298 Or 1, '' is Oregon’s lead inventory case. InAtkinson'', the Supreme Court dealt with police inventory of the contents of an impounded car and noted,

“There are occasions when state officials * * * have an administrative or civil duty, authority or responsibility to take custody of personal property. When governmental authorities have lawful ‘administrative’ custody of personal property, then they may take a series of reasonable steps which * * * can include a detailed ‘inventory’ of the property’s contents.”

Atkinson, 298 Or at 8. Atkinson’s holding and analysis apply to the inventory of any property that police have impounded or otherwise taken into “administrative custody.” Atkinson, 298 Or at 8. Atkinson sets forth three criteria that an inventory must meet for evidence that the police find during the inventory to be admissible against a defendant at trial:

(1) the property must be lawfully impounded

(2) the inventory policy must not permit the person doing the inventory to exercise discretion as to when and how to conduct the inventory, and

(3) the person conducting the inventory must not deviate from the policy.

Challenge the admission of evidence found during in inventory through a motion to suppress, arguing that one or more aspects of the impound or inventory violated the defendant’s right to be free from unreasonable search and seizure under Article I, section 9, and/or the Fourth Amendment. The following questions based on the Atkinson criteria will help frame such a motion and argument.

(1) Did the state identify the source of the duty, authority, or responsibility to impound and inventory the defendant’s property?

If the state fails to demonstrate the existence and nature of such authority, argue that the impound and subsequent inventory are invalid. See State v. Custer, 126 Or App 431, 435 (1994) (“[A] reviewing court must make a determination as to the authority under which the police impounded and inventoried the vehicle, as a prerequisite to declaring the inventory valid.”). But see State v. Boone, 327 Or 307, 314, 959 P2d 76 (1998) (law enforcement agency impliedly authorized to adopt its own inventory policy by city ordinance authorizing impound).

(2) Was the rule or policy properly promulgated?

Look to constitutional provisions, charter provisions, ordinances, the Administrative Procedures Act and case law on administrative law in making this analysis. Keep in mind, however, that a law enforcement agency may promulgate its own inventory policy, so long as the policy comports with Atkinson. Boone, 327 Or at 314-16. '''' If there is any problem with promulgation of the rule, argue that the impoundment is invalid.

(3) Were the officers aware of the rule or policy and did they follow it to the letter when they impounded and inventoried the property?

If not, argue that the inventory is invalid.

(4) Does the rule or policy prevent the exercise of discretion by the officer?

If the rule allows officer discretion, argue that the inventory is invalid.

Practice Tip

The Atkinson requirements have teeth—use them. Get copies of the inventory policies that govern law enforcement agencies in your jurisdiction. Know the ins and outs of the policies better than the officers and better than the prosecutors. If the policies do not comply with Atkinson’s requirements, any inventory conducted under their authority is invalid. If the officers do not comply with the policy, the inventory is invalid.

A. The Property Must Be Lawfully Impounded

“Lawful impoundment of a vehicle is a necessary prerequisite to an inventory of its contents by the government or its agents.” Atkinson, 298 Or at 8 (internal citation omitted). The police must act under some authority to take custody of private property. There are many likely sources of such authority, including departmental and agency inventory policies and statutes or city ordinances such as those that authorize police to take custody of abandoned vehicles, to move vehicles that have been involved in accidents, and to seize property the ownership of which is disputed by two or more claimants. The authority to conduct inventories need not come exclusively from the legislature or another politically accountable body: a law enforcement agency may adopt an inventory policy so long as the policy comports with Atkinson. Boone, 327 Or at 314-16.

If there is no lawful basis for impoundment, the evidence must be excluded: “If the government agents had no authority to take custody of the property, then there can be no lawful intrusion upon it. The inquiry ends there.” Atkinson, '''' 298 Or at 9.

Practice Tip

The police usually do have authority to impound our clients’ cars or personal property, but defense counsel should still make the state prove it. There will be times when the officer or the prosecutor does not know or cannot satisfactorily demonstrate to the court what the source of the impound authority is. Be ready to take advantage of such unpreparedness. If the state can’t prove the source of authority, argue that the impound is invalid and the inventory therefore unlawful.

Where the impound is invalid any subsequent search is invalid. See State v. Gonzales, 236 Or App 391, 236 P3d 834 (2010) (holding that the impound of the defendant’s car from his own driveway was not a valid community caretaking impound; thus, the inventory conducted pursuant to that impound was therefore also invalid). In State v. Smith, 72 Or App 130, 694 P2d 1013 (1985), there was no impound at all—the officer was going to leave the car locked on the street—and the court held that without an impound, the officer was not permitted to inventory the car.

Suspicion of criminal activity can play no part in the decision whether to impound the vehicle. State v. Lambert, 265 Or App 742, 746, 338 P3d 160 (2014); see also State v. Gaunce, 114 Or App 190, 194, 834 P2d 512 (1992) (holding that “an officer’s decision to impound a vehicle may involve the exercise of discretion,” but the officer’s suspicions of criminal activity “can play no part in the discretion that he exercises when deciding whether to impound a car”).

A fundamental tension exists, however, between on the one hand, State v. Willhite,110 Or App 567, 573, 824 P2d 419 (1992) (holding that an inventory policy must “adequately eliminat[e] individual discretion about whether to inventory a vehicle” (emphasis in original)), and on the other cases like Gaunce and State v. Penney, 252 Or App 677, 288 P3d 989 (2012), which allow for some exercise of discretion. This is a rather gaping hole in Atkinson’s scheme: in effect, it allows officers to decide which cars will be inventoried and which will not. Given that Atkinson does not want officers to be able to decide even what containers to open during an inventory, Gaunce and Penney may irreconcilably conflict with Atkinson and may therefore be ripe for challenge.


2024 Update

“The lawful seizure of a person does not justify the warrantless seizure of the person’s property.” State v. Wilcox, 323 Or App 271, 276 (December 14, 2022), rev allowed, 371 Or 60 (2023) (citing State v. Edwards, 304 Or App 293, 294 (2020).

B. Properly Authorized Administrative Program

The inventory must be conducted pursuant to a properly authorized administrative program that is designed and administered so that the inventory involves no exercise of discretion by the law enforcement person directing or conducting the inventory.

Atkinson provides some guidance concerning what the phrase “properly authorized administrative program” means. The program must be “designed” to preclude any exercise of discretion by the inventorying officer. Arguably, that is a requirement for written, specific rules. In addition, the program must be “systematically administered” so that the officer cannot exercise discretion. That translates to a requirement that the officer who conducts the inventory must know what the inventory rules are. It also suggests that some form of administrative oversight must ensure that officers comply with established policy.

Practice Tip

In addition to obtaining the inventory policy itself, try to get details of the officer’s training (1) about the inventory policy itself, and (2) how to conduct an inventory. Argue that any shortcomings in officer training—whether at the DPSST or the individual agency level—undercut the validity of the inventory.

Willhite, 110 Or App 567, for example, ' holds' that a policy that is too general offends Article I, section 9. The Willhite “policy” was a memo directing officers simply to complete a “Property Inventory” and separately list and store all property of “great value.” That policy was too broad because it more or less demanded that an officer look anywhere and everywhere in a car to ferret out valuable property, but left it to the officer to decide where. The policy must be specific enough that any officer can determine what the established inventory procedures are and act accordingly without having to decide independently what to inventory and what to leave alone. “The only question the trial court should have to ask is: Would the evidence have been discovered by any person following the established policy or procedures?” Willhite, 100 Or App at 574 (emphasis added). See also State v. Davis, 262 Or App 555 (2014) (policy that directed officers to impound and inventory a long and varied list of arrestee’s property at jail book-in was unconstitutional because it effectively authorized search of all property).

The scope of the inventory must be limited to just that: an inventory. An inventory policy may not permit or require officers to examine the contents of closed containers such as fishing tackle boxes, Keller, 265 Or 622, suitcases, Perry, 298 Or 21, or small opaque boxes. State v. Ridderbush, 71 Or App 418, 692 P2d 667 (1984). However, police may examine the contents of containers (such as bindles) which announce their contents, State v. McCrory, 84 Or App 390, 734 P2d 359 (1987), and containers that are expressly designed to contain valuables. State v. Mundt/Fincher, 98 Or App 407, 780 P2d 234 (1989). The fact that an officer may be able to tell that contraband is inside a closed container does not render that container one that announces its contents. State v. Kruchek, 156 Or App 617 (1998), aff’d by an equally divided court, 331 Or 664 (2001) (closed cooler from which smell of marijuana emanated did not “announce its contents”).

Practice Tips

1. The Oregon Supreme Court has never fully recognized the “containers designed to carry valuables” exception to the rule that opaque closed containers may not be opened during an inventory. The Court of Appeals created that exception in Mundt/Fincher and has built on it since. The Supreme Court impliedly accepted the exception in State v. Connally, 339 Or 583, 588 n 3, 125 P3d 1254 (2005), but noted that the defendant did not challenge the scope of the policy at issue in that case. The Supreme Court has never considered the exception further. In Mundt/Fincher, the Court of Appeals held that the opening of a wallet and a purse were permissible because those containers are primarily intended to store valuables, and the purpose of an inventory is to secure valuables. 98 Or App at 412. In State v. Cleland, 289 Or App 379, 410 P3d 386 (2017) (James, J. concurring), Judge James noted, “If this court’s jurisprudence were an art museum, our decisions on inventory searches would surely hang in the impressionists wing, where patrons are not encouraged to inquire too closely, lest the illusion of a coherent picture be dispelled.” Judge James reviewed the case law for inventories and provides a persuasive case for arguing that the line of cases authorizing searches of closed containers designed to hold valuables is wrongly decided.

2. In State v. Swanson, 187 Or App 477, 480-81, 68 P3d 265 (2003), the state conceded that a lockbox did not fall within the terms of an inventory policy that directed officers to open and inventory the contents of “purses, wallets, backpacks, and other similar items designed to contain valuables * * * .” The Court of Appeals explained that that was so because even though the lockbox was designed to contain valuables, it was not “similar” to the other listed items in the policy. Think back to your canons of statutory construction and pull out * * * ejusdem generis (literally, “of the same kind”): when statutory (or inventory policy) text “consists of a general term accompanied by examples of the general term[,] “ejusdem generis serves to confine the interpretation of the general term according to one or more common characteristics of the listed examples.” State v. Kurtz, 350 Or 65, 74, 249 P3d 1271 (2011). If the inventory policy you are challenging contains a list like the one in Swanson, and the closed container that the police opened was not the same kind as the ones in the list, use ejusdem generis to argue that the policy didn’t apply to your closed container and therefore the container should not have been opened.

C. Person Must Not Deviate

The person who conducts the inventory must not deviate from the established policy or procedures, and the officer carrying out the inventory must follow the policy to the letter.

Under Atkinson, the inventory is reasonable only because it is a limited intrusion driven by a legitimate specific purpose. If an officer deviates from the policy, the inventory becomes impermissibly intrusive and is therefore transformed into an unreasonable search or seizure that violates Article I, section 9.

In State v. Kendall, 173 Or App 487, 24 P3d 914 (2001), for example, the court found that a key case was not a container designed to carry valuables within the meaning of the City of Portland inventory ordinance because a key is not an item of value as described by that ordinance; therefore, the officer strayed impermissibly beyond the limits of the policy in opening the key case. And in State v. Guerrero, 214 Or App 14, 162 P3d 1048 (2007), a Marion County Jail inventory policy that was silent regarding the opening of closed containers did not authorize officers to open a black opaque case marked “Panasonic light scope.” Although the policy directed officers to take and count an arrestee’s money and jewelry, thus impliedly authorizing officers to open containers designed to contain money or jewelry, the policy did not authorize the opening of closed containers designed to hold generic “valuables.”

Importantly, in State v. Fulmer, 366 Or 224, 460 P3d 486 (2020), the Oregon Supreme Court settled an important unresolved question that repeatedly crops up in inventory cases. The court held that, as a matter of first impression, (1) before a vehicle inventory is conducted, police must notify vehicle’s occupants who are present that they may remove readily removable items from the vehicle; (2) if police fail to thus notify the occupants, the inventory exceeds the purposes that justify the inventory exception to the warrant requirement; and (3) such an inventory violates Article I, section 9. See also State v. Schmidt, 303 Or App 500, 464 P3d 481 (2020) (applying Fulmer to hold that the inventory violated Article I, section 9, because the officer did not give the defendant, a passenger in a stopped vehicle that the officer later impounded, notice that she could take her backpack with her when she got out of the car, even though she at first denied ownership of the backpack).

Practice Tip

Fulmer! This case is huge—use it. Schmidt too. Given how recent these cases are (at the time of this writing), it is possible if not likely that many law enforcement officers are still doing inventories the old way, i.e., under the assumption that they can get people out of the car and refuse to let them take their belongings with them, thereby rendering all of those belongings subject to “inventory.” Make sure the court knows that this simply does not pass constitutional muster anymore.


2024 Update

Under State v. Fulmer, 366 Or 224 (2020), police are required to provide the warnings before the inventory begins. State v. Sevits, 333 Or App 745 (2024) (holding that police violated Article I, section 9, when they began an inventory of defendant’s car and, 40 seconds into it, asked defendant if he wanted to remove any items).

III. Specific Types of Inventories

A. Automobile Inventories

Atkinson, 298 Or 1. Oregon’s lead case on inventories. The vehicle must first be lawfully impounded. Inventory must be conducted pursuant to a properly authorized administrative program. Person conducting the inventory must not deviate from the policy in any way.

Keller, 265 Or 622. This was the lead case until Atkinson came along. Officers could not open and search a closed fishing tackle box as part of a car inventory even after they discovered drug paraphernalia in plain view during the inventory. Absent any exigency, they had to get a warrant to open and search the tackle box.

Practice Tip

Keller shows how inventories and searches can overlap. Police often seem to think that if they find contraband in plain view during an inventory, the inventory magically transforms into a lawful search and they can then search anywhere they like. This is not the case. A warrantless search requires probable cause and an exigency. No exigency? The inventory stays an inventory until someone gets a warrant.

Practice Tip

Don’t let the state use the automobile exception as an exigency to transform an inventory in which police find contraband into a full-blown search. Once a car is impounded, the automobile exception no longer applies because any exigency resulting from the car’s mobility has been extinguished by the impound. Kruchek, 156 Or App 617.

1. Unlawful Impound

State v. Smith, 72 Or App 130, 694 P2d 1013 (1985). “[L]awful impoundment of a vehicle is a necessary prerequisite to an inventory of its contents * * * .” Officer conducted inventory because he was going to leave the car locked on the street, not because the car would be impounded. No impound = no inventory.

Gaunce, 114 Or App 190, rev den, 315 Or 271 (1992). '' The defendant had been lawfully stopped for a traffic infraction. The officer arrested him on an outstanding warrant. The officer decided to have the car towed because it was blocking trafficand'' because the officer suspected that the car and tires had been stolen and that the car might contain drugs. He conducted a “standard inventory search” of the car and found a bag containing cocaine. The Court of Appeals held that an officer’s suspicion that a car contains evidence of a crime may play no part in the officer’s exercise of discretion in deciding whether to impound and thus inventory the car. If it does, then the decision to impound effectively singles the car out for special searching attention. Police may not embark on a criminal investigation by conducting an “inventory” as a substitute for a lawful search.

State v. Corey, 123 Or App 207, 859 P2d 560 (1993), rev den, 318 Or 351 (1994). An inventory of the contents of an automobile is not an unreasonable search if certain requirements are met. One of these requirements is that the car has been lawfully impounded. Under PCC section 16.040.020, when the person in possession of a vehicle owned by another is arrested, a city officer may order the vehicle towed when no other reasonable disposition of the vehicle is available. The reasonableness of other dispositions is based on facts, and inferences based on those facts, known to the officer at the time of the decision to tow the vehicle.

State v. Gonzales, 236 Or App 391, 236 P3d 834, adh’d to as mod on recons, 238 Or App 541 (2010). An officer saw defendant pull into his own driveway after committing a traffic infraction. The officer learned that defendant’s license was suspended and impounded the vehicle. He conducted an inventory and seized evidence. The court ruled that community caretaking does not extend to impounding a car from a defendant’s driveway. The impound was invalid and therefore the inventory was invalid.

State v. Balabon, 292 Or App 870, 426 P3d 133 (2018). Officers pulled defendant over for a traffic infraction. She presented a valid license and a card that included insurance information but did not indicate a time period for insurance coverage or a reference to any vehicle insured. The officers cited the defendant for driving uninsured and impounded her vehicle, which they then inventoried, finding methamphetamine. The Court of Appeals held that the impound and inventory were unlawful because the officers did not have probable cause to believe that defendant was driving uninsured as required to impound her car.

2. Scope of Inventory Policy – Overbreadth

Willhite, 110 Or App 567. Automobile inventory. Tigard police inventory policy was “too general” because it did not curb officer discretion as to the scope of the inventory and thus did not satisfy the Atkinson criteria.

State v. Martin, 124 Or App 459, 863 P2d 1276 (1993). An officer’s testimony that it was standard procedure to tow vehicles, and to search them before towing, is insufficiently specific to satisfy Atkinson. Note that while Martin says that the state must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that its policies and procedures satisfy Atkinson, the Atkinson opinion does not actually specify an evidentiary standard.

State v. Guynn, 162 Or App 594, 986 P2d 1190 (1999). Inventories are neither searches nor seizures but are limited administrative acts designed to protect a person’s property while it is in police custody. Therefore, if an officer develops probable cause during an inventory to believe that a closed container holds evidence of a crime, the officer cannot seize the container without first obtaining a warrant, or establishing that an exception to the warrant requirement permits the warrantless seizure. Container at issue here was a zippered duffel bag in an impounded car.

State v. Eldridge, 207 Or App 337, 142 P3d 82 (2006). The policy at issue was overbroad because it commanded that all impounded vehicles “be completely searched and inventoried” and that officers “make a complete inventory of all vehicle contents.” Testimony from officers also disclosed that there was no oversight of inventory practices, or any guidance or documentation for inventory procedures. A bindle found in center console was suppressed.

State v. Nordloh, 208 Or App 309, 144 P3d 1013 (2006). Grants Pass inventory policy was unconstitutional because it required the opening and inventory of all closed containers in an impounded vehicle, regardless of whether a container was designed to hold valuables. The closed container here was a small zippered pouch.

State v. Williams, 227 Or App 453, 206 P3d 269 (2009). Prineville ordinance regarding the impounding of vehicles was too broad because it authorized police to inventory the contents of all closed containers, not just those likely to contain valuables. Container at issue was an eyeglasses case.

State v. Stone, 232 Or App 358, 222 P3d 714 (2009). Court of Appeals held that city inventory policy that required officers to inventory any closed container that “[t]he officer reasonably believes * * * contains valuable or dangerous personal property” eliminated officer discretion as required by Atkinson and thus passed constitutional muster. Container was a drawstring bag.

Practice Tip

The holding in Stone may be subject to challenge, i.e., that a policy that relies on an officer’s “reasonable belief” to determine when to conduct an inventory effectively eliminates officer discretion. If the state relies on this case, argue that “reasonable belief” is a concept that often contains within it discretion of the kind that Atkinson forbids. An inventory policy must dictate the same result every single time. Officers’ “reasonable beliefs” will likely differ, thus resulting in different results. In fact, State v. Willhite, 110 Or App at 574, expressly rejected reliance on any “reasonable belief” or “probable cause” type inquiry.

3. Source of Authority for Inventory Policy

State v. Haney, 195 Or App 273, 97 P3d 1211, adh’d to as modified on recons, 196 Or App 498 (2004). Even if the statute requiring reports to be filed after automobile injury accidents authorizes administrative searches, the state made no showing that the police agency here had adopted such an administrative search policy; or, if it had, that the policy eliminated officer discretion.

Practice Tip

Even a perfectly designed and implemented inventory policy must be properly authorized for admission of evidence discovered during the inventory to comport with Article I, section 9. If the state can’t prove where the authority underlying the policy comes from, argue that the inventory is invalid.

4. Sufficiency of Evidence that Policy Comported with Atkinson or that Officer’s Conduct Comported with Policy

State v. Herrin, 323 Or 188, 915 P2d 953 (1996). The court held that the record did not support the state’s inevitable discovery by inventory theory because the record was insufficient to establish what politically accountable authority promulgated the inventory policy and did not demonstrate that a closed bag would have been opened as part of the inventory.

Swanson, 187 Or App 477. Where the relevant city ordinance and police department order contain requirements that must be met before a closed opaque container can be inventoried, the record must be sufficient to establish that the inventory was lawful under those requirements. In this case, the container at issue, a small, red “make-up type purse accessory kit[,]” was not in evidence. The officer’s testimony was the only evidence of the nature of the container and was not sufficient to establish that it was designed to hold valuables and thus subject to inventory under the city policy.

State v. Hockersmith, 264 Or App 560, 333 P3d 1085 (2014). The applicable inventory policy required officer to open and inventory any container that based upon its context or physical appearance would reasonably be expected to contain valuables. Officer removed the defendant’s wallet from a pair of shorts left in the vehicle, opened the wallet, and found defendant’s license, thereby identifying him. The inventory was not valid because the state presented no evidence about the context or appearance of the shorts showing that the shorts could reasonably be expected to contain valuables. Noting that inventory cases are extremely fact-specific and that inventory policies vary widely in language and scope, the Court of Appeals rejected the state’s (somewhat comical) argument that the pocket on every pair of men’s shorts is designed to hold valuables and therefore could reasonably be expected to contain valuables.


Practice Tip

The state may attempt to rely on an inventory as a means of inevitable discovery to avoid suppression of evidence obtained during an illegal search or seizure. An inventory may well be a method of inevitable discovery, but the state must prove that the inventory would have comported with Atkinson. A prosecutor does not show inevitable discovery merely by intoning the magic word “inventory” or eliciting testimony from an officer that the defendant’s belongings would have been inventoried at the jail or that the car would have been impounded anyway. See State v. Johnson, 177 Or App 244, 250-52, aff’d 335 Or 511 (2003).

5. Officer Noncompliance with Inventory Policy

Kruchek, 156 Or App 617. The trial court erred when it denied defendant’s motion to suppress evidence of marijuana found by a police officer who opened an opaque ice chest while inventorying the contents of defendant’s van. The fact that the officer could smell the odor of marijuana emanating from the cooler did not give the officer the authority to open the cooler to determine its contents without first obtaining a search warrant. '''' This case illustrates the police tendency to believe that an inventory morphs into a lawful unwarranted search the moment an officer develops probable cause to believe it contains contraband. The state argued that the automobile exception provided the requisite exigency to support a search. But once police impound a vehicle, the automobile exception is “extinguished.”

State v. Resler, 163 Or App 328, 987 P2d 1269 (1999). Opening an opaque container during a vehicle inventory is a search unless the size and unique construction of the container make clear that it could contain nothing other than contraband. Here, an officer opened defendant’s fanny pack during the inventory because he knew it was “of the sort that is commonly used to carry a handgun.” The court held that Kruchek '''' controlled and reversed because the fanny pack was opaque and did not announce its contents.

State v. Walker, 173 Or App 46, 20 P3d 834 (2001). A bubble container like those handed out at weddings so that guests may blow bubbles was not a container of the type that “is invariably used to store and transport controlled substances” even though it “rattled” when shaken and the officer believed that it contained crack cocaine. Under State v. Owens, 302 Or 196, 206, 729 P2d 524 (1986), '' police can lawfully open an opaque container without a warrant in only two circumstances: 1) the container so “announces its contents” that opening it does not invade a protected privacy interest and, thus, does not constitute a search, or 2) the opening is supported by probable causeand some exception to the warrant requirement''. Neither existed in this case.

State v. Delaney, 224 Or App 463, 197 P3d 621 (2008). State conceded that inventory policy did not allow the officer to look through the pockets of the defendant’s jacket found in the defendant’s car during vehicle inventory.

State v. Dean, 227 Or App 342, 206 P3d 206 (2009). During the inventory of the vehicle the officer seized a folding digital scale. He opened the scale and discovered a controlled substance inside. The state argued that the officer lawfully seized the scale pursuant to the terms of the applicable inventory policy, but conceded that he did not have authority to open the scale. The court accepted that concession.

State v. Sparks, 228 Or App 163, 206 P3d 1197 (2009). The defendant was a passenger in a car stopped for a traffic violation. She got out of the car with her purse. When she declined to give permission to the officer to search her purse, he told her he had to search it anyway pursuant to the city’s impound and inventory policy which required him to search everything that was in the car at the time it was stopped. State conceded on appeal that the officer’s search was unlawful because it overstepped the bounds of the city inventory policy.

State v. Keady, 236 Or App 530, 237 P3d 885 (2010). City policy requiring officers to open and inventory contents of containers “designed to hold valuables” did not allow police to open a “fish oil capsule container” because it was not designed to hold valuables. Officer’s belief that the container might contain valuables was irrelevant.

State v. Jorgenson, 245 Or App 494, 261 P3d 96 (2011). The state conceded that removing the console of a car (located between the front seats) exceeded the permissible scope of an inventory.

State v. Dimmick, 248 Or App 167, 273 P3d 212 (2012). Here, the policy was silent as to when closed containers should be seized pursuant to a tow, therefore, the officer was not allowed to seize any closed container. The closed container at issue in this case was a backpack that the defendant asked to take with him when police told him they were impounding his car. The officer denied the request, inventoried the backpack and found methamphetamine. Since the seizure was unlawful, a subsequent warrant derived from that initial unlawful seizure did not cure it.

State v. Hanna, 248 Or App 608, 273 P3d 945 (2012). Officers exceeded the authority of their inventory policy where they searched the bed of a pickup truck secured by a locked tonneau cover. The bed of a pickup truck is neither a “trunk” nor an “external vehicle container[,]” the two possibilities allowed under the inventory policy. A pickup bed is not a trunk: only cars have trunks. That the tonneau cover allowed the bed to function like a trunk did not transform the pickup bed into a trunk.

State v. Rowell, 251 Or App 463, 283 P3d 454 (2012). The Portland City Code inventory policy allowed police officer to open closed containers designed to contain valuables, but only applies to items in the possession of a person placed in custody. The inventory must occur prior to placing such persons into a holding room or police vehicle. Here, defendant was a passenger in a stopped car. He was stopped but he was not “in custody” for purposes of inventory because he was only stopped as a witness. The state could not use the arrested driver’s constructive possession of the defendant’s laptop bag to justify the search because the driver was already in the patrol car.

State v. Krumenaker, 306 Or App 9, 472 P3d 760 (2020). During an inventory of defendant’s impounded car, the officer looked inside a backpack and discovered a zipper case that the officer identified as a pistol case. He felt a gun inside, opened the case, and found the gun. The Tillamook Police inventory policy appropriately provided that closed containers designed for carrying money, small valuables, or hazardous materials be opened and their contents inventoried. However, the Tillamook police chief testified that whether an officer would open a wide variety of different containers “depend[ed] on the circumstances.” The trial court granted suppression and the state appealed. The Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court, holding that, while the policy itself was fine, the Tillamook Police Department’s implementation of the policy was not: as a matter of first impression, the Court of Appeals determined that the way in which the police administer an inventory policy can render an otherwise acceptable policy overbroad.

B. Jail Booking/Detox

1. Source of Authority for Inventory Policy

State v. Layman, 162 Or App 386, 986 P2d 624 (1999). The defendant was arrested on an outstanding warrant and taken to the Lane County Jail. He was searched pursuant to a book-in policy which included an inventory search of his person. He had methamphetamine in his wallet. The state did not establish that either the Lane County Commission or the legislature expressly authorized the jail officials to conduct inventories or to adopt an inventory policy. However, the authority to do so was implied in the commission’s decision to establish a jail. The decision to authorize the police to take custody of people and to hold them in a jail includes an implied grant of authority to the police to control the personal property that is taken by prisoners into the jail and to account for property that is held by the police pending its return to the prisoners or other disposition. That authority includes the authority to conduct an inventory and to adopt a policy by which to conduct it. This case replaced State v. Layman, 147 Or App 225, 935 P2d 1216 (1997).

State v. Ketelson, 163 Or App 70, 986 P2d 1202 (1999). The decision to establish a detox center and to authorize those charged with operating it to hold and treat intoxicated people includes an implied grant of authority to control the personal property that is brought by patients into the facility and to account for that property pending its return to the patients or other disposition. That authority includes the authority to conduct an inventory and to adopt policies by which to conduct it.

2. Unlawful Impound

Obviously, automobile impound does not arise in the context of jail or detox center booking. Depending on the circumstances of your case, however, you may be able to argue analogously that your client was not lawfully arrested and therefore should not have been subject to the inventory process in the first place. Sections II, IV, and V of Chapter 3C deal with whether an arrest is lawful.

3. Scope of Inventory Policy – Overbreadth

Perry, 298 Or 21. Perry is the lead jail inventory case, although it actually involved a detox hold rather than jail booking. The defendant was publicly drunk. Police detained him and took him and his belongings to the police station for detoxification purposes. After laying the defendant on a mattress in the “detox” area of the station, police opened and “inventoried” his suitcase. They found marijuana and the defendant was charged with PCS. The Supreme Court held that it was unreasonable for police to open the defendant’s suitcase, noting that the inventory in a noncriminal process such as detox detention should be less intrusive than what is reasonable in a criminal case. Note: the civil/criminal dichotomy posited in this case has not developed much further in the inventory case law.

State v. Ridderbush, 71 Or App 418, 692 P2d 667 (1984). During booking, jail staff opened a small black box found in the defendant’s pocket and discovered a straight razor. The Court of Appeals held that under Keller (the tackle box case) and Perry, “no closed, opaque container may be opened” during inventory to determine what is inside.

Mundt/Fincher, 98 Or App 407. In this case, the Court of Appeals created the container-designed-to-contain-valuables “exception” to the general rule that closed containers are not subject to inventory. In Mundt, the court held that the search of a wallet at the jail pursuant to an inventory policy was reasonable. The drugs obtained from the wallet were admissible. In Fincher, the search of a purse pursuant to an inventory policy was reasonable but the closed containers inside the purse could not be searched. See also State v. Johnson, 153 Or App 535, 153 P2d 887 (1998) (applying Mundt/Fincher exception).

Johnson, 177 Or App 244. A jail inventory is not a search. The jail becomes the bailee of the incarcerated person’s belongings, but the jail “has not ‘seized’ the inmate’s property in the sense that the facility may now treat that property as it desires.” In this case, the state offered a jail inventory as a method of inevitable discovery to render admissible the evidence discovered as a result of an earlier, warrantless seizure of the defendant’s clothes and boots. The court noted: “even if the record establishes that the jail staff would have taken custody of defendant’s clothing and boots as part of booking him[, it] does not show that the police would have used that clothing for the purposes of this criminal investigation.” (To see what happened next in this case, see Johnson, 335 Or 511, 73 P3d 282 (2003).

State v. Taylor, 250 Or App 90, 279 P3d 254 (2012). An inventory policy cannot give police blanket authority to open closed containers. The scope of the inventory must be limited so that objects are scrutinized only to the extent necessary to complete the inventory. Umatilla County Jail inventory policy authorizing search of all closed containers was impermissibly overbroad, in violation of Article I, section 9.

State v. Cordova, 250 Or App 397, 280 P3d 1036 (2012). An inventory policy is overbroad when it effectively authorizes inspection of all closed containers. Here, the Marion County Sheriff’s General Order authorized inspection of the contents of “all closed containers that could contain valuables.” Because valuables can be extremely small, any container “could” contain valuables.

State v. Cruz-Renteria, 250 Or App 585, 280 P3d 1065 (2012). The fact that reasonable people, including reasonable officers, might disagree about the purpose of the containers indicated that the policy itself possibly allowed impermissible discretion.

State v. Jansen, 261 Or App 117, 323 P3d 332 (2014). State conceded that jail inventory policy which gave officers unbridled discretion with respect to opening closed containers was unconstitutionally broad. Quotes Cordova, supra, noting that “we have been scrupulously rigorous in our construction of the terms of inventory policies.” Container at issue here was a zippered pouch.

State v. Cherry, 262 Or App 612, 325 P3d 813 (2014). The state conceded and the Court of Appeals held that the Marion County Jail inventory policy was unconstitutional because it required authorized deputies to open all closed containers.

State v. Hite, 266 Or App 710, 724, 338 P3d 803, 811 (2014). Lane County Jail inventory policy that required inventory of impounded property to determine whether, inter alia, the property contained perishable food, medical marijuana, alcohol or hazardous or dangerous substances was unconstitutionally overbroad because it implicitly authorized officers to open just about any closed container. Oregon’s general rule is that “an inventory policy may not authorize the police to open all closed, opaque containers. Although [the Court of Appeals has] recognized an exception to that rule for containers that are designed to or likely to contain valuables, [the court has] not extended that exception to containers that are objectively likely to contain weapons, drugs, or other contraband.” Inventory of the defendant’s backpack pursuant to the overbroad policy was unconstitutional.

State v. Cleland, 289 Or App 379, 410 P3d 386 (2017) (James, J. concurring). Judge James concurred to note a tension in the case law: that by applying different precedent, the Court of Appeals could have found the search in this case unlawful and reversed rather than affirming: “If this court’s jurisprudence were an art museum, our decisions on inventory searches would surely hang in the impressionists wing, where patrons are not encouraged to inquire too closely, lest the illusion of a coherent picture be dispelled.” Judge James reviewed the case law for inventories and provides a persuasive case for arguing that the line of cases authorizing searches of closed containers designed to hold valuables is wrongly decided.

State v. Steele, 290 Or App 675, 414 P3d 458 (2018). Defendant challenged an inventory, arguing that the policy was unconstitutionally overbroad because it authorized the search of all closed containers—even though the evidence at issue was not in a closed container. The Court of Appeals agreed, based on the record made in this case. Police discovered methamphetamine in defendant’s pocket, and he was taken to jail. The jail deputy explained that he conducts his “own search” of everyone who is booked into the jail and that he opens all closed containers pursuant to the inventory policy. That was the only evidence of the contents of the inventory policy and was a sufficient basis to establish that the jail inventory policy was unconstitutionally overbroad “in that it categorically sanctioned the search of all closed containers.” Id. at 684.

Judge DeVore concurred to note that whether an inventory policy that is flawed in part is invalid in whole has not expressly been decided, and is not decided in this case. He took great pains to distinguish two cases: Nordloh, 208 Or App 309, and Cherry, 262 Or App 612, which seem on their face to support a non-severability doctrine. However, until a majority opinion expressly undermines the broad language used in those cases, they permit a reasonable argument that an inventory policy that is invalid in one respect renders a search conducted pursuant to a lawful and unrelated portion of the policy unconstitutional.


Practice Tip

Steele provides a legitimate basis to argue that even if the evidence at issue is not discovered as a result of the invalid portion of the inventory policy, you may still be able to get the evidence suppressed. Always, always get a copy of the inventory policy! Even if at first blush it seems like the contraband turned up in a legitimate way, the policy itself may be fatally flawed in a different way that you can use to your client’s benefit.

State v. Barnett, 299 Or App 656, 633 (2019) (per curiam) (James, J., concurring). '''' Expanding on the view he took in hisClelandconcurrence, Judge James argues that the inventory exception has become unmoored from its foundational cases,AtkinsonandKeller, and that the Court of Appeals has and will inevitably continue to permit inventories of virtually all closed containers, save an angler’s tackle box, whichAtkinson'' expressly held could not be opened.

4. Sufficiency of Evidence that Policy Comported with Atkinson or that Officer’s Conduct Comported with Policy

State v. Petri, 214 Or App 138, 162 P3d 1053 (2007). The state posited jail inventory as a method of inevitable discovery of drug paraphernalia found in a warrantless search of the defendant’s sunglasses case. The Court of Appeals held that the record did not contain evidence to support the state’s inevitable discovery argument. The only mention of jail inventory was the officer’s testimony that he would have searched the defendant “to inventory the contents of his person prior to placing him in the police vehicle.”

[http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=State+v.+Medinger,+235+Or+App+88&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=11431238308393177306&scilh=0 State v. Medinger, 235 Or App 88, '' 230 P3d 76 (2010)]. The state did not prove that contraband would have been inevitably discovered pursuant to detox/treatment facility inventory because the record did not contain sufficient evidence to establish by a preponderance that the facility’s policy comported withAtkinson''’s requirement.

5. Officer Compliance With Inventory Policy

State v. May, 174 Or App 560, 26 P3d 188 (2001). State conceded that a closed, opaque candy bag that police found in the defendant’s pocket when he was arrested should not have been subject to inventory, and that the jail inventory policy did not comport with Atkinson.

State v. Dillon, 182 Or App 308, 50 P3d 1172 (2002). The Court of Appeals held that the city ordinance and police policies at issue in this case did not authorize the inventory of the defendant because they applied only to “prisoners.” Here, the defendant was not a prisoner and had not been arrested. The police acted without authority when they inventoried the defendant’s property.

Guerrero, 214 Or App 14. Marion County Jail inventory policy did not authorize police to open a black opaque case marked “Panasonic light scope.” The policy also did not authorize the opening of closed containers designed to hold generic “valuables.” Police did not act in conformity with the inventory policy when they opened the case, and therefore their inventory was invalid.

State v. Keady, 236 Or App 530, 237 P3d 885 (2010). An officer’s belief that a closed opaque container “might contain” valuables is irrelevant to whether it was “designed” to do so. Only those containers “designed to hold valuables” may be inventoried pursuant to the City of Salem inventory policy.

State v. Garcia-Cruz, 287 Or App 516, 402 P3d 750 (2017). Police arrested defendant on a probation violation. At the jail, pursuant to an inventory policy, police inspected defendant’s wallet. Police found and opened two folded pieces of paper, one of which contained methamphetamine.

The Court of Appeals concluded that the paper fold did not by its nature reveal its contents, even though the officer felt a granulated substance. The contents of the folded paper were not so “plainly obvious” that the feel of them revealed that the paper contained methamphetamine. “In this case, the feel of the substance in the folded paper did not reveal the contents of that paper to be methamphetamine, by its very nature, because what the officer felt only indicated that the paper could have contained a number of granulated crystal-like substances, nothing more specific. In sum, notwithstanding that it was highly probable that the folded paper contained methamphetamine, that opaque receptacle did not reveal its contents.” Id. at 524.

2024 Update

In State v. Wilcox, 371 Or 756 (2023), the defendant was intoxicated and belligerent at a hospital, and police officers were asked to assist in effectuating his discharge. Id. at 758-59. The police handcuffed the defendant and told him they were taking him to a detox facility. Id. The defendant had a backpack with him. In the parking lot, before putting the defendant in the patrol car, one officer searched the defendant’s person, and the other “conducted an inventory” of the backpack and found a restricted weapon. Id.

The court issued two significant holdings: (1) Police seized the defendant’s backpack when they removed it from his person (and certainly at the moment the police opened it), id. at 764-65, and (2) when a person or property is seized pursuant to the public intoxication statute, ORS 430.399, the seizure is an “administrative” noncriminal seizure and must comply with requirements in State v. Atkinson, 298 Or 1 (1984). Id. at 766-68.

The state argued that it would be unreasonable for police officers to require an intoxicated person to leave a bag with unknown contents in a public parking lot because it would be unsecured and could create a nuisance or pose a safety hazard to anyone who might find it. Id. at 768.

The Supreme Court agreed that an officer might reasonably have those concerns, “but a perception of reasonableness by the executive actor is not a substitute for the extra-executive control provided by “legislative enactments defining and limiting official authority[,]” as required by the “administrative” search and seizure exception. Id.

That is, the issue is not the reasonableness of an individual officer’s desire, or even need, to seize property, it is the source of authority for that seizure. In Oregon there is no generalized police community caretaking authority:

“‘There is no generic “community caretaking function.” Whether law enforcement officers have specific functions is a matter of statutory law. * * *Any intrusion of state power upon a constitutionally protected interest, be it for civil or criminal investigative purposes, must comply with constitutional standards.’”

Id. at 769 (quoting State v. Bridewell, 306 Or 231, 239 (1988)).

Thus, generalized concerns for safety or liability, even reasonable ones, are not the equivalent of an Atkinson­-compliant grant of authority to seize or search property. Id.

Practice Tip

Whenever a person or property is seized pursuant to the non-criminal detox statute—or pursuant to any other noncriminal authority—trial attorneys should be prepared to cite Wilcox and argue that the search or seizure must comply strictly with Atkinson.  

C. Inventory of Personal Property Incident to Arrest

1. Unlawful Impound

As with a jail or detox inventory, a critical question in the inventory incident to arrest context is whether the defendant is lawfully arrested. See Chapter 3C for more on determining the lawfulness of your client’s arrest. If the arrest is not lawful, any inventory conducted pursuant to the arrest should be invalid.

State v. Tyler, 168 Or App 600, 7 P3d 624 (2000). Officer arrested the defendant for jaywalking, then inventoried her belongings pursuant to the arrest and found drugs. The inventory was invalid because the officer did not have the authority to arrest the defendant and take her into custody: jaywalking is not an arrestable offense. Arrest inventory must be based upon a valid arrest. No valid arrest = no valid inventory.

State v. Lovaina-Burmudez, 257 Or App 1, 303 P3d 988 (2013). Officer treated the defendant’s possessions as evidence to be maintained for future prosecution rather than as inventoried property, including photographing the soles of his shoes. Six months later, the police obtained a warrant and seized the property as evidence. The Court of Appeals held that when a defendant’s personal property is lawfully secured pursuant to an inventory policy, processing the property as evidence exceeds the scope of the inventory and thus constitutes an unlawful search and seizure. For purposes of the inevitable discovery doctrine, the court held that the state failed to show that the defendant’s clothing and shoes would still have been available to seize six months later had the items been handled according to the inventory policy rather than as evidence.

State v. Stinstrom, 261 Or App 186, 322 P3d 1076 (2014). The officer encountered the defendant in a park. One of the defendant’s companions took off the backpack he was wearing, set it next to defendant and said, “This is your bag.” The defendant began to reach for it, saying, “This is my bag.” The officer instructed him to stop. The officer arrested the defendant on an outstanding warrant and began to walk him to the police car, leaving the backpack where it sat. Part way to the car, the officer said, “Oh, I forgot your backpack.” The defendant said the backpack was not his. The officer brought it along anyway and searched it, finding contraband and a notebook containing the defendant’s name inside. The Court of Appeals reversed, holding that the officer’s warrantless seizure of the backpack was not authorized by the inventory policy. An inventory policy, the court explained, does not give an officer authority to seize property; rather, it governs the examination of property that is already seized.

State v. Olendorff, 267 Or App 476 (2014). After arresting the defendant for driving while suspended, officers unlawfully seized the defendant’s purse for an inventory after the defendant had requested that officers give her purse to her boyfriend. The police gave no reason to believe that there was anything harmful in the purse or any evidence of the crime of arrest. Defendant’s ultimate admission that there was “something” illegal in her purse as well and drugs found therein had to be suppressed because the officers exploited the unlawful seizure of the purse when they wrongfully limited her choices. The officer presented defendant with two options: (1) grant permission to search the purse or (2) be charged with supplying contraband to the jail if she did not tell them about any drugs that they suspected they would find in her purse. Because the police had no authority to prevent defendant from transferring her property to her boyfriend, the police unlawfully constrained defendant’s options and her statements had to be suppressed.

2. Scope of Inventory Policy

State v. Davis, 262 Or App 555, 325 P3d 790 (2014). While arresting the defendant on an outstanding warrant, the deputy removed a closed white sock from the defendant’s pocket and looked inside. The sock contained a meth pipe. Citing State v. Taylor¸ 250 Or App 90 (2012), the Court of Appeals held that the Lane County Sheriff’s Office inventory policy was constitutionally deficient because it did not impose any limitations on the officer’s discretion to open closed containers. The pipe and the defendant’s statements about the pipe should have been suppressed.

3. Sufficiency of Evidence that Policy Comported with Atkinson or that Officer’s Conduct Comported with Policy

State v. Mituniewicz, 125 Or App 41, 864 P2d 1359 (1993). An officer’s testimony that it was standard procedure to remove everything from an arrestee’s pockets before transporting the arrestee was insufficient to show that the purported inventory policy authorized the opening of containers. Even assuming there was a proper policy, the evidence did not demonstrate that the policy required the opening of any containers, such as the paper bindle at issue in this case. Likewise, the evidence did not support a finding that the bindle would inevitably have been opened during jail booking.

4. Officer Compliance with Inventory Policy

State v. Maynard, 149 Or App 293, 942 P2d 851 (1997). The defendant was arrested for various offenses. Before the officer placed her in the patrol car, he conducted an inventory of her possessions in accordance with a Portland city ordinance. During the inventory, he found a small wooden matchbox of the “strike anywhere” variety. He opened the box and discovered methamphetamine. The officer did not comply with the inventory policy when he opened the matchbox to determine its contents. The inventory was not valid.

State v. Myers, 156 Or App 561, 967 P2d 887 (1998). The City of Portland cannot lawfully authorize a police officer who is conducting an inventory to open a closed container to determine its contents before deciding whether to return the container to a person in custody. Under those circumstances, opening a container to determine its contents is not an inventory but is a search.

State v. May, 162 Or App 317, 986 P2d 608 (1999). Officer unwrapped a cloth bundle found in the defendant’s pocket and found drugs and drug paraphernalia. The bundle was a closed container which the officer had opened without establishing a proper basis for doing so. The officer exceeded the scope of the inventory policy.

Kendall, 173 Or App 487, 24 P3d 914 (2001). A key case is not a container designed to carry valuables within the meaning of the Portland inventory ordinance because a key is not an item of value as defined in that ordinance. Police discovered the key case inside the defendant’s shaving kit. The Court of Appeals noted that the defendant did not challenge the opening of the shaving kit and therefore confined its analysis to the key case rather than addressing the shaving kit. The implication is that the police likely overstepped the inventory when they opened the shaving kit.

2024 Update

A person’s lawful arrest does not allow for the seizure of all the arrestee’s personal property. It may authorize a seizure of “narrow categories” of personal effects, such as effects related to the probable cause for arrest or readily apparent contraband. But the defendant’s backpack did not fall within such narrow categories. State v. Wilcox, 323 Or App 271, 276 (December 14, 2022), rev allowed, 371 Or 60 (2023) (citing State v. Edwards, 304 Or App 293, 294, 466 P.3d 1034 (2020).

D. Courthouse

Scope of Inventory Policy

State v. Snow, 247 Or App 497, 268 P3d 802 (2011). Courthouse security deputy opened a cigarette package that the defendant had in his pocket and found methamphetamine. The county security policy authorized deputies to search people and their belongings to prevent anyone from entering the courthouse with dangerous or hazardous items. The Court of Appeals held that the policy afforded deputies too much discretion in deciding whom to search, which items to search, and how intensely to search. Because the policy was too broad, the administrative search was unlawful.
