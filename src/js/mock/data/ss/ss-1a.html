I. Introduction

Three preliminary questions must be answered to determine if an individual’s right to be free from unreasonable searches and seizures was violated. The first is whether the conduct at issue was performed by government agents or private citizens, because Article 1, section 9, and the Fourth Amendment limit only governmental action. State v. Tanner, 304 Or 312, 321, 745 P2d 757 (1987); United States v. Walther, 652 F2d 788 (9th Cir 1981). That is usually obvious in the case of federal, state, or local law enforcement officers, but can require some analysis in less common situations.

Once that is established, the second inquiry is “whether the [governmental] conduct at issue was a search or a seizure” because “if the police conduct at issue was not a search or a seizure, the constitutional protection does not apply.” State v. Nagel, 320 Or 24, 28, 880 P2d 451, 454 (1994). While the legal principles involved in answering the second question are straightforward, this manual provides myriad examples of common factual situations that courts have deemed sufficient to constitute constitutionally cognizable searches or seizures.

Third, if a search or seizure by a governmental agent did occur, a court must determine whether that search or seizure satisfies the constitutional requirement of reasonableness. On that question, both the state and federal constitutions have incorporated a presumption that a search or seizure conducted without a warrant is per se unreasonable in the constitutional sense. Only if the government can prove that its conduct fell within one of several recognized exceptions to the warrant requirement will a warrantless search or seizure be considered constitutionally “reasonable.” Nagel, 320 Or at 36; Katz v. United States, 389 US 347, 357, 88 S Ct 507, 19 L Ed 2d 576 (1967); State v. Davis, 295 Or 227, 237, 666 P2d 802, 809 (1983); United States v. Hoffman, 607 F2d 280, 283-84 (9th Cir 1979).

Chapter 1 concerns the first two of those inquires: (1A) was there government action? and (1B) was there a search or a seizure.

II. Was There Governmental Action?

As noted, Article 1, section 9, and the Fourth Amendment limit only government action. State v. Olsen, 212 Or 191, 193, 317 P2d 938 (1957) (“It is a rule of universal application that the prohibition of the Fourth and Fifth Amendments of the Constitution of the United States and similar provisions contained in the state constitutions which protect individuals against illegal searches and seizures are limitations upon the powers of the Federal Government and state governments and their officers while acting in their official capacity or under color of right or authority of the governing body.”); Walther, 652 F2d 788 (a wrongful search or seizure by a private party does not violate the Fourth Amendment; however, where a private party acts as an “instrument or agent” of the state in effecting a search or seizure, Fourth Amendment interests are implicated).

A. What Constitutes State Action Under Article 1, Section 9?

A search or seizure by a private citizen becomes “state action” within the meaning of the state constitutional prohibition against unreasonable search and seizure if the citizen was acting on the “state’s behalf and at the state’s behest,” according to common-law principles of agency. State v. Sines, 359 Or 41, 55, 59, 379 P3d 502 (2016).

Common-law agency exists where a principal “manifests assent to another person”—the agent—“act[ing] on the principal’s behalf and subject to the principal’s control, and the agent manifests assent or otherwise consents so to act.” Id. at 55.

The considerations relevant to the existence of an agent’s actual authority to act on behalf of the principal focus on the “principal’s manifestation to an agent that, as reasonably understood by the agent, expresses the principal’s assent that the agent take action on the principal’s behalf.” Id.

In Sines, the defendant’s housekeeper called the Oregon Department of Human Services (DHS) to report suspected sexual abuse after finding, among other things, stained children’s underwear in the defendant’s house. The housekeeper told the DHS employee that she was considering taking the underwear from the defendant’s house. The DHS employee told her several times that he could not tell her to take that kind of action, and that it was her decision. She asked what they could determine from underwear, and the DHS employee said there was a lab locally and they could “probably tell a lot.” He then gave her his direct phone number.

The Oregon Supreme Court held that the statements made by the DHS worker were insufficient to cause a reasonable person to believe that he or she was authorized to act as an agent of the state. The DHS employee did not direct or request the housekeeper to take the underwear. The idea of taking evidence from the house, and of taking underwear in particular, came from the housekeeper. There was little, if any, such affirmative encouragement, initiation, or instigation.

However, “If a state officer requests a private person to search a particular place or thing, and if that private person acts because of and within the scope of the state officer’s request, then Article I, section 9, will govern the search.” State v. Tucker, 330 Or 85, 90, 997 P2d 182, 184 (2000) (when a private party, a tow truck driver, conducts a search at the request of a law enforcement officer, the search is subject to Article I, section 9). In addition, Article I, section 9, prohibits government “officials from participating or engaging indirectly in searches [or seizures] which would be illegal if conducted by the official.” State v. Becich, 13 Or App 415, 419, 509 P2d 1232 (1973) (where a private party “volunteered” to conduct a search in response to an officer’s demand for the return of stolen property and the officer drove by the defendant’s home while the private party conducted the search, the search was subject to Article I, section 9).

2024 Update

In State v. Hall, 324 Or App 802 (March 22, 2023), the court treated Sines and State v. Lien/Wilverding, 364 Or 750 (2019), as bookends in assessing whether the conduct of a private person constitutes state action. (See discussion of Sines in body of Section 1A-2.)

In Lien/ Wilverding, the court found the kind of agency relationship that did implicate Article I, section 9. There, the police “asked” a garbage company to pick up the defendant’s garbage and deliver it to the police. Id. at 752-53. The garbage company’s manager complied and delivered the defendant’s bin to the police. Id. at 753. The trial court found that the manager had acted “exclusively at the request and direction of the police,” a finding that was binding on the court. Id. at 768. On those facts, the court had little problem concluding that under Sines, the police, as principals, and the garbage company’s manager, as the agent, had entered into an agency relationship, transforming the manager into a state actor.

In Hall, an officer arrested the defendant in the lobby of his apartment building. The officer knocked on the door of the defendant’s apartment and the defendant’s friend, Smith, answered. The officer told Smith that the defendant had been arrested and that he was looking for the defendant’s phone and shoes. The officer asked if Smith had seen those items, and Smith said that they were in the apartment. The officer told Smith that he was not asking or telling her to retrieve the items for him. Nonetheless, Smith offered “on her own volition” to get the items for the officer, and the officer said, “Okay.” Hall, '''' 324 Or App at 803-05.

The Court of Appeals held that, given the trial court’s findings that the officer had not directed or encouraged Smith in anyway and that Smith volunteered “on her own volition” to get the items, the case was more similar to Sines, and ultimately that “no principal/agent relationship formed that transformed Smith’s actions into state action for purposes of Article I, section 9.” Id. at 808.

However, Hall’s heavy reliance on the existence (or lack thereof) of a “principal/agent relationship” may be slightly misplaced. The Oregon Supreme Court has since discussed Sines at length in State v. Benton, 371 Or 311, 313 (Aug 10, 2023), where the question was whether the state’s dealings with a jailhouse informant were sufficient to render the informant’s questioning of the defendant state action in violation Article I, section 11. The court explained that although “the appropriate focus is on the objective manifestations of the state and the informant, we disagree that Sines requires that, in every instance, those manifestations must be sufficient to satisfy common-law agency requirements before Article I, section 11, will apply.” Id. at 323. The court went on to explain:

“There is no single metric for evaluating the sufficiency of the state’s involvement. Accordingly, in conducting that evaluation, this court has phrased the inquiry a few different ways. For example, this court has queried whether the police were ‘“directly or indirectly involved to a sufficient extent in initiating, planning, controlling or supporting”’ the informant’s activities, Smith, 310 Or at 13 (quoting Lowry, 37 Or App at 651-52); whether the police had ‘sufficient involvement in controlling and directing’ the informant’s activities, Acremant, 338 Or at 329; whether the state had ‘provided such affirmative encouragement and authorization,’ Sines, 359 Or at 62; or whether there was sufficient ‘state instigation or direction,’ id. at 61, such that the conduct of the informant ‘should be attributed to the government,’ id. at 55. This court has also noted that, in cases where agency principles are helpful, the emphasis is on whether the state’s objective, observable conduct would communicate to the informant that they were acting on the state’s behalf. Id. at 55-56. In the end, although the precise nature of the state’s involvement may vary from case to case, the ultimate inquiry is whether the state was involved enough in guiding, encouraging, or supporting the informant’s activities that the informant’s conduct is fairly attributable to the state.”

Benton, 371 Or at 324-25 (emphasis added).

Thus, rather than focusing exclusively on agency, defense lawyers should emphasize the state’s objective acts that may have encouraged, guided, directed, or communicated tacit approval to the person such that the person’s subsequent search or seizure is “fairly attributable” to the state.

B. Non-Police Officers Determined to be State Actors

Article I, section 9, protects against unlawful seizures by state actors—not simply law enforcement officers. See, e.g., State v. Killion, 229 Or App 347, 353, 211 P3d 367, 371 (2009), (Oregon Department of Fish and Wildlife biologist); Sines, 359 Or 41 (a DHS employee); State v. Okeke, 304 Or 367, 745 P2d 418 (1987) (an employee of a state-funded detoxification center); State v. Scatchard, 208 Or App 315, 145 P3d 237, rev den, 342 Or 254, 149 P3d 1213 (2006) (a volunteer firefighter). Juv. Dept. v. M. A. D., 348 Or 381, 389-91, 395, 233 P3d 437 (2010)] (assistant principal at a public high school and a “staff learning specialist”); State v. Walker, 19 Or App 420, 528 P2d 113 (1974) (assistant principal at a public school); State ex rel Juv Dept v. Rohlffs, 147 Or App 565, 570, 938 P2d 768, 770 (1997) (teacher at a public school); See also State v. Codon, 282 Or App 165, 174, 386 P3d 45, 50 (2016), rev den, 361 Or 240 (2017) (assuming but not deciding that a DHS caseworker was an agent of the police in determining whether the case worker was required to readvise the defendant of Miranda rights). Moreover, an argument can be made that medical personnel who disclose evidence of a defendant’s blood alcohol content to police after an alcohol-involved vehicular crash are state actors because ORS 676.260 requires them to make that disclosure to law enforcement. That analysis could carry over into any other private enterprise upon whom the state has instituted crime-reporting requirements, e.g. banks, internet service providers, and even mandatory child abuse reporters.

Finally, although unsettled in Oregon, other states generally hold that loss-prevention officers, like private security guards, are not state actors unless a specific agency relationship has been established. Paige v. State, 226 Md App 93, 112, 126 A3d 793, 804 (2015) (listing cases); State v. Petersen, 469 SW3d 737, 740 (Tex App 2015) (applying general agency test to determine whether LPO was not acting as an agent of police); but see Peoples v. State, 615 So.2d 1265 (Ala.Crim.App.1992) (holding that when a police officer was present in the room, the defendant should have been given Miranda warnings before being questioned by LPOs).

In State v. Jensen, 83 Or App 231, 730 P2d 1282, (1986), the defendant was observed through the slats in a department store fitting room, placing unpaid-for merchandise into a diaper bag. Guards then followed the defendant out of the store, where they briefly detained her and took back the merchandise. The defendant argued that the guards had violated her privacy rights under Article I, section 9, by peering into the changing room.

She argued that, although there was no official police involvement in the search, the search constituted state action because the security agent was performing a delegated police function in furtherance of a state interest and pursuant to statutory authority (ORS 131.655, which provides that a merchant or a merchant’s employee (or a peace officer) who has probable cause for believing that a person has shoplifted may detain and interrogate the person in a reasonable manner and for a reasonable time without incurring criminal or civil liability). Id. at 234.

The court reasoned that “[a]lthough ORS 131.655 authorizes store security agents to detain and interrogate suspects under certain circumstances, it stops short of authorizing, in any form, a predetention search, and defendant cites no other statutory authority which authorizes one.” That is, “in detecting the crime, as distinct from detaining the offender, [the private security guards were] not acting pursuant to any state statute.” Id. at 235.

Consequently, under that reasoning, the question remains open whether “seizures” conducted pursuant to statutes that authorize private parties to lawfully detain individuals (e.g. citizen’s arrest (ORS 133.225) or merchant’s privilege ORS (131.655)) might constitute state action such that such “seizures” would be governed by Article I, section 9, and possibly Article I, section 12, (requiring Miranda warnings prior to custodial interrogation).

C. What Constitutes Government Action Under the Fourth Amendment

The test under the Fourth Amendment is whether the private party in light of all the circumstances of the case must be regarded as having acted as an “instrument” or agent of the government. Coolidge v. New Hampshire, 403 US 443, 487, 91 S Ct 2022, 2049, 29 L Ed 2d 564 (1971). United States v. Smythe, 84 F3d 1240 (10th Cir 1996). “The government must be involved either directly as a participant or indirectly as an encourager of the private citizen’s actions before we deem the citizen to be an instrument of the state [and] [t]he requisite degree of governmental participation involves some degree of knowledge and acquiescence in the search.” Walther, 652 F2d 788 (citations omitted) (“[T]wo of the critical factors in the ‘instrument or agent’ analysis are: (1) the government’s knowledge and acquiescence, and (2) the intent of the party performing the search.”); Compare United States v. Cleaveland, 38 F3d 1092 (9th Cir 1994) (electric company had legitimate, independent motivation and therefore was not agent of government during search where electric company, not police, initiated plan to inspect meter, electric company broke off search once it discovered defendant was stealing power, and police officer’s only purpose for being in area was to ensure safety of electric company’s employee); with [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=United+States+v.+Reed,+15+F3d+928&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38&case=17676171324106276044&scilh=0 United States v. Reed, ' 15 F3d 928 (9th Cir 1994)]' (hotel employee’s warrantless search of defendant’s room was government action, in light of presence of police lookouts to protect employee during search, and in light of employee’s intent to help police gather proof of narcotics trafficking rather than independent motivation to ensure hotel property was not damaged). Thus, the intent or motive of the party conducting the search is an important factor in cases involving private party searches.

Although the two-part test is generally used by the federal courts, many courts also expressly include a requirement that the government do something affirmatively to encourage the search. For example, the First Circuit considers three factors: (1) the extent of the government’s role in instigating or participating in the search; (2) the government intent and the degree of control it exercises over the search and the private party; and (3) the extent to which the private party aims primarily to help the government or to serve its own interests. United States v. Cameron, 699 F3d 621, 637 (1st Cir 2012), cert den, ___ U.S.___, 133 S Ct 1845, 185 L Ed 2d 850 (2013). The Fourth Circuit considers whether the private citizen was an instrument or agent of the government, given the government’s participation or affirmative encouragement. United States v. Richardson, 607 F3d 357, 364 (4th Cir 2010). See also Smythe, 84 F3d 1240 '''' (for private search to be state action, government must “affirmatively encourage, initiate or instigate the private action”).
