I. Introduction

An informant is anyone who gives information to the police. State v. Villagran, 294 Or 404, 657 P2d 1223 (1983). But in the context of search and seizure, an informant is one whose hearsay statements form part of the basis for an arrest and search. That is the definition of “informant” that will be used here. These persons may be named or unnamed, paid or volunteer, co-conspirators or disinterested citizens; they can even be unwitting informants, including the targets of the search or seizure (see section II. B. 1. c., “Note on Double Hearsay,” below).

This chapter will discuss two aspects of the law of informants: 1) informants as the source of probable cause in search warrants, and 2) identifying the anonymous informant in the face of the so-called “informant’s privilege.”

II. Informants as the Source of Probable Cause in Warrant Searches

By far, the most prevalent use of informants that comes to the attention of the criminal practitioner is when their statements are used as the source of probable cause for a search warrant. Particularly in cases involving contraband, an attack on the legitimacy of a search warrant is the only effective defense available. A considerable body of law has emerged in both the federal and state courts concerning the validity of search warrants based on informants’ hearsay statements, which could fairly be described as unsettled. The following discussion undertakes to describe the current state of the law for the Oregon practitioner.

A. Sources of the Law

1. Oregon Law: A Mix of Statute and Constitution

Oregon affords protection to persons subject to warrant searches based on informants’ hearsay statements.

Where there is hearsay information in a warrant affidavit, that information can be tested under ORS 133.545, which provides in pertinent part:

“(3) Application for a search warrant may be made only by a district attorney or by any police officer.

“(4) The application shall consist of a proposed warrant in conformance with ORS 133.565, and shall be supported by one or more affidavits particularly setting forth the facts and circumstances tending to show that the objects of the search are in the places, or in the possession of the individuals, to be searched. If an affidavit is based in whole or in part on hearsay, the affiant shall set forth facts bearing on any unnamed informant’s reliability and shall disclose as far as possible, the means by which the information was obtained.”

Practice Tip

There has been no case on whether ORS 133.565 can still be a basis for suppression in light of ORS 136.432, which states that “a court may not exclude relevant and otherwise admissible evidence in a criminal action on the grounds that it was obtained in violation of any statutory provision unless exclusion of the evidence is required by: (1) The United States Constitution or the Oregon Constitution; (2) The rules of evidence governing privileges and the admission of hearsay; or (3) The rights of the press.”

If you seek suppression under the statute, be prepared to argue that (1) the statute codifies the Oregon Constitution’s requirements and (2) exclusion is still required by statute under the analysis of State v. Thompson-Seed, 162 Or App 483, 491, 986 P2d 732 (1999) (“ORS 136.432 is construed only to constrain the courts from creating new rules of exclusion and not to repeal existing statutory rules of exclusion.” Id.).

a. Requirements of the Statute

The limits of ORS 133.565 apply to the examination of search warrants issued on the basis of unnamed informant hearsay. Those limits require examination of Fourth Amendment standards prior to Illinois v. Gates, supra.

In a series of cases between 1960 and 1969, the United States Supreme Court established what came to be known as the “Aguilar-Spinelli” two-pronged test for examination of hearsay in affidavits for search warrants. Jones v. United States, 362 US 257, 80 S Ct 725, 4 LEd2d 697 (1960); Rugendorf v. United States, 376 US 528, 84 S Ct 825 11 LEd2d 887 (1964); Aguilar v. Texas, 378 US 108, 84, 84 S Ct 1509, 12 LEd2d 723 (1964); United States v. Ventresca, 380 US 102, 85 S Ct 741, 13 LEd2d 684 (1965); and Spinelli v. United States, 393 US 410, 89 S Ct 584, 21 LEd2d 637 (1969). These cases established that where a search warrant has been issued on the strength of hearsay information, i.e., an informant’s tip, the issuing magistrate must have been provided with the underlying facts which support the affiant’s belief that probable cause to issue the warrant exists. These facts or circumstances must demonstrate:

1. what circumstances lead to the conclusions presented in the affidavit, i.e., that contraband is located at a specific address (the “basis of knowledge” prong); and

2. what circumstances allow the affiant to credit the information given by the hearsay informant (the “credibility or reliability” prong).

This is the Aguilar-Spinelli rule.

The Oregon Supreme Court followed these rules as they were developed and used them in Oregon courts as applications of the Fourth Amendment protections against search and seizure. State v. Tacker, 241 Or 597, 407 P2d 851 (1965); State v. Dunavant, 250 Or 570, 444 P2d 1 (1968); and State v. Flores, 251 Or 628, 447 P2d 387 (1968).

In 1973, the Aguilar-Spinelli rule was codified in Oregon under ORS 133.545, in the language of subsection (4): “If an affidavit is based in whole or in part on hearsay, the affiant shall set forth facts bearing on any unnamed informant’s reliability and shall disclose, as far as possible, the means by which the information was obtained.”

It was the intent of the legislature to adopt the Aguilar-Spinelli rule; see OR Crim L Rev Comm, proposed Oregon Criminal Procedure Code, section 133, commentary at 72 (1972). The Oregon Supreme Court has recognized that this was the intent of the legislature. State v. Montigue, 288 Or 359, 605 P2d 656 (1980); State v. Carlile, 290 Or 161, 619 P2d 1280 (1980); State v. Villagran, 294 Or 404, 657 P2d 1223 (1983); and State v. Russell, 293 Or 469, 650 P2d 79 (1982).

Thus, despite the holding in Illinois v. Gates, discussed below, the Aguilar-Spinelli standard continues to be the law in Oregon for cases involving hearsay by unnamed informants. See State v. Horwedel, 66 Or App 400, 674 P2d 623 (1984); State v. Middleton, 73 Or App 592, 700 P2d 309 (1985); and State v. Smith, 73 Or App 800, 700 P2d 311 (1985).

b. Oregon Constitutional Law

Where the statute does not apply or is determined not to compel suppression, warrants will be tested by the requirements of Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution which provides:


“No law shall violate the right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable search, or seizure; and no warrant shall issue but upon probable cause, supported by oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the person or thing to be seized.”

If an affidavit in support of a search warrant contains the hearsay statement of an unnamed informant, the hearsay must be examined to ensure that it complies with the Aguilar-Spinelli test. Montigue, 288 Or 359. However, the Oregon Supreme Court has held that the Oregon Constitution does not require Aguilar-Spinelli scrutiny in cases involving named informants. State v. Farrar, 309 Or 132, 786 P2d 161 (1990).

In Farrar, the Oregon Supreme Court held, “where the information in the Affidavit is provided by a named informant, as in the instant case, the Aguilar-Spinelli standard is not required by statute or by the Oregon Constitution.”

In Villagran, 294 Or at 408, this court stated:

“Our function, when faced with such an argument, is to determine whether a neutral and detached magistrate would conclude, based on the facts and circumstances shown by the affidavit, that there was probable cause to believe that the search would discover things specified in the affidavit in the places requested to be searched. See ORS 133.555(1), (2). We are to construe the supporting affidavit in a common sense and realistic fashion * * *”

309 Or at 144, 145 (citations omitted). Thus, one cannot analyze the hearsay statements of a named informant appearing in an affidavit for a search warrant under the Aguilar-Spinelli. Precisely what the Oregon Constitution does require is still open to question, however. (See section II. B. 2. c., “Attacking the Named Informant,” below.)

2. Federal Law: The “Good Faith” Exception

The fundamental law of search and seizure at the federal level is, of course, the Fourth Amendment to the United States Constitution, which provides:


“The right of the people to be secure in their persons, houses, papers, and effects, against unreasonable searches and seizures, shall not be violated, and no Warrants shall issue, but upon probable cause, supported by Oath or affirmation, and particularly describing the place to be searched, and the persons or things to be seized.”

Between 1964 and 1983, the Fourth Amendment was the central source of protection from searches based on insubstantial informant hearsay, but the federal law with regard to the establishment of probable cause based on hearsay statements in affidavits for search warrants was fundamentally altered in Illinois v. Gates. In Gates, the Supreme Court examined a case involving a search warrant based on a completely anonymous informant who had given the police information by a letter. In its decision, the Court rejected the prior “Aguilar-Spinelli” rule developed in Aguilar v. Texas, and Spinelli v. United States (discussed more above), and adopted a “totality of the circumstances” examination of all questions of probable cause, which the Court believed to be “far more consistent with our prior treatment of probable cause....” Id. at 462 US 230.

The Gates ruling could have resulted in an entirely new set of Fourth Amendment decisions that would instruct as to when the “totality of the circumstances” provided probable cause under the Fourth Amendment. However, the Court eliminated the need for any such discussion by its decision in United States v. Leon, 468 US 897, 104 S Ct 3405, 82 LEd2d 677 (1984), and its companion case Massachusetts v. Sheppard, 468 US 981, 104 S Ct 3424, 82 LEd2d 737 (1984).

Principally in Leon, the Supreme Court held that where an officer has made a search based on a warrant issued by a magistrate, evidence obtained as a result of that search will not be suppressed, even if the search violated the Fourth Amendment, so long as the officer’s “reliance on the magistrate’s probable-cause determination and on the technical sufficiency of the warrant he issues [is]...objectively reasonable....” Id. at 468 US 922. Thus, henceforth Fourth Amendment attacks on a warrant search will only be allowed when the defendant can show that both the search violated the Fourth Amendment and the state cannot “reasonably” rely upon “good-faith” exception to the warrant.

The opinion goes on to describe situations in which suppression might still be had where the officer has acted in bad faith, for instance, by providing the magistrate with facts the officer knew to be untrue or should have known to be untrue except for the officer’s reckless disregard for its truth or falsity. Or, where the magistrate “wholly abandoned his judicial role” in issuing a warrant “so lacking in indicia of probable cause as to render official belief in its existence entirely unreasonable,” Id. at 468 US at 925. Or, the circumstance where the warrant itself is so facially deficient as to be presumably invalid, i.e., where the place to be searched is not particularly described.

It is apparent that this rule virtually eliminates the suppression of evidence obtained under a warrant based on the protection of the Fourth Amendment, except in the rare circumstance where no effort has been made on the part of the police to obtain an even facially correct search warrant, or the even rarer circumstance where the defendant can show that the officers either lied or otherwise acted in “bad faith.”

Leon is not a case interpreting the warrant requirement, but rather a case modifying the exclusionary rule based on the principle that the rule, under the Constitution, is based on deterrence. The decision nonetheless virtually eliminates a challenge to a search warrant based on the insufficiency of an informant’s tip to establish probable cause. However, some principal challenges to warrant searches do remain under the Fourth Amendment, and certainly where there has been a search impermissible under the Fourth Amendment, a record should be established for the purposes of any post-conviction challenges in federal court and in anticipation of any possible (however unlikely) changes in the federal attitude toward suppression.

B. Suppression: Sufficiency of Warrants Based Upon Hearsay

When examining the affidavit in support of a search warrant for sufficiency, the practitioner should ask three questions:

•       Does the affidavit, as a whole, state probable cause? If it does not, then the warrant should be attacked on this basis. See Chapter 5A, Search Warrants: Motions to Suppress.

•       Does the affidavit contain hearsay statements? If it does, can the hearsay statements contribute to a finding of probable cause? This will depend upon an analysis of each hearsay statement under the rules explained in this chapter. If the statement should be excised, then the warrant should be reexamined for probable cause without the hearsay statement.

•       Finally, one should ask whether or not there are inaccuracies or untruths in the affidavit which can be attacked by motion to controvert? See Chapter 5B.

1. Adequacy of the Hearsay: Attacking the Tip

The object of examining the hearsay in an affidavit in support of a search warrant is to find reason for the court to excise that hearsay as too unreliable to serve as a basis for a magistrate’s determination of probable cause. Analysis will differ depending on whether the informant is named or unnamed. Moreover, keep in mind that courts often confuse the ultimate assessment of probable cause with the specific task of determining whether a particular hearsay statement is satisfactory. Thus, we begin with a general discussion of the appellate point of view.

a. Attacking the Unnamed Informant

If an affidavit in support of a search warrant contains the hearsay statement of an unnamed informant, the hearsay portion of the affidavit which served as the basis for the probable cause finding must be examined to ensure that it complies with the Aguilar-Spinelli test. This test, restated in Montigue is: “1) The affidavit must set forth the informant’s basis of knowledge. 2) The affidavit must set forth facts showing the informant’s veracity either by showing: a) the informant is credible, or b) that the informant is reliable.” 288 Or at 362.

If the hearsay statement fails the test and the affidavit does not otherwise provide probable cause, then the warrant should fail and any evidence seized on the basis of the warrant should be suppressed. Dunavant, 250 Or 570, and Diaz, 29 Or App 523. This is the statutory standard set out in ORS 133. 545.

In State v. Worsham, 114 Or App 170, 834 P2d 1033 (1992), the Court of Appeals made it clear that unnamed informants and the so-called CRI (confidential reliable informant) must meet both prongs of the Aguilar-Spinelli test required by ORS 133.545(4).

If one informant acts as a conduit for another informant, the court must assess their basis of knowledge and veracity separately. State v. Chezem, 125 Or App 341, 865 P2d 1307 (1994).

Where the affidavit establishes neither the basis of knowledge nor the veracity or reliability of the unnamed informant, the statement must be disregarded. State v. Poppe, 131 Or App 14, 883 P2d 905 (1994).

i. Basis of Knowledge

In order for an informant’s statement appearing in an affidavit for a search warrant to establish probable cause to issue the warrant, it must first “set forth [the] informant’s basis of knowledge.” Montigue, 288 Or at 362.

The question of basis of knowledge does not concern itself with what facts were related by the informant, but only with whether or not there is a showing of the means by which the facts were obtained.

Fundamentally, this requirement exists so that the magistrate reviewing the affidavit can know, from the affidavit, that the information was obtained in a reliable way and not, for instance, through unsupported hearsay. See Spinelli v. United States, 393 US at 416–17.

If circumstances showing a basis of knowledge are not provided, then the magistrate cannot rely upon the informant’s statement to establish probable cause. Aguilar v. Texas, supra; Spinelli v. United States, supra; State v. Dunavant, supra; State v. Flores, supra; State v. Smith, 73 Or App 800, 763 748 (1985).

Personal Observation

Perhaps the simplest and most common way in which an informant’s statement shows basis of knowledge is by the simple claim that the informant personally observed the facts stated. The cases demonstrating this are legion. See, e.g., State v. Fink, 79 Or App 590, 594, 720 P2d 327 (1986); State v. Shutvet, 105 Or App 97, 803 P2d 287 (1991).


 Substantial Detail

Courts have occasionally held that the “basis of knowledge” prong of Aguilar-Spinelli can be satisfied through examination of the detail of the informant’s statement. This proposition comes from the statement in Spinelli that “in the absence of a statement detailing the manner in which the information was gathered, it is especially important that the tip describe the accused criminal activity in sufficient detail that the magistrate may know he is relying on something more substantial than a casual rumor circulating in the underworld or an accusation based merely on an individual’s reputation.” Spinelli v. United States, supra, 393 US at 416.

This proposition should be carefully examined each time it is raised. The careful reading of Spinelli shows that the statement quoted above was based upon an earlier case, Draper v. United States, 358 US 307, 79 S Ct 329, 3 LEd2d 327 (1959). Spinelli goes on to say that the reliance on the excessive detail provided by the informant in Draper was sufficient to meet the basis of knowledge requirement because the information itself was detailed and the detailed information was corroborated. See Spinelli v. United States, supra, 393 US at 417–18.

This is also the case in Oregon where basis of knowledge has been satisfied by a detailed statement of facts, and much of the detail was “verified 100 percent.”[http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=.,+State+v.+Delker,+26+Or+App+497&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=12793915521751390654&scilh=0 State v. Delker, 26 Or App 497, 552 P2d 1313 (1976)].

Simply providing a great number of facts does not satisfy the basis of knowledge prong. In State v. Hall, 79 Or App 597, 720 P2d 376 (1986), a warrant was issued on an extensive affidavit which is set out in three full pages in the opinion. The affidavit was based on the statements of two unnamed informants. The Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court, which held that the basis of knowledge had not been sufficiently shown to comply with Aguilar-Spinelli. The problem, as pointed out by the Court of Appeals, was that “the affidavit does not tell us the basis of the CRC’s knowledge concerning the most critical information that he related to the affiant [citation omitted]. To infer on the basis of the ‘detail’ exhibited, as the state would have us do, that the CRC’s knowledge must have been based on his personal observations and that he had been in the [defendant’s] residence many times would, for all practical purposes, do away with the requirement that the affidavit disclose the means by which the information was obtained.” Id. at 603.

Therefore, though an affidavit and statement by an informant may be lengthy and detailed, it may not be the sort of detail which allows the court to infer a personal basis of knowledge.

ii. Veracity

The second “prong” of the Aguilar-Spinelli test is the veracity prong. Here, the object is to test the informant’s statement to see if the magistrate had reason to credit the information, either by establishing that the informant is a credible individual or by establishing that the information given by the informant is reliable. Spinelli v. United States, supra, 393 US at 412–13, and 417–19.

Some examples in Oregon where there was not sufficient showing that the informant was credible or information reliable are: State v. Carlile, 290 Or 161, 612 P2d 1280 (1980), where the named case was affirmed but the two companion appellate cases (State v. Reiter and State v. Shaw) were reversed because no basis of credibility was established for the named informant; [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=State+v.+Liberman,+51+Or+App+345,&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=911234828028399790&scilh=0 State v. Liberman, 51 Or '' App 345, 625 P2d 678 (1981)], where again anamedinformant’s statement was not credible underAguilar-Spinelli'' because there had been no sufficient corroboration of the named informant’s statement; State v. Tidyman, 30 Or App 537, 568 P2d 666 (1977), where no information was given as to the informant’s reliability; and State v. Brust, 94 Or App 416, 765 P2d 1246 (1988), where the information of one of the informants was disregarded because the only corroboration was of “innocent and easily obtainable information such as a suspect’s name and address.” 94 Or App at 420; however, the case was affirmed because a second informant’s statement, which was sufficient to supply probable cause in and of itself, was corroborated sufficiently to show it reliable.

Where an affidavit relates the declarations of an unnamed informant, it must set forth facts showing the informant’s veracity. If it does not, those declarations must be disregarded. State v. Russell, 122 Or App 261, 857 P2d 220 (1993).

The affidavit supporting the warrant did not set forth sufficient facts to show that the informant was credible or her information reliable. Without the informant’s statement, the affidavit did not establish probable cause. State v. Cotter/Ray, 125 Or App 210, 864 P2d 875 (1993).

State v. Pelster/Boyer 172 Or App 596, 21 P3d 106 (2001). The “veracity” of an informant refers to the extent to which the informant can be considered trustworthy. Veracity can be established in several ways, including corroboration by the police, corroboration by another informant, the informant exposing himself to liability for filing a false report, or by the informant’s statements against penal interest.

Where the veracity prong has been a question, the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court have been innovative in finding ways to establish veracity or reliability of the information. The following examples demonstrate the ways which the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court have found to satisfy the veracity prong.

Affiant’s Corroboration

Veracity can be established through showing that the information is reliable by way of corroborating the facts contained in the informant’s tip. State v. Brust, 94 Or App 416, 765 P2d 1246 (1988). In this case, informant’s statements were corroborated by the police with facts which were somewhat incriminating but would probably not, by themselves, have established probable cause. However, they were found sufficient to show that the informant’s information was reliable. State v. Binner/Walker, 128 Or App 639, 877 P2d 642 (1994). Veracity may be established by facts showing that the information from the informant is reliable. Police corroboration of information supplied by an informant establishes the informant’s reliability and permits an inference that other information supplied by that informant is also reliable. That some of the corroborated details concern nonincriminating facts does not adversely affect their worth for establishing the informant’s veracity.

The Controlled Buy

A “controlled buy” is a situation where the police officer gives marked or otherwise identified bills to an informant who then proceeds to a location to make a buy of contraband while being observed by the police officer, and then returns immediately to show the police officer the drugs purchased. Closely associated with corroboration by the previous activity and corroboration by investigation, the controlled buy is itself sufficient to establish the reliability of the information given by the informant. See State v. Middleton, 73 Or App 592, 700 P2d 309 (1985); and State v. Wilson/Helms, 83 Or App 616, 733 P2d 54 (1987). But see State v. Berardinelli/Berardinelli, 95 Or App 364, 769 P2d 235 (1989), holding that the controlled buy plus some corroboration was sufficient to establish the veracity of the informant.

Prior Useful Tips

Perhaps the most common type of information establishing the informant’s veracity is the affiant’s recitation that the informant has given information in the past resulting in successful searches and seizures. State v. Nuttall, 97 Or App 776, P2d 26 285 (1989); State v. Shutvet, 105 Or App 97, 803 P2d 287 (1991) (the fact that the informant had given the police 50 items of information which led to the issuance of a search warrant and three arrests was sufficient to establish the veracity prong.).

Admissions Against Penal Interest

Admissions against penal interest will serve to satisfy the veracity prong of Aguilar-Spinelli. These occur when the informant admits involvement of a crime in the course of giving the information which appears in the affidavit. In some cases, this may be information given to the police after the person is apparently caught in the act; see, e.g., State v. Carlile, supra, or when the person unknowingly gives the information to the authorities while acting as a middleman in an illegal transaction; see, e.g., State v. Middleton, supra. It can also be the case where the persons who are the focus of the investigation itself unknowingly make admissions to another informant whose statement satisfies Aguilar-Spinelli; see, e.g., State v. Alvarez, 308 Or 143, 776 P2d 1283 (1989). In this case, the court concluded that a declaration against penal interest is entitled to some weight in determining the reliability of information conveyed by an informant. The court also stated that reliability of an informant’s information is distinct from the credibility, character or reputation of truthfulness of the informant and the issues of whether the unnamed informant is credible and whether the information is reliable must be considered separately.


 Polygraph Verification

The Court of Appeals has held since 1986 that the reliability of an informant’s information could be established by a polygraph examination. See State v. Fink, 79 Or App 590, 720 P2d 372 (1986), at 79 Or App 595. The Oregon Supreme Court has taken up the matter in State v. Coffey, 309 Or 342, 788 P2d 424 (1990). In Coffey, the court held that a polygraph examination could be used to support an unnamed informant’s reliability. It held, moreover, that even though an affidavit does not state it, an inference could be made that the polygraph examiner mentioned in an affidavit was licensed. However, “where the polygraph examiner’s opinion [is] the sole fact bearing on reliability, the affidavit would not be sufficient to support a determination of probable cause. The insufficiency of the polygraph examiner’s opinion is due to doubts about the validity and potential rate of error of the polygraph.” 309 Or at 347, footnote 7.

b. Attacking the Named Informant

When the hearsay in the affidavit is from the lips of a named informant, ORS 133.545 no longer applies, so there is no requirement of the hearsay statement passing the Aguilar-Spinelli test. State v. Farrar, supra.

However, the statement is still to be examined in the abstract under the provisions of Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution and if it is not sufficiently reliable, it appears that it will be treated the same as any other unreliable hearsay statement and the affidavit should be examined without the inclusion of that hearsay.

In State v. Farrar, after holding that Aguilar-Spinelli no longer applies to named informants, the court nonetheless examined the two hearsay statements involved under an Aguilar-Spinelli-like '' framework. This analysis is noted and has been followed by the Court of Appeals: State v. Young, 108 Or App 196, 816 P2d 612 (1991), and State v. Evans, 110 Or App 46, 822 P2d 1198 (1991). Both cases note thatState v. Farrarheld thatAguilar-Spinellidoes not apply in named informant cases, and then each notes that the Supreme Court inState v. Farrarapplied theAguilar-Spinelli'' framework as a useful basis for analysis, even in named informant cases. Each then proceeds to examine the given named-informant hearsay, first looking for a basis of knowledge and then testing reliability.

So far, the change in the law in Farrar has made little difference in analysis of hearsay informant cases. This is because the courts have never found great difficulty in passing named informant’s statements, even where the Aguilar-Spinelli test was applied. See, e.g., State v. Villagran, 294 Or 404, 657 P2d 1223 (1983), and State v. Montigue, 288 Or 359, 605 P2d 656 (1980), cert den 449 US 846 (1980). Particularly in the case of police officers where reliability is seen as inherent see, e.g., State v. Fink, supra; State v. Milks/Sales, 127 Or App 397, 872 P2d 988 (1994). In Milk/Sales, the court reiterated that the Aguilar/Spinelli test, although not directly applicable to named informants, provides a framework for evaluating the totality of circumstances. Under that test, an affidavit must disclose the informant’s basis of knowledge and it must set forth facts demonstrating the informant’s veracity, by indicating either that the informant is credible or reliable. An informant who has two convictions for filing false police reports is not credible. An informant with a motive to lie and minimal corroborated information is not reliable. Admissions against penal interest are entitled to belief; however, if they come through an informant, that informant must be proved to be credible. When the informant is not credible and not reliable, without independent verification, those admissions are entitled to no more belief than any other information from that informant.

c. Note on Double Hearsay

Where an affidavit relies on double hearsay, that is, when one hearsay informant is repeating the statement of yet another hearsay informant, each hearsay informant must pass the applicable test. Thus, any informant in the chain that is unnamed must pass Aguilar-Spinelli scrutiny. And any named informant will be given the lesser scrutiny required by Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution. See Alvarez, 308 Or 143; State v. Kreutzer/Kreutzer, 138 Or App 306, 909 P2d 175 (1995). State v. Fink, supra; and State v. Young, supra.

State v. Miller, 254 Or App 514, 295 P3d 158 (2013). An affidavit in support of a warrant based on hearsay statements of an unnamed informant must contain facts detailing the informant’s reliability and, to the extent possible, disclose the source of the informant’s information. The remedy where the affidavit does not contain such facts is that the informant’s statements must be stricken from the affidavit and reassessed for probable cause.

If the affiant relied on an unnamed primary informant for facts to form probable cause to obtain a search warrant, the court applies ORS 133.545 to all of the primary informant’s statements, including information that he heard from a named person. State v. Evans, 110 Or App 46, 822 P2d 1198 (1992).

III. Note on Warrantless Searches

Warrantless searches commonly follow this pattern: investigative stop-arrest-search incident to arrest. Hearsay informants can be involved at two points in a warrantless search. A hearsay informant can be the cause for a stop, see, e.g., State v. Black, 80 Or App 12, 721 P2d 842 (1986); or the informant (rarely) can be the source of probable cause for an arrest or a search; see, e.g., State v. Hodge, 11 Or App 525, 504 P2d 143 (1972).

A reliable report from a citizen informant may be sufficient on its own to provide reasonable suspicion. But “[w]hen reasonable suspicion is based solely on a citizen informant’s report, that report must contain some indicia of reliability.” State v.Villegas-Varela, 132 Or App 112, 115, 887 P2d 809 (1994) (emphasis added). There are three factors that are important in determining the reliability of a citizen informant’s report. Id.

The first factor is “whether the informant is exposed to possible criminal and civil prosecution if the report is false.” Id. That factor is satisfied if the informant gives his or her name to law enforcement or delivers the information in person. Id.

The second factor is “whether the report is based on the personal observations of the informant,” which may be inferred by an officer if the information in the report contains sufficient detail to show that it was not fabricated and “the report is of the sort which in common experience may be recognized as having been obtained in a reliable way.” Id. (Internal quotation marks and citations omitted.) Even if the second factor is not satisfied “in the strictest sense,” because an informant is relaying information second-hand, this “does not necessarily undermine the reliability of the report.” State v. Mitchele, 240 Or App 86, 92-93, 251 P3d 760 (2010). To assess the reliability of the informant’s information we examine the circumstances to assure “that the particular information is indeed trustworthy on a specific occasion.” State v. Alvarez, supra.

The final factor is “whether the officer’s own observations corroborate the information provided by the informant,” which may occur when the officer observes the illegal activity or finds the person, vehicle, or location substantially as the informant describes. Mitchele, 240 Or App at 91. The three factors “are not determinative of whether a citizen informant’s report is reliable; they are merely intended to serve as an aid in evaluating the reliability of such a report.” Id. at 92-93.

State v. Hunt, 265 Or App 231, 335 P3d 288 (2014). In Hunt, although the second factor—whether the report is based on the personal observations of the informant—is not satisfied “in the strictest sense,” it does not “undermine the reliability of the report.” Motel guests called the front desk clerk on three separate occasions to complain about defendant, suggesting that the guests were not fabricating the story. The information that the guests gave the clerk included details such as the number of “girls” staying in that particular room and that there was a white Lexus parked directly in front of the room where the “girls” were staying. Further, the clerk obtained the Lexus’s license number. Those details are sufficient to show that neither the front desk clerk nor the guests had likely fabricated the report. Those facts in addition to facts satisfying the first and third factor, established that the report was sufficiently reliable to establish reasonable suspicion.

In State v. Hames, 223 Or App 624, 196 P3d 88 (2008), the court explained that any facts observed by
the officer which contradict the informant’s report must be considered in determining the reliability of the report.

The situation with regard to probable cause for arrest is even more problematic. At one point, the law clearly required hearsay information used as the basis of a warrantless arrest or search to comport with the requirements of Aguilar-Spinelli. See State v. Hodge, supra. However, given the holding in State v. Farrar, supra, that the Oregon Constitution does not require Aguilar-Spinelli examination of named informant’s statements, the question arises as to what would be required as to any hearsay informant in warrantless searches since there is no statute addressing that circumstance. It seems one could argue that an Aguilar-Spinelli standard should be a minimum requirement in warrantless cases. This is because there is no intervening protection of a magistrate’s scrutiny for determination of probable cause, which is the reason such deference is shown to hearsay informants’ statements that appear in affidavits for search warrants. In the warrantless case there is no affidavit, there is no scrutiny by a magistrate, and there is no protection for the person subject to the search or arrest in the form of a record from which a subsequent court could review the appropriateness of the officer’s behavior.

IV. Identifying the Anonymous Informant

A. Sources of the Law

In Oregon, an evidentiary “informant’s privilege” is established by statute in the Oregon Evidence Code, ORS 40.275, OEC 510:

“(1) As used in this section, ‘unit of government’ means the federal government or any state or political subdivision thereof.

(2) A unit of government has a privilege to refuse to disclose the identity of a person who has furnished information relating to or assisting in an investigation of a possible violation of law to a law enforcement officer or member of a legislative committee or its staff conducting an investigation.

(3) The privilege created by this section may be claimed by an appropriate representative of the unit of government if the information was furnished to an officer thereof.

(4) No privilege exists under this section:

(a) If the identity of the informer or the informer’s interest in the subject matter of the communication has been disclosed to those who would have cause to resent the communication by a holder of the privilege or by the informer’s own action, or if the informer appears as a witness for the unit of government.

(b) If it appears from the evidence in the case or from other showing by a party that an informer may be able to give testimony necessary to a fair determination of the issue of guilt or innocence in a criminal case or of a material issue on the merits in a civil case to which the unit of governments is a party, and the unit of government invokes the privilege, and the judge gives the unit of government an opportunity to show, in camera facts relevant to determining whether the informer can, in fact, supply that testimony. The showing will ordinarily be in the form of affidavits, but the judge may direct that testimony be taken if the judge finds that the matter cannot be resolved satisfactorily upon affidavit. If the judge finds that there is a reasonable probability that the informer can give the testimony, and the unit of government elects not to disclose identity of the informer, the judge on motion of the defendant in a criminal case shall dismiss the charges to which the testimony would relate, and the judge may do so on the judge’s own motion. In civil cases, the judge may make any order that justice requires. Evidence submitted to the judge shall be sealed and preserved to be made available to the appellate court in the event of an appeal, and the contents shall not otherwise by revealed without consent of the unit of government. All counsel and parties shall be permitted to be present at every stage of proceedings under this paragraph except a showing in camera, at which no counsel or party shall be permitted to be present.”

(c) If information from an informer is relied upon to establish the legality of the means by which evidence was obtained and the judge is not satisfied that the information was received from an informer reasonably believed to be reliable or credible, the judge may require the identity of the informer to be disclosed. The judge shall, on request of the unit of government, direct that the disclosure be made in camera. All counsel and parties concerned with the issue of legality shall be permitted to be present at every stage of proceedings under this paragraph except a disclosure in camera, at which no counsel or party shall be permitted to be present. If disclosure of the identity of the informer is made in camera, the record thereof shall be sealed and preserved to be made available to the appellate court in the event of an appeal, and the contents shall not otherwise be revealed without consent of the unit of government.”

A discovery privilege is also established by statute. ORS 135.873 provides:

(1) Upon a showing of good cause, the court may at any time order that specified disclosures be denied, restricted or deferred, or make such other order as is appropriate.

(2) Upon request of any party, the court may permit a showing of good cause for denial or regulation of disclosures, or portion of such showing, to be made in camera. A record shall be made of such proceedings.

(3) If the court enters an order granting relief following a showing in camera, the entire record of the showing shall be sealed and preserved in the records of the court, to be made available to the appellate court in the event of an appeal. The trial court, in its discretion, may, after trial and conviction, unseal matters previously sealed.”

OEC 510 represents a codification of the “informant’s privilege” as it had been established in case law up to 1980 when the code was formulated. See Kirkpatrick, Reforming Evidence Law in Oregon, 59 OLR 43, at 70, footnote 141 (1980). ORS 135.873 simply establishes the same rule in a discovery context.

The “informant’s privilege” is basically an evidentiary privilege against giving out the name or personal information about a confidential informant.

The privilege belongs to the state or “governmental unit” in statutory terms, and not to the informant or to the witness. See State ex rel Kerns v. Reed, 266 Or 382, 513 P2d 1160 (1973), and OEC 510(2).

The “informant’s privilege” is a common-law evidentiary rule which came to constitutional prominence with the identification of a defendant’s Sixth Amendment interest in the matter by the United States Supreme Court. In Roviaro v. United States, 353 US 53, 77 S Ct 623, 1 LEd2d 639 (1957), the Supreme Court indicated that when an unnamed informant might be able to offer evidence on the matter of the defendant’s guilt or innocence, the common law “informant’s privilege” would be overridden by the defendant’s Sixth Amendment right of confrontation. In a later case, McCray v. Illinois, 386 US 300, 87 S Ct 1474, 18 LEd2d 62 (1967), the Supreme Court indicated that where the informant’s privilege was raised by the state with regard to questions involving issues of probable cause, and not guilt or innocence, the informant’s privilege would stand unless the defendant could show that the informant’s identity was necessary to establish probable cause. The Court suggested that in this context the Sixth Amendment was not involved—it was merely a question of the Fourth Amendment protection against illegal search and seizure, which was satisfied by the sworn testimony of officers indicating the factual background on which they relied for probable cause.

The Oregon Supreme Court followed the lead of the United States Supreme Court interpreting these questions as matters of Fourth Amendment and Sixth Amendment concern. See State v. Penney, 242 Or 470, 410 P2d 226 (1966); State v. Cortman, 251 Or 566, 446 P2d 681 (1968); and State v. Williams, 253 Or 613, 456 P2d 497 (1969).

Whether the identity of a CRI must be disclosed is governed by OEC 510(1), which provides that the state has the privilege to refuse to disclose the identity of an informant. That privilege can be extinguished only if the three criteria specified in OEC 510(4)(b) are satisfied and the court finds that a reasonable probability exists that the informant will be able to give necessary testimony. First, it must appear from evidence in the case or another showing by defendant that the informant “may be able to give testimony necessary to a fair determination of the issue of guilt or innocence in a criminal case. Second, the state must invoke the privilege. Third, the court must give the state “an opportunity to show in camera facts relevant to determining whether the informer can, in fact, supply that testimony.” Without some evidence about what the informer would testify, the court had no factual basis for finding that the identity needed to be disclosed. '''' State v. Juhl, 150 Or App 585, 946 P2d 683 (1997).

OEC 510 fairly gives the state of the law at the time of the adoption of the Oregon Evidence Code.

B. How to Get the Informant’s Identity and Testimony

Frustrating aspects of dealing with informants include the inability to establish who the informant is, getting the informant into court to testify, or even discovering whether or not the informant exists. The problem is excruciatingly set out in State v. Waterbury, 50 Or App 115, 622 P2d 497 (1981).

In Waterbury, a search warrant was based on what the state claimed was a “private search” by the unnamed informant. The defendant could in no way rebut the statements of the officer who obtained the search warrant as to whether or not the private search by the informant involved governmental action because the defendant had no access to the informant. The Court of Appeals, relying on McCray v. Illinois, supra, affirmed the trial court’s denial of defendant’s motion to reveal the name of the informant and produce the informant. The court held, in essence, that the defendant’s Fourth Amendment interests were sufficiently protected by the police officer/affiant’s sworn statements as balanced against the state’s interest in withholding the informant’s identity.

The following discussion describes how counsel can at least try to gain identification of the informant and production of the informant, and the remedies for failure to produce where the court orders production but the state declines to produce or identify.

1. Procedural Questions

Where the defendant desires the identity of an informant, a pretrial motion for an order that the state reveal the informant’s identity and location and produce the informant should be filed. See ORS 135.873. Failure to ask for such an order by way of specific motion may waive the issue for appeal. See State v. Varney, 244 Or 583, 419 P2d 431 (1966). The motion should be accompanied by an affidavit of the defendant and any other relevant witnesses, personally, raising every possible ground for necessity of production and identification of the informant. See State v. Wright, 266 Or 163, 511 P2d 1223 (1973); State v. Varney, supra; State v. Vance, 24 Or App 283, 545 P2d 604 (1976); and State v. Elliott, 276 Or 99, 533 P2d 1058 (1976).

An evidentiary hearing should also be requested so that the defendant can cross-examine the appropriate police officer with regard to knowledge of the whereabouts of the informant and control of the informant.

An example of the procedural problem is set out in State v. Elliott, supra. In Elliott, the defendant moved for the production of an informant, and his lawyer made a claim that the defendant would assert an entrapment defense if he had the informant’s testimony. It was shown that the state had sent the informant (who was named) out of the state and was keeping him there and unavailable as part of the “informant’s privilege.” The defendant did not testify at any hearing nor did the defendant offer an affidavit with regard to the need for the informant’s testimony. In the face of the defendant’s neglect to raise the issue personally (and noting that the defendant had a privilege that his statements made in preliminary hearing could not be used against him), the Supreme Court affirmed the defendant’s conviction. (It is interesting to note that because the state had revealed the name of the informant prior to the hearing, the evidentiary privilege as is established in OEC 510 may not have applied since that privilege had been waived. Elliott seems to hold that there is a broader privilege applying to production, as well as identity, which is to be examined by balancing the defendant’s constitutional interests with the state’s interests in the privilege of not producing.) Elliott points out one of the problems with the “informant’s privilege”—the evidentiary nature of the informant’s privilege is actually of little note. It would be the rare case that simply the name of the informant is of any evidentiary value whatever. It is clear, however, that the name and location of the informant are of substantive value for the purpose of making the informant available as a witness. This is why the pretrial motion is essential to show prejudice. Though the “informant’s privilege” is in the Oregon Evidence Code and is stated as an evidentiary matter, it will be difficult for a defendant at trial to show how the failure of an officer to answer a question as to the informant’s identity or location based on the informant’s privilege has denied him a right to confrontation because the informant’s name and location are of little substantive evidence value in and of themselves.

2. Grounds for Demanding Identity

OEC 510 and the federal and state case law set out two separate lines of authority with regard to stating grounds for production of the name, location and person of an informant. These are: a) for purposes of confrontation as to the matter of guilt or innocence; and b) for purposes of questioning whether or not probable cause is established.

a. Questions of Guilt or Innocence

The fundamental “informant’s privilege” case in federal case law is Rovario v. United States, supra. This case established the basic rule that the defendant’s confrontation interest under the Sixth Amendment overrode the government’s interest in protecting the identity of informants. However, it has long been held that it is the defendant’s burden to show the relevance of the informant’s testimony. The defendant must make the best showing possible of how the informant’s testimony will support his defense. See State v. Elliott, supra; State v. Gill, 22 Or App 484, 539 P2d 1138 (1975); State v. Braun, 22 Or App 592, 540 P2d 1029 (1975); State v. Smith, 40 Or App 91, 594 P2d 860 (1979); and State v. Dunning, 81 Or App 296, 724 P2d 924 (1986). A barren claim by the defendant that the defendant was entrapped or that the informant would testify that they planted the evidence will not be sufficient to gain the informant’s identity or production. See State v. Gill, supra, where the defendant’s testimony as to entrapment was insufficient; and State v. Braun, supra, where the defendant’s assertion that the informant planted the drugs was insufficient to establish a need for the informant’s identity.

Under OEC 510, the informer’s privilege may be overcome only if the informant can provide evidence useful to the defense. State v. Vatland, 123 Or App 577, 860 P2d 820 (1993).

b. The Issue of Probable Cause

The defendant’s weakest argument for the need of production of an informant is the claim that the informant is needed to establish that there was no probable cause. This is the issue discussed in McCray v. Illinois, supra, and in the leading Oregon cases of State v. Penney, supra; State v. Cortman, supra; and State v. Williams, supra. In these cases the balance between the defendant’s constitutional interests and the state’s interest in keeping the identity of the informant secret is struck heavily in favor of the state. However, it is possible to prevail in these cases and it is always worth the effort if there is some basis or showing that the informant’s testimony would be relevant.

An example of how an effort made can end with a good result, even in the face of overwhelming odds, is demonstrated in State v. Swain-Goldsmith, 267 Or 527, 555 P2d 443 (1974), facts contained in State v. Swain-Goldsmith, 13 Or App 600, 510 P2d 1341 (1973). In this case, a preliminary hearing was held on a district court information to determine whether or not the defendant should be bound over to circuit court. At the hearing, the defendant attacked the search warrant by motion to suppress on which the seizure of evidence and arrest was based. Defendant produced witnesses who called into question the existence of the informant named by the officer in the officer’s affidavit. The evidence that the defendant produced raised a substantial question in the mind of the judge presiding at the preliminary hearing regarding probable cause. The judge ruled that he would allow cross-examination of the testifying officer about the description of the person who the officer claimed was the unidentified informant. At the judge’s order the state moved to dismiss the information and to further procedurally confuse the matter the court ultimately granted the motion to suppress.

The state subsequently obtained a grand jury indictment in the same case and the defendant was ultimately convicted on the basis of the indictment. The defendant claimed res judicata on the issue of suppression. Ultimately, the Supreme Court determined that the state should have appealed the original suppression order and dismissal by the district court judge and reversed the defendant’s conviction.

The point of this description is to show that despite the uphill burden of trying to impeach an affiant’s claim of probable cause by seeking the informant’s name, it can be a worthwhile exercise if some independent evidence can be found which would, in the first instance, call into question the affiant’s description of the informant’s statements upon which probable cause is based. A trial court order to produce or identify forces the state to dismiss or concede a motion.

C. Remedy for Nondisclosure

As indicated in the preceding section, perhaps the most motivating factor in determining whether or not to demand the identity and production of an unnamed informant is the remedy for nondisclosure. Where the identity is shown to be necessary with regard to questions of guilt or innocence, the remedy is dismissal. OEC 510(b), State v. Fleischman, 10 Or App 22, 495 P2d 277 (1972); State v. Martinez, 97 '''' Or App 170, 776 P2d 3 (1989). If the issue arises in a motion to suppress where the defendant has established to the magistrate’s satisfaction the need for the informant’s identity in order to establish that probable cause existed, and the state refuses to follow the court’s direction, the remedy should be granting of the motion to suppress. ORS 133.703.

In State v. Strance, 118 Or App 645, 848 P2d 1226 (1993), the court held that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in sanctioning the state’s disobedience of its order to submit the CRI’s files for an in camera inspection by striking all information provided by the CRI, rather than dismissing the indictment.

D. Informant’s Identity Under the Oregon Constitution

It may have come to the attention of the reader that the case law previously cited is either United States constitutional law or interpretation of statute. Although Oregon’s confrontation provision in Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution is occasionally mentioned in the context of discussions of the informant’s privilege, see, e.g., Waterbury, 50 Or App 115, the cases cited and the discussion presented are always in terms of the statute, the Fourth and Sixth Amendments to the United States Constitution, or the cases interpreting those provisions.

This raises questions which allow the Oregon practitioner to seek a different treatment of the “informant’s privilege” under the Oregon Constitution. For instance:

1. Identity Desired for Guilt-Innocence Purposes

Does Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution allow any balancing—where the defendant insists he requires the informant’s testimony to establish his defense and makes some factual showing that this is the case? Article I, section 11, clearly provides more protection to the defendant than the provisions of the Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution. In State ex rel Gladden v. Lonergan, 201 Or 163, 269 P2d 491 (1954), the Oregon Supreme Court held that Article I, section 11, gives a defendant a right to meet his own witnesses face to face and to examine them orally in the presence of the court and jury. Furthermore, it is stated that “we have the firm opinion that importance must be attached, and the fact given...[to this right]....” Id. at 201 Or 176–77. Moreover, the same case provides that this provision “gives no rights to the state. The rights so guaranteed to the accused may be waived by him but they cannot be denied him. As to the right to have witnesses in his favor attend before court and jury to testify orally on his behalf, accused’s further right to compulsory process affords the means for its enforcement.” Id.

Thus, there is the question as to whether the state can prevent the defendant from calling a witness in his favor claiming an “informant’s privilege” in the face of a direct constitutional guarantee that he will be able to call such witnesses.

2. Identity Desired for Probable Cause Purposes

A second question has to do with motions to suppress. McCray v. Illinois, supra, limits the application of Sixth Amendment rights in the case where Fourth Amendment questions are being decided. There appears to be no Oregon case which concludes that the defendant’s right to confrontation under Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution does not apply to pretrial evidentiary hearings in Oregon, such as in a suppression hearing. Thus, where the defendant is presented with a situation such as the defendant in State v. Elliott, supra (where the defendant’s only opportunity to impeach the sheriff’s testimony would come through a disclosure of the only other witness, the unnamed informant), does the defendant have a right of confrontation under the Oregon Constitution? This question appears not to have been discussed in the case law.

3. Conclusion

There is fertile ground for establishing additional rights of defendants as to the whereabouts, production and name of unnamed informants under the Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution that has yet to be explored. Furthermore, no arguments have been made with regard to the defendant’s potential rights under Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution. The practitioner should not fail to raise these questions any time the identity or production of an informant is being sought.
