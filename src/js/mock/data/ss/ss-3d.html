I. Test for Consent: Actual, Voluntary Consent to the Specific Search

“Voluntary consent is a recognized exception to the warrant requirement, and the state bears the burden of proving that consent was obtained validly and voluntarily.” State v. Edgell, 153 Or App 108, 111, 956 P2d 988 (1998).

The Oregon Supreme Court “has described consent to a search as an ‘exception’ to the warrant requirement under Article I, section 9 * * * not because we excuse the failure to obtain a warrant for an exceptional reason, but because consent relinquishes a person’s privacy interest in property so that there is no unlawful intrusion under Article I, section 9.” State v. Bonilla, 358 Or 475, 480, 366 P3d 331 (2015).

Thus, a defendant’s voluntary consent can justify a warrantless search of his person or property. To determine whether a defendant’s consent is voluntary, the court will examine “‘whether, in the light of the totality of the circumstances, [the] defendant’s consent was a product of his own free will or was the result of coercion, express or implied.’” Id.; see also State v. Wolfe, 295 Or 567, 572, 669 P2d 320 (1983) (stating standard).

The trial courts should look to a variety of factors for guidance to determine whether the defendant’s consent was voluntary or was a result of coercion, such as “whether physical force was used or threatened”; “whether weapons were displayed”; “whether the consent was obtained in public”; “whether the person who gives consent is the subject of an investigation”; “and whether the atmosphere surrounding the consent [was] antagonistic or oppressive[.]” State v. Larson, 141 Or App 186, 198, 917 P2d 519 (1996). The court must view the voluntariness of consent from the perspective of a reasonable person in the defendant’s circumstances. State v. Marshall, 254 Or App 419, 428, 295 P3d 128 (2013).

In the context of confessions, the court has explained:

“Voluntariness requires that neither duress nor intimidation, hope nor inducement caused the defendant to confess. The test for voluntariness under Article I, section 12, is whether, under the totality of the circumstances, the waiver of rights and the confession were the product of an essentially free, unconstrained and informed choice or whether the accused’s capacity for self-determination was critically impaired. Threats, promises, inducements, and similar overreaching by the police have consistently led to suppression of a defendant’s resulting statements and confessions; by contrast, in the absence of police overreaching, challenges to the voluntariness of a defendant’s statements or confessions have consistently failed. Under Article I, section 12, police overreaching is an essential predicate of a challenge to the admissibility of a statement or a confession as involuntary.”

State v. Tanner, 236 Or App 423, 431, 236 P3d 775 (2010) (internal quotation marks, citations, and alterations omitted; emphases added).

II. Burden of Proof

The state has the burden of proving, by a preponderance of the evidence, that a defendant’s consent to search is “voluntary.” State v. Williamson, 307 Or 621, 625, 772 P2d 404, 406 (1989).

Importantly, this means that the state may not place on the defendant the burden of producing evidence of lack of consent. Thus in '' State v. Blair, 361 Or 527, 539, 396 P3d 908 (2017), the Supreme Court rejected the state’s “default rule” that consent to search an area always includes consent to search closed containers within it: “In the absence of evidence bearing on the scope of consent, the evidence could be deemed to be in equipoise, resulting in the conclusion that the state had failed to meet its burden of persuasion.”Id''. In other words, the state’s burden includes the burden of proving the fact of consent, the validity of consent, and the scope of the consent granted.

III. Standard of Review

Appellate courts are bound by the trial court’s findings of historical fact, so long as there is constitutionally sufficient evidence to support them; however, the determination of whether consent was voluntary is a legal issue that is reviewed independently with no deference given to the trial court’s conclusion. State v. Acremant, 338 Or 302, 324, 108 P3d 1139 (2005).

IV. Scope of Consent

A. Containers within Containers

The scope of a grant of consent is a purely factual question for the trial court to resolve. State v. Winn, 361 Or 636, 396 P3d 926 (2017); Blair, 361 Or at 539. Those cases expressly hold that whether a defendant’s consent to the search of a single closed container (such as a purse) extends to additional closed containers within that container is a factual question: Did the defendant actually intend to relinquish his or her privacy interest in the second container? In determining whether a particular search falls within the scope of defendant’s consent, the trial court will determine, based on the totality of circumstances, what the defendant actually intended.

B. Scope Limited by the Understood Purpose of Search

Scope of consent was explored in State v. Quale, 225 Or App 461, 201 P3d 273 (2009). There, the defendant consented to a search of his person for weapons. The arresting officer found a piece of foil in defendant’s jacket pocket. Inside the foil the officer discovered opium. The Court of Appeals reasoned that defendant’s consent “extended to opening the foil only if, in the eyes of a reasonable person, the foil could have contained a weapon.” The court concluded that no reasonable person could have concluded that the foil could have contained a weapon.

“Just as the scope of a defendant’s consent turns on what a ‘typical reasonable person would have understood by the exchange between the officer and the suspect[,]’ so, too, does the temporal duration of a given search and, with it, the duration of a suspect’s consent.” State v. Marino, 259 Or App 608, 616-17, 314 P3d 984 (2013) (internal citation omitted); see also (the scope of consent “turns on what a reasonable person would have intended”).

By the same token, a defendant’s consent to search a vehicle does not automatically contain consent to search everything inside it. In State v. Sunderman, 304 Or App 329, 467 P3d 52 (2020), an officer stopped a driver and ultimately searched her purse and found the evidence at issue. During the stop, the officer asked for and obtained consent to search the defendant’s car. But the officer could not remember exactly how he asked for consent, or her exact response other than that she gave him consent to search the car and did not believe she placed any limitations on it. 304 Or App at 334. The defendant testified, on the other hand, that after she gave consent to search the car, she placed her purse outside it and explicitly denied consent to search it. Id. The Court of Appeals held that the state “failed to offer sufficient evidence to prove that [the] defendant actually consented to a search of her purse” when she consented to a search of the car. Id. at 336.

2024 Update

Police exceeded the scope of consent in State v. Soprych, 324 Or App 659 (March 22, 2023). There, police responded to a report that the defendant had “pistol whipped” his roommate. The defendant denied doing so and gave police consent to search the house for “guns” or “weapons.” The defendant’s bedroom door was locked, and police asked if they could “look in there[.]” The defendant replied, “Yeah, do you want me to unlock it?” and pointed the officers to his keys, which were lying on the ground. The police then used the key to open the bedroom lock and used a different key on the ring to open a safe inside the bedroom, where they found evidence of a crime.

The Court of Appeals held that those facts were insufficient to establish that defendant had intended to consent to a search of the locked safe in his bedroom. Specifically, the court reasoned, “When one person hands another their key ring for the purpose of using a specific key to open a specific lock, it is not reasonable to infer that the owner of the keys intends to consent broadly to using other keys on different locks; a person using other keys on the ring for other purposes would transgress social norms.” Id. at 665.

V. Mere Acquiescence

“Mere acquiescence” to police authority is not voluntary consent. State v. Davis, 133 Or App 467, 474, 891 P2d 1373 (2005). Such acquiescence occurs when an individual is not given a reasonable opportunity to choose to consent, or when he or she is informed that a search will occur regardless of whether consent is given. State v. Guzman, 164 Or App 90, 99, 990 P2d 370 (1999).

State v. Briggs, 102 Or App 647, 796 P2d 656 (1990), is illustrative. In that case, a police officer told the defendant that he was at her residence “to pick up the marijuana plants that she was growing.” Id. at 649, 796 P2d 656 (internal quotation marks omitted). When the defendant did not respond, the officer repeated that he was there to pick up the plants and that he “wanted to do it as calmly [and] efficiently as possible” (Id., internal quotation marks omitted; brackets in original). The court held that the officer’s statement could not be characterized as a request for consent:

“The words used in the first phrase are unconditional; they do not invite a response other than acquiescence. In contrast, the words in the second phrase are supplicatory and do invite a response. Read together, the officer’s statement told defendant that she had no choice whether a search would occur; her only option was whether the search and seizure was to be ‘calm and efficient.’”

Id. at 652.

When an officer states an intent to enter a space, and the person holding a privacy interest says something like, “Go ahead,” that likely constitutes mere acquiescence rather than consent, because the officer did not request consent. For example, in State v. Stanley, 287 Or App 399, 404 P3d 1100 (2017), police responded to a call from the victim alleging that defendant had attacked her. The victim said she was upstairs and “felt safe” and defendant was waiting for the police outside. Defendant was calm and compliant. One officer said, “I’m going to go inside and check” on the victim, and defendant said, “Go on ahead. She’s inside.” The officer later testified that he entered the house to see if anyone was injured and “to make sure the victim was okay.” Police discovered evidence of the assault in the home. The Court of Appeals concluded that defendant did not consent to police entering his home. The officer’s declaratory statement that he was going inside was “not a request for consent to enter the house and invited no response other than acquiescence.” Id. at 4.

Mere acquiescence also does not constitute consent to search simply because the defendant puts him or herself voluntarily into a situation where there is bound to be police contact. In State v. Powell, 288 Or App 660, 406 P3d 1111, the defendant heard that there had been a shooting at a high school where his younger sister attended, so he headed there to check on his sister—with a handgun tucked into his pants’ waistband. Officers were conducting weapons checks on everyone in the area, and the defendant acquiesced to that weapons check. The Supreme Court held that the defendant’s submission to the search did not constitute voluntary consent. 288 Or at 669. It reasoned that, even if the defendant knew that police contact “was bound to happen[,]” the state failed to establish that the defendant also reasonably would have understood that he would be subjecting himself to a search and seizure by arriving outside the school. Id.

VI. Authority to Consent

A. Third Party Consent to Search of Personal Property

Where a person other than the defendant consents to a search of the defendant’s property, the state must prove that the person who granted the consent had actual authority to do so. Edgell, 153 Or App at 12. Apparent authority is no justification; there is no “good faith” exception under Article I, section 9. State v. Carsey, 295 Or 32, 644 P2d 1085 (1983).

Therefore the state must prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that someone having the actual authority to do so voluntarily gave police consent to search the defendant’s property and that any limitations on the scope of the consent were complied with. Bonilla, 358 Or at 480. In Bonilla, officers entered a converted storage area of a garage and searched a wooden box within that area. The officers believed that the occupants who consented to that search had the authority to do so. But because the people who consented did not actually have that authority, the Supreme Court held that the search of the wooden box was not justified under the consent exception to the warrant requirement. 358 Or at 495.

A cohabitant may consent to search of an area over which he or she shares common authority with the person having a privacy interest. With respect to cohabitants, the court looks “at whether the effects are communally used by the parties * * * or exclusively used by one party.” State v. Fuller, 158 Or App 501, 506, 976 P2d 1137 (1999). For example, in State v. Carrillo, 304 Or App 192, 466 P3d 1023 (2020), the defendant shared his home (that he owned) with his ex-wife, and the two shared the bedroom as well. The ex-wife allowed officers to search the bedroom for evidence that the defendant had previously shot bullets into the bed while she had been lying in it. The officers searched the bed, carpet, and some items under the bed and found bullet holes. 304 Or App at 197. The Court of Appeals held that, while common authority over a space does not necessarily equate to common authority over all items within the space, Id. at 198, in that case the state had met its burden of proving that the ex-wife had authority over the items searched: She had been living in the house for six years, slept in the bed and shared the bedroom, kept her clothes in the bedroom closet, and had her personal items all over the bedroom. Id. at 199.

In Edgell, although the driver had actual authority to permit a search of the vehicle, the driver did not have actual authority to consent to a search of the defendant’s bag; thus the evidence found therein should have been suppressed. Edgell, 153 Or App at 12. See also State v. Barndt, 68 Or App 755, 683 P2d 166 (1984) (holding that search of the defendant’s bag was conducted without consent, where third party’s consent to search the truck in which the bag was found did not extend to defendant’s closed athletic bag, officer knew that not all luggage belonged to person who gave him consent, and defendant gave no separate consent to search).

Presence in, and control over, a vehicle does not necessarily translate to authority to consent to a search of the vehicle.

In State v. Kurokawa-Lasciak III, 249 Or App 435, 278 P3d 38 (2012), the defendant’s girlfriend did not have actual authority to consent to search the defendant’s van. The girlfriend had been left in charge of the van as the defendant was getting arrested and after he had declined consent to search. He instructed her to lock the van, take care of the dog, and wait for him to get back. Under Article I, section 9, the court held, such limited authority does not extend to the authority to grant consent to a search.

Instead, when determining whether a person has implicit authority to consent to a search of property they do not own, courts look to the circumstances of the person’s control over the property—and the important question is whether the state can establish that there was an agreement between the owner and the controller that would expand to authority to consent to a search. For example, in State v. Solorio, 304 Or App 666, 468 P3d 522 (2020), officers searched a safe that was inside the defendant’s van while the defendant was in jail and the van was in control of his girlfriend. The girlfriend told the officers that they could search the safe, and that she had the combination and knew what was inside it. But when she tried to open it, the combination appeared to be wrong (so the officers pried the safe open). 304 Or App at 670. The Court of Appeals held that the state failed to prove that the girlfriend had authority to consent to a search of the safe. Id. at 677. It reasoned that, when evaluating third-party consent, courts look to the relationship of the third party to the premises or things searched. Id. at 676. And in that case, the girlfriend’s relationship to the safe did not appear to include authority to access it—even if she had been allowed to access it in the past. Because the state did not prove that the defendant gave his girlfriend authority to consent to a search of the safe—even though he allowed her to use his van—the consent exception based on her “consent” did not authorize the search of the safe. Id. See also State v. Jones, 280 Or App 135, 380 P3d 1132 (2016) (consent to search a van did not extend to consent to search containers, which belonged to someone else, inside the van).

Officers may not search a minor under the consent exception based on the parents’ consent to the search. Matter of H.K.D.S., 305 Or App 86, 469 P3d 770 (2020). In H.K.D.S., officers obtained a DNA sample from a 12-year-old juvenile to investigate whether his seminal fluid was on his four-year-old stepsister’s underwear. The youth complied with the swab after the officers described the process to him and his mother answered that it was “okay.” 305 Or App at 88. The Court of Appeals held that the search was not justified under the consent exception, and violated Article I, section 9. Id. at 101-02. The court reasoned that, while parents can and should be able to ask for police assistance, “A child suspected of a crime and facing a potential criminal prosecution—along with a lifetime of potential attendant consequences—is entitled to receive the same protection that Article I, section 9, indisputably provides adults.” Id. at 101.

B. Third-Party Consent to Enter a Residence

“[A] warrantless search of a shared dwelling for evidence over the express refusal of consent by a physically present resident cannot be justified as reasonable as to him on the basis of consent given to the police by another resident.” Georgia v. Randolph, 547 US 103, 120, 126 S Ct 1515, 164 L Ed 2d 208 (2006). In State v. Caster, 236 Or App 214, 234 P3d 1087 (2010), the Court of Appeals held that under the Fourth Amendment, arresting the defendant did not vitiate his previously registered objection to a search of his home, and the co-occupant’s subsequent consent to the search was ineffective as to defendant.

Although under United States v. Matlock, 415 US 164, 94 S Ct 988, 39 L Ed 2d 242 (1974), consent by a co-occupant is generally sufficient to justify a search of premises, that case did not involve a second physically present occupant’s refusal to permit entry permission to search. Caster, 236 Or App at 220.

The holdings in Matlock and Randolph both turned significantly upon the Court’s understanding of society’s shared social expectations. Cohabitation, the Court noted, is subject to certain commonly held understandings—including the understanding that co-occupants “assum[e] the risk” that “any one of them may admit visitors, with the consequence that a guest obnoxious to one may nevertheless be admitted in [that occupant’s] absence by another.” Randolph, 547 US at 111.

Thus, the dispositive question turned on the “reasonableness of police entry in reliance on consent by one occupant subject to immediate challenge by another”—in terms of whether there exists a “common understanding” regarding the rights of disagreeing co-occupants. On that question the court held, “there is no common understanding that one co-tenant generally has a right or authority to prevail over the express wishes of another, whether the issue is the color of the curtains or invitations to outsiders.” Caster, 236 Or App at 221-22 (quoting Randolph, 547 US at 114).

“In analyzing third-party consent to search under Article I, section 9, the Oregon courts have not specifically adopted the bright-line rule announced in Randolph. State v. Wynne, 258 Or App 787, 793, n 3, 311 P3d 978 (2013).

VII. Circumstances Rendering Consent Involuntary

A. False Promises (Illusory Inducements)

State v. Marshall, 254 Or App 419, 428, 295 P3d 128 (2013): The defendant refused to consent to a search of a trailer unless he had it “in writing” that he would not be charged with a crime related to the contents of the trailer. In response, the officer modified a consent document to state that defendant “is not responsible for the property stored inside the RV trailer.” The Court of Appeals concluded that defendant reasonably believed he was being promised immunity and relied on that promise in giving his consent. The court explained:

“[W]e need not determine here whether, if ever, the provision of some real inducement as consideration for consent to search could ever abrogate the voluntariness of such consent because here, at least, Wilson’s false inducement subverted defendant’s autonomous choice, rendering his purported consent involuntary.”

Id. at 433.

B. Threat to Obtain a Warrant Without Probable Cause

In State v. Berg, 223 Or App 387, 196 P3d 547 (2008),the police requested consent to search mother’s residence. Mother refused consent. The officer then told her that “he would be requesting a search warrant.” He further stated that, “during his request for the warrant, she would not have free roam of the property” and that “he would seal the property until he had obtained the warrant. Mother consented to the search.

On appeal, the defendant argued that the officer did not have the authority to threaten to “seal the property” while he tried to obtain a warrant. That argument was not made below and the court refused to entertain it on appeal. The court held that the state met its burden of showing that the consent was not “mere acquiescence.”

In State v. Hirsch, 267 Or 613, 622, 518 P2d 649 (1974), where the officer had probable cause sufficient to justify the issuance of a warrant,

“‘The officer’s threat that he would obtain a warrant if defendant did not consent to the search did not constitute the kind of coercion that renders a search involuntary. Concededly such a threat may be coercive in the sense that an accused would not have consented to the search in the absence of the threat. But not all coercion inducing consent to a search is constitutionally impermissible. If the officers threaten only to do what the law permits them to do, the coercion that the threat may produce is not constitutionally objectionable.’”

Id. (citing State v. Douglas, 260 Or 60, 488 P2d 1366 (1971)).

See also State v. Rodal, 161 Or App 232, 242, (1999) (if officers threaten only to do what the law permits them to do, “the coercion that the threat may produce is not constitutionally objectionable.”); State v. Barber, 279 Or App 84, 379 P3d 651 (2016) (armed with reasonable suspicion that the defendant possessed drugs, the officer could lawfully threaten that he would call for drug dog unless the defendant consented to a search of the vehicle).

C. Threats or Material Misrepresentations

In State v. Williamson, 307 Or 621, 627, 772 P2d 404 (1989) (Carson, J., concurring),where police lack probable cause, the officer “could not lawfully assert that he would detain the pickup; his statement that he would do so was a material misrepresentation of what the law permitted, consequently invalidating defendant’s consent to search. See also State v. Hall, 339 Or 7, 21, 115 P3d 908 (2005) (validating “material misrepresentation” argument).

In State v. Powelson, 154 Or App 266, 961 P2d 869 (1998), police were conducting a “knock and talk” at defendant’s residence. Once the defendant allowed the police inside, they told the defendant they had information that he was growing marijuana inside. They asked for consent to search, and defendant asked them what would happen if he refused. Police said that they would either put him in a holding cell pending a request for a warrant or detain him at his residence while they applied. Defendant consented. The court held that the officer’s actions constituted an unlawful seizure of his person. The unlawful police conduct in this case rendered his consent involuntary.

And in Berg, 223 Or App at 387, an officer threatened to seal the defendant’s mother’s home while he sought a search warrant. That threat, the defendant argued, constituted a material misrepresentation of what the law permitted because the officer “lacked authority to seize the property and oust [the defendant’s mother] from it.” Such a misrepresentation, according to defendant, rendered the consent involuntary. The state conceded that consent to search may be rendered involuntary if given in response to an officer’s false claim of authority. However, the court did not resolve the issue, concluding that the defendant had failed to preserve it.

D. Overbearing Police Conduct

In State v. Magana/Rivera, 257 Or App 251, vac’d and rem’d, 354 Or 837, 325 P3d 738 (2014),[1] The Court of Appeals concluded that the show of force in this case rendered the defendant’s consent involuntary:

“There were five officers present at the apartment and a narcotics canine. All of the officers were wearing ballistic vests. Two of those officers were visible from the front door of the apartment. When the police knocked on the door, the defendant cracked it open, saw them standing outside the apartment, and immediately slammed the door without saying anything. Despite that clear message, the officers continued to knock and identify themselves. Magana opened the door again, stepped outside the apartment, and attempted to close the door. As Magana was closing the door, one officer told Magana to put his hands up and patted him down. The officers did not explain why they were at the apartment. Instead, immediately after the pat-down, a visibly armed police officer asked Magana if the officers could search his apartment with the narcotics canine.

“Although the trial court found that Magana was ‘calm’ when he consented to the search, the atmosphere surrounding his consent was aggressive and intimidating: First, the officers made a repeated effort to talk to Magana after he slammed the door. Second, once Magana opened the door, the officers had Magana put his hands up and searched him. Third, the officers immediately asked for consent to search the apartment for drugs with a narcotics dog, without explaining any other reason why they were at the apartment. Last, Magana’s consent was obtained while he was in the presence of several officers wearing ballistic vests, with the implication that at least one other officer and the dog were also present for the search and that Magana was the subject of investigation. We conclude that that combination of circumstances made the interaction implicitly coercive.”

In Wolfe, 295 Or at 567, the Supreme Court held that the flagrancy of police misconduct in questioning an in-custody defendant without Miranda warnings was a key factor in vitiating consent, even after Miranda warnings were given.

In State v. Burdick, 57 Or App 601, 646 P2d 91 (1982), the Court of Appeals held that deceptive practices designed to secure permission to search and confession from in-custody defendant, including staging false identification witness, falsifying reports about defendant’s incriminating statements, accusing defendant’s wife of having affair with victim, and implied suggestions that cooperation by defendant might lead to lower charges rendered involuntary the defendant’s consent to search.

In State v. Ward, 37 Or App 591, 588 P2d 72 (1978), aff’d per curiam at 289 Or 211 (1980), the court held that police misconduct, including illegal entry and five-hour illegal detention, were key factors in invalidating consent to search.

E. Knowledge of Right to Refuse

The police need not advise the defendant of his right to refuse consent to search. United States v. Watson, 423 US 411, 96 S Ct 820, 46 LEd2d 598 (1976); State v. Flores, 280 Or 273, 570 P2d 965 (1977). However, whether the defendant knew of his right to refuse to give consent is always a factor in the totality of circumstances analysis. Schneckloth v. Bustamonte, 412 US 218, 93 S Ct 2041, 36 LEd2d 854 (1973) (listing factors weighing against voluntariness of consent including the lack of any advice to the accused of his constitutional rights, his lack of education, the youth of the accused and his low intelligence level); State v. Gaither, 76 Or App 201, 708 P2d 646 (1985) (stating failure of police to advise the defendant of his right to refuse consent to search is only one factor in the analysis).

However, police conduct can convey that the defendant may not refuse consent, and therefore the defendant is merely acquiescing to police authority. In Matter of Nelson, 299 Or App 62, 447 P3d 1212 (2019), officers entered the home where the defendant had been staying as a guest, shortly after the defendant had reportedly driven intoxicated and violated a restraining order while out. Another occupant of the home allowed the officers to come inside, and they encountered the defendant outside his bedroom door, in the middle of the night, when he was awakened from sleep by his daughter telling him the police were there. The officers never asked the homeowner for consent to enter the bedroom where the defendant was staying. Instead, they asked the homeowner, “Are we good?” when they had entered the bedroom. 299 Or App at 75. The Court of Appeals held that, under the totality of the circumstances, the state failed to meet its burden to prove the consent exception. Id.

F. Education and Sophistication of Defendant

The Supreme Court held in State v. Quinn, 290 Or 383, 623 P2d 630 (1981), that the fact that the defendant was of low intelligence and was developmentally disabled was considered; but the court ultimately found the consent valid in that case.

G. Voluntary Intoxication

Generally consent has been held voluntary despite intoxication, but cases involving statements rendered involuntary due to intoxication include: State v. Culley, 25 Or App 387, 549 P2d 1130 (1976); State v. Lloyd, 22 Or App 254, 538 P2d 1278 (1975); State v. Robinson, 3 Or App 200, 473 P2d 152 (1970). The test is whether the defendant is able to understand the basic nature of the consent to search.

Practice Tip

Attorneys should renew this issue in light of Blair and Winn when intoxicated defendants give vague answers to consent to search—particularly where the search involves more than one container, or containers within the general space that was the subject of the search. In light of those cases, the state will likely have to prove that the defendant, while intoxicated, understood the scope of the consent to which he or she was agreeing.

VIII. Admissibility of a Refusal to Consent

A defendant’s refusal to consent to a breath test may not be used as evidence of guilt in a criminal proceeding. State v. Banks, 364 Or 332, 434 P3d 361 (2019), reinforced the principle that “the invocation of a constitutional right cannot be admitted at trial as evidence of a defendant’s guilt.” Banks, 364 Or at 334. In the DUII context, because a breath test qualifies as a search in the constitutional sense, Id. at 337, a refusal to consent to a breath test cannot be used as evidence of guilt in a DUII prosecution. Id. at 349. In Banks, police arrested the defendant, read him the implied-consent warnings, and asked him, “[W]ill you take a breath test?” Id. at 334-35. He refused. The defendant moved to suppress his refusal, arguing that its admission would violate the above-stated principle. The trial court denied the motion. The Oregon Supreme Court reversed. The court explained that although asking a person to take a breath test could be either a request for physical cooperation or a request for consent to a search, to admit evidence of a refusal, the state must prove that the request “could reasonably be understood only as a request to provide physical cooperation and not as a request for constitutionally significant consent to search.” Id. at 343 (Emphasis added). The court held that the defendant’s refusal was inadmissible, because the officer’s request was ambiguous as to whether it sought constitutional consent or mere physical cooperation. Id. The court further noted:

 “An individual should be able to act on the presumption that a warrantless search is unreasonable. Permitting the state to adduce evidence of the exercise of that right would place an impermissible burden on its assertion. We reject the state’s argument that because the police had a lawful basis for obtaining a breath sample from defendant without a warrant—probable cause and exigent circumstances—defendant’s refusal to provide consent is admissible as evidence of his guilt.”

Id. at 348-49.

2024 Update

Applying State v. Banks, 364 Or 332, 434 P3d 361 (2019), the state failed to establish “precisely how” the officer asked the defendant to take the breath test, nor did it provide any other information to illuminate the nature of the request. And the defendant's “ambiguous” communication—agreeing to take the breath test but only pretending to blow—was insufficient to establish that the defendant's noncompliance was a conscious refusal to cooperate physically and not a refusal to consent to a warrantless search. Thus, the state failed to meet its burden. State v. Brandes, 317 Or App 672, 506 P3d 431 (2022).

2024 Update

State v. Hamilton, 330 Or App 258 (2024)

Under State v. Banks, 364 Or 332, 434 P3d 361 (2019), refusals to perform FSTs—like refusals to perform breath tests—may not be admitted as evidence of guilt unless the state proves that law enforcement’s requests to perform the tests could be understood only as a request to submit to the physical act, and not as a request that defendant provide constitutionally significant consent to the tests.

In Hamilton, the defendant was charged with two DUIIs.  During the first DUII stop, an officer had asked defendant whether FSTs were “something that you would voluntarily consent to tonight?”  Id. at 269.   She refused.  Id.  During the second DUII stop, officers asked the defendant if she “would be willing to consent to some field sobriety tests” or would “volunteer to do some field sobriety tests,” and she again refused.  Id. at 270.  The Hamilton court held that the state failed to satisfy its burden to prove that both requests could only be understood as requests for physical cooperation, and accordingly, both FST refusals should have been suppressed.  Id. at 269-270.
