I. Officer Safety Stops

Officer-safety stops usually arise when an officer orders an individual to do something, not because they suspect the person has committed a crime, but because they are concerned that the individual may cause the officer harm. They usually take the form of a verbal directive such as “put your hands on the steering wheel”; “take your hands out of your pockets”; or “sit down over here.” But they can also take the form of questions about weapons or pat-downs for weapons.

A. The Bates Standard

What level of justification must police have to “stop” a person, i.e. restrict a person’s liberty for officer-safety reasons?

When an officer develops a reasonable suspicion that a person may pose an “immediate threat of serious physical injury” to the officer or to others present, the officer may take reasonable protective measures, including a limited search or seizure, without a warrant and without violating Article I, section 9. State v. Bates, 304 Or 519, 524, 747 P2d 991 (1987). The inquiry into “reasonableness” requires consideration of the nature and magnitude of the perceived danger and the degree of intrusion resulting from the officer’s conduct. State v. Rickard, 150 Or App 517, 526, 947 P2d 215, rev den, 326 Or 234 (1997). Intuition and generalized fear do not give rise to reasonable suspicion of an immediate threat. State v. Hendricks, 213 Or App 360, 365, 160 P3d 1014 (2007). Rather, there must be specific and articulable facts to justify the officer’s conclusion that a particular person presents an immediate threat of harm. Id.

2024 Update

The standard has both an objective and a subjective component, and the state bears the burden of proof and persuasion for both components. An officer’s subjective concerns must be from an immediate threat. State v. Harmon, 329 Or App 639 (2023) (citing State v. Ramirez, 305 Or App 195, 205, 468 P3d 1006 (2020)).

B. Interference with a Suspect’s Constitutional Privacy and Possessory Interests

What types of interference with a suspect’s constitutional privacy and possessory interests are allowed for officer-safety purposes?

Oregon courts have recognized two degrees of interference with a suspect’s constitutional privacy and possessory interests for officer-safety purposes. First, officers can take “reasonable precautions” for safety during a traffic stop, such as inquiring about weapons, when “the officer has reasonable, circumstance-specific concerns for the officer’s safety or the safety of other persons who are present.” State v. Jimenez, 357 Or 417, 429, 353 P3d 1227 (2015). Notably, that standard prohibits officers from making “routine weapons inquiries in all traffic investigations.” Id. at 430.

Second, under Bates, officers can conduct a pat-down of a defendant if “the officer subjectively believe[s] that the defendant pose[s] a threat” and “that the officer’s belief [is] objectively reasonable.” State v. Rodriguez-Perez, 262 Or App 206, 213, 325 P3d 39 (2014) (emphasis added) (concluding that the fact defendant became “very agitated” during jaywalking stop did not support reasonable belief that the defendant might pose an immediate danger, where the defendant did not make aggressive or threatening movements or statements); see also Jimenez, 357 Or at 429 (noting that the Bates rule applies specifically to warrantless searches for weapons).

In State v. Miler, 363 Or 374, 383, 422 P3d 240, adh’d to as modified on recons, 363 Or 742, 428 P3d 899 (2018), this court refined the rule from Jimenez and held that an officer’s circumstance-specific concerns need not be particular to the individual but may arise from other factors or conditions.

In other words, officers can typically ask questions about weapons based on circumstance-specific officer-safety concerns, and officers can perform pat-down searches for weapons based on defendant-specific officer-safety concerns. But the officer safety doctrine typically does not justify further intrusive searches for weapons. State v. Moreno, 150 Or App 306, 946 P2d 317 (1997).

Furthermore, the officer-safety exception does not typically excuse searches or seizures that occur during unlawful stops. State v. Kreis, 365 Or 659, 451 P3d 954 (2019). ' Kreis held that' “an order that is not consistent with Article I, section 9, or that is issued in violation of that provision, is not a “lawful order” for purposes of ORS 162.247 (1)(b) [interfering with a peace officer].”Kreis'', 365 Or at 669.

There, the defendant was stopped without reasonable suspicion, and during the course of that stop bladed his stance, balled his hands into fists, and declared, “I will not be arrested.” For officer-safety reasons, the officer ordered the defendant to turn around and place his hands behind his back. The defendant refused and was subsequently charged and convicted of interfering with a police officer for refusing to obey a “lawful order.” ORS 162.247 (1)(b).

The defendant argued that the officer-safety doctrine contains an express prerequisite—it “applies only ‘during the course of a lawful encounter.’” Kreis, 365 Or at 673 (quoting Bates, 304 Or at 524). Thus, it could not provide the foundation for a “lawful” order if that order came during the course of an unlawful stop. The Supreme Court agreed, noting that “[t]he officer-safety doctrine is not a general exception to the warrant requirement; it is a rule of necessity that enables officers to take reasonable measures to carry out lawful police activity.” Id. at 676. Accordingly, the court explained, that when an officer’s initial seizure is unlawful, “the officer-safety doctrine does not permit the officer to impose continued, and even more stringent, restraint to effectuate that unlawful seizure.” Id. at 677.

Because the officer had seized the defendant without reasonable suspicion, his subsequent order that the defendant turn and be handcuffed could not have been justified by the officer-safety exception and therefore the order that the defendant disobeyed was not a lawful one. Id. at 677-78.

2024 Update

The rule from State v. Jimenez, 357 Or 417, 353 P3d 1227 (2015), extends beyond weapons inquires to all questions posed during the course of a lawful stop. State v. Gilkey, 317 Or App 752, 759, 505 P3d 1029, 1035 (2022). In Jimenez, the Supreme Court explained that “when an officer has seized an individual and has a constitutional basis to continue to temporarily detain and question him or her,” the officer may ask questions that are “reasonably related to and reasonably necessary to effectuate” the officer’s investigation. Jimenez, 357 Or at 428-29.

For the state to establish that a question survives the Jimenez test, the state must present evidence that (1) the officer perceived a circumstance-specific danger, and that perception was objectively reasonable, and (2) the officer decided that the questions asked were necessary to address that danger, and it is objectively reasonable that those questions would ameliorate or clarify the safety concern. Gilkey, 317 Or App at 559.

Gilkey focused on that final element: the objective reasonableness of an officer's response to a perceived safety concern, specifically, questioning a defendant about his tattoos and prison history.

There, the officer testified that it would be unsafe to check the defendant’s VIN number if the defendant was armed; however, the officer’s questions about the defendant’s tattoos and incarceration history were not reasonably calculated to address that concern. Thus, they were unlawful. Id. at 761.

C. Conduct or Circumstances

What types of circumstances or conduct can give rise to reasonable officer-safety concerns?

Generally, the defendant must have either engaged in some sort of hostile or threatening conduct toward the officers or suddenly reached into a concealed area that could contain a weapon.

For the most part, where defendant has not suddenly reached into a pocket or other location capable of containing a weapon, this court requires some indication of threatening or hostile conduct on the part of the defendant:

State v. Peterson, 143 Or App 505, 923 P2d 1340 (1996) (“Even if [the officer] correctly suspected that defendant was under the influence of controlled substances, there was nothing in defendant’s behavior to suggest imminent aggressiveness or hostility toward [the officer,] and [n]o weapons were visible and defendant did not make any movements towards the jacket in [the officer’s] presence, as if to retrieve a weapon for immediate use.”);

State v. Dyer, 157 Or App 326, 970 P2d 249 (1998) (holding that although defendant had previously been convicted of a weapons-related crime, possessed “a large folding knife in a sheath on his belt,” and wore “outlaw biker style dress,” defendant had been cooperative and had not said or done anything that could be interpreted as creating an immediate threat of serious physical harm);

State v. Knox, 134 Or App 154, 894 P2d 1185 (1995), vac’d on other grounds, 327 Or 97, 957 P2d 1209 (1998), adhering to holding on remand, 160 Or App 668, 671, 984 P2d 294 (1999) (rejecting officer-safety justification where defendant had a reputation for carrying weapons but “never before exhibited any violence towards the police or made threats to them[,]” and the defendant “was not acting in any unusually angry or strange manner that would give the officer any immediate concern”);

Jimenez, 263 Or App at 161 (noting, “Defendant was neither uncooperative nor evidently hostile” in concluding that the officer lacked an objectively reasonable fear for his safety);

State v. Rodriguez-Perez, 262 Or App 206, 215, 325 P3d 39 (2014) (holding that police officers lacked objectively reasonable suspicion that defendant posed an immediate threat of serious physical injury where the “defendant did not make any aggressive, hostile, or threatening movements, nor did he make any threatening remarks”);

State v Morgan, 348 Or 283, 230 P3d 928 (2010) (upholding officer-safety search where the defendant suddenly and unexpectedly got out of the car, her demeanor quickly changed from calm to visibly nervous, and “What tipped the scales, however, was [the] defendant’s act of reaching into the purse,” after the officer had already expressed his concern about weapons);

State v. Lee, 264 Or App 350, 332 P3d 894 (2014) (affirming officer-safety seizure where vehicle’s occupants began moving around as the officer approached the stopped car; a backseat passenger was “reaching to his side,” indicating the possible presence of a weapon; the defendant was a known felon, wearing a shirt associated with an area biker gang whose members were known to carry weapons and drugs; the defendant and his passengers outnumbered the police and they disobeyed an instruction to keep their hands in sight while the officer went back to his vehicle, and the defendant, specifically, “reached down to the floorboard of the vehicle”);

State v. Ruiz, 196 Or App 324, 326, 101 P3d 824 (2004) (concluding that the officer’s fear was reasonable where the defendant (1) reached into his pocket in the middle of a drug-related police investigation; (2) the investigation occurred in a high-crime area; and (3) defendant was right next to the subject of the investigation, increasing the level of threat they presented).

A person who removes their hands from their pockets during an interaction with the police does not give rise to an officer-safety justification for the police to search the pockets once the hands are removed from the pocket. Moreno, 150 Or App at 310. And that fact alone has never been held sufficient to authorize the police to order a defendant to remove their hands from their pockets. Cf Ruiz, 196 Or App at 326 (concluding that such an order was justified where the defendant reached into his pocket in the middle of a drug-related police investigation; the investigation occurred in an area with a “bad problem with controlled substances”; defendant was right next to the subject of the investigation, increasing the risk of a coordinated attack; and his companion had just been caught with a bindle of cocaine in his mouth, causing the officer to call for backup).

Objectively, Ruiz appears to involve the least threatening circumstances that this court has found sufficient to invoke the officer-safety exception. Moreover, four judges dissented, making it particularly ill-suited for the state to fact match.

In Moreno, 150 Or App 306, (1997), the defendant had his hands in the pockets of his jacket when officers approached him, asked for his name, and then said, “Cuchillos or pistolas?” which means knives or guns in Spanish. Defendant did not respond. Defendant then put his hands deeper into the pockets and continued to stare at one of the officers, who repeated, “Cuchillos or pistolas?” Defendant responded, “No.”

One officer testified that because defendant was so tense and acting wary, he thought that “it was possibly dangerous to us right then, the way he was acting.” The other testified that defendant’s response was not consistent with his body language because defendant maintained his physical stance and eye contact. He said that he was concerned because defendant’s motion could have indicated that defendant was seeking to obtain a better grip on a weapon that might be in his pocket. Id.

The officer used his hands to ask defendant to “take your hands out of your pockets.” Defendant appeared to freeze even more and stared intently at him. The officer motioned again for defendant to remove his hands from his pockets, and defendant slowly took his hands out of his pockets. Id.

That officer “was convinced that [defendant] actually had something in his pocket and that he had left it in the pocket and was showing me his bare hands.” He patted down defendant’s left pocket for weapons. He felt objects that he thought were drugs. He then reached into defendant’s pocket and removed the bindles of cocaine from his pocket. Id. at 309.

The Court of Appeals concluded that “[o]nce defendant complied with [the officer’s] request and showed his hands, the officers were not justified in conducting the pat-down of defendant.” Id. at 310. The court reasoned that the officers’ testimony did not support the conclusion that the defendant posed an immediate threat to the officers’ safety. Id. The officers only expressed a generalized concern for their safety that “would be present in any encounter” and there were no articulable facts such as “gestures or threats” that demonstrated objectively that the officers were in danger. Id.

D. Can reasonable officer safety concerns dissipate?

State v. Madden, 363 Or 703, 427 P3d 157 (2018) held that officer-safety concerns initially justified the seizure of the defendant, principally to maintain the element of surprise, as police sought to execute a search warrant at a high-occupancy drug house. Police encountered the defendant sitting in a car in the driveway of the target residence, the defendant furtively attempted to hide something, and he was accompanied by a known drug dealer who regularly carried weapons.

Despite the lawfulness of the initial seizure, the court held that the officers’ articulated safety concerns dissipated once the residence had been secured and that the continued detention and questioning of the defendant could not be justified under the officer-safety exception.

E. Common Fact Patterns Insufficient to Meet the Bates Standard

1. Present Possession of a Weapon

Present possession of a weapon does not establish reasonable suspicion of an immediate threat to officer safety where the suspect has not said or done anything threatening. State v. Dyer, 157 Or App 326, 332, 970 P2d 249 (1998). In Dyer, the defendant was stopped outside of his car for a traffic violation. As the defendant was leaning into his car to retrieve his driving documents, the officer noticed that the defendant had “a large folding knife in a sheath on his belt.” Dyer, 157 Or App at 328. The defendant’s “outlaw biker style dress,” heightened the officer’s safety concerns. Id. Dispatch informed the officer that the defendant had previously been convicted for unlawful possession of a weapon in a public building. Id. at 329.

This court held that those facts were insufficient to meet the Bates standard, explaining that the defendant had been cooperative and had not said or done anything that could be interpreted as creating an immediate threat of serious physical harm. Id. at 332. Thus, the officer had an insufficient basis on which to invoke the officer-safety exception. Id.

Notably, the presence of a secured weapon is also normally insufficient, by itself, to give rise to officer-safety concerns. State v. Smith, 277 Or App 298, 373 P3d 1089, rev den, 360 Or 401 (2016). Furthermore, the presence of a single firearm, without more, does not permit an inference that additional firearms are present. Id. In Smith, a forest service officer’s warrantless pat-down of the defendant was unlawful despite the fact that (1) the officer found the defendant in a group of people target-shooting guns at trees, (2) the officer was alone, and (3) potential backup was hours away. The defendant and his companions complied with the officer’s request to secure their weapons prior to the pat-down and were not difficult or threatening. Id. at 301. Because the defendant and his companions had secured their weapons, “there was no evidence that [the] defendant had access to a weapon or was armed at the time of the search.” Id. at 310. The fact that the defendant might have committed a violation by illegally damaging forest property, and that the defendant’s clothing was capable of concealing another weapon, were not sufficient to create a reasonable believe that the defendant posed an immediate threat. Id. at 308-09.

Smith noted previous cases in which this court held that the presence of one weapon “supported the conclusion that additional weapons might be present.” Id. at 307 (citing State v. Redmond, 114 Or App 197, 834 P2d 516 (1992), and State v. Pope, 150 Or App 457, 946 P2d 1157 (1997), rev den, 327 Or 521 (1998). The Smith court distinguished those cases by noting that they involved suspects who were members of violent motorcycle gangs. Id. The court held that a suspect’s possession of a single weapon does not support reasonable suspicion that the suspect may possess additional weapons unless “the totality of the circumstances permit the inference that [the] suspect is a member of a specific violent group and is, therefore, likely to be carrying multiple weapons[.]” Smith, 277 Or App at 308.

2024 Update

State v. Harmon, 329 Or App 639 (2023)

Discovery of a gun wedged between the front seats of a stopped vehicle did not justify officer-safety search of the passenger’s backpack, which had been left in the car after the vehicle’s occupants had exited the vehicle, because (1) the police did not identify any threat associated with the backpack and (2) “A warrantless search to protect an officer’s safety ‘will be justified only when the area searched is still within the defendant’s control, so that the defendant would be able to obtain a weapon stashed in the area[.]’” Id. at 643 (quoting State v. Krause, 281 Or App 143, 146 (2016)).

2. Reputation for Carrying Weapons

“That [the] defendant ha[s] a reputation for carrying weapons by itself, is insufficient to create a reasonable suspicion that defendant might use them during the otherwise routine traffic stop.” Knox, 134 Or App 154, vac’d on other grounds, 327 Or 97, 957 P2d 1209 (1998), adhering to holding on remand, 160 Or App 668, 671, 984 P2d 294 (1999).

3. Furtive Movements

“Furtive movement” alone does not permit a reasonable inference that the defendant was about to inflict serious physical injury upon an officer State v. Amell, 230 Or App 336, 345, 215 P3d 910 (2009); State v. Senn, 145 Or App 538, 545, 930 P2d 874 (1996).

Furtive movements, without any indication of aggression or hostility toward the police, do not justify questions about the defendant’s criminal history. Peterson, 143 Or App 505, rev den 327 Or 521, 971 P2d 408 (1998).

In Peterson, the defendant made a furtive movement and appeared to be under the influence of a controlled substance. The Court of Appeals found that officer safety concerns did not permit the officer to ask the defendant a question about his criminal history. There, the defendant swerved into the officer’s lane, requiring the officer to stop in order to avoid a collision. 143 Or App at 507. The officer pulled the defendant over. As the officer approached the defendant’s car, he saw the defendant moving toward the passenger seat area. Id. A jacket was lying on the passenger seat. Id. The defendant appeared nervous, he was stuttering, and his eyes were darting around. Id. The officer suspected that the defendant was under the influence of a controlled substance. Id. at 509 n 3.

After the officer had completed his investigation related to the traffic infraction, he inquired about defendant’s criminal history. Id. at 507. The court held that officer safety concerns did not justify the officer’s extension of the stop to question defendant about his prior arrest history. Id. at 510. Specifically, the court explained, “Even if [the officer] correctly suspected that defendant was under the influence of controlled substances, there was nothing in defendant’s behavior to suggest imminent aggressiveness or hostility toward [the officer]. Id. at 511. “No weapons were visible and defendant did not make any movements towards the jacket in [the officer’s] presence, as if to retrieve a weapon for immediate use.” Id. Therefore, the court held that the officer’s subjective concerns were not reasonable and reversed the defendant’s conviction.

4. Present Intoxication

“Even if [the officer] correctly suspected that defendant was under the influence of controlled substances, there was nothing in defendant’s behavior to suggest imminent aggressiveness or hostility toward [the officer]. Peterson, 143 Or App at 511, rev den 327 Or 521, 971 P2d 408 (1998).

5. High Crime Area

Although confronting a suspect in a high-crime area can contribute to officer-safety concerns, the officer confronted the defendant at midday and at a busy commercial intersection, not at a late hour, in a darkened area, where the officer had recently encountered armed gang members as had occurred in State v. Miglavs, 337 Or 1, 13, 90 P3d 607 (2004). Jimenez, 263 Or App at 159-60; State v. Zumbrum, 221 Or App 362, 364-65, 189 P3d 1235 (2008).

6. Making (or Not Making) Eye Contact

In Rodriguez-Perez, 262 Or App 206, the defendant just stared at the officer when he was asked whether the defendant was armed, and later in the encounter the defendant “would not make eye contact with him”; however, the Court of Appeals held that those facts, among others, were insufficient to establish an officer safety threat.

In Moreno, 150 Or App at 310, the defendant did not respond to the officer’s question about weapons and was tense and staring at the officer. After being asked for the second time to take his hands out of his pockets, the defendant “appeared to freeze even more and stared intently at [the officer].” The court held that once defendant took his hands out of his pocket, there was no officer safety justification to pat down the defendant’s pockets.

7. Nervous Demeanor

Zumbrum, 221 Or App at 364-65 (holding that officer-safety exception not satisfied where defendant was (1) on post-prison supervision, (2) staying in an apartment complex associated with illegal drug activity, including methamphetamine labs, (3) his girlfriend was on probation and known to be involved with mid- to upper-level drug dealers, (4) defendant had an “abnormally nervous demeanor,” and (5) defendant and the officer were confined in a narrow hallway).

8. Multiple Factors Found Insufficient

In State v. Nyes, 295 Or App 559, 435 P3d 805 (2019), the court held that the following facts were “legally insufficient to support an objectively reasonable fear that defendant posed an immediate threat of serious physical injury”:

“1) defendant was agitated, restless, fidgeting, and made furtive movements; 2) defendant appeared to be under the influence of a stimulant or amphetamine; 3) [the officer] had been trained as a Drug Recognition Expert for four years, and knew that people who use controlled substances often carry weapons and may be more likely to fight; 4) defendant was guarding his left side as if to hide something; and 5) defendant had bulges in his pockets[.]”

In State v. O’Dell, 291 Or App 359, 421 P3d 417 (2018), an officer stopped defendant for a traffic infraction, and during the encounter noted that defendant was “abnormally nervous” but cooperative. A backup officer arrived and noticed a standard folding knife in defendant’s front pocket. The officer asked defendant to step out of the car because he was concerned about defendant’s ability to use the knife “very quickly.” The officers conducted a pat-down search and, after removing the knife, continued to pat down defendant, finding pills. The officers then secured defendant’s consent to search his car and his phone and found incriminating evidence. Defendant later confessed to selling the pills illegally.

The Court of Appeals concluded that the officer’s subjective belief that defendant posed an immediate threat was not objectively reasonable.

“Here, the evidence establishes the presence of a folding knife in defendant’s pocket, when he was inside a car and not in any position to harm officers outside the car. Although the state points to defendant’s ‘abnormal’ nervousness, evasion of questions, and failure to proactively inform officers of the folding knife, defendant was otherwise cooperative and made no movements toward the knife, and the officers had not asked him about weapons. The evidence on which the state relies supports no more than general concerns for the officers’ safety, rather than specific facts demonstrating that defendant posed a threat. As a result, any subjective belief that defendant posed an immediate threat was not objectively reasonable, and the order to exit the car was not justified by the officer-safety exception.”

In State v. Gaylor, 287 Or App 495, 404 P3d 1140 (2017), a police officer observed defendant and a companion parked in a car outside of an abandoned house. There had been a burglary in the area recently. The officer decided to investigate, pulled into the next driveway and parked. He saw that defendant and his companion had gotten out of the car and defendant was walking along the road toward him. The officer turned around and stopped by the men in the road. The officer asked for identification. The companion began searching for his ID in the car, and the officer was unable to see his hands or upper body. Defendant replied to the officer’s request in a “sharp” tone and asked why he was required to provide ID. The officer decided to cite defendant for walking in the roadway. Meanwhile, defendant’s companion climbed over a fence and started jogging away. The officer shouted “Hey,” and the man responded “I’ll be right back” but kept jogging. The officer did not think he would come back but because he was not sure of the man’s intentions, he feared that he would pose a safety risk. The officer detained defendant, conducted a pat-down search, and found a pipe with methamphetamine residue.

The Court of Appeals concluded that the officer’s search of defendant was not supported by the officer safety exception because even if the officer had a subjective belief that defendant posed an immediate threat, that belief was not objectively reasonable. Even though defendant used a “sharp” voice, he was otherwise cooperative. And defendant’s companion did not increase the risk that defendant posed a threat because the companion left the scene and the officer did not think he would return.

“[A] warrantless search or seizure of a person based on officer safety concerns requires an objectively reasonable belief that that person poses an immediate threat of harm at the time of the pertinent warrantless search or seizure. Uncertainty about the location of the person’s associate, without more, does not make it objectively reasonable for an officer to believe that the person poses a threat of immediate physical harm to the officer.”

Id. at 502.

In State v. Easton, 264 Or App 339, 340, 332 P3d 315 (2014), at about 4:30 a.m., two officers responded to a 9-1-1 call and found a woman, under the influence of methamphetamine, lying on the ground in front of her apartment with a 9mm gun a few feet in front of her. The officers seized the gun and arrested the woman. The woman said that her houseguest had given her the gun, that he was inside, and that they could search her apartment. In the experience of one of the officers, “where there’s one weapon, there’s often more than one.” Id. at 340.

The apartment door was open and the lights were on inside; the officers were concerned that someone could shoot them from the apartment; and the officers did not know whether other people or weapons were inside the apartment. Id. at 343.

Before entering, the officers announced their presence several times, but received no answer. The officers went inside and discovered defendant lying on his stomach on a bed in a bedroom with the door open and the light on. Standing outside the bedroom door, the officers announced their presence again, but defendant did not respond. Id. at 341.

The officers were concerned that defendant was faking sleep and that he was hiding weapons underneath his body. Id. As the officers handcuffed defendant and rolled him over they found methamphetamine in the bed. Id.

The Court of Appeals held that the trial court should have suppressed the methamphetamine because the officers did not have an objectively reasonable basis for believing that defendant posed an immediate risk of serious physical injury. Id. at 343. The officers did not have any information about defendant that would cause them to believe that he might commit violence against police officers. Id.

There was no evidence in the record that defendant made any movements or that his hands
were concealed. Id. Although defendant did not respond to the officers and the officers were concerned that defendant was pretending to be asleep, defendant was not required to respond to the officers. Id.

Although it might be prudent for an officer to assume that “where there’s one weapon, there’s often more than one,” “where no articulable facts support the assumption, it is not a sufficient basis for a warrantless search.” Id. at 344.

In Rodriguez-Perez, 262 Or App 206, Officer Gryphon testified that the following facts led him to be concerned that the defendant might be armed: (1) the defendant was staring at [Officer] Ockunzzi’s interaction with [the defendant’s] brother; (2) the defendant did not answer Gryphon’s questions and would not make eye contact with him; (3) the defendant was glancing around and looked over his shoulder; (4) the defendant took steps away from Gryphon; (5) the defendant asked Gryphon why the officers had to search them; and (6) the defendant just stared at Gryphon when Gryphon asked whether the defendant was armed. Ockunzzi testified that the brother’s prior concealed-firearm arrest made him think that either man might be armed.”

The court held, “[A]t the point that Ockunzzi discovered the brother’s prior arrest, both defendant and his brother had been compliant and nothing about their behavior indicated that either of them posed an immediate threat of serious physical injury.”

      II.  Material Witness Stops

Until recently, traffic/non-criminal stops, investigatory criminal stops, and officer-safety stops, were the only permissible justifications for a non-arrest-level seizure. However, the Oregon Supreme Court created an additional exception in 2013:

“[T]he stop and temporary on-the-scene detention of a likely material witness will be constitutional if: (1) the officer reasonably believes that an offense involving danger of forcible injury to a person recently has been committed nearby; (2) the officer reasonably believes that the person has knowledge that may aid the investigation of the suspected crime; and (3) the detention is reasonably necessary to obtain or verify the identity of the person, or to obtain an account of the crime.”

State v. Fair, 353 Or 588, 609, 302 P3d 417 (2013). The Oregon Supreme Court in Fair for the first time in this state’s history suggested that the warrant requirement applies to searches of persons, places, and things and to seizures of places and things—but not to seizures of persons:

“Contrary to the premise of defendant’s position, however, the warrant requirement traditionally has not extended to the same extent and in the same way to seizures of persons, as it has to searches and seizures of premises and property. At common law, a police officer could arrest a citizen if that citizen committed a crime in the officer’s presence; the officer was not required to first obtain a warrant.

“* * * * *

“Indeed, were a warrant, or an exception to a warrant, required for a seizure of a person outside the home, no Cloman- or Terry-like stop of a criminal suspect based on reasonable suspicion would be constitutionally permissible. The only warrant known to the law for a seizure of a criminal suspect is an arrest warrant. There is not, nor could there be, a warrant to detain on reasonable suspicion; a constitutionally sufficient warrant must be supported by probable cause. See Article I, section 9 (‘no warrant shall issue but upon probable cause’); Fourth Amendment (same). Exceptions to the warrant requirement excuse the necessity of obtaining a warrant from a neutral magistrate in advance of a search or seizure; they do not change the requirement that police have probable cause to support their action. In other words, the very premise of defendant’s position runs counter to the developed law regarding temporary detentions of citizens for investigatory purposes.”

Id. at 604-05. The Supreme Court’s “reasoning” appears to have ignored the (1) officer-safety doctrine, (2) emergency-aid doctrine, (3) stop-and-frisk doctrine; (4) consent doctrine, (5) administrative search doctrine, and (6) school-safety doctrine, each of which the Oregon Supreme Court has referred to as an “exception to the warrant requirement” and each of which employs the reasonable suspicion standard or something other than probable cause to support the seizure of a person. The majority’s approach in Fair is not faithful to the Supreme Court’s own precedent and should be consistently challenged in the trial and appellate courts until it is abrogated.

2024 Update

In State v. Cage, 324 Or App 430 (March 8, 2023), the court held that the stop of the defendant was not lawful under the material-witness exception, because the officers did not have an “objectively reasonable basis to believe that defendant was a material witness to a crime.” In Cage, the police received a 9-1-1 call from a motel employee, reporting a dispute with a gun-possessing suspect near the motel’s vending machines. When the police arrived, they observed the defendant and H in a car parked in the lot near the vending machines. H was evasive and appeared to have been crying. In determining that the material-witness exception could not justify the stop of the defendant, the court contrasted the situation to the facts presented in State v. Fair, 353 Or 588 (2013), where the court had upheld a seizure under the material-witness exception.

In Fair, the defendant called 9-1-1 herself, which gave the officers reason to believe that the defendant had witnessed a crime. But in Cage, police had no reason to believe that the defendant was involved in reporting the crime to law enforcement. Additionally, in Fair, the combination of the defendant’s 9-1-1 call, her swollen eye, and her husband's apparent anger and evasiveness, created “an objectively reasonable basis to believe that [the] defendant was a victim of a domestic assault that had just occurred at the home and likely possessed information material to that crime.”

By contrast, in Cage, the court held that the defendant’s proximity to the reported crime was “not sufficiently specific or individualized to trigger the material-witness exception,” especially when the state presented no evidence regarding how long the car may have been parked in that location.  Unlike the officers’ observations of an ongoing assault in Fair, the officers did not arrive in the middle of an exigency or observe any specific crime. H showed no signs of injury, she provided an alternative explanation for her apparent crying, and “uncooperative and evasive behaviors alone do not indicate that a recent or ongoing assault had taken place.” Cage, 324 Or App at 443 (citing State v. Garcia, 276 Or App 838, 852-53, 370 P3d 512 (2016) (holding that the Fair doctrine did not apply when the witness seemed “kind of frazzled,” had been crying, denied that any argument with the defendant had occurred, showed no signs of injury, and did not request assistance)).

III.       Non-Traffic Violations

A. Reasonable Grounds to Believe

Reasonable grounds to believe that a person has committed a violation other than a traffic violation can justify a limited stop to investigate the violation.

ORS 153.039 governs a police officer’s authority to stop a person for the commission of a violation. “An enforcement officer may stop and detain any person if the officer has reasonable grounds to believe that the person has committed a violation.” ORS 153.039(2). “[T]he period of detention may be only as long as is necessary to:

“(a) Establish the identity of the person, firm, corporation or organization believed to have committed the violation;

“(b) Conduct any investigation reasonably related to the violation; and

“(c) Issue a citation for the violation.”

ORS 153.039(3).

B. What does “reasonable grounds to believe” mean?

In State v. Regnier, 229 Or App 525, 527, 212 P3d 1269 (2009), police responded to a report of minors drinking on the beach, a class B violation, ORS 471.430 (MIP). The trial court ruled that the words “reasonable grounds” in ORS 153.039(2) impose a higher standard than the “reasonable suspicion” of criminal activity standard in ORS 131.615(1), relying on the reasoning in State v. Gulley, 324 Or 57, 921 P2d 396 (1996).

In Gulley, the defendant moved to suppress evidence seized during the search of his residence pursuant to a condition of his probation that required him to “submit person, residence, vehicle and property to search by a probation officer having reasonable grounds to believe such search will disclose evidence of a probation violation.” For purposes of that statute, the Supreme Court held that “reasonable grounds” refers to a “quantum of information greater than that required to justify the stop of a person under ORS 131.615.” Gulley, 324 Or at 65.

In Regnier, the state asked the court to import the definition of “reasonably suspects” in ORS 131.605(5) into ORS 153.039(1). The court declined to resolve the issue, stating,

“[E]ven if we give the state the benefit of its proposed statutory interpretation, we need not reach the issue of whether the trial court correctly interpreted ORS 153.039(1) if [the officer] did not have a reasonable suspicion (a standard requiring a quantum of information less than required for ‘reasonable ground,’ under Gulley ) that defendants were in possession of alcoholic beverages at the time that she restrained their liberty of movement.”

Regnier, 229 Or App at 531. Consequently, the Regnier court concluded that regardless of the standard, MIP requires personal possession of an alcoholic beverage and that the officer lacked individualized reasonable suspicion that the defendants ever physically or constructively possessed any alcohol. Id. at 536.
