I. The Arrest Must Be Lawful to Justify the Search

An officer has “authority to conduct a warrantless search incident to arrest only if the arrest itself was lawful.” State v. Meier, 259 Or App 482, 485, 314 P3d 359 (2013) (emphasis added).

By the same token, if what would otherwise be a lawful arrest occurs during or after an unlawful seizure, the exception will not apply unless the arrest is attenuated from the preceding unlawful seizure. ii. State v. Almahmood, 308 Or App 795, 482 P3d 2021 (2021).

A search incident to arrest may precede any arrest as long as there was probable cause for the arrest prior to the search and the search was related to the reason for the arrest. State v. Anfield, 313 Or 554, 561, 836 P2d 1337 (1992). See also [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=17119904402657849573&q=State+v.+Sanchez-Anderson,+300+Or+App+767&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 State v. Sanchez-Anderson, ' 300 Or App 767, 782-83, 455 P3d 531 (2019)]' (holding that the search of the defendant incident to arrest was unlawful because the officers lacked probable cause of constructive possession of the drugs that were in the car where the defendant had been a passenger).

Probable cause has a subjective and an objective component: the officer must subjectively believe that it is more likely than not that a crime has been committed and that belief must be objectively reasonable. State v. Owens, 302 Or 196, 204, 729 P2d 524 (1986). The relationship of the search to the crime and the reasonableness of time, space, and intensity of search are judged as of the time the officer has probable cause to arrest for the crime. Id. at 205.

But a custodial arrest does not by itself authorize police to search the defendant and his belongings. Instead, a search incident to arrest must be justified by the circumstances surrounding the arrest. State v. Caraher, 293 Or 741, 757-59, 653 P2d 942 (1982). That is, the search must be justified by a constitutionally permissible purpose, and it must be reasonable in time, place, and intensity under the circumstances. Those two requirements inform each other; the reasonableness of the time/scope/intensity of a search is often based on what the justification was for the search. For all the requirements, the court must look to the circumstances surrounding the search, so attorneys should try to distinguish seemingly similar fact patterns justifying a search where possible.

2024 Update

The Subjective Prong Matters

An officer’s belief that a person “possibly” committed a crime is insufficient to satisfy the subjective prong of probable cause. State v. Harmon, 329 Or App 639, 644 (2023) (internal quotations and citations omitted). “The test is not simply what a reasonable officer could have believed when he conducted a warrantless search or seizure, but it is what this officer actually believed[.]” Id. (internal quotations and citations omitted). In Harmon, the defendant was the passenger in a stopped vehicle. Id. at 641. After arresting the driver, police saw a gun wedged between the front seats and asked the defendant to get out of the car. Id. The defendant complied and left her backpack in the car. Id. The defendant claimed that the gun belonged to her, but both the body cam and the officers’ testimony at the suppression hearing revealed that the officers did not subjectively believe that the gun belonged to her. Id. Because the officers lacked subjective probable cause to arrest defendant for a firearm offense (or any other offense), the subsequent search of her backpack could not be justified as a search incident to her arrest. Id. at 644.

II. Justification Requirement: Search for Safety, Destruction of Evidence, Finding Evidence

Authority to make “a valid custodial arrest is necessary, but not sufficient, to trigger the exception.” State v. Hite, 198 Or App 1, 7, 107 P3d 677 (2005). In Oregon, there are only three valid justifications for a search incident to a lawful arrest: “[1] to protect the officer’s safety, [2] to prevent the destruction of evidence, [3] and to discover evidence relevant to the crime for which the defendant was arrested.” State v. Hoskinson, 320 Or 83, 86, 879 P2d 180 (1994).

A. Search for Officer Safety or to Prevent the Destruction of Evidence

The permissible scope of a search for weapons or means of escape is limited by “the facts surrounding the arrest.” Hoskinson, 320 Or at 87. Although a “pat-down or limited search for weapons to protect the officer or to prevent escape” is “justified whenever a person is taken into custody,” Owens, 302 Or at 200, '' a more intrusive search, such as reaching into pockets or opening closed containers, is justified only if the officer develops “reasonable suspicion, based on specific and articulable facts, that the person in custody poses a serious threat of harm or escape and that a search would lessen or eliminate that threat.”Hoskinson'', 320 Or at 87.

For a pat-down—incident to the arrest—to lawfully elevate to a more intrusive search for weapons, the state must establish that the officer had a reasonable belief that the items felt (for instance, a syringe) could be a weapon. “In summary, an officer may conduct a pat-down of a person under the officer-safety exception if the officer has a reasonable basis to believe that the person poses a threat to the officer’s safety. However, an intrusion into a person’s clothes requires something more.” State v. Castillo-Lima, 274 Or App 67, 76, 360 P3d 567 (2015) (holding that the search of clothing was reasonable because the defendant was uncooperative, believed to be armed, the situation was “highly charged,” and the officers had reason to believe that others were nearby who had been involved in a violent assault).

Thus, an officer may not search a defendant’s pockets under the officer safety prong of the search incident to arrest exception to the warrant requirement without a reasonable belief that the pocket contains a weapon or some means of escape. See State v. Weems, 190 Or App 341, 347, 79 P3d 884 (2003) (holding that a knife on the seat of the defendant’s truck and the fact that the defendant had a “felony-caution” warrant were insufficient to justify an officer safety search of the defendant’s pockets).

B. Search for Evidence

“Under Article I, section 9, police can, without a warrant, search items, including closed containers, ‘found on or immediately associated with the arrestee,’ when the officer reasonably believes that the item constitutes, or may contain, evidence relating to the crime of arrest.” So an officer may search incident to arrest for evidence of the crime of arrest. State v. Brownlee, 302 Or App 594, 603-04, 461 P3d 1015 (2020); State v. Washington, 265 Or App 532, 536, 335 P3d 877 (2014).

But a search for evidence incident to arrest is valid only “when it relates to a crime which there is probable cause to believe the arrestee has committed, and when it is reasonable in all the circumstances.” Owens, 302 Or at 204.

As the Court of Appeals recently clarified, “a search incident to arrest for evidence of the crime of arrest is limited to that crime, and evidence of that crime. The exception does not permit an investigation into other potential crimes[.]” State v. Ramirez, 305 Or App 195, 215, 468 P3d 1006 (2020) (internal quotations omitted) (emphasis in Ramirez).

Thus, the third justification (discovering evidence of the crime of arrest) does not give police plenary authority to search for evidence during every valid arrest. State v. Newport, 204 Or App 489, 493, 130 P3d 792 (2006). “[I]f the person is arrested for a crime which ordinarily has neither instrumentalities nor fruits which could reasonably be concealed on the arrestee’s person or in the belongings in his or her immediate possession, no warrantless search for evidence of that crime would be authorized as incident to that arrest.” Owens, 302 Or at 200.

To be justified under this purpose, the search may only be in places where evidence of those crimes reasonably could be found. State v. Mazzola, 356 Or 804, 811-12, 345 P3d 424 (2015). The limitations on when and where officers may search for crime evidence incident to arrest is discussed further below, in section III (the Reasonableness Requirement).

A search for this purpose—finding evidence of the crime of arrest—may lawfully take place even when a defendant is separated from the object of the search. State v. Krause, 281 Or App 143, 146-47, 383 P3d 307 (2016), rev den, 360 Or 752, 388 P3d 725 (2017).

To search for evidence incident to the arrest, the officer must have both a subjective and objective belief that, more likely than not, the defendant had committed the crime actually associated with the evidence. State v. Brownlee, 302 Or App 594, 611, 461 P3d 1015 (2020). Thus, in Brownlee (discussed more below in the section on reasonable place/space of a search), the officer could lawfully search the defendant’s backpack for evidence of the crime of theft because he had probable cause that she had committed that crime. But the subsequent search of a scale inside the backpack was not justified as a search for evidence, because even though the record established that the officer perhaps could have believed the defendant had committed a controlled substance offense, the record did not establish that the officer subjectively did believe the defendant had committed a controlled substance offense. Id. at 610-11.

To determine whether a search could reasonably be for the discovery of evidence of the crime, courts look to the specific circumstances. For example, in State v. Curtis, 307 Or App 297, 475 P3d 942 (2020), the Court of Appeals held that officers could search an eyeglass-shaped container—in a car where the defendant had just been found—for evidence that the defendant had committed the crime of criminal trespass in the car. The court reasoned that “[b]reaking into a car often requires some sort of tools[,]” and those could be found in the container that was similar to an eyeglass case. Curtis, 307 Or App at 299-300.

In State v. Bladorn, 289 Or App 1, 408 P3d 240 (2017), the Court of Appeals clarified that an officer who arrests someone for unlawful possession of a firearm may search the person’s belongings, incident to that arrest, for evidence of more firearms. In so holding, the court likened firearm possession to the unlawful drug possession in State v. Caraher, 293 Of 741, 653 P2d 942 (1982), that gave officers probable cause to search for more possession of more drugs. Bladorn, 289 Or App at 8.

In State v. Ramirez, 305 Or App 195, 468 P3d 1006 (2020), the Court of Appeals held that officers may search a defendant for identification under this justification (searching incident to arrest for evidence of the crime of arrest) when the crime of arrest was giving false information. The court noted that most crimes of arrest do not justify searching a defendant for his or her identification because proof of identification is rarely a fruit or instrumentality of the crime. Ramirez, 305 Or App at 217. But the particular crime of giving false information to a peace officer is an exception. Id. (citing State v. Fesler, 68 Or App 609, 613, 685 P2d 1014, rev den, 297 Or 547, 685 P2d 997 (1984)).

An arrest for unlawful possession of a weapon can justify a search, incident to that arrest, for more weapons. Bladorn, 289 Or App at 8.

A search incident to arrest for failure to display a driver’s license ordinarily “is limited to a [pat down] search for weapons, because there is no reason to search an individual for evidence of that crime, which is complete upon noncompliance.” State v. Bishop, 157 Or App 33, 43 n 4, 967 P2d 1241 (1998).

Oregon’s appellate courts have not decided the question whether a DUII breath test is categorically permissible as a search incident to arrest. But in State v. Banks, 364 Or 332, 434 P3d 361 (2019), Justice Kistler wrote a dissenting opinion essentially for the purpose of pointing out that the state had not meaningfully argued that a breath test was permissible as a search incident to arrest. Banks, 364 Or at 349 ) (Kistler, J., dissenting). The Supreme Court of the United States resolved that question against the defendant in Birchfield v. North Dakota, __ US __, 136 S Ct 2160, 195 L Ed 2d 560 (2016).

Practice Tip

Argue that the per se search-incident-to-arrest exception should be abolished as inconsistent with McCarthy, and that the state must prove an actual exigency in order to search a person or their belongings incident to arrest.

Although McCarthy '' did not address the search-incident-to-arrest exception, its reasoning applies equally to that exception. The search-incident-to-arrest exception is a subset of the exigency exception that creates aper seexigency upon an arrest. Thatper se'' exigency should be abolished for the same reasons as the automobile exception.

The search-incident-to-arrest exception under Article I, section 9, was recognized by a divided Oregon Supreme Court in State v. Caraher, 293 Or 741, 653 P2d 942 (1982). Under the exception, police may search the areas that were under an arrestee’s immediate physical control prior to the arrest for three reasons: (1) to protect officer safety, (2) to prevent the destruction of evidence, and (3) to discover evidence related to the crime of arrest. State v. Hernandez, 299 Or App 544, 548, 449 P3d 878 (2019), rev den, 366 Or 292 (2020). The first two reasons are already covered by the standard exigency exception. Therefore, the practical application of the search-incident-to-arrest exception is largely focused on the third purpose: searches for evidence related to the crime of arrest. Such searches must be “reasonable” in terms of “time, scope, and intensity.” State v. Owens, 302 Or 196, 200, 729 P2d 524 (1986).

The Oregon Supreme Court has explained that the search-incident-to-arrest exception was born out of the exigency exception. See State v. Mazzola, 356 Or 804, 812, 345 P3d 424 (2015) (“The [search-incident-to-arrest] doctrine exists because ‘[a]n arrest * * * creates a type of exigency justifying a warrantless search of the arrested person.’”) (citation omitted); State v. Milligan, 304 Or 659, 668-69, 748 P2d 130 (1988) (noting in search-incident-to-arrest cases, “the fact of arrest motivates the arrested person to take immediate steps to destroy any incriminating evidence on his or her person. An arrest thus creates a type of exigency justifying a warrantless search of the arrested person for, inter alia, evidence that he or she has committed a crime.”). Indeed, the “time, scope, and intensity” limitation effectuates the exigency purpose of the exception. See Mazzola, 356 Or at 820 (“[T]he issue of exigency should be assessed in light of the reasonableness of the search in time, scope, and intensity.”).

Notwithstanding the above principles, in State v. Krause, 281 Or App 143, 383 P3d 307 (2016), rev den, 360 Or 752 (2017), the Court of Appeals held that the search-incident-to-arrest exception is not always premised on exigent circumstances as the justification for a warrantless search. It explained that, while “[t]he first two * * * purposes underlying the exception to the warrant requirement relate, in some ways, to exigency,” “the same is not true of the third basis for a search incident to arrest: to discover evidence of the crime of arrest.” Id. at 146 (quotation marks omitted).

In his dissent, Judge Egan argued that the majority was improperly severing the connection between the exigency rationale and a search incident to arrest for evidence:

“I do not agree that exigency should be taken out of the equation when the third justification for search incident to arrest is relied upon by the state. Nor do I believe that a search for evidence of the crime of arrest is justified when the area of the search is not within the defendant’s immediate control at the time that probable cause is developed. Exigency is the backbone of the search-incident-to-arrest exception to the warrant requirement.”

Id. at 151 (Egan, J., dissenting). Judge Egan also argued that no per se exigency should be relied upon:

“ * * * I do not believe we should be taking such a categorical approach to the search-incident-to-arrest doctrine. Rather, we must consider the individual circumstances of each case prior to determining whether the police may search the interior of a vehicle, and any closed containers within it, upon the arrest of a driver.”

Id. at 152. Judge Egan concluded, “[W]here there was no exigency present and there was sufficient time * * * to obtain a search warrant, I would find that the search * * * exceeded the scope of a reasonable search incident to arrest.” Id. at 153.

Subsequent developments in the law show that the majority opinion in Krause is plainly wrong and that the correct legal analysis is contained in Judge Egan’s dissent.

In recent years, the Supreme Court has either narrowed the scope of or completely disavowed exceptions to the warrant requirement, and in doing so created a framework for applying those exceptions that conflicts with the Krause analysis. In light of State v. Fulmer, 366 Or 224, 460 P3d 486 (2020), and McCarthy, warrant exceptions must be strictly limited to their animating purposes, and the Supreme Court’s repudiation of the automobile exception urges a refocusing of the search-incident-to-arrest exception on its animating exigency rationale. In short, those cases are inconsistent with the Krause majority holding and show that it was based on an erroneous assumption: that a search incident to arrest for evidence of a crime can be untethered from the exigency rationale. It cannot. After all, if the third rationale for a search incident to arrest has no basis in exigency, then what purpose does it serve beyond enabling warrantless searches for evidence?

Thus, the third purpose of the exception—to discover evidence of the crime of arrest—is premised on the notion that an arrest creates a per se exigency to search for evidence of the crime of arrest. But for the same reasons that the Supreme Court disavowed the per se exigency of the automobile exception, Oregon courts should disavow the per se exigency underpinning the search-incident-to-arrest exception. A search incident to arrest “must be applied consistently with the purpose[] animating the exception,” Fulmer, 366 Or at 233-34, which is to allow warrantless searches when exigent circumstances are created by an arrest. That means, that a warrantless search incident to arrest should occur only when an actual exigency is present. Stated differently, the exception does not apply when the search can be delayed to obtain a warrant, unless doing so would result in an imminent threat to officer safety or the destruction of evidence.

In State v. Although the Oregon Supreme Court denied review, two justices (Flynn, C.J., and DeHoog, J.) observed in the denial order that the “petition raises an important legal issue that the court should consider in an appropriate case.” Accordingly, trial attorneys should continue raising the argument that the state cannot rely on the search-incident-to-arrest exception when officers can reasonably obtain a warrant (e.g., when there is no exigency). Anecdotal evidence suggests that police may be using the search-incident exception to replace ­pre-McCarthy style, automobile-exception searches, by conducting warrantless searches of cars after the driver is arrested and handcuffed.

III. The Reasonableness Requirement: Reasonable in Time, Place, Intensity

Not only must a search incident to arrest be justified by one of the three permissible purposes, but it must also be reasonable in its time, space, and intensity in light of the relevant circumstances. Owens, 302 Or at 204; Caraher, 293 Or at 758.

A. Reasonable Intensity Requirement

The Oregon Supreme Court elaborated on the requirement of reasonable “intensity” in Chinn:

“Even if a search is reasonable with reference to time and space, it may be held unreasonable if it is of undue intensity. The nature of the crime for which an arrest is made and the character of the articles seized should have a direct bearing upon the question of reasonableness * * * Ordinarily, the seized articles must be in plain view, or, if not, then within the immediate control of the prisoner and accessible without unreasonable exploring, rummaging or ransacking.”

State v. Chinn, 231 Or 259, 267-68, 373 P2d 392 (1962). The intensity requirement acknowledges that some types of searches are too intrusive to fall within the search incident to arrest exception.

For example, a strip search, incident to arrest, is not reasonable in scope or intensity under Article 1, section 9, when the crime of arrest is unlawful delivery of a controlled substance. State v. Scruggs, 274 Or App 575 (2015). There, police observed the defendant conduct two hand-to-hand drug deals. When they tried to approach the defendant, he ran. They momentarily lost sight of him prior to apprehending him. During the arrest, they searched his pockets and found cash, but no drugs. They took him to a private room at the precinct and performed a strip search, ultimately recovering drugs from between his buttocks. The court held that such a search exceeded the reasonable intensity requirement.

In a different context, the Fourth Amendment also appears to treat a blood draw for evidence of intoxication as too invasive to be justified under the search incident to arrest exception. Although the issue has not been analyzed under Article I, section 9, in Schmerber v. California, 384 US 757, 770-71, 86 S Ct 1826, 16 L Ed 2d 908 (1966), the Court held that the search incident to arrest exception does not apply to searches beyond the body’s surface. 384 US at 769-770. It explained that the rationales underlying the search incident to arrest exception and determined that they “have little applicability with respect to searches involving intrusions beyond the body’s surface.” Id. at 769. Analogizing to the privacy afforded in one’s home, the Court noted that a warrant is required to conduct such an intrusive search when there are no exigent circumstances:

“Search warrants are ordinarily required for searches of dwellings, and absent an emergency, no less could be required where intrusions into the human body are concerned * * * The importance of informed, detached and deliberate determinations of the issue whether or not to invade another’s body in search of evidence of guilt is indisputable and great.”

Id. at 770. The same analysis could be extended to the collection and testing of urine samples under both federal and Oregon law. That is, the collection of a urine sample cannot be based on the search incident to arrest exception alone, because it is dramatically different in kind and intensity from the type of search that normally attends an arrest, such as a pat-down or pocket emptying. Urinalysis is not similar to searching a wallet, a cigarette case, or a compact, because it invades the privacy of the suspect’s body.

In Weber v. Oakridge Sch. Dist. 76, 184 Or App 415, 56 P3d 504 (2002), the Court of Appeals recognized the deep privacy interest that individuals have in bodily fluids such as blood and urine:

“Both [blood and urine] are bodily fluids that are not ordinarily available for public inspection except by intrusive means. To be sure, blood is drawn by means of a surgical intrusion. But obtaining urine is no less intrusive to an individual’s privacy.

“* * * * *

“Both fluids contain information that is of a most personal and private nature and that is otherwise not available to public inspection without consent. Examination of urine may reveal a host of medical facts, including whether an individual is diabetic, epileptic, or—in the case of a woman—pregnant. None of that information, to put it plainly, is the public’s business if the individual does not want it to be.”

184 Or App 415, 427, 56 P3d 504 (2002). Moreover, Oregon courts have recognized that “the final bastion of privacy is to be found in the area of human procreation and excretion and the nudity which may accompany them”; Sterling v. Cupp, 44 Or App 755, 761, 607 P2d 206 (1980), aff’d as modified, 290 Or 611 (1981). Thus, because both the collection of urine and the testing of urine are remarkably different in intensity than the search of an arrestee’s pockets for weapons or evidence of the crime for which he has been arrested, advocates should argue that such a search cannot be justified solely by the fact of arrest.

B. Reasonable Place/Space Requirement

1. When Searching for Crime Evidence

Officers searching for crime evidence may not search for evidence of other crimes; therefore, they may only search in places where evidence of the crime of arrest may reasonably be found. A search of crime evidence, incident to arrest, does not “otherwise justify an exploratory seizure of everything in the arrestee’s immediate possession and control upon the prospect that upon further inspection some of it might prove to have been stolen or to be contraband[.]” Owens, 302 Or at 205-05.

When searching for crime evidence, officers may search the person and items “immediately associated” with the arrestee’s person for items of the particular crime of arrest, if it is reasonable to believe that that the evidence could be contained in the places or items searched. State v. Brownlee, 302 Or App 594, 603-04, 461 P3d 1015 (2020).

In State v Newport, 204 Or App 489, 493-95, 130 P3d 792 (2006), police asked the defendant if he would come outside of the tavern he was in to speak with them. He agreed, and they walked to a spot approximately 10 feet from his truck. There was a backpack on the front seat of his truck.

The officer had probable cause to believe that the defendant’s backpack would contain evidence of drug-related crimes. The court first concluded that a non-consensual search of the backpack was reasonable in scope and time. However, the court went on to determine that the search of a container inside of a car that the defendant was 10 feet away from was unreasonable with respect to place.

In both State v. Meharry, 201 Or App 609, 120 P3d 520 (2005), rev’d on other grounds, 340 Or 106, 129 P3d 219 (2006), and Newport, a police officer had seen a vehicle driven by a person suspected of criminal activity. The officer in each case then found the suspect in a nearby commercial establishment and asked the suspect to accompany him outside near the vehicle. After developing probable cause to arrest the suspect, the officer searched the vehicle for evidence related to the offense for which he had made the arrest.

In Meharry, the court held,

“[a] lawful search incident to an arrest is limited in scope to the person and to the belongings in the person’s immediate possession because there is no further intrusion into the possessory and privacy interests that an arrested person has in his or her person and in the belongings in the person’s immediate possession. Such an intrusion has already occurred as a result of the arrest * * *”

Id. at 615. The privacy and possessory interests in other objects in an arrestee’s immediate possession, however, have not been relinquished. Id.

Finding the situation indistinguishable from Meharry, the court in Newport, held that the search of the backpack inside of the car 10 feet away from the defendant was unreasonable with respect to place.

2024 Update

The arrest of a person in a vehicle, without more, does not provide the authority to search through the personal effects of another, nonarrested individual. State v. Harmon, 329 Or App 639 (2023). In resolving the tension between privacy rights of passengers and law enforcement’s need to search for evidence of a crime after arresting a driver, the court has emphasized the importance of a nexus between an arrestee and the item to be searched incident to arrest: To justify the search of a container in the vehicle, the state must establish that it was “intimately associated” with or in the “immediate control” of the arrestee at the time of his arrest. Id. at 646-47. Police may not search a nonarrested vehicle passenger’s property if it clearly belongs to the passenger, is within the passenger’s control, and no independent facts connect it to the arrestee. Id. at 647.

2. When Searching for Officer Safety or to Prevent the Destruction of Evidence

A search for officer safety, or for the destruction of evidence, cannot take place outside those areas and circumstances where those things would be a reasonable concern. In State v. Groom, 249 Or App 118, 122, 274 P3d 876 (2012), the Court of Appeals held that (“[T]he interior of the car was not within defendant’s reach at the time of the search, so the search took place beyond the permissible space.”). In Groom, an officer ran a DMV check on a vehicle he was following and determined that the owner had an outstanding warrant for a drug offense. The officer lost sight of the vehicle momentarily, and when he relocated it, it was parked and the defendant was standing beside it. The defendant acknowledged that she had been driving and that she owned the car. The officer arrested her and placed her in the back of the patrol car. A drug dog alerted to the defendant’s purse, and the police opened the purse and found drugs.

The court explained the rationale for the search incident to arrest exception: “The justification for this exception to the warrant requirement is that such searches are necessary in order to protect the arresting officer in case the suspect has a weapon within reach and to prevent the suspect from reaching and destroying evidence.” Id. However, at the time police searched the purse, the defendant was handcuffed in the back of the patrol car, not within reach of her purse. Thus, the court concluded that the search failed the requirement that it be reasonable with respect to “space.” Id.

In State v. Ramirez, 305 Or App 195, 468 P3d 1006 (2020), the Court of Appeals noted that officers could not, for officer safety reasons, search an Altoids tin that was in a backpack in a car, incident to the arrest of a defendant who was in handcuffs and separated from the backpack. Ramirez, 305 Or App at 216. The court reasoned that “[w]hatever safety concerns might have existed had defendant had access to the backpack were neutralized through separation.” Id. (The court in Ramirez ultimately held that the search incident to arrest was lawful for the separate purpose of finding crime evidence.)

In Brownlee, 302 Or App 594, the Court of Appeals held that a search of the defendant’s backpack incident to arrest, for the purpose of finding evidence of the crime of third-degree theft, was reasonable even though the defendant was no longer near the backpack because she flung it over a fence before the actual arrest. The court reasoned that the defendant in that case had been in control of the backpack just before the arrest, and that nothing in Owens required the arrestee’s items to be on the arrestee’s person at the time of the search.

C. Reasonable Time Requirement

A search incident to arrest that is justified by either officer safety or by preventing the destruction of evidence has, at its root, an exigency-driven purpose. Those searches are therefore limited by the exigency presented. “When the exigency of the arrest dissipates, the police must seek a warrant before searching further.” State v. Clarke, 110 Or App 114, 117, 822 P2d 138, 139 (1991). “Although it may be constitutionally permissible to defer the search of a seized item until it is moved to a police controlled environment, exigent circumstances do not last longer than the exigency.” Id. “‘[I]f the requirement that decisions to search must be judicial is to have any effect and the exigent circumstance doctrine is to have any temporal limitation, then a deferred warrantless search must be commenced as promptly after the seizure as is reasonable in the circumstances.’” Id. at 118 (quoting State v. Quinn, 290 Or 383, 392, 623 P2d 630 (1981).

A search conducted 20 to 30 minutes after arrest has been held reasonable in time when the officers had to first remove the items from the location of arrest to avoid risk of loss or contamination. State v. Hernandez, 199 Or App 566, 113 P3d 437 (2005); see State v. Zigler, 100 Or App 700, 788 P2d 484 (1990) (holding that a 45-minute delay between arrest and search was reasonable when “[t]here is no suggestion in the record or in defendant’s argument that the search was deliberately delayed or that the police were doing anything other than necessary and appropriate tasks in the interim”); State v. Nix, 236 Or App 32, 45, 237 P3d 842 (2010) (40-minute delay between defendant’s arrest and the search was reasonable).

By contrast in Groom, 249 Or App 118 (discussed above), the search “occurred approximately an hour after defendant was arrested, during which the police made no attempt to obtain a warrant; that is an unreasonable amount of time and not within the permissible time frame.”

If in the course of a lawful inventory [see Chapter 4], the officer develops probable cause to believe that the arrestee has committed a crime other than the one for which he was arrested, the officer may conduct a search incident to the presumed arrest for evidence of that other crime in places where such evidence might reasonably be located. See State v. McCrory, 84 Or App 390, 394, 734 P2d 359 (1987) (seizing and opening paperfold discovered during inventory was justified by probable cause to believe that the defendant was in possession of controlled substance).

A search incident to arrest for evidence does not require that the state prove that an exigency existed to justify the timing of their search. State v. Senin, 301 Or App 358, 365, 456 P3d 334 (2019); but the search must still be “reasonable” in time, as well as in scope and intensity. Id. at 365. Typically, if searches for evidence incident to arrest happen after the arrest or stop, those searches are held reasonable as to time when they occur just after the officer actually develops probable cause of the crime that ultimately relates to the search. See, e.g., State v. Burgholzer, 185 Or App 254, 256–57, 59 P3d 582 (2002) (search of car incident to arrest was permissible when, after the defendant performed field sobriety tests, an officer concluded that he had probable cause to arrest the defendant for DUII); State v. Clew, 187 Or App 322, 324–25, 328–29, 67 P3d 420 (2003) (“[A]fter ordering a person whose driver’s license was suspended to get out of his car so it could be towed, a police officer discovered that the person possessed drugs, arrested him for that crime, and then searched the car incident to that arrest; we held that the search was reasonable in time, scope, and intensity in part because it occurred immediately following the defendant’s arrest.”). But where the search is not conducted within 45 minutes to an hour of the arrest or development of probable cause, Groom, 249 Or App 118, supports an argument that the search is unreasonable in time.
