I. Introduction

The Oregon and United States Constitutions favor search warrants. See State v. Villagran, 294 Or 404, 409, 657 P2d 1223 (1983). Unlike a warrantless search, if the search or seizure was conducted pursuant to a warrant, the burden of proof is on the defendant to overcome its presumed validity. State v. Johnson, 335 Or 511, 520-21 73 P3d 282 (2003) (ordinarily, a search performed under authority of a warrant is subject to a presumption of regularity, and the party challenging the evidence bears the burden to prove the unlawfulness of the search or seizure). Additionally, courts have traditionally held that close cases are to be decided in deference to the issuing magistrate’s probable cause determination. State v. Harvey, 53 Or App 478, 482, 632 P2d 487 (1981); State v. Nelson, 307 Or App 226, 232, 476 P3d 100 (2020). But see the discussion of State v. Castilleja, 345 Or 255, 265-66, 192 P3d 1283, on recons, 345 Or 473, 198 P3d 937 (2008) below calling the principle into question.

In practice, however, despite the constitutional preference for search warrants, they often present a real opportunity for defense lawyers to obtain suppression. That is because Article I, section 9, imposes two significant requirements related to search warrants: (1) “no warrant shall issue but upon probable cause” and (2) “particularly describing the place to be searched, and the person or thing to be seized.” A warrant that fails either requirement is invalid, and any evidence seized pursuant to it should be suppressed.

The sections that follow discuss both requirements in turn and should help the zealous advocate find the fatal flaws that exist in many search warrants.

II. Probable Cause Based Challenges to the Warrant

A. The Affidavit Does Not Establish Probable Cause for the Place to Be Searched

The affidavit does not establish probable cause for the place to be searched, i.e., there is an insufficient nexus between the evidence sought and the place searched.

Under the statutory and constitutional standards, “probable cause” exists when the facts set out in the warrant “‘lead a reasonable person to believe that seizable things will probably be found in the location to be searched.’” State v. Goodman, 328 Or 318, 325, 975 P2d 458 (1999) (quoting State v. Anspach, 298 Or 375, 381, 692 P2d 602 (1984)).

This is often referred to as the “nexus” requirement—there must be a nexus between the evidence sought and the place to be searched. State v. Webber, 281 Or App 342, 348, 383 P3d 951 (2016) (“[P]robable cause exists only if the affidavit sets forth facts that create a nexus between the place to be searched and the objects to be found.”).

For example, the Court of Appeals has reversed in several cases where the affidavit lacked sufficient information to justify the search of a suspected drug dealer’s residence. In State v. Miller, 254 Or App 514, 295 P3d 158 (2013), the affidavit asserted that the defendant repeatedly left from and returned to his house to conduct deals. The court held that the evidence was sufficient to permit a search of his car but not his house.

“Here, at most, [the officer’s] affidavit established that (1) defendant sold a small amount of methamphetamine to the [informant] during each of three controlled drug buys that occurred within two weeks of the issuance of the warrant at an undisclosed location; (2) defendant lived at the residence * * * ; and (3) defendant drove directly from the residence to the third controlled buy and returned thereafter.”

Id. at 527 [emphasis added]. Miller is also notable because the court rejected the notion that an officer’s “generic averment” that in his “training and experience” drug dealers customarily keep drugs in their homes was sufficient to establish probable cause linking the defendant’s drug activity to his residence. Id. at 523-24.

Similarly, in State v. Van Osdol, 290 Or App 902, 910, 417 P3d 488, 493 (2018), the court held that evidence of two recent controlled buys at a residence where the dealers did not live did not provide probable cause to search the residence. The affidavit established that two drug dealers—neither identified as a resident of the house to be searched—engaged in a single drug transaction at that house on separate occasions, the most recent of which was 96 hours earlier. The defendant was in the house during the drug buys but not alleged to have been in the room. The court held that in light of the proneness of drugs to staleness, there was not probable cause to believe that evidence of delivery of a controlled substance would be found in the home. As in Miller, the officer’s averments of training and experience were insufficient to establish that drugs would likely be found in the home.

In State v. Williams, 270 Or App 721 (2015), the court held that a search warrant affidavit failed to provide probable cause that evidence would be found in a place to be searched.

The state argued that the affidavit was sufficient because it established a connection between the defendant’s car and a drive-by shooting that is strong enough to allow a neutral and detached magistrate to conclude that, more likely than not, evidence related to the shooting would be found in the defendant’s car.

That connection, according to the state, was established by five key facts: (1) Riley was “indisputably connected to” the shooting; (2) Riley was taken to the hospital in the defendant’s vehicle; (3) the defendant appeared to be nervous when questioned by police; (4) although someone on the sidewalk returned fire at the northbound car, Riley denied that he or anyone else in his group had had a gun; and (5) Goodwin’s training and experience told her that people “involved in the unlawful use of firearms” often leave firearms or other evidence in vehicles. Those facts, however, were insufficient to establish a probability, as opposed to a mere possibility, that evidence would be found in the defendant’s vehicle.

In this case, the gap between the facts and a finding of probable cause simply was too large to be bridged by the officer’s training and experience. There is also a short but worthwhile discussion on the (in)significance of being nervous when questioned by the police.

See also Webber, 281 Or App 342 (insufficient nexus to the defendant’s residence when affidavit described drug trafficking from the defendant’s vehicle; training and experience did not create a nexus); [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=3130634713485188529&q=State+v.+Nelson,+307+Or+App+226&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 Nelson, ' 307 Or App 226] (insufficient nexus to the defendant’s residence when affidavit described boyfriend’s drug sales from shipping container on the same property);' State v. Evan, et al, 119 Or App 44, 849 P2d 539 (1993) ' (common practice of marijuana growers and distributors insufficient to show marijuana would be in the home); State v. Maxfield, 133 Or App 371, 891 P2d 1342 (1995)' (merely because the defendant had pictures of marijuana crops does not mean that a person who takes pictures of marijuana probably grows marijuana and keeps it at home).

But there are plenty of cases where the court has found a nexus between the items sought and a defendant’s residence. For example, in State v. Henderson, 341 Or 219, 142 P3d 58 (2006), the court concluded that the affidavit established probable cause to believe that stolen rings would be found in the defendant’s home:

“[The officer’s] affidavit stated that defendant had possessed the rings at some point in the preceding three weeks. The affidavit also described where defendant lived, how [the officer] had learned that information, and the steps that he had taken to verify it. The affidavit included statements based on [the officer’s] experience that it is common for persons who possess stolen property to hide that property in or around their homes and vehicles or to convert that property to personal use. Moreover, and even without [the officer’s] statements about his experience, we think that the magistrate could rely on his own common sense and draw reasonable inferences from [the officer’s] information about the rings and about defendant. The rings were nonperishable items of high value that would be easy to conceal, that retain their value, and that some people might find attractive to keep for personal use. On the basis of all the information discussed above, we conclude that the evidence before the magistrate supported the conclusion that there was probable cause to search defendant’s residence.”

(Emphasis added.)

The different outcome in Henderson results, in part, from the distinct qualities of the items the officer is looking for. But note that in State v. Heyne/Yunke, 270 Or App 601, 348 P3d 1170 (2015), the Court of Appeals upheld a search warrant authorizing the search of the defendant’s residence:

“The affidavit linked defendant to the marijuana found in a car and linked him to his home, which, in light of the statement in the officer’s affidavit that people who sell controlled substances conceal them in vehicles and buildings that they control, provided a basis to believe that evidence of marijuana possession or dealing would be found at defendant’s home. The fact that defendant’s Lebanon home also was identified as the place at which defendant had been authorized under his expired medical-marijuana card to grow and keep marijuana further tied defendant’s home to the marijuana found in defendant’s car. Hence, a reasonable magistrate could conclude that it was likely that marijuana and marijuana-related items would be found at defendant’s home.”

Because a boilerplate statement in an affidavit that drug dealers may conceal evidence in any property they control would be constitutionally problematic if found to be sufficient to search a residence without extrinsic evidence linking the property to the crime, attorneys should argue that the court must have intended that the expired marijuana-growing license tipped the scales in favor of probable cause.

B. The Affidavit Fails to Provide Probable Cause that Evidence Exists

1. Training and Experience Fails to Establish Probable Cause that Evidence Exists

It is not uncommon for affidavits to rely heavily on the affiant’s training and experience, and some of the cases above note when such training and experience is insufficient to establish probable cause for a location to be search. Evans, et al, 119 Or App 44. See also State v. Beagles, 143 Or App 129, 136, 923 P2d 1244, 1248 (1996), rev den, 324 Or 487, 930 P2d 852 (1996) (“For such averments to be given weight, there must be some nexus between the other information in the affidavit and the officer’s prior experiences that would permit a reasonable magistrate to authorize the seizure of the things described in the warrant.”).

A wonderful quote on this topic, albeit in the context of a warrantless search is from State v. Alvarado, 257 Or App 612, 631, 307 P3d 540 (2013), “the phrase ‘training and experience’ is not a magical incantation with the power to imbue speculation, stereotype, or pseudoscience with an impenetrable armor of veracity.”

But such training and experience is also cited as grounds for the type of evidence to be found. Affiants usually cite training and experience for the proposition that “drug dealers often take photographs of their drugs and drug paraphernalia” or “drug dealers commonly keep drug records of their transactions.” And those claims are the basis for seeking to seize cameras and videos and any paperwork found in a location.

There are two problems with this. First, what drug dealers “often” do may not even be enough to allege the probable component of probable cause. The Court of Appeals hinted at this argument in a footnote in State v. Keerins, 197 Or App 428 (2006):

“No question is raised on appeal about whether statements in the affidavit that drug-related evidence is ‘often’ found at the residence of someone engaged in the distribution of controlled substances are sufficient to establish that it is probable that such evidence will be found at the residence to be searched. We therefore do not consider that question.”

If there is no probable cause to seize the videos or cameras or paperwork, the defendant may be able to get that evidence suppressed, even when there is probable cause to search and seize drugs and drug paraphernalia.

State v. Carter/Grant, 316 Or 6, 848 P2d 599 (1993). In the affidavit supporting a search warrant, an officer who was trained and experienced in identifying marijuana swore that he saw through binoculars a plant consistent in color and stem with marijuana in the defendant’s residence. The officer did not identify or state that he believed the plant he saw was marijuana. Therefore, his statements in the affidavit amounted to no more than a commonplace observation of a green plant in a residence window. A green plant in a window combined with high power consumption did not establish probable cause.

As noted, the Court of Appeals has made useful statements regarding the limits of an officer’s training and experience in many of the nexus cases '''' discussed above.

2. Stale Facts

Probable cause to believe that seizable objects will be found at the location to be searched must exist at the time the warrant is issued. Articles observed on an earlier date but not reasonably likely to remain on premises will not suffice to support a warrant. The nature of the offense and the particular evidence sought will define the length of the delay permitted. No hard and fast rule may be applied. State v. lngram, 251 Or 324, 445 P2d 503 (1968). Thus, where facts recited in the affidavit support a conclusion that the evidence sought will likely remain in an automobile for a substantial period of time, 90 days may not be too long. State v. Veley, 40 Or App 235, 594 P2d 1285 (1979). Photographs of sexual interaction may support a search two years or more after their creation when they have been seen twice within that two-year period. State v. Kirkpatrick, 45 Or App 899, 609 P2d 1312 (1980). An affidavit seeking sex devices used in connection with rape and sexual abuse was not stale when it described use of the items sought within the preceding six months. State v. Harwood, 45 Or App 931, 609 P2d 433 (1980).

Drug evidence, in particular, is susceptible to staleness concerns because it is highly moveable and consumable. See Van Osdol, 290 Or App 902, 908 (“We have long held that drug evidence is prone to staleness.”); State v. Corpus-Ruiz, 127 Or App 666, 670, 874 P2d 90 (1994) (“Heroin is a substance that has a relatively long shelf life, but can be consumed in a short period of time and is easily moved.”). The following cases involve staleness in drug cases.

State v. Keerins, 197 Or App 428, 106 P3d 166 (2005). Simply coming out of a house with drugs does not establish probable cause that more drugs are in the house. But that, coupled with observations of drug activity at the residence made eight months earlier, “refreshed” the earlier observations and established probable cause.

State v. Urbach, 83 Or App 39, 730 P2d 571 (1986). A search warrant affidavit, which stated that an unnamed informant was in the defendant’s residence within the past 48 hours and at that time observed from one-quarter to one-half ounce of methamphetamine, failed to establish probable cause to believe that illegal drugs and related paraphernalia would still be present in the defendant’s residence or car when the warrant was issued.

State v. Black/Black, 36 Or App 613, 585 P2d 44 (1978). When the most recent observation of a controlled substance was three to four weeks earlier but the informant had been visiting the defendant’s residence weekly for a year or two and the defendant was a “large supplier,” it was reasonable to infer from the facts provided in the affidavit that ongoing narcotics transactions were likely. The resulting warrant was not stale.

2024 Update

In State v. Colgrove, 334 Or App 128 (2024), the search warrant affidavit failed to establish probable cause to search the defendant’s apartment, because it failed to establish a sufficient nexus between drug activity and the apartment. The affidavit disclosed that on three occasions police observed the defendant’s cotenant leave their apartment, drive for an undisclosed distance to a controlled buy at an undisclosed location at an undisclosed time, and then drive back to the apartment. Id. at 129-131.

The state argued that the absence of information about the route and length of travel time between the apartment and the controlled buys “suggests that [cotenant] did not make any significant detours or stops between his apartment and the meeting place,” thereby allowing the magistrate to reasonably infer a nexus between suspected drug activity and the apartment.” Id. at 135.

The Court rejected that argument, stating:

“That argument turns the warrant requirement on its head. Warrant affidavits are required to provide information to a magistrate so that the magistrate can make an independent decision about probable cause.  The complete absence of facts does not fulfill that requirement.”

Id.

C. The Affidavit Contains Statements from Informants

“If an affidavit is based in whole or in part on hearsay, the affiant shall set forth facts bearing on any unnamed informant’s reliability and shall disclose, as far as possible, the means by which the information was obtained.” ORS 133.545(6). The issuing magistrate should not simply rely “upon bald conclusions from anonymous informants,” for if it did “the independent probable cause review by a neutral magistrate promised by the Warrant Clause will be transformed into a rubberstamp of police warrant requests.” Villagran, 294 Or at 410.

Therefore, when an affidavit “relates the declarations of an unnamed informant, it must set forth (1) the informant’s basis of knowledge, and (2) facts showing the informant’s veracity.” State v. Binner, 128 Or App 639, 647, 877 P2d 642 (1994) (internal citations omitted).

An informant’s veracity may be established in two ways. The affidavit may demonstrate that the informant is credible, or the affidavit may establish that the informant’s information is reliable. State v. Alvarez, 308 Or 143, 147, 776 P2d 1283 (1989).

“Credibility refers to an informant’s ‘inherent and ongoing character as a person—his reputation as a truth-speaker or his demonstrated history of truth-speaking.’… When we focus upon whether an informant is credible, we often conclude that the informant should be believed because he or she has provided truthful information in the past.”

Id. The reliability of an informant’s statement, however, is distinct from credibility. “The reliability of an informant’s information refers to circumstances assuring that the particular information is indeed trustworthy on a specific occasion.” Id.

“Police corroboration of information supplied by an informant establishes the informant’s reliability and permits an inference that other information supplied by that informant is also reliable[.]”

Binner, 128 Or App at 647 (internal citations omitted). Corroboration entails “an independent investigation made by the affiant, which directly corroborates in essential particulars information furnished by the informant” and “it is not sufficient for the officer to corroborate only innocent and easily obtainable information such as a suspect’s name and address.” State v. Brust, 94 Or App 416, 420, 765 P2d 1246 (1988).

Importantly, the facts establishing the credibility and reliability of the informant must be established in the affidavit in support of the search warrant—they cannot be supplemented in an evidentiary hearing. Binner, 128 Or App 646-47.

Even when the informant is deemed credible, and he has personal knowledge for some of his statements, there must be a basis of personal knowledge for every assertion. And this is especially true when the informant is relaying information from an “unwitting informant,” i.e., someone who is not the main target of the search warrant but who provided information to the informant. See Miller, 254 Or App 514 (when affidavit is based on double hearsay, “each level of hearsay must be examined to determine if the basis of knowledge” requirement is satisfied (citing State v. Kreutzer, 138 Or App 306, 314, 909 P2d 175 (1995))).

Striking such information from the affidavit may mean the difference between an affidavit that establishes probable cause and one that does not.

See also '''' State v. Hall, 79 Or App 597, 720 P2d 376 (1986), where critical information contained in the search warrant affidavit was derived solely from conclusory statements and others for which no source of knowledge was shown, and for which detail was insufficient to permit an inference that they were based on the informant’s personal observations rather than speculation, the trial court properly suppressed the evidence seized as the product of the warrant search.

D. The Trial Court’s (and Appellate Court’s) Review for Probable Cause

Each reviewing court looks at the affidavit to determine if probable cause exists as a pure matter of law, yielding only one correct answer. This standard of review was set forth in State v. Castilleja, 345 Or 255, 265-66, 192 P3d 1283, on recons, 345 Or 473, 198 P3d 937 (2008). The court explained:

“[t]he sufficiency of the search warrant affidavit presents a legal question for each reviewing court, beginning with the trial court. That is, as in the trial court, the question before the Court of Appeals [is] whether the issuing magistrate could have concluded that the affidavit (excluding the excised parts) established probable cause to search defendants’ home. As to that question, the Court of Appeals [is] in the same position as was the trial court to evaluate the sufficiency of the facts alleged in the affidavit, the reasonableness of any inferences involved in resolving the legal question presented by the probable cause determination, and, ultimately, the existence of probable cause to support the warrant. No appellate court deference to the trial court’s findings or conclusions [is] appropriate or warranted.”

But the Court of Appeals has noted that, in close cases, the reviewing court should defer to the issuing magistrate’s probable cause determination. See, e.g., Nelson, 307 Or App at 232 (the affidavit is viewed “in a commonsense, nontechnical and realistic fashion,” with doubtful cases to be resolved in favor of the magistrate’s determination of probable cause.”); '' State v. Henderson, 341 Or 219, 225, 142 P3d 58 (2006) (“While adhering to the probable cause requirement, we resolve doubtful or marginal cases in favor of the preference for warrants.”). AlthoughNelsonwas decided afterCastilleja, the source of the questionable rule predatesCastillejaand appears to be facially irreconcilable with the analysis employed inCastilleja''.

When arguing that there is insufficient probable cause, it may be worth reminding the court that it should make a fresh probable cause determination, as Castilleja directs.

Note: the appellate courts do not defer to the trial court’s probable-cause determination, though it has deferred to the issuing magistrate’s determination in pre-Castilleja cases. State v. Chamu-Hernandez, 229 Or App 334, 341, 212 P3d 514 (2009).

III. Particularity-based Challenges to the Search Warrant (Specificity and Overbreadth)

As noted above, in addition to the command that “no warrant shall issue but upon probable cause” a proper warrant must also “particularly describ[e] the place to be searched, and the person or thing to be seized.” Article I, section 9.

The Oregon legislature has implemented the constitutional requirement of particularity through the enactment of ORS 133.565(2), which has been interpreted to be “at least as restrictive as the constitutional prohibitions against general warrants.” Reid, 319 Or at 69. ORS 133.565(2) provides:

“The warrant shall state, or describe with particularity:

“(a) The identity of the judge issuing the warrant and the date the warrant was issued;

“(b) The name of the person to be searched, or the location and designation of the premises or places to be searched;

“(c) The things constituting the object of the search and authorized to be seized[.]”

ORS 133.565(2). The Supreme Court has interpreted that statute to impose two limitations: (1) “a warrant may not authorize a search that is broader than the supporting affidavit supplies probable cause to justify[]” the overbreadth limitation; and (2) “a warrant must be definite enough to identify with a reasonable degree of certainty what is to be searched” the specificity requirement. Reid, 319 Or 65.

A. The Warrant Is Overbroad Relative to Persons to Be Searched

A warrant may be overbroad when it permits police to search individuals who are unconnected to the basis for probable cause. For example, in State v. Reid, 319 Or 65, 872 P3d 416 (1994), the warrant violated the first aspect of particularity because it purported to permit a search of all “persons present” at a residence. The court concluded that the description was “definite and unambiguous” in that it did not permit the police to exercise discretion. Id. at 71. However, “persons present” was overbroad in that it permitted the search of persons that the affidavit had not established probable cause to search. The court observed that it authorized the search of “a uniformed mail carrier or package delivery person, a volunteer soliciting donations for charitable purposes, or a neighbor seeking to borrow a cup of sugar.” Id.

The affidavit established probable cause to believe that the defendant himself was associated with the criminal activity taking place at the residence. But because “the affidavit did not demonstrate probable cause to believe that all ‘persons present’ on the premises would be associated with the criminal activity taking place there,” the warrant failed to satisfy the particularity requirement. Reid, 319 Or at 71 (emphasis added). Accordingly, the court held that the methamphetamine found during the search of the defendant’s person should have been suppressed.

A search warrant containing incorrect first and middle names is not insufficiently particular where the officer is aware of and uses personal knowledge to remedy the incorrect information in the warrant. Even though there were errors in the warrant, the warrant did contain the defendant’s correct last name and birthdate which, in conjunction with the officer’s personal knowledge of the defendant, including that the defendant was the very person for whom the officer had sought the warrant in the first place, “it was clear that defendant, and not someone else, was the particular person from whom the warrant authorized a blood draw. State v. Kauppi, 277 Or App 485 (2016).

B. The Warrant Is Overbroad Relative to Vehicles Being Searched

In State v. Ingram, 313 Or 139, 831 P2d 674 (1992), the court examined a warrant that implicated the second of the particularity limitations. 313 Or at 145. There, police officers executed a warrant that, inter alia, purported to authorize a search of “all vehicles determined to be associated with the occupants of said premises.” Id. at 141. The court found that statement “so ‘ambiguous that it is impossible to identify with a reasonable degree of certainty the particular [vehicles] authorized to be searched.’” Id. at 144-45 (quoting Blackburn/Barber, 266 Or at 34-35).

The court reasoned that the warrant was faulty because it “directs the executing officer to ‘determine[]’ facts existing only at the time the warrant is executed, and the phrase ‘all vehicles…associated with the occupants of said premises’ is so standardless that the executing officer must employ discretion in deciding which vehicles to search.” Id. at 145 (alterations in original).

In State v. Leathers, 106 Or App 157, 806 P2d 718 (1991), the defendant was a visitor on a premises searched pursuant to a search warrant. The warrant contained a general description of vehicles. Search of the defendant’s vehicle was invalid because the supporting affidavit did not establish probable cause to search every vehicle found on the premises.

C. The Warrant Is Overbroad as to Objects Sought

Both Ingram and Reid interpreted ORS 133.565(2)(b), which requires that the warrant state with particularity “[t]he name of the person to be searched, or the location and designation of the premises or places to be searched[.]” In State v. Beagles, 143 Or App 129, 135, 923 P2d 1244 (1996), the Court of Appeals interpreted ORS 133.565(2)(c), which requires particularity with respect to “[t]he things constituting the object of the search and authorized to be seized[.]” The court held that the “overriding inquiry is necessarily the same.” Id. at 135. A warrant fails the particularity requirement if “the language in the warrant permits the seizure of things for which no probable cause exists, thereby invading a privacy interest of defendants in the things seized.” Id.

State v. Reger, 277 Or App 81 (2016), the court applied that principle but concluded that all of the objects for which the court had authorized the police to search for were supported by probable cause. There, the defendant was charged with attempted murder and conspiracy to commit murder. Part of the accusations against the defendant included the allegation that she lied about shampooing carpets found in her living room in order to lure the victim into her garage, where her husband would shoot him. After the shooting, police obtained a warrant to search the defendant’s home for, among other things: “fibers,” “DNA evidence,” “blood or blood spatter evidence,” and “hair/trace evidence.” In the course of the search, officers examined the entryway rugs and the rug shampoo device. Police felt that they were all-dry and took the rugs into evidence. The defendant filed a motion to suppress evidence related to the rug cleaning, on the basis that the warrant did not meet the particularity requirement. The trial court denied the motion, the defendant was convicted, and appealed.

On appeal, the Court of Appeals affirms the trial court’s finding that the warrant was sufficiently
particular:

“It was reasonable to infer that the rugs and shampooer could have been a source of the types of evidence listed in the warrant. The warrant stated that the police could search a particular place—defendant’s home— for specific kinds of evidence—fibers, DNA, blood or blood splatter, hair, and other trace evidence—in connection with the officers’ probable cause to believe that defendant committed first-degree assault, attempted murder, and conspiracy to commit those crimes…[B]ecause the warrant limited police to search for particular types of evidence at a particular location in connection with a specific set of alleged crimes, it was sufficiently particular.”

See also the discussion below concerning electronic searches, in particular Cannon and Savath.

D. The Particularity Requirement and Searches of Electronic Devices

See also Chapter 8, Digital Data.

1. The Mansor Decision

The particularity requirement is often implicated in searches of electronic devices. State v. Mansor, 363 Or 185, 421 P3d 323, 344 (2018), is a game-changing decision for cases involving warrants for digital devices and data, and it also contains a thorough recounting of the prevailing overbreadth and particularity principles that should prove helpful when challenging search warrants generally.

In Mansor, the defendant was charged with murder-by-abuse following the death of his infant son. He told police that he had conducted an internet search in the 15 minutes preceding his call to 9-1-1 reporting that his son had stopped breathing. Police obtained a warrant and conducted
a wide-ranging search of the defendant’s computers, revealing evidence that was ultimately used at trial.

The Oregon Supreme Court issued three main holdings in support of its conclusion that the warrant was sufficiently particular and targeted, but that any evidence discovered on the computer that was beyond the scope of what the warrant specifically authorized should have been suppressed.

a. How specific must the warrant describe the digital information sought?

“To meet the particularity requirement of Article I, section 9, a warrant to search for and seize a computer—and to search the computer itself for information related to a crime—must be based on probable cause to believe that such evidence will be found on the computer and must describe the information the state seeks (the ‘what’) with as much specificity as reasonably possible under the circumstances, including, if available and relevant, a temporal description of when the information was created, accessed, or otherwise used.”

The “what” is a description of the information related to the alleged criminal conduct which there is probable cause to believe will be found on the computer, not necessarily the type of computer file that could contain evidence.

b. Was the warrant at issue specific enough?

“The warrant, read in conjunction with and limited by the affidavit, met the particularity requirement of Article I, section 9, as we have articulated it above. It sufficiently described the ‘what’ to be searched for and the relevant time frame: The June 12 internet search history.”

That is, “As [the Supreme Court] read the warrant, it authorized the police to search only for the June 12 internet search history.” (Emphasis added.)

Thus, the court held that the search of the defendant’s computer was “supported by probable cause, was sufficiently specific, and was not overbroad.”

c. Use Restrictions on Nonresponsive Evidence

But, the Supreme Court explained that nonresponsive evidence that exceeds the scope of the warrant must be suppressed unless some other exception to the warrant requirement exists:

“When the state conducts a reasonably targeted search of a person’s computer for information pursuant to a warrant that properly identifies the information being sought, the state has not unreasonably invaded the person’s privacy interest, and the state may use the information identified in the warrant in a prosecution or any other lawful manner. But when the state looks for other information or uncovers information that was not authorized by the warrant, Article I, section 9, prohibits the state from using that information at trial, unless it comes within an exception to the warrant requirement.

“To restore defendant to the position he would have been in had the police not obtained that additional evidence, the evidence other than the June 12 internet search history should have been suppressed.”

This ruling leaves open many questions, including whether police can seize evidence in plain view while conducting a broad forensic search. Compare [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=11100745107771394348&q=United+States+v.+Comprehensive+Drug+Testing,+Inc.,+621+F3d+1162&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 United States v. Comprehensive Drug Testing, Inc., 621 F3d 1162, 1170 (9th Cir 2010)] (rejecting plain view doctrine in a digital search case) with United States v. Williams, 592 F3d 511, 521-24 (4th Cir. 2010) (applying plain view doctrine in a digital search case).

Application of the plain-view exception in digital search cases would basically transform every digital search warrant into an unconstitutional general warrant. It would essentially eviscerate the Mansor court’s “use restriction.” This issue is sure to arise soon. But in the interim, argue that a broad plain-view exception would upset the careful balance crafted in Mansor and the explicit trade-off that the court endorsed. The Mansor court, by imposing a use restriction, contemplated that police would find other incriminating evidence outside the scope of the warrant while searching for the evidence that was authorized by the warrant and held that to use such evidence would violate a defendant’s Article I, section 9, rights.

2. Post-Mansor Decisions

There have been several post-Mansor cases that apply various aspects of that decision. In State v. Cannon, 299 Or App 616 (2019), the Court of Appeals explained that

“[P]robable cause must exist for each [electronic] device that a warrant authorizes to be searched.”

Cannon, 299 Or App at 629. '''' The court expressly declined “the state’s invitation to craft a categorical rule for searching multiple devices in child pornography cases.”Id.'' at 630.

The affidavit contained multiple, generic “training and experience” averments such as

“people who are sexually attracted to children will store illegal images ‘on their computer or other digital storage device such as USB flash drive, thumb drive, SD cards, Micro SD cards, Compact Disks[,] DVDs, and cell phones.’”

Id. at 631. The officer also attested that individuals who possess or distribute child pornography more likely than not “keep such material on mobile electronic devices such as “cell phones, computers, laptops, tablet computers, thumb drive, SD cards, or any other mobile devices they have for easy access to.” Id.

The court concluded that those averments were insufficient because they fail to provide a basis to conclude that such individuals likely keep child pornography on every electronic device they own. Id. at 631-32.

For another useful case in this area, decided before the Supreme Court decided Mansor, see State v. Friddle, 281 Or App 130, 381 P3d 979 (2016). In Friddle, the defendant was suspected of assaulting his then girlfriend. Police knew that the defendant had a security system that may have recorded the assault, and a police officer had personally observed the defendant accessing an audio recording of the assault on his cellular phone. Based on that information, the police applied for a warrant to seize and analyze the contents of a broad array of the defendant’s personal electronic devices. Id. at 133-34. The affiant alleged that, based on his “training and experience,” individuals involved in “criminal activity regularly use” cellular phones to “record and store photos, audio recordings, and video recordings of their crimes.”

The Court of Appeals found the affidavit was insufficient to support probable cause to examine all of the electronic devices included in the warrant. It noted that the affiant’s description of his training and experience was “generic,” and nothing in the affidavit indicated that the affiant had professional training or experience specifically related to personal electronic devices and their contents as evidence in criminal investigations. Second, even assuming that the affidavit demonstrated the requisite training and experience, and assuming that the facts alleged could support the possibility that the evidence might be stored on multiple devices, the affidavit failed to substantiate a probability that evidence of the alleged assault would be found on all of the defendant’s personal electronic devices.

And in State v. Savath, 298 Or App 495 (2019), the court explained that identifying the types of files is not sufficient; the warrant must identify the information in an electronic device that law enforcement is searching for. Savath, 298 Or App at 500. '''' A warrant for an electronic search “must identify, as specifically as reasonably possible in the circumstances, theinformationto be searched for.”Id.(quoting Mansor, 363 Or at 218 (emphasis in Savath). As Mansor explains, the essential “thing” about which a warrant must be particular is the probative information, not types of files or their location within the computer’s file-management system[.]”Id.''

The warrant at issue authorized the search of various file types and categories of communications data that might be found within the phone: “all names and telephone numbers,” “all messages both voice and text, text drafts and emails,” and “all photos, videos[,] and downloaded items” for “evidence related to controlled substance offenses.” Id. at 501.

Because “such details regarding specific “locations” within the phone do little if anything to satisfy the particularity requirement,” id., the court concluded that the limiting clause identifying the type of crime under investigation was too vague to enable an officer, “with reasonable effort[, to] ascertain those items [to be seized and examined] to a reasonable degree of certainty.” Id. at 503.

In state State v. Burnham, 289 Or App 783 (2018), the court held that an “impermissibly overbroad portion of the warrant ‘may be excised and the balance of the warrant upheld’ and that only those items ‘seized under the invalid portion of the warrant must be suppressed.’”

2024 Update

In some circumstances the affidavit itself may serve to narrow the scope of a warrant. State v. Breedwell, 323 Or App 172 (December 14, 2022). To do so, it must reasonably communicate to the executing officers that the warrant and affidavit are functionally one document, such that the officers would be expected to rely on the contents of the affidavit in limiting their search. Id. at 181. In challenging the lawfulness of a warrant-based search, the defendant bears the burden of establishing facts pertaining to his challenge to the validity of the warrant itself. Thus, the defendant must present some evidence to establish that “the affidavit was not sufficiently connected to the warrant at the time the warrant was executed.” “The relevant inquiry does not reduce solely to whether an affidavit and warrant are cross-referenced, stapled, paperclipped, or stored within a single folder.” Id. at 189. A variety of factual scenarios may support a defendant’s arguments that it was not objectively reasonable for the officers who executed the search to understand the supporting affidavit as functionally part of the warrant and a limit on their search authorization. Id. For instance, a circumstance where the affidavit was not present at execution would be strong support for such an argument.

E. The Particularity Requirement and Searches of Electronic Devices

2024 Update

1. The Mansor Decision

2. Post-Mansor Decisions

In State v. Turay, 313 Or App 45, 47–49, 493 P3d 1058, (2021), rev allowed, 369 Or 69 (2021), the court answered the following question: What should a court do when it concludes that some, but not all, of the search commands in a digital data warrant are insufficiently particular? The court held that in such situations the trial court must hold a hearing wherein the state, as the party with the burden, must establish that the evidence sought to be utilized was discovered through a search or forensic analysis responsive to the surviving, constitutionally particular, portion of the warrant, and not derived from the unconstitutional portion of the warrant. 

State v. Turay, 371 Or 128 (2023) (June 16, 2023)

Facts: State obtained a warrant to search defendant’s phone for nine categories of information relating to the crimes of compelling prostitution.  Police suspected that the defendant and Gregg (an adult woman) were compelling a 17-year old, J, into prostitution.

Legal Standard: “[T]he description in the warrant ‘must identify, as specifically as reasonably possible in the circumstances, the information to be searched for,’ Mansor, 363 Or at 218, and the description must permit law enforcement, exercising reasonable effort, to identify the information sought with a reasonable degree of certainty. If the warrant describes the information sought with that degree of specificity, and if the supporting affidavit supplies probable cause to justify the described search, then the warrant satisfies the particularity requirement of Article I, section 9.”  Turay, 371 Or at 150.

Held (merits): Five of the nine search categories failed to satisfy particularity requirement.

– Insufficiently particular commands:

·       (1) “Any and all communications (voice, e-mail, text, or otherwise) between J, Defendant, and/or Gregg.”

·       (2) “Evidence related to the relationship between J, Gregg, and/or defendant.”

·       (7) “Any evidence related to the use of Uber or other ride-sharing or taxicab companies.”

·       (8) “Any evidence regarding the locations, including geolocation information, of the phones for a period of time from 5/15/2017 to 9/06/2017.”

·       (9) “Any other evidence relating to the crimes of Prostitution, promoting prostitution, and/or compelling prostitution”

– Sufficient commands:

·       (3) “Evidence regarding any communications (voice, email, text, or otherwise) involving prostitution related activities.

·       (4) “Any photos of J, defendant, or Gregg that show an association with prostitution including any profiting from prostitution.

·       (5) “Images, videos and/or data which depict J or Gregg in sexually explicit positions or conduct that relate to internet postings or advertisements.”

·       (6) “Any evidence related to the use of internet sites associated with prostitution * * * for a period of time 6/15/2017 to 9/06/2017.”

Held (suppression): When a defendant establishes a minimal factual nexus between a constitutional violation and challenged evidence that was obtained pursuant to a warranted search, there is a presumption that the challenged evidence must be suppressed, but the state has the opportunity to rebut that presumption.  Turay, 371 Or at 164.  Given the nature of a search for digital data, everything obtained from a defendant’s phone is connected—to some extent—to the search categories described in the warrant, some of which were unlawful due to insufficient particularity.  Consequently, all evidence obtained during the execution of that warrant must be suppressed unless the state can prove that a particular item of evidence was untainted by the prior illegality (by showing independent source, inevitable discovery, or attenuation).  The court noted, without deciding, that that may require the state to prove that the evidence in fact ‘was discovered while police were executing one of the lawful search commands as opposed to one of the invalid commands.’”  Id. at 168.

State v. Vesa, 324 Or App 674 (2023) (March 22, 2023)

Held: (1) “Both we and the Supreme Court have explained that a warrant authorizing a search for all ‘evidence of a particular crime’ is not sufficiently specific to pass constitutional muster.”  Id. at 687 (citing State v. Bock (A169480), 310 Or App 329, 336 (2021)); (2) Warrant commanding a search for all “videos” and “photographs” on defendant’s cellphone was insufficiently specific and did not meet particularity requirement; (3) Second warrant for defendants cellphone was tainted by the first warrant because officers used information from the first warrant to procure it.

State v. Serrano, (A173250), 324 Or App 453 (March 8, 2023)

Held: As we understand Mansor, preventing the state from “using” nonresponsive evidence discovered during execution of a lawful digital warrant means “not permitting use of it: not at trial, not to obtain a warrant as part of an investigation, or, as in this case, not as the basis for a new investigation that ultimately leads to probable cause for a new warrant.” Id. at 466.

State v. Serrano, (A174829) 324 Or App 475 (March 8, 2023)

Held: The trial court should have suppressed some of the state’s trial evidence, because that evidence was uncovered incidentally during a search of defendant’s cellphone and was nonresponsive to the warrant; Mansor prohibited the use of that non-responsive material as the basis of the new investigation.

State v. Bock, 310 Or App 329 (2021)

Held: The plain view doctrine could not be used authorize use of a gun photo found while searching cell phone for location data.  “The breadth of a digital search is what renders the renders the plain view doctrine inapplicable; the alternative would sanction the sort of general warrant that the plain view doctrine was never meant to authorize.” Id. at 340.

F. There Is Probable Cause that Evidence Will be Found at the Location to be Searched, But the Evidence Is Not Identified With Enough Specificity in the Warrant

2024 Update

The affiant must specifically describe those articles for which he seeks a warrant to search and seize. The warrant itself must also particularly list those items which are the object of the search. Since a warrant permits only a limited intrusion, it must articulate the scope to be allowed. Thus, when police have information suggesting that the defendant possesses a large amount of coins and jewelry but fail to indicate such items in the warrant, seizure of items within that class is not authorized as it is beyond the scope of the warrant. State v. Jones, 58 Or App 277, 283-284, 648 P2d 869, rev den (1982); State v. Skrelunas, 1 Or App 182, 460 P2d 869 (1969).

State v. Houchins/Massey, 79 Or App 66, 717 P2d 665 (1986). Although police had probable cause to believe a large sum of money would be found on the defendant’s premises, the warrant to search did not list currency as an object of the search. Suppression of currency seized as evidence and return of the currency to the defendant was held appropriate.

However, as long as the warrant describes objects of the search with sufficient particularity to permit the executing officer to identify the property to be seized and to limit his intrusion to those areas where such legitimate objects of search might reasonably be found, a general description such as “receipts,” “tools” or “photographs depicting the crime of” is adequate.

Furthermore, when police are not able to precisely identify what items they seek, they may draft the affidavit and warrant by describing the articles as “evidence of the crime of….” State v. Hodges, 43 Or App 547, 603 P2d 1205, (1979); State v. Farrar, 309 Or 132, 786 P2d 161 (1990).

IV. Other Constitutional Challenges to a Search Warrant

A. The Search Warrant Is Based on Constitutionally Tainted Information

The independent source doctrine “permits the introduction of ‘evidence initially discovered during, or as a consequence of, an unlawful search, but later obtained independently from activities untainted by the initial illegality.’” State v. Johnson, 335 Or 511, 519, 73 P3d 282 (2003) (quoting Murray v. United States, 487 US 533, 537, 108 S Ct 2529, 101 L Ed 2d 472 (1988)).

The state generally cannot cure an unlawful seizure by obtaining a warrant to search the seized evidence. Johnson, 335 Or at 526; see also [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=13889584327994418876&q=State+v.+Lambert,+263+Or+App+683,&hl=en&as_sdt=6,38 State v. Lambert, '' 263 Or App 683, 328 P3d 824 (2014)] (Lambert I),modified on recons, 265 Or App 742 (2014) (Lambert II). In order to rely on unlawfully obtained information, the state must show that it would have inevitably discovered the information. The inevitable discovery requires the state to show that “the evidencewould have been'' discovered absent the illegality by proper and predictable investigatory procedures and that ‘it is not enough to show that the evidence “might” or “could have been” otherwise obtained.” State v. Lovaina-Burmudez, 257 Or App 1, 16, 303 P3d 988 (2013).

When the evidence referenced in an affidavit is itself based on an unconstitutional search or seizure, the evidence obtained from the unlawful search should be excised from the affidavit a determination made “whether the remaining information is sufficient to establish probable cause.” State v. Binner, 128 Or App 639, 645, 877 P2d 642, rev den, 320 Or 325, 883 P2d 1303 (1994).

However, the Oregon Supreme Court is currently considering State v. DeJong, 305 Or App 325, 338, 469 P3d 253 (2020), rev allowed, 367 Or 496 (2021), where the defendant is asking the court to apply the standard suppression analysis described in State v. Unger, 356 Or 59, 333 P3d 1009 (2014), which would require suppression of all evidence derived from a prior unlawful search or seizure, even if the warrant affidavit would be sufficient to establish probable cause without it. That is, if the discovery of the evidence contributed in any way to the officer’s subsequent efforts to obtain a warrant, to the magistrate’s decision to grant the warrant, or to the officer’s execution of the warrant, then any evidence discovered pursuant to the warrant would necessarily be tainted by initial police illegality, and should be suppressed.

2024 Update

Under Article I, section 9, when a defendant seeks to suppress evidence discovered pursuant to a warrant search that was conducted after the police had subjected the defendant to an unlawful search or seizure, the defendant bears an initial “minimal” burden to show a factual nexus between the illegal seizure and the subsequent warrant search. State v. DeJong, 368 Or 640, 642, 497 P3d 710 (2021).

That burden can be satisfied if information obtained as a result of the illegality was included in the warrant affidavit—such as the identity of the defendant, the items sought, the relationship between the defendant and the items sought, or the locations in which they might be found. Id. at 654-55.

The burden then shifts to the state to prove that the evidence was untainted by the prior illegality. Id. 656. When the state relies on the inevitable discovery doctrine to prove a lack of taint, it must show that the evidence would have remained in a location that was available to the police—and not removed, hidden, or destroyed, between the time that the illegal seizure ended and the time that the warrant search was conducted. Id. at 656-57.

B. The Affidavit Misidentifies the Place to Be Searched

If a warrant authorizing a search is so ambiguous that those executing it would be unable to distinguish between the premises to be searched and adjoining properties, it may not be acted upon. State v. Blackburn/Barber, 266 Or 28, 34-35, 511 P2d 381 (1973).

The court upheld a search when the warrant gave the wrong address, but the police searched the correct address, which was the only address to correspond to the description and directions in the warrant. State v. Edwards/Edwards, 149 Or App 702, 945 P2d 553 (1997). See also State v Kadin/Coleman, 172 Or App 353, 8 P3d 484 (2001) (while the warrant contained some ambiguity, the correct premises to be searched could be identified with a reasonable degree of certainty).

C. The Affidavit Fails to Distinguish Between Multiple Units at a Single Address

Where the building is divided into distinguishable subunits, and police have reason to know that it is other than a single residence, the warrant must specify which apartment or subunit to be searched. State v. Devine, 307 Or 341, 768 P2d 913 (1989). But, when subunits are not distinguishable, police may reasonably search the entire structure. '''' State v. Willcut, 19 Or App 93, 526 P2d 607 (1974).

“[G]enerally, a search warrant issued for the search of a residential unit authorizes the search of the entire residence unless it is shown that there are separate units or subunits in the residence.… Further, the fact that there are multiple persons living in a residence does not automatically make the residence a multiple-unit structure. Rather, the objective characteristics of the living unit must be considered to determine if it is one living unit or is made up of separate units…. Information such as the actual physical structure of the residence, whether there are separate entrances or separate numbers on the doors in the residence, whether the doors to different parts of the living unit have locks and are locked, and whether occupants of the unit have restricted access in any way to other occupants are pertinent consideration.”

State v. Ramirez, 223 Or App 241, 245-6, 195 P3d 460 (2008) (internal citations omitted).

2024 Update

In State v. Breedwell, 323 Or App 172 (December 14, 2022), the defendant did not meet his initial burden to show that the affidavit should not be considered when evaluating warrant’s particularity. The warrant in that case specified the address of defendant’s apartment building, but not his name or apartment number.  However, the court found it to be sufficiently particular when considered with affidavit that did include that information.

D. The Warrant Is an Unlawful General Warrant Under Federal Law

General warrants are unconstitutional under the Fourth Amendment.

“The Warrant Clause of the Fourth Amendment categorically prohibits the issuance of any warrant except one ‘particularly describing the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized.’ The manifest purpose of this particularity requirement was to prevent general searches.” Maryland v. Garrison, 480 US 79, 84 (1987). See also United States v. Vasquez, 654 F3d 880, 884 -885 (9th Cir 2011); United States v. Adjani, 452 F3d 1140, 1147-48 (9th Cir 2006).

The Ninth Circuit, along with other lower courts, refer to analysis under the Fourth Amendment’s Warrant Clause in terms of a “specificity” test, which encompasses two respects: particularity and breadth. United States v. Hill, 459 F3d 966, 973 (9th Cir 2006); United States v. Kow, 58 F3d 423, 426-28 (9th Cir 1995); United States v. Schesso, 842 F Supp 2d 1292, 2011 WL6989822, *3 (W D Wash Nov. 9, 2011).

1. Particularity

A particular warrant states clearly what is sought and is sufficiently specific to allow the person executing the warrant to know what items are to be seized. [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=United+States+v.+SDI+FutureHealth,+Inc.,+568+F3d+684,+&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38&case=11788956814365356517&scilh=0 United States v. SDI FutureHealth, Inc., 568 F3d 684, 702 (9th Cir 2009)]; Hill, 459 F3d at 973. This particularity requirement makes “general searches under [a warrant] impossible and prevents the seizure of one thing under a warrant describing another. As to what is to be taken, nothing is left to the discretion of the officer executing the warrant.” Marron v. United States, 275 US 192, 196 (1927).

2. Overbreadth

Overbreadth deals with the requirement that the scope of the warrant be limited by the probable cause on which the warrant is based. Generic classifications in a warrant are acceptable only when a more precise description is not possible. In re Grand Jury Subpoenas, 926 F2d 847, 856-57 (9th Cir 1991); Hill, 459 F3d at 973-76; SDI Future Health, Inc., 568 F3d at 702-03.

3. The Spilotro Three-Factor Test for Particularity and Overbreadth

In assessing particularity and overbreadth, the court looks to one or more of three factors: (1) whether probable cause exists to seize all items of a particular type described in the warrant; (2)whether the search warrant sets out objective standards by which officers can distinguish between items subject to seizure and those not; and (3) whether the government could have described the items to be seized with more particularity in light of the information available at the time of the search warrant. Vasquez, 654 F3d at 884-85; Adjani, 452 F3d at 1147-48; United States v. McGrew, 122 F3d 847, 849 (1997).

This three-factor test originated in United States v. Spilotro, 800 F2d 959, 963 (9th Cir1986), where it was articulated as the proper test to assess whether a warrant was “sufficiently precise.” Subsequent precedent refer to the Spilotro test as measuring both specificity and particularity in warrants. Adjani, 452 F3d at 1148 (referring to this as particularity test); United States v. Lacy, 119 F3d 742, 746 n 7 (9th Cir 1997) (referring to this as specificity test). Regardless of the nomenclature, the case law is clear that courts are to apply the three factor test in evaluating warrants under the Warrant Clause. McGrew, 122 F3d at 849 (noting three factors are “well settled law of this circuit”).

V. Obtaining a Warrant: Oregon’s Statutory Process

ORS 133.545 and 133.555 set out the requirements for obtaining a warrant within the state of Oregon. Those statutes impose restrictions upon the state in seeking and securing a warrant, such as requiring that a magistrate with authority issue the warrant; that a police officer or district attorney make application; that application be made by affidavit setting forth probable cause or alternatively that an oral statement under oath be given; that the warrant list only items subject to seizure under ORS 133.535 and that the warrant be executed within five days unless extension is allowed. Each of these requirements is discussed below. It is important to remember, however, that ORS 136.432 prohibits the exclusion of relevant evidence based on a statutory—as opposed to a constitutional—violation. For that reason, any failure to comply statutory requirements, particularly those dealing with the warrant’s execution, will rarely lead to suppression.

ORS 133.545(3) places greater restrictions upon the use of information gathered from an informant than federal law requires. In effect it continues the federal Aguilar-Spinelli test for reliability of informants in Oregon, notwithstanding Illinois v. Gates, 462 US 213, 103 S Ct 2317, 76 LEd2d 527 (1983). See State v. Horwedel, 66 Or App 400, 674 P2d 623 (1984).

The old Aguilar-Spinelli test has been largely eroded by Illinois v. Gates, '''' abrogating the government’s obligation to establish the reliability and basis of knowledge of an unnamed informant in every instance, and by United States v. Leon, 468 US 897, 104 S Ct 3405, 82 LEd2d 677 (1984), which created the “good faith” exception to the exclusionary rule.

A. Offenses for Which a Warrant May Be Obtained

Law enforcement authorities may seek and obtain a warrant to search for or seize evidence of a felony, misdemeanor or other crime, as well as evidence of an offense designated as an infraction. ORS 133.535(3); State v. Weist, 302 Or 370, 730 P2d 26 (1986).

B. Permissible Objects of Search

ORS 133.535 details those categories of objects for which law enforcement authorities may legitimately search. They include the following:

•       Evidence or information concerning the commission of a criminal offense.

•       Contraband, the fruits of crime, or things otherwise criminally possessed.

•       Property that has been used, or is possessed for the purpose of being used to commit or
conceal the commission of an offense.

•       A person for whose arrest there is probable cause or who is unlawfully held in concealment.

C. Authority to Issue

ORS 133.545 (1) and (2) authorize a justice of the Oregon Supreme Court or judge of the Oregon Court of Appeals to issue a warrant to search or seize anywhere within the boundaries of the state. A circuit court judge may issue a warrant to search anywhere within his district. Generally, a district court judge or justice of the peace may endorse a warrant to search within his county. A municipal court judge authorized to exercise the powers and perform the duties of a justice of the peace may only authorize a search within the municipality in which his court is located. A circuit or district court judge may legitimately authorize a search or seizure beyond district or county limits when the application for warrant establishes that one or more objects of the search relate to an offense committed or triable within the district or county in which the court is located.

State v. Effinger, 98 Or App 651, 780 P2d 768 (1989). A search warrant issued by a justice of the peace pro tempore after her 60-day appointment had expired is not valid where nothing in the record gave her any color of authority.

State v. Clapper, 216 Or App 413, 173 P3d 1235 (2007). Because the search warrant incorporates the search warrant affidavit, the warrant complies with the requirement, pursuant to ORS 133.545, that the court find that one or more of the objects of the search relate to an offense committed or triable within the judicial district of the issuing court.

D. Who May Apply for Warrant?

ORS 133.545 (3) advises that only a district attorney or police officer may apply for a warrant to search.

E. Form of Application

ORS 133.545(4) directs that the application shall consist of one or more affidavits particularly setting forth facts and circumstances tending to show that permissible objects of a search are in the places or in the possession of the individual(s) to be searched. The statute further indicates that an affidavit based in whole or part upon the hearsay statements of an unnamed informant must provide facts establishing the informant’s reliability and basis of knowledge.

The affiant need not be a police officer or a district attorney; the affiant may be a private citizen. State v. Ferris, 108 Or App 81, 813 P2d 1123 (1991).

Additional materials, such as maps or reports prepared by an officer, attached to the affidavit and submitted in support of the warrant are considered part of the affidavit. State v. Chambless, 111 Or App 76, 824 P2d 1183 (1992). A search warrant may incorporate another document when the document is attached to and referenced in the affidavit. The document need not be separately sworn to and signed. State v. Moore, 113 Or App 66, 831 P2d 70 (1992).

Under ORS 133.544(5), the issuing magistrate may take an oral statement under oath when circumstances exist making it impracticable for a district attorney or police officer to obtain a warrant in person. The statement must be recorded and transcribed. The statute requires that the recording of the sworn oral statement and the transcribed statement be retained as part of the record of the proceedings and that the transcribed statement be considered to be an affidavit for purposes of ORS 133.545. Note, the entire conversation between the officer and magistrate need not be recorded. It is sufficient if the oral statement establishing probable cause was recorded. See State v. Ferris, 108 Or App 81, 813 P2d 1123 (1991); State v. Fox, 93 Or App 565, 763 P2d 186 rev den (1988).

F. Supplemental Facts

ORS 133.555(1) permits the magistrate to place the affiant or other witnesses produced by the affiant or believed by the magistrate to be necessary to a decision under oath and to examine them. If this supplementing process is undertaken the magistrate must make and keep a record of the testimony. The record will then be available and admissible as evidence at hearing on a motion to suppress. Caveat: A '' violation of this statute by failure to make or keep a record of testimony supplementing the affidavit will not necessarily result in suppression of the search results absent a showing of prejudice. State v. Mathis, 24 Or App 53, 544 P2d 170,rev den'' (1976). Among the bases of prejudice one might explore is the claim that absent the testimony the defense is unable to investigate or fashion a motion to controvert.

G. Telephonic Warrants

Oregon law authorizes telephonic search warrants, contemplating that a magistrate may take an oral affidavit over the phone or by other electronic communications. ORS 133.545(7)-(8).

State v. Wynn, 102 Or App 1, 792 P2d 1234 (1990). Evidence that the defendant was planning to move and that at one time had said that he and his father listened to police broadcasts did not provide sufficient exigent circumstances to dispense with a warrant when a telephonic warrant could have been obtained. Police unfamiliarity with telephonic warrant process did not excuse failure to obtain a warrant.

State v. Evans, 110 Or App 46, 822 P2d 1198 (1992). When a judge issues a telephonic warrant, ORS 133.545(5) requires him to make a record of the officer’s articulation of facts so that a reviewing court can determine whether the warrant was properly issued. This requirement is intended, in part, “to avoid the possibility of justification for a search or an arrest based upon facts or evidence discovered in the course of the execution of the warrant.”

VI. Execution of the Warrant

A. Time Limits

ORS 133.565(3) provides that a search warrant must be served within five days from the date of issuance. The statute also provides that an extension of time for service may be made by the judge issuing the warrant. The extension may authorize execution of the warrant after five days but not more than 10 days from the date of issuance.

A search warrant expires if not executed within the time requirements of ORS 133.565(3). Service of a warrant beyond the five-day time limit must be viewed as a warrantless search. Suppression of evidence is required, unless an exception to the warrant requirement justifies the search. State v. Daw, 94 Or App 370, 765 P2d 341 (1988) (“A search based on an expired warrant is a warrantless search.”). For that reason, the Daw rule should control despite the enactment of ORS 136.432 relating to the nonexclusion of relevant evidence based on a statutory violation.

But, “execution” of a search warrant does not require the state to complete the search. State v. Monger, 306 Or App 50, __P3d __ (2020). In Monger, after seizing the defendant’s computer and cell phone, police obtained a search warrant that authorized them to “‘search and/or seize and/or analyze and/or photograph and/or digitally replicate’ defendant’s electronic devices and required the return of the warrant within 30 days of its execution.” Id. at 53. The next day, the officer who obtained the warrant sent a copy of it to a forensic analyst and instructed him to conduct the search. The actual imaging and search of the hard drives, however, did not occur until over a month later. Id. This court concluded that executing a warrant “does not require completion of every action authorized by a search warrant” and because the warrant had been sent to the forensic analyst within the statutory timeframe, the warrant had been executed. Id. at 61. Importantly, the court referred to Daw and did not question the fundamental holding in that case. Id. at 54.

B. Knock and Announce

The knock-and-announce rule requires police to identify themselves and their purpose before entering the premises. ORS 133.575(2). However, a violation of the “knock and announce” rule is not a basis for suppression under either the Oregon or Federal constitutions. State v. ValentinelDarroch, 264 Or 54, 504 P2d 84 (1972); Hudson v. Michigan, 547 US 586, 126 S Ct 2159 (2006).

C. Night Searches

Unless special authorization is provided on the face of the warrant itself, the warrant must be executed between the hours of 7:00 a.m. and 10:00 p.m. Authorization for night search may be made upon a showing of need. See State v. Ness, 294 Or 8, 653 P2d 548 (1982); State v. Cochrane, 294 Or 12, 653 P2d 549 (1982); violation of the night endorsement provision does not result in suppression of the evidence gathered as the product of the search. State v. Brock, 294 Or 15, 653 P2d 543 (1982).

D. Reading Warrant/Leaving Copy

ORS 133.575(3) requires that a copy of the warrant be read and given to the defendant. The police failure to comply with that requirement does not require suppression because the statute provides for no exclusion remedy. State v. Blasingame, 127 Or App 382, 873 P2d 361 (1994).

VII. Miscellaneous Special Circumstances

A. Wiretaps

State v. Stockfleth/Lassen, 99 '''' Or App 72, 781 P2d 1220 (1989). If an application for a wiretap fails to set forth facts sufficient to establish the statutory requirement that before the wiretap order was issued normal available investigative techniques were tried in good faith and failed, it is inadequate and evidence derived from the illegal order must be suppressed.

This case was affirmed by the Supreme Court different grounds. State v. Stockfleth/Lessen, 310 Or 40, P2d (1991). The court held that the first wiretap order was not supported by the requisite showing of necessity and the second order was issued invalidly as a result of the defective first order.

The defendant cannot challenge the lawfulness of body wire evidence by an oral motion in limine. ORS 133.736 requires a written motion, filed prior to trial. State v. Rodriguez, 115 Or App 281, 840 P2d 711 (1992).

Even in the absence of exigent circumstances, a “body wire order” is not necessary if an officer has probable cause to believe the suspect is about to commit a drug crime. State v. Casteel, et al, 122 Or App 218, 857 P2d 204 (1993).

The use of a body wire did not constitute a search where the defendant invited the informant into his home. State v. Bass, 126 Or App 303, 868 P2d 761 (1994).

An ex parte order obtained under ORS 133.724 is necessary to provide police with authority to obtain evidence of oral communications through the use of a device, compliance by police with ORS 165.540(5)(a)(B) does not obviate the necessity of complying with ORS 133.724, and evidence of an oral communication obtained through the use of a body wire in compliance with ORS 133.724 is subject to suppression. State v. Cleveland, 331 Or 531, 16 P3d 514 (2000); State v. Fleetwood, 331 Or 511, 16 P3d 503 (2000).

2024 Update

For purposes of the federal wiretap statute, the State of Oregon cannot permit the elected District Attorney of a county to delegate his or her authority to apply for federal wiretaps to their deputy district attorneys. The “principal prosecuting attorney” of the jurisdiction must make the application or at least approve the particular application. State v. Harris, 369 Or 628, 636, 509 P3d 83 (2022).

B. Attorney Records

Notwithstanding ORS 133.535, the files, papers and effects of work premises of a lawyer relating to the provision of legal service by the lawyer shall not be subject to search or seizure by law enforcement authorities either by search warrant or otherwise unless there is probable cause to believe the lawyer has committed, is committing or is about to commit a crime. ORS 9.695.

C. Media Records

No papers, effects or work premises of person connected with, employed by or engaged in any medium of communication to the public shall be subject to a search by legislative, executive or judicial officer or body or any other authority having power to compel the production of evidence by search warrant or otherwise, unless there is probable cause to believe that the person to be searched has committed, is committing or is about to commit a crime. ORS 44.520(2).

D. Blood Test Evidence

State v. Shantie, 193 Or App 813, 92 P3d 746 (2004). Where blood test evidence is obtained pursuant to a valid search warrant, it is admissible pursuant to ORS 813.320(2)(b) in a prosecution for DUII regardless of whether it comports with implied consent laws.

E. Anticipatory Warrants (Impermissible)

2024 Update

State v. Lee, 371 Or 200 (June 28, 2023) 

Facts: Police believed the defendant was dealing drugs and planned a controlled buy.  In advance, police sought an anticipatory warrant to search the defendant’s residence from the result of the controlled buy once the buy was completed.  The Court of Appeals had upheld the use of the anticipatory warrant under Article I, section 9.

Held: (1) Anticipatory warrants conflict with Oregon’s statutory warrant requirements, which require warrants to be based on a showing of present probable cause; ' the statutes require that “there is probable cause to believe that the search will discovery things specified in the application” ORS 135.555(2), and the warrant must “particularly set forth the facts and circumstances tending to show that the objects of the search are in the places * * * to be searched.”  ORS 133.545(6).  (2) Suppression of the fruits of the warrant search was required under ORS 133.673(1)''', reasoning that ORS 136.432 constrained courts from creating new rules of exclusion but did not repeal existing statutory rules of exclusion.
