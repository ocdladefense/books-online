I. Introduction

More than a half century ago, the United States Supreme Court held in Katz v. United States, 389 U.S. 347 (1967), that subject to few exceptions the government violates the Fourth Amendment right to be free from “unreasonable” searches and seizures if the search or seizure is conducted without a warrant:

“Over and again this Court has emphasized that the mandate of the [Fourth] Amendment requires adherence to judicial processes and that searches conducted outside the judicial process, without prior approval by judge or magistrate, are per se unreasonable under the Fourth Amendment—subject only to a few specifically established and well-delineated exceptions.”

Id. at 357 (alteration original) (citation omitted) (internal quotation marks omitted) (footnote omitted). That doctrine became known as the warrant-preference rule or, alternatively, as the per se rule, i.e., a warrantless search is per se unreasonable. And the Oregon courts adopted it under Article I, section 9, in State v. Peller, 287 Or 255, 260, 598 P2d 684, 687 (1979). However, what began as a “few specifically established and well-delineated exceptions” is today a vast complex of exceptions with such broad application that relatively few searches and seizures are actually conducted pursuant to prior judicial authorization.

For that reason, it is critical to understand the contours of the various exceptions, because if the defense can articulate how an officer’s warrantless search or seizure fails to qualify for an exception, only then will the defendant be in a position to benefit from the warrant-preference rule. This chapter presents the primary warrant exceptions recognized by Oregon Courts as follows:

II. Exigent Circumstances Defined

“One exception—the exigent circumstances exception—allows the police to conduct a search without a warrant if the search is both supported by probable cause and conducted under exigent circumstances.” State v. Snow, 337 Or 219, 223, 94 P3d 872 (2004). Exigent circumstances exist if the situation “requires the police to act swiftly (1) to prevent danger to life, or (2) serious damage to property, or (3) to forestall a suspect’s escape, or (4) the destruction of evidence.” See State v. Stevens, 311 Or 119, 126, 806 P2d 92 (1991).

“Examples [of exigent circumstances] include cases involving danger to the lives of the arresting officers or others; cases where the delay attendant to issuance of a warrant would lead to destruction of evidence; cases involving ‘hot pursuit’ by a police officer; and an incredibly diverse number of others. See generally, Dorman v. United States, 435 F2d 385 (1970) (listing factors to be considered in determining whether ‘exigent circumstances’ or ‘urgent need’ are present).” Peller, 287 Or at 260 (internal citations omitted).

One factor that cuts against exigency is that police have already secured the container or the defendant, State v. Dickerson, 135 Or App 192, 898 P2d 193 (1995); State v. Jones, 103 Or App 316, 797 P2d 385 (1990). A second factor that cuts against exigency is the “availability of a telephonic warrant.” Stevens, 311 Or at 130; State v. Wise, 305 Or 78, 82, n 3, 749 P2d 1179 (1988). A third factor is whether the police acted swiftly to prevent the destruction of evidence. See State v. Matsen/Wilson, 287 Or 581, 601 P2d 784 (1979) (“(T)he officer cannot create exigent circumstances by his own inaction.”); State v. Chinn, 231 Or 259, 268, 373 P2d 392 (1962) (noting that “a search might be held unreasonable if the officers had had ample opportunity to consult a magistrate and obtain a search warrant but failed to do so”). “[T]he passage of time detracts from a claim that an exigency existed.” State v. Bridewell, 306 Or 231, 236, 759 P2d 1054 (1988).

The burden of proving any exception to the warrant requirement rests solely on the state. By statute:

“Where the motion to suppress challenges evidence seized as the result of a warrantless search, the burden of proving by a preponderance of the evidence the validity of the search is on the prosecution.”

ORS. § 133.693(4); State v. Newport, 204 Or App 489, 492, 130 P3d 792 (2006) (“A warrantless search is presumed to be unreasonable” as a matter of Oregon constitutional law.).

III. Probable Cause of a Crime Requirement

In order to rely on the exigent circumstances exception, the state must first establish that the officers had probable cause of a crime. In State v. Kelly, 274 Or App 363 (2015), the defendant’s DHS caseworker and several police officers visited the defendant’s house after receiving several anonymous phone calls suggesting that she had resumed using methamphetamine. The defendant had begun methamphetamine recovery more than a year prior to the visit. When DHS and the officers arrived, the garage door was raised about eight inches. The officers announced their presence and heard the defendant moving furtively in the garage while telling someone to “hide that.” The officers then raised the garage door.

The Court of Appeals held that the officers lacked probable cause: The prior-history-of-drug-use evidence was stale. The anonymous reports were unreliable because (1) the callers provided no indicia of first-hand knowledge. See State v. Koroteev, 222 Or App 596, 600, 194 P3d 842 (2008) (“The reasonableness of [the officer’s] belief is diminished by the conclusory nature of the caller’s description of defendant and his conduct. Neither the unidentified caller nor the persons who pointed toward defendant indicated how—or why—they apparently had determined that he was committing a crime.”); (2) the officers did not make any corroborating observations; (3) saying “hide that” is not sufficiently indicative of criminal activity and that furtive statement alone does not amount to probable cause of a crime.

IV. Preventing Imminent Serious Harm

Where police have probable cause to believe that a victim’s life is in imminent danger police can make a warrantless entry to save the victim. Stevens, 311 Or at 129. In Stevens, officers had probable cause to believe that the defendant, who had a history of violence and sexually abusing children, had kidnapped three young girls. They further believed that the children were with the defendant and in imminent danger of serious harm. The court held that the officers’ warrantless entry and search of the defendant’s house to endeavor to rescue the children did not violate Article I, section 9. Id. .

In cases not involving dwellings, the court has applied the exigent circumstances exception when a crime is in progress and warrantless action is necessary to apprehend or detain a suspected perpetrator. See Snow, 337 Or at 225 (risk that defendant might escape created exigent circumstance justifying warrantless search). Additionally,

“An officer who has probable cause to believe that a perpetrator is in the process of causing unlawful harm has a responsibility to apprehend the perpetrator to prevent the perpetrator from causing further imminent harm to a victim.”

State v. Fessenden, 355 Or 759, 773, 333 P3d 278 (2014). Fessenden involved an animal welfare officer’s trespass onto the defendant’s pasture to rescue a starving horse. The officer had probable cause to believe that the defendants were committing animal neglect. Id. The officer reasonably believed that the horse was at risk of serious imminent injury or death. Id. He also believed that it would take at least four, and possibly as long as eight, hours to obtain a warrant. Id. The court held:

“Thus, the officer had probable cause to believe that a crime was in progress and, based on specific, articulable facts, determined that warrantless action was necessary to prevent an ongoing criminal act from causing further serious imminent harm to the victim of the crime—an animal entitled to statutory protection.”

Id. (emphasis added). By contrast, in State v. Pierce, 226 Or App 336, 203 P3d 343 (2009), police were dispatched to a residence regarding a noise disturbance at 1:00 a.m. When they arrived, they could hear people yelling and screaming in the back yard. The officer believed that the crime of disorderly conduct was being committed. An officer walked along the side the property and peered through a fence, saw the defendant attempting to destroy a few small marijuana plants, and arrested him. The Court of Appeals concluded that regardless of probable cause, the warrantless invasions into the side and back yard of the residence were not justified by any exigency. The state argued that the noise indicated that people were “‘having a disagreement’ that presented the danger of someone being injured.” The court held:

“[T]he crime of disorderly conduct—based merely on people loudly ‘having an argument’ in the middle of the night—did not require ‘the police to act swiftly to prevent danger to life or serious damage to property, or to forestall a suspect’s escape or the destruction of evidence.’ '''' To be sure, defendant’s conduct may have irritated [the] neighbors and disturbed their sleep, but it did not rise to the level of a constitutionally cognizable ‘exigency.’”

Id. at 341. ' The following cases present additional scenarios that the court held insufficient to constitute exigent circumstances. Bridewell,' 306 Or at 231; State v. Davis, 295 Or 227, 666 P2d 802 (1983); State v. Lynch, 135 Or App 528, 900 P2d 1042 (1995).

But the courts have adopted a near per se “meth lab” exception with two requirements: (1) the officer must have probable cause to believe that the premises contains a “working methamphetamine lab,” i.e., a lab where methamphetamine is being manufactured and chemical reactions are taking place; and (2) it must be in a residential area or neighborhood. State v. Chapman, 107 Or App 325, 333-34, 813 P2d 557 (1991).

V. Destruction of Evidence

A. Intentional Destruction of Evidence

Exigent circumstances include, among other things, situations in which a reasonable person in the police officer’s position would determine that, under the circumstances, “immediate action is necessary to prevent the disappearance, dissipation, or destruction of evidence.” State v. Meharry, 342 Or 173, 177, 149 P3d 1155, (2006) (emphasis added); Snow, 337 Or at 225; State v. Heikkinen, 94 Or App 472, 475-76, 765 P2d 1252 (1988) (finding exigent circumstances where the officers heard the toilet flush repeatedly as they were standing at the door, and they knew from experience that drugs were often destroyed by that technique); '' State v. Jangala, 154 Or App 176, 179-80, 961 P2d 246 (1998) (finding exigent circumstances where officers were breaking up an underage drinking party and saw people fleeing the scene and telling others that the police had arrived, and the officers knew from their training and experience that evidence, like the keg, was likely to have disappeared by the time they obtained a warrant);[2] State v. Porter, 198 Or App 274, 108 P3d 107 (2005) (finding exigent circumstances where officers had observed a person smoking methamphetamine through an open second-story window and, after being allowed inside, the officers were told that no one was upstairs, but they heard a person upstairs and they reasonably believed the person could have been destroying evidence);butsee'' State v. Girard, 276 Or 511, 515, 555 P2d 445 (1976) (officers’ entry was not justified to forestall destruction of burglary evidence because that evidence, which included a tape recorder, silver, and similar items, was not easily disposed of).

Preservation of “readily destructible” DNA evidence may also constitute an exigent circumstance. Although it does not appear that this issue has been analyzed under Article I, section 9, the United States Supreme Court has analyzed it under the Fourth Amendment. In Cupp v. Murphy, 412 US 291, 93 S Ct 2000, 36 L Ed 2d 900 (1973), Murphy voluntarily went to the police station to discuss his wife’s death by strangulation, which had occurred earlier that evening. An officer noticed dried blood on Murphy’s finger. The officer asked Murphy for a sample of scrapings from his fingernails. When Murphy declined, he put his hands behind his back and appeared to rub them together. He then put his hands in his pockets, and a “metallic sound, such as keys or change rattling” could be heard. The police took a sample over his protest and without a warrant.

In upholding the search, the Court concluded that the police officers had probable cause to arrest Murphy for the murder and the question “sufficiently apprised [Murphy] of his suspected role in the crime to motivate him to attempt to destroy what evidence[.]” Id. at 296. According to the Court, the exigency created by the readily destructible nature of the evidence, coupled with Murphy’s motivation to destroy the evidence, justified a warrantless search to preserve the evidence. Id.

B. DUII—Blood/Breath/Urine Tests

1. Dissipation of Alcohol

In State v. Machuca, 347 Or 644, 657, 227 P3d 729 (2010), the Oregon Supreme Court held that “the evanescent nature of a suspect’s blood alcohol content is an exigent circumstance that will ordinarily permit a warrantless blood draw[.]” Id. at 657 (emphasis added).

However, the court also noted “that particular facts may show, in the rare case, that a warrant could have been obtained and executed significantly faster than the actual process otherwise used under the circumstances.” Id. at 657 (emphasis in original). In that situation, Article I, section 9, requires a warrant.

By contrast, '' the Supreme Court of the United States in Missouri v. McNeely, 596 US 141, 133 S Ct 1552, 185 L Ed 2d 696 (2013), established a test that is more protective of individual privacy thanMachuca''.[3] The Court announced the following rule:

“In those drunk-driving investigations where police officers can reasonably obtain a warrant before a blood sample can be drawn without significantly undermining the efficacy of the search, the Fourth Amendment mandates that they do so.”

Id. at 152 (emphasis added). Thus, under the Fourth Amendment, the state must prove that a warrant could not have reasonably been obtained “without significantly undermining the efficacy of the search.” Whereas, Article I, section 9, only requires a warrant if “a warrant could have been obtained and executed significantly faster than the actual process otherwise used.” Machuca, 347 Or at 657. Thus, the federal rule is substantively more protective, as it places a heavier burden on the state to justify the warrantless search.

To clarify the contours of the rule under the Fourth Amendment, a plurality of the justices expressly considered and rejected a rule proposed by Chief Justice Roberts—and even that rule is more protective of individual privacy than the rule announced in Machuca. The Chief Justice proposed the following rule:

“The natural dissipation of alcohol in the bloodstream constitutes not only the imminent but ongoing destruction of critical evidence. That would qualify as an exigent circumstance, except that there may be time to secure a warrant before blood can be drawn. If there is an officer must seek a warrant. If an officer could reasonably conclude that there is not, the exigent circumstances exception applies by its terms, and the blood may be drawn without a warrant.”

McNeely, 569 US at 166-67 (C.J., Roberts concurring) (emphasis added). The plurality rejected that rule, describing it as a “modified per se rule” “under which a warrantless blood draw is permissible if the officer could not secure a warrant (or reasonably believed he could not secure a warrant) in the time it takes to transport the suspect to a hospital or similar facility and obtain medical assistance.” McNeely, 569 US at 156-57. The plurality faulted it for focusing solely on the comparison between the time it took to get the sample and the time it would have taken to get a warrant, because it could “distort law enforcement incentives.” Id. The Court wrote:

“As with the State’s per se rule, the Chief Justice’s rule might discourage efforts to expedite the warrant process because it categorically authorizes warrantless blood draws so long as it takes more time to secure a warrant than to obtain medical assistance. On the flip side, making the requirement of independent judicial oversight turn exclusively on the amount of time that elapses between an arrest and BAC testing could induce police departments and individual officers to minimize testing delay to the detriment of other values.”

Id. At 157 (emphasis added). In the end, the majority concluded:

“Although we agree that delay inherent to the blood-testing process is relevant to evaluating exigency, we decline to substitute the Chief Justice’s modified per se rule for our traditional totality of the circumstances analysis.”

Id. To determine whether police officers reasonably could have obtained a warrant without significantly undermining the efficacy of the search, the Court directed reviewing courts to consider the following factors: (1) whether technologies exist to expedite the warrant process (e.g., obtaining a warrant by telephone, email, or video conference or using standard forms for warrant applications); id. at 155 (“[T]echnological developments that enable police officers to secure warrants more quickly * * * are relevant to an assessment of exigency.”);[4] (2) whether another officer could have assisted the arresting officer in performing routine tasks, allowing the arresting officer to obtain a warrant without significantly increasing the delay in administering the test; id. at 152 (“[If] an officer can take steps to secure a warrant while the suspect is being transported to a medical facility by another officer * * * there would be no plausible justification for an exception to the warrant requirement.”); (3) whether “practical problems [exist to] obtaining the warrant within a timeframe that still preserves the opportunity to obtain reliable evidence[.]” (e.g., investigating a car accident, attending to injured persons, transporting the defendant to the hospital, unavailability of a magistrate). Id. at 164, 150.[[#_ftn5|[5]]]

Returning to Oregon law, the Machuca court clearly avoided declaring a per se exigency rule, recognizing that the “evanescent nature of a suspect’s blood alcohol content” is not the only relevant factor in determining whether such a search is constitutional. 367 Or at 656-57. The court reiterated that the other factor is the delay that would have been involved in obtaining a warrant. The court stated although such cases may be rare, it would be unconstitutional to compel a search “where a warrant could have been obtained and executed significantly faster than the actual process otherwise used under the circumstances.” Id. (emphasis in original).

In State v. Martinez-Alvarez, 245 Or App 369, 376, 263 P3d 1091 (2011), the Court of Appeals explained,

“[W]hatever else a ‘rare case’ may mean—and we do not purport to define the extremely limited universe of ‘rare cases’—it necessarily includes a case in which an objectively reasonable officer would have understood at the time of the DUII stop and arrest that a warrant could have been obtained significantly more quickly than the actual time (and consequent dissipation of alcohol) between the probable cause determination and the administration of the breath test or blood draw. Our emphasis is quite conscious: The standard is not one of ‘20/20 hindsight’—rather, it is predicated on an officer’s contemporaneous perspective based on information known or reasonably discernible in the totality of the circumstances of the particular encounter.”

Id. at 376.

2024 Update

If an officer can obtain a blood sample from a suspect faster than she can obtain a warrant from a magistrate, then, under Machuca, a warrantless blood draw is permissible. State v. Portulano, 320 Or App 335, 349, 514 P3d 93 (2022). However, under the Fourth Amendment, the totality of circumstances did not support the existence of an exigency. Considering the factors set out in Missouri v. McNeely, 569 US 141, 148, 133 S Ct 1552, 185 L Ed 2d 696 (2013), the officers had the necessary equipment to utilize the legislatively authorized remote warrant process, judicial officers were available in the county to issue warrants remotely, there was no indication that applying for an electronic warrant would have been incompatible with management of the accident scene, and police on the scene were operating as if there was a per se exigency rule for warrantless blood draws in the particular county—so they did not even consider getting a warrant. Id. at 357-59. Therefore, the warrantless blood draw violated the Fourth Amendment. Id. at 359.

2. Dissipation of Controlled Substances

Under State v. McMullen, 250 Or App 208, 279 P3d 367 (2012), probable cause to believe that evidence of any controlled substance will be found in a suspect’s urine, plus evidence of the evanescent nature of “at least one” controlled substance, amounts to an exigency justifying a warrantless seizure and search. 250 Or App at 213; State v. Fuller, 252 Or App 245, 254 287 P3d 1147 (2012); State v. Walker, 251 Or App 651, 652, 284 P3d 576 (2012).

Additionally, in State v. Mazzola, 356 Or 804, 820, 345 P3d 424, 435 (2015), the court held that once the officer had probable cause to believe that a driver is impaired by a controlled substance, the exigent circumstances exception allowed police to compel the driver to perform field sobriety tests in order to collect physical evidence impairment before that evidence dissipated.

The rule of law from McMullen is suspect, and defense counsel should argue that McMullen is plainly wrong. McMullen suffers from two distinct analytical flaws. First, it extends Machuca’s holding concerning the “highly evanescent” nature of blood alcohol to all controlled substances no matter how slowly they are metabolized. Second, the state’s interest in quickly obtaining a breath or blood sample in DUII-alcohol cases is significantly greater than obtaining a urine sample in DUII-CS cases.

The Machuca court reached its conclusion after considering more than 30 years of case law recognizing the “highly evanescent” nature of “blood alcohol” in particular. Id. at 652-56 (discussing State v. Heintz, 286 Or 239, 594 P2d 385 (1979), State v. Milligan, 304 Or 659, 748 P2d 130 (1988), and State v. Moylett, 313 Or 540, 836 P2d 1329 (1992). Nothing in Machuca suggests that the court would have reached a similar conclusion with regard to a different substance, controlled substances in general, or any evanescent evidence whatsoever no matter its rate of dissipation. Under Machuca, alcohol’s particular evanescence will ordinarily be sufficient, but the presence of a less evanescent substance might not.

The second analytical failure in McMullen is its failure to account for the fact that the state’s interest in obtaining a breath or blood sample as close to the time of driving as possible is significantly more compelling in DUII-Alcohol cases than obtaining a urine sample in DUII-CS cases. Alcohol testing is quantitative, whereas urine testing is not. In alcohol cases, “minutes count” as evidence of BAC at the time of driving is disappearing at “some significant rate.” Machuca, 347 Or at 657, 655. That is, the person’s BAC itself is in the process of decreasing during any extra time it would take to seek a warrant. By contrast, in DUII-CS cases, minutes do not count. Because the quantity of a particular controlled substance in urine is not an element of the offense nor is it tested for, the urgency is far less.

The following example illustrates the problem. An officer develops probable cause to believe that the defendant has driven exclusively under the influence of marijuana (detectable in urine for s
even to 10 days); however, if there is evidence at the suppression hearing that any drug (cocaine, for example) breaks down in the urine, those facts establish exigent circumstances under McMullen. The problem with that analysis is that the destruction of evidence was not imminent, police did not have to act swiftly, and it entirely ignores whether the officers were responding to the practical necessities of the situation, or whether a warrant could have been obtained without sacrificing the evidence.

VI. Home Entry

A. The Home: “Nothing Is More Inviolate”

“The very purpose of [Article I, section 9,] was to protect a person’s home from governmental intrusions. This right against intrusion should be stringently protected by the courts.” State v. Davis, 295 Or 227, 243, 666 P2d 802 (1983) (citation omitted). '' “A government intrusion into the home is at the extreme end of the spectrum: ‘Nothing is as personal or private. Nothing is more inviolate.’ State v. Fair, 353 Or 588, 600-01, 302 P3d 417 (2013) (citing [file:///C:/Users/jennifer/Documents/Publications/S&S%20Manual/%202014%20edition%20/august%202014%20chapters%20from%20Ryan%20/3.%20warrantless%20searches/final%20from%20ryan%209.5.14/3-g%20residences/State%20v.%20Tourtillott,%20289%20Or%20845,State v. Tourtillott,'' 289 Or 835, 865, 618 P2d 423 (1980)].

B. Probable Cause Plus Exigent Circumstances

1. Can police enter a residence on probable cause to arrest a suspected felon?

The exigent circumstances exception comes into play only if probable cause of a crime exists. Bridewell, 306 Or at 235-36. Both the state and federal constitutions “dictate that where exigent circumstances * * * do not exist, probable cause to arrest does not justify a forcible entry into the home of the suspect.” State v. Olson, 287 Or 157, 164-65, 598 P2d 670 (1979). As the Oregon Supreme Court noted in Peller, 287 Or at 261-62:

“[P]robable cause by itself ‘justifies only the issuance of a warrant.’ In order to justify a warrantless entry such as occurred in this case, the state must show that ‘the exigencies of the situation made the course imperative.’”

Id. (citations omitted). The United State Supreme Court has held that line even in cases involving homicide. See Mincey v. Arizona, 437 US 385, 394, 98 S Ct 2408 (1978) (“We decline to hold that the seriousness of the offense under investigation itself creates exigent circumstances of the kind that under the Fourth Amendment justify a warrantless search.”); '''' Welsh v. Wisconsin, 466 US 740, 753, 104 S Ct 2091, 80 L Ed 2d 732 (1984) (“[N]o exigency is created simply because there is probable cause to believe that a serious crime has been committed[.]”); Payton v. New York, 445 US 573, 576, 590, 100 S Ct 1371, 63 L Ed 2d 639 (1980) (Although police had probable cause that defendant had committed murder, “the Fourth Amendment has drawn a firm line at the entrance to the house. Absent exigent circumstances, that threshold may not reasonably be crossed without a warrant.”).

2. DUII—Home Entry: Can police enter a home to collect evidence of DUII?

To justify a home entry to obtain evidence of DUII, police must first establish how long it would take to obtain a search warrant, and must also show that police had “an objectively reasonable belief that the circumstances were such that, had they waited for that warrant, the suspect’s blood would have lost all evidentiary value.” State v. Ritz, 270 Or App 88, 347 P3d 1052 (2015).

In State v. Sullivan, 265 Or App 62, 333 P3d 1201 (2014), The Court of Appeals reiterated the Oregon Supreme Court’s observation that,

“[t]he degree to which law enforcement conduct intrudes on a citizen’s protected interest in privacy and liberty is significantly affected by where the conduct occurs, such as in the home, in an automobile, or on a public street. A government intrusion into the home is at the extreme end of the spectrum: Nothing is as personal or private. Nothing is more inviolate.”


 Id. at 78-79 (emphasis in Sullivan). The court concluded its opinion with the following pronouncement:

“[T]he one thing that is certain is that a sine qua non to determining the necessity of an immediate home entry is some showing as to how long it would have taken to obtain a warrant under the circumstances. Without requiring the state to make any showing on that point as part of its burden to justify a warrantless home entry, it is impossible to say to what degree—if any at all—that the potential evidence sought would have been ‘sacrificed.’”

Id. at 80 (emphasis in original).

Moreover, in State v. Ritz, 361 Or 781, 399 P3d 421 (2017), the court held that the state failed to establish that the entry would preserve the evidence that was dissipating:

“The metabolic process causing the blood-alcohol evidence to dissipate does not stop simply because officers have entered a defendant's home. Instead, to preserve a defendant's BAC, officers entering a home must then also obtain and test a sample of the defendant's breath or blood. As a result, the appropriate question in determining whether there was an exigency is not whether obtaining a warrant would have delayed entering defendant's home. The appropriate question is whether obtaining a warrant would have delayed obtaining and testing a sample of defendant’s breath or blood.” (Id. at 795.) ''''''

“The record does not establish that the officers reasonably believed, at the time that they entered defendant's home, that obtaining a warrant would have delayed preserving evidence that was dissipating. The state therefore failed to establish that the officers reasonably believed that they were faced with an exigency in this case.” ''''''

Id. at 799. To satisfy its burden to prove the existence of an exigency here, the state is required to develop a record that would permit an assessment of whether, at the time he entered the property, the officer reasonably believed that the blood-alcohol evidence he sought was at risk of complete dissipation in the time it would take to get a warrant. State v. Perrott, 288 Or App 837, 407 P3d 892 (2017).

C. Hot Pursuit

1. Must the defendant be suspected of a felony?

In the case of continuous or “hot” pursuit, the Oregon courts have authorized warrantless entries regardless of whether the suspected crime is a misdemeanor or a felony. See, e.g., [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=State+v.+Niedermeyer,+48+Or+App+665,+&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=1615212216491002444&scilh=0 State v. Niedermeyer, ' 48 Or App 665, 617 P2d 911 (1980)] (Class A misdemeanor). However, Niedermeyer rested on now defunct' Oregon case law and '''' United States v. Santana, 427 US 38, 96 S Ct 2406, 49 L Ed 2d 300 (1976), interpreting the Fourth Amendment. 48 Or App at 670. Thus, it does not appear that the issue has been independently analyzed under the state constitution.

Moreover, the holding of Santana has been significantly limited. Currently, under the Fourth Amendment, the gravity of the underlying offense is of major significance in determining whether an exigent circumstance exists sufficient to make a warrantless entry reasonable to recover evidence of that crime:

“[A]n important factor to be considered when determining whether any exigency exists is the gravity of the underlying offense for which the arrest is being made. Moreover, although no exigency is created simply because there is probable cause to believe that a serious crime has been committed, * * * application of the exigent-circumstances exception in the context of a home entry should rarely be sanctioned when there is probable cause to believe that only a minor offense, such as the kind at issue in this case, has been committed.”

Welsh v. Wisconsin, 466 US 740, 753, 104 S Ct 2091, 80 L Ed 2d 732 (1984) (emphasis added).

D. Imminent Escape

The police may enter when in possession of facts indicating the imminent flight of a suspect. State v. Girard, 276 Or 511, 515, 555 P2d 445 (1976) (“It is true that defendant’s admonition, ‘Hurry, they are coming’ may not have given the officers reason to enter to prevent evidence from being concealed or destroyed because the burglary evidence was not easily disposable. However, the officers still could have understood the statement to mean, ‘Let’s get out of here; the officers are coming,’ in which case entry would be justified to effect an arrest to avoid escape.”).

However, if the police can prevent removal or escape merely by staking out the premises, Oregon authority requires that a search warrant be obtained. Peller, 287 Or at 264 (“Absent any indications that escape was imminent, the officers * * * should have staked out the house and obtained a warrant before entering.”); State v. Stoudamire, 198 Or App 399, 411-13, 108 P3d 615 (2005); '' State v. Wynn, 102 Or App 1, 4, 792 P2d 1234, 1235-36 (1990);see also'' State v. Court, 81 Or App 598, 726 P2d 1205 (1986); State v. Nicholson, 89 Or App 306, 311, 748 P2d 1028 (1988) (“Assigning [an officer] to watch the car while the officer obtained a warrant would have been the correct procedure.”).

E. Factors that Cut Against Exigent Circumstances

The “exigency” requirement imposes three critical limitations on the circumstances under which the state can properly claim the exception. The first is that the necessity must be real, not created by the police. Matsen/Wilson, 287 Or at 587. The second is that the emergency may not be the result of police inexperience or ignorance of the warrant application procedure. [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=State+v.+Roberts,+75+Or+App+292&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38&case=1200209121720903743&scilh=0 State v. Roberts, '' 75 Or App 292, 706 P2d 564 (1985)]. And third is whether a less intrusive alternative such as surrounding the house while awaiting a warrant was available.Peller, 287 Or at 265.''

1. Police Cannot Create the Exigency

In Matsen/Wilson, the court explained:

“[T]he state failed to prove that destruction of contraband or the escape of the defendants was imminent. The fact that drugs are usually of a destructible nature, and the fact that suspects are likely to run out the back door when police enter the front door does not ipso facto create exigent circumstances. Nor does the fact that the deliveries were sporadic mean that when [the suspected supplier] did arrive, exigent circumstances then arose. The police cannot weave together a web of information, then claim exigent circumstances when the suspect arrives and can conveniently be snared.

287 Or at 587 '' (internal citations omitted); State v. Collicott, 56 Or App 605, 610, 642 P2d 1187 (1982) (holding that the exigent circumstances exception wasnotapplicable when the police had information at least two days before the search that drugs would be found at defendant’s house, but failed to seek judicial authorization for the search);see also'' State v. Price, '''' 92 Or App 669, 759 P2d 1130 (1988) (holding that the warrantless seizure of defendant’s purse was not justified by the exigent circumstances because “defendant was bringing marijuana to work on a regular basis and frequently smoking it on her breaks. The risk that she might attempt to conceal or destroy the evidence arose as a result of the officer’s own inquiry, but an officer cannot create exigent circumstances by his own action or inaction. The inconvenience and delay in obtaining a warrant does not, alone, justify a warrantless seizure”).

2. The State Cannot Rely on an Officer’s Ignorance of the Warrant Application Procedures

Second, the inability to secure a warrant must be reasonable, not the result of police inexperience or ignorance. Roberts, 75 Or App at 296. There, the court unequivocally explained:

“The evidence offered by the state in this case consisted of officers’ testimony that obtaining a warrant would have taken an entire day, because the officers had no familiarity with the procedure. Police officers cannot create their own exigencies by failing to familiarize themselves with constitutionally mandated procedures.”

Id.

3. Failure to Take Advantage of an Opportunity to Secure the Premises

In Stoudamire, 198 Or App at 411-13, two police officers responded to a home security alarm at the defendant’s residence. Police entered the house without a warrant or the defendant’s consent. The court held:

“Even assuming that there was probable cause to believe that the police would find a burglar or evidence of a burglary in defendant’s house, there were no exigent circumstances that would justify the decision by the police to enter defendant’s home[.]”

Id. at 410-11. The dissent concluded that an immediate entry was necessary to prevent escape by the burglar or damage to defendant’s property. The majority disagreed because the officers could and should have requested backup to surround the house while they sought a search warrant. Id. see also Peller, 287 Or at 265 (“Absent any indications that escape was imminent, the officers in the present case should have staked out the house and obtained a warrant before entering.”).

The majority also criticized the dissent for assuming that securing a warrant would have required at least one of the officers to leave the house for a significant time. The majority noted,

“Warrants are available by telephone, ORS 133.545(5) and 133.555(3), and the availability of such a warrant is to be considered in determining the existence of exigent circumstances. See Stevens, 311 Or at 129-30, 806 P2d 92 (citing State v. Wise, 305 Or 78, 82 n 3, 749 P2d 1179 (1988)). Here, the state had the burden of establishing the existence of exigent circumstances, and it offered no evidence on the availability of a telephonic warrant.

“* * *

“Because a telephonic warrant could be obtained without either officer leaving defendant’s home, none of the circumstances that Judge Deits identifies created an exigency that allowed the officers to search the home for a burglar without getting a warrant.”

Stoudamire, 198 Or App at 411-13; see also State v. Wynn, 102 Or App 1, 4, 792 P2d 1234 (1990) (“If the officers were concerned that defendant would be gone before they could get a warrant, they could have watched the house for the time it would have taken to get the warrant.”).

4. Failure to Seek a Warrant, Including a Telephonic Warrant

In the exigency analysis, “One relevant consideration is whether the police attempted to get a telephonic warrant, and it is no excuse that police are unfamiliar with the procedure.” Wynn, 102 Or App at 4; see also Court, 81 Or App at 603 (failure to seek a telephonic warrant negated state’s claim that one officer would have had to leave a potentially dangerous situation to obtain a warrant). “Without any evidence of the length of time necessary to obtain a warrant or any explanation for not trying to obtain a warrant, we will not assume the officers could not obtain one within a reasonable time.” State v. Rubert, 46 Or App 843, 847, 612 P2d 771 (1980).

VII. Case Law Suppressing Contents of Closed Containers

A. Lack of Probable Cause

State v. Lane, 135 Or App 233, 240, 898 P2d 1358 (1995): After arresting the defendant for DUII, officer performed an inventory search and found a gun, a marijuana pipe with residue, a small scale, and a small, black film canister. The officer opened the film canister and found a plastic bag of methamphetamine.

The court held that the officer did not have probable cause to open the film canister to determine whether it contained a controlled substance. Probable cause to arrest could not have been based solely on the residue of marijuana discovered in the pipe. And the observation of less than one ounce of marijuana could not, by itself, have created probable cause to believe that there was enough contraband in the vehicle to justify an arrest.

Additionally, the film canister was not a type of container so uniquely associated with controlled substances that its presence combined with the scales found in the truck, and defendant’s nervousness and agitation, would constitute probable cause to believe he was in possession of the necessary quantity of controlled substances.

B. Lack of Exigent Circumstances

State v. Walker, 173 Or App 46, 52, 20 P3d 834 (2001): Probable cause alone is not sufficient to justify a warrantless search of a closed container. Police can lawfully open an opaque container without a warrant in only two circumstances: (1) the container so “announces its contents” that opening it does not invade a protected privacy interest and, thus, does not constitute a search,[6] or (2) the opening is supported by probable cause and some exception to the warrant requirement, like officer safety, exigent circumstances, or the automobile exception. Id. at 51-52.

State v. Hite, 198 Or App 1, 3, 107 P3d 677 (2005): The defendant was arrested and admitted to stealing a car that was about 25 yards away from where they were standing. Police opened the trunk and removed a backpack. The officer asked if there was a gun in the bag. Defendant said, “There might be.” The officer searched and found evidence. Among other justifications, the state attempted to rely on exigent circumstances. The court held:

 “Assuming that the risk of a gun accidentally firing [during transport] could justify searching in a place beyond an arrestee’s control, the circumstances here were not sufficiently immediate to excuse the need for a warrant * * * [The officer] did not suggest that there was any risk as long as the backpacks remained in the trunk of the stolen car. Nor did he offer any reason to believe that the backpacks had to be moved before a warrant could be obtained. The officer safety ground for the exception is applicable only when it would be unreasonable to expect an officer to obtain a warrant because the officer is faced with an immediate risk of harm. The risk in this case could have been delayed simply by leaving the backpacks in the car where it was parked while the officers obtained a warrant. Police officers cannot rely on exigencies of their own creation to justify a warrantless search. See State v. Price, 92 Or App 669, 673, 759 P2d 1130 (1988) (‘[A]n officer cannot create exigent circumstances by his own action or inaction. The inconvenience and delay in obtaining a warrant does not, alone, justify a warrantless seizure.’ (Citation omitted.)); see also State v. Chinn, 231 Or 259, 268, 373 P2d 392 (1962) (noting that ‘a search might be held unreasonable if the officers had had ample opportunity to consult a magistrate and obtain a search warrant but failed to do so’). Because the possibility that the backpacks might have contained a gun presented no immediate risk of harm, the search was not justified as a search incident to arrest for the purpose of ensuring officer safety.”

Id. at 10.

State v. Dickerson, 135 Or App 192, 195, 898 P2d 193 (1995): Officer knew that the tires of several cars in the area had recently been slashed. Officer arrested the defendant on an outstanding warrant. During a pat-down search incident to arrest, the officer removed a folding pocket knife. The officer opened the knife and found rubber tire residue on the blade. Id. at 194. The court held, “Although here the officers were justified in removing the pocketknife for their own protection or to prevent defendant from escaping, once the knife was in their possession, they no longer had reason to believe that it posed a threat to them. State v. Jones, 103 Or App 316, 797 P2d 385 (1990).”

State v. Meyer, 120 Or App 319, 852 P2d 879 (1993) (“Suppressing evidence found in a box in the defendant’s car where the officer had probable cause to believe there was a gun in the car ‘somewhere,’ but not probable cause to believe that it was in the box * * * The burden was on the state to prove that the weight of the box gave the officer probable cause to believe that there was a gun in it. Furthermore, once it was seized, even if he had probable cause to seize it, there was no need for haste, and safety concerns did not create exigent circumstances sufficient to justify the failure to obtain a search warrant.”).

State v. Barnum, 136 Or App 167, 174-75, 902 P2d 95 (1995) (although exigent circumstances may have justified the warrantless invasion of defendant’s possessory interest in the notebook, they did not justify the warrantless invasion of defendant’s privacy interest in it. Once the police had the notebook in their possession, the officers no longer had reason to fear that the contents would be lost or destroyed; the exigency had expired. Absent another justification for the ensuing search of the notebook without a warrant, the search was invalid).
