Chapter 8. She Said What?: Hearsay Evidence

By Bryan Francesconi

I. Introduction

Many of Oregon’s hearsay rules are either specific to sex crimes cases or likely to arise in that type of prosecution. This chapter provides an in-depth analysis of those rules, including case law and practice tips. Confrontation Clause principles that further limit the scope of these rules when the statements are “testimonial” and offered for the truth of the matter asserted are first examined and then discussed in the context of each of the following hearsay rules:

  • Complaint of sexual misconduct under OEC 803 18(a)(b)

  • Statements concerning an act of abuse under OEC 803 18(a)(b)

  • Statements for purpose of medical diagnosis under OEC 803 (4)

  • Excited utterance under OEC 803(2)

  • Existing mental, emotional or physical condition under OEC 803(3)

  • Domestic violence exception under OEC 803(26)

  • Residual hearsay exception under OEC 803(28a) and OEC 804(3)(f)

  • Multiple hearsay under OEC 805

It must always be kept in mind that the hearsay rules and the Confrontation Clause are not coextensive: evidence may be admissible under the Confrontation Clause, yet be inadmissible under Oregon’s hearsay rules; conversely, evidence may be admissible as a hearsay exception, yet be inadmissible under the Confrontation Clause. If the hearsay declarant testifies at trial and is subject to full and effective cross-examination about the statement, the confrontation requirement is satisfied. California v. Green, 399 US 149 (1970). Crawford does not change that.

The chapter concludes with an examination of several other Oregon evidence rules that commonly arise in sex crime cases: recanted hearsay and impeachment of an absent declarant under OEC 806; witness competency under OEC 601; and learned treatise and the basis for expert opinion under OEC 703 and 706.

II. The Constitutional Framework of Admissibility of Accuser’s Prior Statements

When the state seeks to introduce statements of an accuser, three distinct rights of a criminal defendant come into play. First, a criminal defendant has the right to exclude hearsay statements from her trial under the Oregon Evidence Code. Second, a defendant has a right to confront one’s accuser under the Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution. Third, and distinct from the Oregon analysis, a criminal defendant has the right to challenge hearsay evidence under Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution.

Crawford v. Washington, 541 US 36 (2004), provides the federal confrontation analysis which prohibits the “admission of testimonial statements of a witness who did not appear at trial unless he was unavailable to testify, and the defendant had a prior opportunity for cross-examination.”

For determining admissibility of hearsay statements under Oregon’s constitution, our courts use the two-part test set out in Ohio v. Roberts, 448 US 56 (1980). State v. Campbell, 299 Or 633 (1985): courts must evaluate whether the declarant is “unavailable” at the time of trial and must determine whether the statement qualifies under a firmly rooted hearsay exception or itself possesses “sufficient indicia of reliability.” Note that the Oregon Evidence Code definition of “unavailable” is not necessarily the same as under state law or the federal confrontation clause. See, e.g., [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=1665187790609905707&q=See,+e.g.,+State+v.+George,+146+Or+App+449&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 State v. George], 146 Or App 449, 453 n 2 (1997), discussed infra.

A. The Federal Confrontation Clause

1. When is a statement “testimonial” for purposes of the Confrontation analysis?

In Crawford, the United States Supreme Court turned the focus of the confrontation analysis toward whether the statements at issue were “testimonial.” 541 US at 59. It did so in order to address the “principal evil” targeted by the Confrontation Clause, which was the “use of ex parte examinations as evidence against the accused.” Id. at 50. The Court identified a “testimonial” statement as one that was “made under circumstances which would lead an objective witness reasonably to believe that the statement would be available for use at a later trial.” Id. at 51-52. The Court observed that “testimony” is “[a] solemn declaration or affirmation made for the purpose of establishing or proving some fact.” Id. at 51. In Davis v. Washington, 547 US 813 (2006), the Court offered an expanded discussion of what else constitutes a testimonial statement:

Statements are nontestimonial when made in the course of police interrogation under circumstances objectively indicating that the primary purpose of the interrogation is to enable police assistance to meet an ongoing emergency. They are testimonial when the circumstances objectively indicate that there is no such ongoing emergency, and that the primary purpose of the interrogation is to establish or prove past events potentially relevant to later criminal prosecution.

Although that definition focused on the purpose of an “interrogation,” the Court emphasized that statements made in the absence of interrogation were not necessarily nontestimonial: “[I]t is in the final analysis the declarant’s statements, not the interrogator’s questions, that the Confrontation Clause requires us to evaluate.” Id. at 822–23 n 1.

In Ohio v. Clark, 135 S.Ct. 2173 (2015), the Supreme Court stated the test for whether a statement is “testimonial” is whether it was given with the primary purpose “to create an out-of-court substitute for trial testimony,” at 2180. In Clark, the Court held that a three-year-old victim’s statement to his preschool teacher identifying defendant as the person who had caused his injuries was not testimonial.

2. Does it matter if the statement was made to a private citizen through CARES
or DHS?

In Ohio v. Clark, the Supreme Court addressed for the first time “whether statements to persons other than law enforcement officers are subject to the Confrontation Clause,” 135 S.Ct. at 2181, and concluded: “Because at least some statements to individuals who are not law enforcement officers could conceivably raise confrontation concerns, we decline to adopt a categorical rule excluding them from the Sixth Amendment’s reach. Nevertheless, such statements are much less likely to be testimonial than statements to law enforcement officers.” Id.

In State v. Mack, 337 Or 586 (2004), the Oregon Supreme Court confronted the question of whether videotaped statements made by a three-year-old to a DHS caseworker were testimonial. On two separate occasions, the investigating police officer videotaped a three-year-old child witness’s interview by a DHS caseworker, whom police had asked to conduct the interviews. Arguing that the statements were nontestimonial, the state relied upon the fact that the interview itself had not been performed by a police officer. The court did not find the distinction critical, concluding that there was no relevant distinction between a child’s statements to the caseworker and the testimonial statements the court held to be inadmissible in [https://a.next.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=Y&serNum=2004190005&originatingDoc=I1218813474b511dbb29ecfd71e79cb92&refType=RP&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.UserEnteredCitation) Crawford.] The court reasoned that the caseworker was functioning as an agent for the police because the questioning had been at the officer’s request and structured with law enforcement goals in mind. The court concluded that when a private citizen is working as a “proxy” for the police and gathering information to assist a police investigation, the gathered statements shall be deemed testimonial. [https://a.next.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=Y&serNum=2004190005&originatingDoc=I1218813474b511dbb29ecfd71e79cb92&refType=RP&originationContext=document&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.UserEnteredCitation) Id.] at 593.

In State ex rel. Juvenile Dep’t of Multnomah Cty. v. S.P., 346 Or 592 (2009), a pediatrician and a social worker, both working for CARES, interviewed a child. The state argued that there is a “world of difference” when medical personnel and a social worker, rather than DHS staff, are involved. The Oregon Supreme Court disagreed. “Service as a proxy for the police appears to be a primary function of CARES. CARES receives a significant amount of its funding from the Department of Justice. As a condition of receiving those funds, CARES must train its workers in ‘legally sound’ interview techniques.” Id. at 620. “We cannot see why the involvement of physicians and social workers, if those physicians and social workers are acting as proxies for police officers, presents any less risk [in the production of testimonial evidence].” Id. at 621.

3. Is the testimonial determination based on the perspective of the declarant or
the listener?

In Ohio v. Clark, supra, the Supreme Court examined both the primary purpose of the listener and the primary purpose of the hearsay declarant. Id. at 2181-82.

In Mack, the Oregon Supreme Court focused on the law enforcement goals of the listener (the DHS worker) in determining the conversation was testimonial. Does that mean that it is the listener’s motivations that are paramount? Not necessarily. By comparison, in State v. Camarena, 334 Or 28 (2008), the Oregon Supreme Court articulated that when evaluating the testimonial nature of a 9-1-1 call, the relevant inquiry can be the perspectives of the declarant. 344 Or at 40 (statements made to 9-1-1 about an attack were partially testimonial). In reaching its conclusions about which parts of the call
were testimonial, the court focused on the language in Davis that the Confrontation Clause requires analysis of “the declarant’s statements, not the interrogator’s questions.” Id. at 40 (quoting Davis, 547 US at 822–23, n 1). Thus, a court should consider the perspectives of both, the listener and the declarant.

If either party had goals associated with law enforcement, the encounter was testimonial. In Multnomah Cty. v. S.P., supra, 346 Or at 613, the Oregon Supreme Court made clear that the testimonial analysis is not to be focused on the intent of one party, but rather the totality of the circumstances surrounding the declaration. If any party to the conversation had law enforcement goals, those statements were testimonial. “Thus, in Mack and Camarena, this court emphasized that whether a statement is testimonial depends on an objective analysis of the contents and circumstances of the statement, rather than an attempt to determine only the subjective intentions of the questioner or the declarant. We infer the purpose of an interrogation from the totality of the circumstances in which it took place and the results that it yielded.” Id.

Practice Tip

If either the declarant who made the statement or the listener who heard the statement had motivations to aid a police investigation, those motivations should establish that the statement was testimonial and your client has a right to cross-examine the declarant.

B. Analyzing Hearsay Under the Oregon Confrontation Clause

Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution will often substantially curtail state efforts to introduce hearsay evidence in a criminal trial. Under the Ohio v. Roberts test adopted by the Oregon Supreme Court in Campbell, supra, and reaffirmed in State v. Moore, 334 Or 328 (2002), hearsay statements are admissible in Oregon trial courts, absent a testifying witness, only when the state makes two showings: (1) unavailability and (2) “indicia of reliability.” Reliability can be presumed if the statements are being introduced under a “firmly rooted hearsay exception.” If not, reliability can be established upon a showing that the statement bears “particularized guarantees of trustworthiness.” The proponent of the evidence has the burden to prove each of these elements by a preponderance of the evidence. State v. Nielson, 316 Or 611 (1993).

1. What constitutes unavailability under the Oregon Constitution for admission
of hearsay evidence?

Article I, section 11, and the Sixth Amendment provide a more stringent showing of unavailability by the prosecution than is required by the Evidence Code. See, e.g., State v. George, 146 Or App 449, 453 n 2 (1997) (“‘Unavailability’ under OEC 804 is not necessarily the same as under the confrontation clauses of Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution, or the Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution”). The state must avail itself of all (or almost all) available methods to produce the witness in court.

In State v. Harris, 279 Or App 446 (2016)1, the Court of Appeals reversed a trial court’s decision to allow in a 9-1-1 call from a minor victim after the minor failed to show on the morning of trial. In concluding that the state’s efforts had been constitutionally sufficient, the trial court relied upon the personal service of the minor, their efforts in the previous days to call her, and the prosecutor’s conversation with the minor’s guardian on the day of trial where they were told that “She is…refusing to appear.” Id. at 449. In opposition, the defense argued that the state still had methods at its disposal and its refusal to use them should be determinative:

When a witness declines to appear despite having been validly subpoenaed, the state has many options to compel that witness to testify, ranging from persuasion to invoking the trial court’s contempt power. If the state declines to pursue any of those options, and the record does not reflect that further efforts would have been futile, then the state has failed to show a good-faith effort.

Id. at 454. The Court of Appeals agreed:

Although frequently phrased in terms of whether the state made a good-faith effort to secure the witnesses’ attendance, the test for unavailability is, ultimately, one of necessity. As the court pointed out in State v. Moore, the unavailability requirement ‘flows logically’ from those cases in which courts allowed the use of out-of-court statements in ‘circumstances of genuine necessity’ – that is, cases in which confrontation, in the classic sense, was impossible.

Id. The Court of Appeals held that properly serving a witness alone is insufficient to meet constitutional unavailability for purposes of Article I, section 11. Id. at 455. In reaching this conclusion, the court highlighted that the case record did not show the witness was beyond the court’s jurisdiction or could not have been located through good-faith efforts. And while the court was not willing to create a per se rule requiring the state to seek contempt to establish unavailability, the court stressed that the state failed in that case to pursue that option, along with the option to request a set-over. See Id. at 456, n 1.

Practice Tip

At a minimum, under Barber v. Page, 390 US 719 (1968), the state must avail itself of applicable statutes in order to secure witnesses. Those statutes include the material witness statute, ORS 136.608 et seq. Similarly, the state must, in the right circumstances, implicate the Uniform Act to Secure the Attendance of Witnesses from Without a State in Criminal proceedings. Page, 390 U.S. at 723-4 n 4. Under Oregon law, State v. Harris, supra, gets quite close to requiring the state to undertake all reasonable means, including sending law enforcement to the declarant’s residence on the day of trial and potentially requesting a material witness warrant.

In State v. Harris, 362 Or 55, 57 (2017), the Oregon Supreme Court reversed the Court of Appeals decision on prejudice grounds. At the trial court, the court had suggested that the trial be set over for one day to give the state additional time to “make whatever further efforts it wishes to make” to obtain the presence of their critical witness. The prosecutor agreed to that plan but the defendant objected. At the Supreme Court, the state first argued that the state had made sufficient efforts to establish constitutional unavailability. The Supreme Court disagreed:

[I]n light of the foregoing case law, the state’s contention that Article I, section 11, requires only that it have subpoenaed a witness is untenable. The constitutional confrontation guarantee requires the state to do more than select one from any number of reasonable means of securing the presence of a witness and call it a day. Reliance on hearsay in lieu of live testimony must be a matter of “necessity.” The state must have exhausted all reasonably available means of producing the witness.

Harris, 362 Or at 62. However, the Oregon Supreme Court then held that a determination regarding whether the state made sufficient efforts was not irrelevant in Harris because the defendant had invited the error by objecting to the set over. “In this case, defendant objected to the state being granted the time to pursue other means of producing the victim as a witness. Under those circumstances, defendant is in no position to complain that the trial court erred in concluding that the victim was unavailable for confrontation purposes and in admitting the 9-1-1 recording of her report.” Id. at 67

Practice Tip

Defense counsel should do your best to not expressly object to the set over. Instead, focus your language and arguments on the reasons any further delay will harm your client such as age of the case, custody status, availability of defense witnesses, and so on. Also, you can focus your arguments on the state’s request to do their jobs now when they could have taken these steps earlier. Finally, if there is going to be a continuance, try and limit it to as short a period of time as the court will allow.

2. What constitutes “reliability” under the Oregon Constitution?

Under Roberts, reliability can be inferred when the evidence falls within a firmly rooted hearsay exception. Without that, a court must exclude the evidence absent a showing of “particularized guarantees of trustworthiness.” The Roberts Court created a shortcut method of establishing reliability in certain situations so as to “respond[] to the need for certainty in the workaday world of conducting criminal trials.” 448 US at 65-66. Applying the “indicia of reliability” requirement, the Oregon courts have already held the following qualify:

  • Complaints of sexual misconduct under OEC 803(18a)(a) – State v. Campbell, 299 Or 633 (1985).
  • Excited utterances under OEC 803(2) – State v. Camarena, 334 Or 28, 38-41 (2008).
  • Statements for purposes of medical diagnosis or treatment under OEC 803(4) – State v. Shelton, 218 Or App 652, 657 (2008).
  • Statements against a party causing unavailability of the declarant under OEC 804(3)(g) – State v. Supanchick, 245 Or App 651, 657-58 (2011).
  • Statements of a co-conspirator under OEC 801(4)(b) – State v. Cornell, 109 Or App 396 (1992).

The following hearsay rules are not firmly rooted:

  • Statements relating to domestic violence under OEC 803(26) – State v. Wilcox, 180 Or App 557, 563-65 (2002).
  • Statements falling under the residual hearsay exception under OEC 803(28)(a) – Idaho v. Wright, 437 US 805 (1990).
  • Statements against penal interest under OEC 804 (3) – State v. Nielsen, 316 Or 611, 623 (1993).

Additionally, one kind of statement falls within its own category, allowing the presumption of reliability despite its status as not firmly rooted because internal procedures related to the statement are sufficient to per se guarantee reliability:

  • Statements concerning an act of abuse under OEC 803(18a)(b) – State v. Renly, 111 Or App 453, 460-62 (1992).

3. What kinds of statements have “particularized guarantees of trustworthiness?”

In situations when a statement’s admissibility is not presumed, a trial court must determine whether a statement has “sufficient indicia of reliability.” Reliability can be established upon a showing that the statement bears “particularized guarantees of trustworthiness” based upon a totality of the circumstances evaluation. Roberts, 448 US at 65–66; State v. Nielsen, 316 Or at 622-23.

In State v. Farber, 295 Or 199 (1983), the Oregon Supreme Court laid out four reliability factors that may be considered when making this totality of the circumstances evaluation: (1) whether the declaration contained assertions of past fact, (2) whether the declarant had personal knowledge of the identity and role of the participants in the crime, (3) whether it was possible the declarant was relying upon faulty recollection, and (4) whether the circumstances under which the statements were made provided reason to believe the declarant had misrepresented the defendant’s involvement in the crime. These factors are not exhaustive, nor are all four required to admit declarations. Id. at 209-10.

It is important to note that when making a reliability determination, a trial court cannot use other evidence presented at trial to corroborate the truth of the contested statement. Instead, the totality of circumstances surrounding the statement itself is determinative. The circumstances are those “surround[ing] the making of the statement and [those] that render the declarant particularly worthy of belief.” Nielsen, 316 Or at 622-23. The United States Supreme Court discussed the reason a trial court cannot consider external corroborative evidence in Idaho v. Wright:

To be admissible under the Confrontation Clause, hearsay evidence used to convict a defendant must possess indicia of reliability by virtue of its inherent trustworthiness, not by reference to other evidence at trial. . . .

“[T]he use of corroborating evidence to support a hearsay statement’s ‘particularized guarantees of trustworthiness’ would permit admission of a presumptively unreliable statement by bootstrapping on the trustworthiness of other evidence at trial, a result we think at odds with the requirement that hearsay evidence admitted under the Confrontation Clause be so trustworthy that cross-examination of the declarant would be of marginal utility.

497 US at 823.

C. Limitations on the Confrontation Protections

Under both Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution and the Sixth Amendment, the Confrontation protections apply only when a declarant does not testify. This is because “[t]he confrontation concerns are assuaged when a defendant has the opportunity to test the declarant under the “crucible of cross-examination.” California v. Green, 399 US 149, 158 (1970).

A particularly frustrating situation occurs when a declarant does testify but a defendant cannot adequately inquire about the declarant’s memory of the contested events due to physical or mental limitations. Unfortunately, the right of confrontation compels physical presence on the stand but not necessarily a meaningful opportunity to delve into particular matters with that individual. In United States v. Owens, 484 US 554 (1988), the Supreme Court held that the Sixth Amendment afforded no protection when a declarant who had previously identified the accused could no longer do so by the time of trial. Admitting the prior identification as substantive evidence, the Court wrote: “It is sufficient that the defendant has the opportunity to bring out such matters as the witness’s bias, his lack of care and attentiveness, his poor eyesight, and even, (what is often a prime objective of cross-examination) the very fact that he has a bad memory.” Owens, 484 US at 559. See also Delaware v. Fensterer, 474 US 15, 19 (1985); State v. Bailey, 270 Or App 146, 149 (where five-year-old victim testified at sex abuse trial that she could not remember most of the events in question, she was not “unavailable”).

A witness’s refusal to answer questions in a helpful or relevant manner does not create constitutional unavailability. See, e.g., State v. Sullivan, 217 Or App 208, 212-13 (2007);
State v. Quintero, 110 Or App 247 (1991) (wife resorted to answer all cross questions with
“I don’t know”).

Additionally, the Confrontation protections only apply if the statement is hearsay, i.e., offered for the truth of the matter asserted. In Crawford, 541 US at 59 n 9, Justice Scalia noted that the Confrontation Clause does not bar testimonial statements when they are not hearsay, because they are not being introduced for the truth of the matter asserted: “The Clause also does not bar the use of testimonial statements for purposes other than establishing the truth of the matter asserted.” Post Crawford, Oregon adopted the same analysis in State v. Nelson, 224 Or App 193, 195-199 (2008): “Admission of that statement was proper under OEC 806 as impeachment of a hearsay declarant and, because the statement was not offered for its truth, defendant’s Sixth Amendment right to confrontation was not violated.”

III. Common Hearsay Issues in Sex Crimes Cases

A. Complaint of Sexual Misconduct – OEC 803(18a)(a)

OEC 803(18a)(a), working in tandem with OEC 803(18a)(b), allows in the fact of a complaint but not any particular details such as location or perpetrator. The rule is intended to give the state a method to rebut the argument that the act was consensual or did not occur and to address situations when a report to law enforcement was delayed. By itself the rule is of minimal utility to the state because incident details may not be introduced under the rule.

A party may present evidence under this rule that a complaint was made, but the party cannot present the details of the complaint, including the identity of the alleged perpetrator or other facts surrounding the alleged offense. Legislative Commentary to Rule 803(18a)(a). The purpose of the exception is to allow corroboration of the victim’s assertion that a sexual act was nonconsensual. State v. Hackett, 49 Or App 857, 861 (1980).

The history of case law in regards to this rule is muddled on whether it includes certain particulars to establish the illicit nature of the conduct and lack of consent. However, when the legislature revamped OEC 803 to include subsection (b), it added the very specific admonishment that subsection (a) shall only be used to establish “the fact the complaint was made.” This legislative clarification implicitly overruled the history of confusing case law on the topic. See, e.g., State v. Waites, 7 Or App 137, 140-41 (1971); State v. Wilson, 20 Or App 553, 557 (1975); State v. Emery, 4 Or App 527, 529 n 1 (1971); State v. Arnold, 133 Or App 647, 651 (1995).

OEC 803(18a)(a) does not preclude use of other hearsay exceptions to introduce details of the alleged crime. The nature of the rule’s purpose creates an overlap between it and OEC 801(4)(a)(B) (admission of prior consistent statements – see State v. Newberry, 39 Or App 119, 121 (1979)) and OEC 803 (2) (excited utterances). However, the state may not introduce evidence under the residual hearsay exception that is otherwise barred by OEC 803(18a)(a).

Confrontation Clause Analysis: OEC 803(18a)(a) is a firmly rooted hearsay exception. See State v. Stevens, 311 Or 119, 142 (1991). But unavailability may prevent the state from introducing evidence under the rule based on federal and state constitutional rights to confront the witness. To the extent the allegation of sexual misconduct is testimonial, it is subject to exclusion unless the declarant testifies at the trial or the defendant had a prior opportunity to cross-examine the declarant about the statement. Generally the state’s attempt to introduce evidence under OEC 803(18a)(a) absent the declarant testifying will be undone by the defendant’s constitutional confrontation rights.

B. Child Hearsay about Sexual Abuse – OEC 803(18a)(b)

When a declarant testifies at trial and is subject to cross-examination, the declarant’s prior statements to other witnesses are generally admissible when they pertain to the current allegations of illicit sexual conduct so long as the proponent of the evidence provided proper notice. This hearsay rule allows the state to call a parade of adult witnesses retelling the child’s story, as well as describing the child’s demeanor when speaking of the abuse. This lends credibility to the child’s testimony through repetition. Furthermore, the testimony of articulate adults may greatly augment the child’s trial testimony, which is not uncommonly incomplete and somewhat inconsistent with earlier statements. See State v. Bailey, supra (no need to assess reliability of statements where child testified even though unable to recall most of the events at issue).

By comparison, when a declarant does not testify at trial, the statements are only admissible under OEC 803(18a)(b) provided all of the following:

  • The victim was chronologically or mentally under 12 years old when they originally

made the statement.

  • The proponent of the evidence provided proper notice.
  • The proponent establishes that the time, content, and circumstances of the statement provide indicia of reliability.
  • The proponent presents corroborative evidence of the act of abuse and of the alleged perpetrator’s opportunity to participate in the conduct.

Practice Tip

The state is rarely able to introduce hearsay statements under this rule absent a declarant testifying. For such an effort to be successful, it must establish all of the following by a preponderance of the evidence for each statement:

  1. The declarant was chronologically or biologically under the age of 12 when the statement was made (requirement of the rule).

  2. There is hearsay corroborative evidence that the declarant suffered an act of abuse (rule requirement).

  3. There is hearsay corroborative evidence that the defendant had opportunity to impose the abuse (rule requirement).

  4. The statements have “reliability” as laid out in the 11-factor OEC 803(18a)(b) test (rule requirement).

  5. The declarant is unavailable (constitutional requirement).

  6. The statements were nontestimonial as defined in Crawford v. Washington (constitutional requirement).

Hearsay statements must meet certain statutorily imposed requirements of OEC 803(18a)(b) regardless of whether the declarant testifies. In both situations, certain notice and particularity requirements must be met.

The proponent of hearsay statements must provide notice to the opposing party no later than 15 days before trial unless good cause is shown justifying the failure. Providing discovery or a witness list in lieu of the notice is insufficient. See State v. McKinzie, 186 Or App 384, 392 (2003) (providing a videotape of hearsay statements was insufficient notice). But new notice is not necessary upon retrial unless the proponent of the evidence seeks to introduce different statements. State v. Kelly, 276 Or App 507 (2016) (notice not required on retrial if properly provided prior to original trial).

The legislature did not provide a specific statutory definition for the term “particular statements.” While the requirement is clearly an anti-ambush provision, case law suggests that goal can be met so long as the defense is given sufficient guidance on where it can find the statements. In State v. Chase, 240 Or App 541 (2011), the Court of Appeals required the proponent of the evidence to lay out the substance of the statement and identify the witness through whom the statement would be introduced. However, subsequent cases have held the state met the particularity requirement by merely identifying the locations of the statements rather than detailing them. State v Riley, 258 Or App 246 (2013) (affidavit directing defense to look to statements on specific pages of discovery sufficient); State v Ashkins, 263 Or App 208 (2014) (despite failing to identify specific pages, notice was sufficient when it referenced the specific date, location, and parties present for the conversation). Still, the court maintains that the state fails to meet particularity requirements when it cites only to the entirety of the discovery. State v. Edblom, 257 Or App 22 (2013).

Admissibility when the declarant is unavailable: The Oregon Evidence Code defines unavailability in OEC 804. The drafters of OEC 803(18a)(b) expanded that definition within the context of this rule, allowing a trial court to find legal unavailability if the declarant has a substantial lack of memory of the subject matter of the statement, is presently incompetent to testify, is unable to communicate about the abuse or sexual conduct based on fear or similar reason, or is substantially likely, as established by expert testimony, to suffer lasting severe emotional trauma from testifying. OEC 803(18a)(b). When the declarant is not testifying for any of these reasons, the court must examine the declarant outside the presence of the jury and on the record to determine whether the declarant is “unavailable.” The court shall conduct the examination immediately prior to the commencement of the trial. The defendant has an Article I, section 11, right to be present and to examine the declarant at the preliminary hearing. State v. Kitzman, 323 Or 589 (1996).

Note that some of the OEC 803(18a)(b) grounds for unavailability would not render the witness “unavailable” under the confrontation clauses; e.g., if the child presents with a substantial lack of memory or is unable to respond to some questions due to emotional trauma. If the declarant is constitutionally “available” and testifies, a defendant cannot use this section to argue exclusion. State v. Bailey, 270 Or App 146 (2015) (defense unsuccessfully argued rule’s reliability analysis required victim’s testimony excluded where complainant was unable to remember nearly anything about the allegation).

If the state proves that the declarant is “unavailable,” the court next determines the reliability of the evidence. OEC 803(18a)(b) lists 11 nonexclusive factors for a court to consider when making a reliability determination. A trial court must also make detailed findings on the record regarding the hearsay corroborative evidence justifying the hearsay’s admission. Renly, 111 Or App at 468. Any exclusion of evidence as a result of this hearing is immediately appealable by the state under [https://www.oregonlaws.org/?search=ORS+138.060. ORS 138.060].

The court must also determine the statements are corroborated. Without the required corroborative evidence, a trial court cannot admit hearsay statements when the declarant is unavailable. The corroborative evidence itself cannot be hearsay. Renly, 111 Or App at 466; see also State v. Mayer, 146 Or App 86 (1997). That safeguard is meant to ensure a defendant is not convicted based upon hearsay alone, even when the hearsay is admissible under some other exception. The corroborative evidence must establish both that sexual abuse occurred and the defendant had the opportunity to perpetrate it. But the evidence can be “general in nature” and need not pertain to specific allegations, and it need not be admissible at trial. State v. Reed, 173 Or App 185, 197 (2001) (corroboration in the form of an eye witness who described uncharged conduct that was at least arguably inappropriate but on a different date held sufficient).

OEC 803(18a)(b) does not preclude the state from attempting to introduce hearsay statements under another hearsay exception. In State v. Leahy, 190 Or App 147 (2003), the Oregon Court of Appeals, in dicta, indicated that the state’s failure to admit hearsay under OEC 803(18a)(b) was not necessarily determinative because the statements could also come in under other provisions. Leahy, 190 Or at 151 (hearsay statements potentially admissible under the medical hearsay exception).

Confrontation Clause Analysis: Regarding reliability, while OEC 803(18a)(b) has been held to not constitute a firmly rooted hearsay exception, its procedures are considered sufficient to protect a defendant’s right to confrontation under the state constitution, thereby creating a de facto exception. Renly, supra at 460. However, the Sixth Amendment and Article I, section 11, provide significant additional safeguards and often call into question the constitutionality of OEC 803(18a)(b) when the declarant is “unavailable.” See, e.g., State v. George, 146 Or App 449, 453 n 2 (1997). See discussion of constitutional “unavailability” earlier in this chapter.

In addition, most statements gathered within the context of OEC 803(18a)(b) are “testimonial.” As a result, the portions of OEC 803(18a)(b) that attempt to allow hearsay statements absent the declarant testifying will usually be undone by the defendant’s confrontation rights. See [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=1689712124358304376&q=State+ex+rel.+v.+S.P.,+346+Or+592+(2009)+&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 State ex rel. v. S.P.], 346 Or 592 (2009) (CARES transcripts excluded where child does not testify).

C. Statements for Purpose of Medical Diagnosis – OEC 803(4)

In State v. Moen, 309 Or 45, 54-65 (1990), the Oregon Supreme Court held there are three requirements for admissibility under OEC 803(4): (1) the statement must be made for the purpose of medical diagnosis or treatment, (2) it must describe or relate medical history, past or present symptoms, pain or sensations, or the inception or character of the cause or source of the pain, and (3) it must be “reasonably pertinent” to diagnosis or treatment. Whether a statement meets the requirements of OEC 803(4) is a preliminary question of fact for the trial court based upon the preponderance of the evidence standard. OEC 104(1); State v. Booth, 124 Or App 282, 286 (1993).

The statements need not be made to a physician. “Statements to hospital attendants, ambulance drivers, or even members of the family or friends may be within the scope of the exception.” Legislative Commentary to Rule 803(4); State ex rel. Juv. Dept. v. Cornett, 121 Or App 264, 270 (1993). The rule also qualifies for statements made to mental health professionals. In re C., 121 Or App 264 (1993).

The declarant does not have to be the victim. OEC 803(4), by its terms, does not require the declarant be the person diagnosed or treated. Rather, the rule refers broadly to “statements” describing their purpose and nature, without any reference to the declarant’s status. See State v. Bauman, 98 Or App 316, 319- 320 (1989) (statements by mother of the victim admissible under this rule).

Admissibility under this rule comes down to whether the statement was gathered for purposes of treatment or for the primary purpose of investigating and prosecuting a crime. Statements to a physician consulted only for the purpose of enabling the physician to testify do not qualify. Henderson v. Union P.R. Co., 189 Or 145 (1950). However, a child abuse evaluative agency’s interview of a victim qualifies under the rule because at least one of the agency’s purposes is to accurately diagnose the child for purpose of therapy. State v. Logan, 105 Or App 556, 561-62 (1991). See also State v. Barkley, 315 Or 420, 426 (1993) (trial court reasonably concluded that the child (a) knew that the purpose of the interview was to obtain a medical diagnosis or treatment and (b) that she had to be truthful to advance that medical diagnosis or treatment and thus statements were “made for purposes of medical diagnosis or treatment”). Likewise, a child’s demonstration on a videotape of the nature of the sexual abuse by using anatomically correct dolls is hearsay, but it is admissible under this rule if it is for the purpose of medical diagnosis or treatment. Barkley, at 423.

Practice Tip

OEC 803(4) is predicated on the theory that hearsay statements to a medical professional are sufficiently reliable because a patient’s desire for proper treatment or diagnosis would outweigh any motive to falsify. Case law supports that a defendant may argue the rule’s inapplicability when the evidence shows that a purported victim was too immature to understand the statements were being made for purposes of treatment or diagnosis. See State v. Booth, 124 Or App 282 (1994) (finding sufficient evidence that the three-year-old child was aware they were talking to a doctor); State v. Mayer, 146 Or App 86 (1997) (finding sufficient evidence in the record the child was aware that purpose of examination and interview was for medical diagnosis and treatment); State v. Alvarez, 110 Or App 230 (1991) (court concluding it was reasonable to infer from all the circumstances that the victim understood she was making statements for purpose of medical examination); State v. Logan, 105 Or App (1991) (“We cannot say as a matter of law that a four-year-old child is too young to understand the nature of a medical examination. The decision must be based on the facts in each case”).

The relevant statement must describe or relate medical history, past or present symptoms, pain or sensations, or the inception or character of the cause or source of the pain. Often
the critical question within the context of OEC 803(4) is one of causation. Statements as to fault do not themselves generally qualify under the rule. Likewise, statements of the identity of the abuser are usually not admissible. However, the household member exception to this rule instructs that identity is admissible when the abuser is a member of the victim’s household. The exception is justified because “statements of identity to a physician by a child sexually abused by a family member are of a type physicians reasonably rely on in composing a diagnosis and course of treatment.” State v. Vosika, 83 Or App 298 (1987); see also Barkley, 315 Or at 426-27; State v. Jensen, 313 Or 587, 590-98 (1992).

Confrontation Clause Analysis: A defendant’s confrontation rights may provide the criminal defendant with significant additional safeguards but only when a declarant does not testify. Oregon case law designates OEC 803(4) as a firmly rooted hearsay exception. State v. Moen, 309 Or 45, 62-63 (1990). See also Mayer, 146 Or App at 575 (court refused to undertake a reliability analysis even though there were internal inconsistencies in the declarant’s statements because 803(4) is a firmly rooted exception). However, if a declarant is not testifying at trial, the constitutional definition of “unavailability” will still prevail.

In State ex rel Juv. Dept. of Multnomah County v. S.P., 346 Or 592 (2009), the Oregon
Supreme Court held that statements made to child abuse professionals are testimonial. While acknowledging that the CARES interview process “serves multiple concurrent purposes,” the court also admitted that involvement of law enforcement in the CARES process is “pervasive” and thus statements to it must be construed as testimonial. Id. at 616. In so concluding, the court rejected the argument that the testimonial label can apply only to police officers or other governmental officials, and it rejected the argument that forensic evaluations are conducted first for purposes of diagnosis and treatment. Id. at 619, 623. See also State v. Pitt, 209 Or App 270 (2006) (statements made to a Lane County Child Advocacy Center interviewer are testimonial).

Unlike statements made to CARES or other child advocacy personnel or doctors, statements made within the hospital setting to nurses or doctors often will not be deemed testimonial. [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=8142038058609832072&q=State+ex+rel.+Juv.+Dept.+v.+K.S.,+229+Or+App+50+(2009&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 State ex rel. Juv. Dept. v. K.S.], 229 Or App 50 (2009). See also State v. Bella, 231 Or App 420, 429 (2009) (rejecting defense argument that a physician’s mandatory reporter status created a per se testimonial situation).

D. Excited Utterance – OEC 803(2)

In order for a hearsay statement to qualify as an excited utterance, it must meet three requirements:

(1) There must be a “startling event or condition.”

(2) The statement must be made while the declarant was under the stress or excitement of that condition, using a subjective standard.

(3) The statement must be related to the event or condition.

State v. Carlson, 311 Or 201 (1991). Case law has further broken down the second element into a factor test in which a trial court should consider: (a) the lapse of time between the event and the statement, (b) the content of statement, and (c) the mental and physical condition of the declarant at the time of the statement. State v. Wolfs, 119 Or App 262 (1993).

For purposes of the exception, the relevant question is not whether “an event or condition is [] inherently startling”; rather, the crucial question is whether the event has a startling effect on the declarant. Carlson, 311 Or at 216 (1991) (wife calling husband a liar during police questioning held to qualify); State v. Cunningham, 337 Or 528, 540 (2004) (phone calls made by victim to mother prior to her death detailing her plans to meet with the defendant qualified).

Practice Tip

Cunningham and Carlson demonstrate the circular logic of the rule—essentially, an event is startling if the declarant is startled. As a barrier to admission of evidence, this element is limited. However, the opponent of the evidence should force the proponent to identify the startling event in order to later limit the scope of statements that relate to it.

The majority of cases analyzing OEC 803(2) focus on the requirement the declarant be under the stress or excitement of the event when the statement was made because it relates directly to the rationale for the exception. To determine this question, the trial court shall consider: (1) time—meaning, before there has been time to contrive and misrepresent, (2) content of the statement, and (3) the mental and physical condition of the declarant at the time of the statement. Cunningham, 337 Or at 543.

Lapse of time: The element of time is the most important of the three factors, but it is also context specific. State v. Underwood, 266 Or App 274, 278 (2014) (statements made five days later was not too long). The factor is less a matter of the actual time passed and more a matter of whether the time passed permitted reflective thought between the event and the statement. Id. Admissibility “is not determined by a stop-watch which counts the number of minutes that elapse from the moment of the exciting event to the utterance of the declaration, but, by a determination of whether or not the sway of the exciting event still dominated the declarant when he spoke.” State v. Hutchison, 222 Or 533, 541–42 (1960). This is “particularly true” when there is evidence the declarant “did in fact engage in a reflective thought process during [the] time interval.” Zeller v. Dahl, 262 Or 515, 519 (1972).

For example, a phone call made approximately three hours after a strangulation to the point of unconsciousness was within the range, State v. Blaylock, 267 Or App 455 (2014), as were statements by a rape victim approximately one hour later, State v. Jones, 20 Or App 533 (1975). However, in State v. Mahoney, 33 Or App 73, 77 (1978), the court held that one hour following an attempted sexual assault and physical assault was too long when the statements were not made at the first opportunity but after about an hour of sobering up and only gradually implicating the defendant. Unlike in Underwood, in Waites, Mahoney, and Emery, there was no showing the declarants were under any ongoing stress or fear.

Practice Tip

The “time” factor is the most important factor in determining whether the statement was made under the stress of the startling event. However, it is the most unpredictable in the case law, because the courts seem particularly concerned not just with the amount of time but what happened during that time. In practice, this means that during a Rule 104 hearing, the sequence of events between the event and the declaration, particularly any statements made by the declarant, are likely to be the most persuasive.

Content of utterance: This factor looks to the content of the statement itself in order to determine whether the statement was made after reflection, i.e., whether at the time of the statement, the stress “propelled” “the event itself to speak through the tongue of the declarant.” State v. Hutchison, 222 Or 533, 537 (1960). See State v. Mace, 67 Or App 753, 757 (1984) (whether statement made in response to questions); Zeller, 262 Or at 520 (analyzing any statements indicating deliberative thought and whether the statement was self-serving); Mahoney, 33 Or App at 76 (whether the declarant actually changed his response to a given question); Cunningham, 337 Or at 544 (adamancy about a particular course of action); State v. Hollywood, 67 Or App 546, 549 (1984) (specificity about when the startling event occurred).

Mental and physical condition of declarant: The mental and physical condition of the declarant typically weigh in favor of admissibility because rarely is the “excited” declarant calm, rational, and disinterested. Case law consistently leans on this element to make a determination about whether a declarant was under the stress of the event. Examples include: declarant appearing visibly upset and crying, Mace, at 759; declarant suffering from the physical effects of the event, Blaylock, at 468; yelling, Carlson, at 216; and even sternness, Cunningham, at 544.

The final requirement is that the statement must be related to the event or condition. It is important to narrow the specific event or condition triggering the statement as much as possible. In cases when the startling event is one of a series of potentially startling events—e.g., an argument, followed by an assault, followed by the assailant’s return to the declarant’s home—the courts will tend to allow statements about prior events as “related.” The “related to” requirement is not a barrier to admission of these statements so long as “the subject matter of the statement is such as would likely be evoked by the event.” State v. Stonaker, 149 Or App 728, 743 (1997) (allowing in multiple conversations over a period of seventeen minutes because second related to first). See also State v. Yong, 206 Or App 522 (2006) (allowing in conversation from five days earlier because “related to” recent conversation).

Confrontation Clause Analysis: The excited utterance exception is the penultimate example a nontestimonial statement under Crawford. Davis v. Washington, the seminal Sixth Amendment case defining testimonial statements, was itself an excited utterance case. See also State v. Camarena, supra, 344 Or at 30 (holding initial conversation between 9-1-1 operator and victim nontestimonial. However, once the initial details of the assault had been gathered, all subsequent statements to the 9-1-1 operator were testimonial). Further, OEC 803(2) is a firmly rooted hearsay exception under Article I, section 11. See State v. Camarena, 208 Or App 575, 581 (2006).

E. Existing Mental, Emotional, or Physical Condition – OEC 803(3)

OEC 803(3) allows in statements by a declarant in the following three situations:

(1) Statements of an existing physical condition to prove the declarant’s physical condition at the time the statements were made.

(2) Statements of existing mental or emotional condition to prove the mental or emotional condition of the declarant at the time the statements were made.

(3) Statements of mental or emotional condition or state of mind, including intent, plan, motive, or design, to prove subsequent conduct in accordance with that state of mind or mental or emotional condition. Importantly, this subsection only applies to the future plans of the declarant.

The plain language of the rule allows for the declarant’s statements about how the declarant was feeling at the time of the event but does not allow for hearsay to prove events from the declarant’s past. So what happens when the declarant’s feelings are based upon events in the past; for example, when someone is currently experiencing fear based, at least partially, on prior experiences?

OEC 803(3) expressly disallows the use of this rule to backdoor in prior bad acts evidence: “A statement of a declarant’s then existing state of mind … but not including a statement of memory or belief to prove the fact remembered or believed… .” See also, Commentary to Rule 803(3)), reprinted in Laird C. Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 803.03(2) (6th ed 2013) (“The subsection excludes statements of memory or belief, to prove the fact remembered or believed. This exclusion is necessary to avoid the virtual destruction of the hearsay rule”).

However, recent case law has interpreted OEC 803(3) to mean that a declarant’s then-existing feelings of fear are admissible, even when probative of prior bad acts of the defendant. For example, in State v. Blaylock, 267 Or App 455, 460-62 (2015), the Oregon Court of Appeals held statements admissible under OEC 803(3) when the victim told a coworker the night before her disappearance, “I hope he is not drunk when I get home.” The state argued that statement was admissible under OEC 803(3) because it showed the “victim’s state of mind on that date when she left work” and was relevant to the defendant’s “claim of self-defense,” as well as the defendant’s theory “that [the victim] was the one with the alcohol problem not [the d]efendant.” The defense argued that the statements were really intended to prove the defendant’s violence prior to the declaration and that he was a drunk (also prior conduct). The trial court admitted the statements and the Court of Appeals affirmed. “[E]ven if a statement merely reflects the declarant’s state of mind or reasonably supports an inference as to the declarant’s state of mind, it constitutes an assertion of the declarant’s state of mind for purposes of OEC 803(3).” A statement of then-existing fear meets the test of OEC 803(3). Blaylock, 267 Or App at 461-62. See also State v. Garcia, 206 Or App 745, 750 (2006) (holding that statements detailing fear were admissible because the victim’s fear was a directly relevant element to the crime of coercion).

The holding in Blaylock is problematic because it did not address the portion of OEC 803(3) that expressly disavows the use of this rule to backdoor in prior bad acts evidence. As the defense in Blaylock correctly argued but the Court of Appeals did not directly address, when the state moves to introduce evidence about a declarant’s then-existing state of mind, it is usually doing so not because the fear itself is relevant to an element, but rather because the fear demonstrates something that happened in the declarant’s past. As Laird Kirkpatrick wrote:

The problem with admitting a victim’s statement that the victim is fearful of the defendant or a third person is that the victim’s state of mind is not a relevant issue in a murder prosecution. The statement generally has relevance only to show that the person feared was the assailant or that the person did something to make the victim fearful. Thus, the purpose for which the statement has relevance is not the usage allowed by Rule 803(3).

Laird C. Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 803.3(3) (6th ed. 2013).

This was also the position the Oregon Supreme Court took in State v. Mendez, 308 Or 9 (1989), when it refused to allow the defendant to introduce a statement by the victim thathe feared a third party, Moen, would kill him. The trial court disallowed the evidence be-cause it was intended to show a past event—namely that Moen had done something to cause the victim to fear him—and the rule did not allow for those kinds of “declarations in mem-ory pointed backwards to the past.” Id. at 17, n 12. The Oregon Supreme Court agreed.

Practice Tip

Focus on why the declarant’s state of mind is itself relevant to the elements of the charged crime. Usually it is not, and the state is really introducing those statements in an effort to establish prior events in the declarant’s life. The rule expressly disallows use of the rule in
this manner.

Statements of a declarant’s future intent are only admissible to prove the declarant’s future conduct, not the future conduct of another person. See Legislative Commentary to OEC 803(3) (the rule “render[s] statements of intent by a declarant admissible only to prove the declarant’s future conduct, not the future conduct of another person”). This includes exclusion of statements by the declarant that the declarant and the defendant intended to do a certain act together. See State v. Engweiler, 118 Or App 132, 36 (1993) (a declarant cannot know of others future intentions with sufficient accuracy). However, statements of future intent from a victim may be deemed admissible to rebut a claimed defense of consent or self-defense. See Engweiler, 118 Or App at 135-36 (victim’s statements of dislike admissible to rebut defense of consent in rape prosecution).

Practice Tip

If a statement comes in under this rule, consider asking for a limiting instruction under Rule 105. Such an instruction may be necessary (even if ineffective) to prevent the jury from considering the statement for the truth of the factual ascertain contained within it. See State v. Johnson, 340 Or 319, 345-46 (2006) (court finds error in defense’s failure to ask for limiting instruction when statement only introduced to prove venue under OEC 803(3)).

Confrontation Clause Analysis: Statements introduced under OEC 803(3) will rarely constitute testimonial assertions in violation of Crawford. The rationale articulated by the Oregon Supreme Court in State v. Johnson, 340 Or 319 (2006), illustrates this. In Johnson, the state sought to introduce statements of the murder victim that helped establish she had traveled to the defendant’s house prior to her death. While the Johnson court did not yet have the benefit of Davis to aid in discerning the meaning of the term “testimonial,” its reasoning nonetheless was persuasive. “Although we are unsure at this juncture of the precise scope of the term ‘testimonial’ as the Supreme Court used it in Crawford, we agree with the state that the concept does not include ‘the statements made by an unsuspecting victim of a crime before that crime has even occurred.’ ” Id. at 347.

Surprisingly, Oregon courts do not appear to have addressed whether OEC 803(3) is a firmly rooted hearsay exception. As a result, defense counsel will need to be prepared to argue both that it is not and that the state failed to make a sufficient showing the statement bears “particularized guarantees of trustworthiness” based upon a totality of the circumstances evaluation. Ohio v. Roberts, 448 US at 65–66; State v. Nielsen, 316 Or at 622-23. Constitutional unavailability also still applies and is required, to the extent the challenged statements are “reliable.”

F. Domestic Violence Exception – OEC 803(26)

A statement regarding allegations of domestic violence is admissible under the rule when it was made within 24 hours of the incident, it was recorded or made to a police officer or medical professional, and the trial court made a reliability determination.

In State ex rel. Juvenile Dep’t of Washington Cty. v. C.M.C., 243 Or App 335, 337-40 (2011), the Court of Appeals held that the domestic violence hearsay exception is intended to refer to persons living in the same residence in a relationship akin to that of spouses. Id. at 337. “[T]he exception does not apply to, for example, simple roommates or dorm mates. Nor, as dispositive here, does it apply to minor children and parents who live in the same residence.” Id. at 339-340.

The reliability factors under this rule are heavily skewed towards admissibility. The defense should instead focus on the constitutionally required factors for reliability under Article I, section 11. As discussed in Kirkpatrick, the statutory “reliability” factors laid out in OEC 803(26) are of minimal practical value and are skewed towards admissibility. Laird C. Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 803.26(3) (6th ed 2013). The first listed factor—personal knowledge—is presumably satisfied by the fact the declarant is the victim. The second listed factor—corroborative evidence—is likely satisfied by the injury. Furthermore, corroborative evidence is not allowed to be used as a factor under the Article I, section 11, test of reliability, as discussed below. The third listed factor—timing of the statement—will skew towards admissibility since the rule requires the statement to have been made within the past 24 hours. The fourth listed factor – whether the statement was made by leading questions – is probably the most neutral of factors and thus itself may be useful. The fifth listed factor – subsequent statements by the declarant – would be helpful if it was neutral. However, it is followed up by the following: “recantation by a declarant is not sufficient reason for denying admission of a statement under this subsection.” With that, the legislature managed to take a neutral and helpful factor, and skew it also in the direction of admissibility.

Furthermore, the rule does not identify reliability factors already constitutionally required, such as such as the circumstances under which the statement was made, the nature of the prior relationship between the parties, whether the declarant had motive to fabricate,
whether there was a history of prior complaints, and whether those prior complaints were well-founded.

No Oregon case has directly and fully analyzed whether the state may introduce a challenged statement regarding sexual abuse under the domestic violence hearsay exception when it fails to avail itself of OEC 803(18a)(b). But the defense should prevail in excluding the statements for three persuasive reasons.

First, OEC 803(18a)(b) was codified to admit hearsay statements in sexual abuse prosecutions predicated on very specific safeguards for admissibility. Use of the residual hearsay exception or the domestic violence exception in lieu of the more tailored exception would abrogate the protections of OEC 803(18a)(b). While cases have found that OEC 803(18a)(b) hearsay evidence may also come in under firmly rooted hearsay exceptions such as excited utterance
or medical hearsay, no court has ruled such evidence may come in under the domestic violence exception. And multiple courts have expressly held that such evidence cannot come in under the catch-all residual exception, OEC 803(28)(a). See e.g. State v. Harris, 78 Or App 490, 492 (1986).

Second, in Harris and Campbell, 299 Or at 640, dicta indicates catch-all provisions cannot be used in lieu of OEC 803(18a)(b).

Third, in State v. Torres, 206 Or App 436, 440-41 (2006), having failed to abide by the pro-per procedures of OEC 803(18a)(b), the state made a series of hearsay exception arguments, including the domestic violence exception. The Court of Appeals summarily disregarded all of those arguments, including the domestic violence exception, as inappropriate vehicles for admissibility.

Confrontation Clause Analysis: Crawford v. Washington and the Sixth Amendment provide significant additional safeguards and call into question the constitutionality of OEC 803(26) when the declarant does not testify, because most statements gathered within the context of the rule are deemed “testimonial.” This is especially true when the statements, as articulated within the statute, are gathered by law enforcement. Statements shall be categorized as testimonial when they “were made under circumstances which would lead an objective witness reasonably to believe that the statement would be available for use at a later trial.” Crawford, 542 US at 52. The statements are also testimonial “when the circumstances objectively indicate that there is no such ongoing emergency, and that the primary purpose of the interrogation is to establish or prove past events potentially relevant to later criminal prosecution.” Davis, 547 US at 822.

In State v. Haggblom, 228 Or App 541, 543 (2009), the state attempted to use the rule to introduce a tape-recorded statement the victim made to police officers. While the trial court did not agree with the defendant’s Crawford arguments, the Court of Appeals did and refused to find the failure “harmless.” “As noted, the state concedes—and we agree—that admission of the victim’s recorded statement violated defendant’s confrontation right. Accordingly, the only question remaining is whether that error was harmless.” Id. at 544-45. See also State v. Mendoza-Lazaro, 225 Or App 57, 62 (2008) (“Here, the court admitted the statements at issue under OEC 803(26)(a), which allows the admission of hearsay statements of the content of domestic violence reports if there are, among other things, ‘sufficient indicia of reliability.’ Because the admission of the statements—again, the only evidence from which a reasonable factfinder could conclude that the victim’s children witnessed defendant’s conduct—was the result of the very type of inquiry of which Crawford disapproved, correcting the error would comport with the ends of justice”).

In addition, OEC 803(26) does not constitute a firmly rooted hearsay exception under Article I, section 11, bringing constitutional reliability into play. In State v. Wilcox, 180 Or App 557, 563-65 (2002), the Court of Appeals addressed the role that corroborative evidence should play in the Oregon constitutional evaluation when the statements are “testimonial.” Turning to the reliability test, the Wilcox court first addressed the role that corroborative evidence should play in the constitutional evaluation: “OEC 803(26)(b)(B) treats the existence of independent corroborative evidence as a factor supporting the reliability of the statements of a domestic violence victim. However, under the constitutional analysis, other evidence presented at trial that corroborates the truth of a hearsay statement cannot be used to show that the statement itself has particularized guarantees of trustworthiness.” Id. at 564 (citing to Nielsen, 316 Or at 628-29).

The Wilcox court did ultimately admit the statements because they were detailed and specific and they were made under surrounding circumstances—including the time of disclosure, the victim’s demeanor, and the lack of any evident motive to fabricate—that support their trustworthiness.

G. Residual Hearsay – OEC 803(28)(a) and OEC 804(3)(h)2

Rule 803(28) and Rule 804(3)(h)3 create catch-all hearsay provisions that are intended to be strictly construed only to circumstances when no other hearsay provision could apply and justice requires admission. Courts have established a six-factor test for admission: (1) the evidence is not admissible under any other exception, (2) the evidence carries equivalent circumstantial guaranties of trustworthiness, (3) the evidence is relevant, (4) the evidence is more probative of the point in question than any other evidence the proponent can introduce, (5) admission of the evidence would serve the general purposes of justice and of the evidence code, and (6) the proponent of the evidence has given reasonable advance notice of the intent to offer it.

In State v. Campbell, 299 Or 633, 638-39 (1985), the Oregon Supreme Court disallowed the trial court’s use of the rule in a sex offense prosecution. As the higher court noted, the legislature made clear within the commentary that it intended to “expressly circumscribe the authority of the judicial system to create categories of hearsay which will be admissible under the residual exception” and “the authority of trial courts is limited to admitting hearsay very rarely in exceptional cases where the particular circumstances of the declarant and the out-of-court statement are demonstrably trustworthy.” Id. at 638-39.

The rule requires that a proponent of a statement must provide proper notice “sufficiently in advance of the trial or hearing, or as soon as practicable after it becomes apparent that such statement is probative of the issues at hand.” The notice must indicate the intent to introduce the statement under the residual hearsay exception, the particulars of the statement, and the name and address of the declarant. “I forgot” is not a sufficient defense, see Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 803.28(3)(b), but providing proper notice under another hearsay provision likely will satisfy, see State v. Rodriguez-Castillo, 210 Or App 479, 495-96 (2007).

The drafters of the Evidence Code were clearly worried that providing for this exception had the potential to imbue courts with the unfettered ability to let in hearsay at their discretion. In an attempt to make clear how rarely courts should apply this rule, the legislature wrote:

Because exceptional cases like Dallas County may arise in the future, the Legislative Assembly adopted this residual hearsay exception in Rule 803 and a similar one in Rule 804(2)(d). It intends that these provisions be used very rarely, and only in situations where application of the hearsay rule and its other exceptions would result in injustice. These rules are not a broad grant of authority to trial judges to admit hearsay statements.

Legislative Commentary to Rule 803(28).

This exception should not be used as a means of avoiding difficult questions regarding whether or not a hearsay statement fits within another exception. Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 803.28(3)(e). The trial court should clearly find that no other exception will authorize the statement’s receipt before admission under this rule is allowed. Id. Further, the exception should not be used as a means of avoiding express restrictions contained in other hearsay exceptions. Id. See also State v. Cazares-Mendez, 350 Or 491, 513 (2011); State v. Campbell, 299 Or at 640; State v. Harris, 78 Or App 490 (1986); State v. Kayfes, 213 Or App 543, 552-54 (2007).

Confrontation Clause Analysis: No Oregon case has analyzed OEC 803(28a) from the perspective of Crawford. In Idaho v. Wright, 437 US 805, 817 (1990), the United States Supreme Court held that the residual hearsay exception should not be considered a “firmly rooted exception” for purposes of confrontation.

However, in State v. Swader, 72 Or App 593, 602-05 (1985) (overruled on other grounds),4 the Court of Appeals started from the proposition that the residual hearsay exception is not firmly rooted. Turning to reliability, the Court of Appeals noted that the constitutionally delineated reliability factors are substantially similar but not synonymous with the OEC 803(24) reliability factors. Rule 803(24) may provide “a convenient supplementary guidepost to confrontation clause analysis without [being] a test of general application.” Id. The guarantees of trustworthiness set a high bar and the proponent must be able to articulate why the circumstances of the statement themselves present a “motivational basis for truth-telling.” In State v. Apperson, 85 Or App 429, 432-33 (1987), the Court of Appeals refused to find trustworthiness in statements of an alleged domestic violence victim to police. Unlike statements made under belief of impending death, statements against pecuniary or proprietary interest, statements concerning family history, and statements made to police “lack the motivational basis for truth-telling that the specific exceptions in the rule have.” Apperson, 85 Or App at 433.

H. Multiple Hearsay – OEC 805

If a layer of hearsay does not fit an exception, the entirety of the statement is inadmissible. Each link in the chain must bear sufficient marks of trustworthiness, as established by the admission of each under an approved hearsay exception or itself qualifying as hearsay. Common examples include an excited utterance in a police report that constitutes a business record, a medical statement in a hospital record that falls under the business record exception.

Identifying Multiple Hearsay Exceptions

Each portion of a multiple hearsay statement must “conform with an exception” to the hearsay rule for double hearsay to be admissible. For example, in State v. Bauman, 98 Or App 316, 320-21 (1989), the Court of Appeals excluded statements of a victim made to an officer through her mother. “Both arguments apply to the issue of admissibility of the child’s statement to her mother, but do not address the issue regarding her mother’s statement to the police officer.” See also State v. Lyon, 83 Or App 592 (1987) (defendant’s statements against interest excluded because told to non-testifying father).

State ex rel Juvenile Dep’t v. Pfaff, 164 Or App 470 (1999), provides instruction on the rule’s applicability within the context of CARES. In Pfaff, a juvenile petitioner moved to suppress as double hearsay statements by the victim brother to his mother, who then repeated the alleged statements to a CARES worker. At trial, all witnesses denied that the alleged conduct had occurred and the state sought to introduce the prior statements under OEC 803(18a)(b). On appeal, the child ultimately conceded that the victim’s statements to mother were admissible under OEC 803(18a)(b), but alleged that the mother’s retelling of the statements to the CARES employee constituted a “second leg” of hearsay that lacked an exception.5 The state responded that the mother’s portion of hearsay should qualify under the medical hearsay exception, or a statement against penal interest, or under the residual hearsay exception. Ultimately the Court of Appeals concluded that the medical hearsay exception applied and admitted the double hearsay statement as substantive evidence. Pfaff, 164 Or App at 475-76.

By comparison, in State v. Beaty, 127 Or App 448 (1991), the defendant sought to introduce statements under circumstances similar to those at issue in Pfaff. The Beaty defendant was seeking to introduce statements allegedly made by the victim and his mother to a third-party witness, who thereafter relayed the statements to CSD. The court refused to allow their introduction because, unlike in Pfaff, the medical hearsay exception was not available:

The CSD worker’s testimony would be hearsay, because he would testify about what Debbie Gray told him about what the mother and the children had said. To be admissible, each level of hearsay must satisfy an exception to the hearsay rule. OEC 805. Even if the mother’s and the children’s statements were relevant and admissible under OEC 803(3), the statement by Debbie Gray to the CSD worker does not fall within any exception in OEC 803 or OEC 804 to the prohibition against hearsay testimony. See OEC 802. The trial court did not err in excluding it.

127 Or App at 454.

This rule controls the admission of translated statements. If the translator is not at trial, the statements are likely inadmissible. In State v. Rodriguez-Castillo, 345 Or 39 (2008), the Oregon Supreme Court held that an interpreter’s translation of a victim’s, witness’s, or accused’s statements constituted multiple hearsay. Each level of multiple hearsay must meet a valid hearsay exception, and the translators work may meet the residual exception. Id. In State v. Ennis, 212 Or App 240, 254-55 (2007), the Court of Appeals held that each level of hearsay in a multiple hearsay situation must satisfy confrontation. Considering Ennis and Rodriguez-Castillo in conjunction, the work of a translator constitutes hearsay that affords confrontation. Thus the state bears the burden of establishing constitutional unavailability if the translator does not appear at trial. Absent such a showing, the statements are inadmissible. It is important to note that no Oregon court has yet to address the issue.

Practice Tip

Along with the Crawford and Roberts objections, defense counsel should object on general hearsay grounds. If the state does not provide proper notice under the residual exception, the state does not properly identify the rule, or the trial court does not make proper findings of fact, the statements should be excluded. Furthermore, complete objections will prevent the Court of Appeals from deeming the statements admissible under the “right for the wrong reasons” doctrine.

Confrontation Clause Analysis: For statements the state seeks to introduce under OEC 805, Crawford bars admissibility only if both of its articulated requirements are violated at any hearsay level. Put another way, exclusion is not appropriate if at each level of hearsay either the statement was not testimonial or the declarant testified. Thus, if one level of hearsay is deemed testimonial but the declarant testifies, the entirety of the statement is admissible. Likewise, if at one level the declarant does not testify but the statement is deemed nontestimonial, the statement is admissible. However, if at one level of hearsay both predicates are violated, the entirety of the statement is excluded at all levels. For example, in State v. Ennis, 212 Or App 240 (2007), defendant sought to exclude statements made by a co-defendant to a cellmate and two of the co-defendant’s friends that were thereafter relayed to police. The defendant argued Crawford barred the testimony of everyone because the co-defendant never testified. The Ennis court agreed that the testimonial analysis articulated in Crawford applied to each level of hearsay:

[I]t seems necessarily to follow from the United States Supreme Court’s analysis in Crawford that the rule of that case would apply to each level of hearsay. Consequently, the Confrontation Clause principle enunciated in Crawford is implicated only if one or more levels of multilevel hearsay involve both a testimonial statement and the unavailability of—and lack of prior opportunity to cross-examine—the declarant of that statement. Conversely, it is insufficient if, for example, an unavailable declarant makes a nontestimonial statement to another person, and that person then makes a testimonial statement regarding the former, but is available for cross-examination. Stated another way, in order for Crawford to apply to a multilevel hearsay statement, the two prerequisites to that application—a testimonial statement and an unavailable declarant—must coincide on at least one level.

212 Or App at 254-55. The Ennis court ultimately found no Crawford violation because the cellmate and his two friends had testified at trial and the co-defendant’s statements to his friends were not testimonial.

Due to the amorphous nature of OEC 805, no Oregon cases have directly addressed an Ohio v. Roberts confrontation challenge. However, as Ennis established, each link in the chain of hearsay must survive constitutional scrutiny. As a result, defense counsel should be prepared to argue that each subsection of “testimonial” hearsay within a multiple hearsay statement is not reliable, does not bear “particularized guarantees of trustworthiness,” and is not admissible under a firmly rooted hearsay exception.

I. Recanted Hearsay and Impeachment of an Absent Declarant – OEC 806

Once a hearsay statement has been admitted, the declarant has in effect become a witness. Therefore, in fairness, the declarant’s credibility has become an issue in the case and the trial court must afford the non-offering party the opportunity to present any evidence relevant to the declarant’s credibility. Such evidence may include inconsistent statements, poor recollection or memory issues, character for lack of truthfulness, prior criminal convictions, and bias or interest. Once a party attacks the declarant’s credibility, the other party can likewise introduce evidence that bolsters the same. The rule does not require a declarant to testify.

OEC 806 allows for all kinds of impeachment evidence, including bias evidence, not just prior inconsistent statements and prior convictions. Furthermore, when evidence is admitted under Rule 806, the procedural requirements of the underlying rules do not apply. For example, in State v. Phillips, 314 Or 460 (1992), in response to the state’s successful introduction of statements of an absent four-year-old declarant victim in a sex abuse prosecution, the defense moved to introduce a prior statement that “her daddy told her she had to say bad things about [defendant] in court.” While agreeing with the state that the particulars of the statement did not constitute a prior inconsistent statement, the Oregon Supreme Court admitted the statement anyway because “[t]he testimony that defendant sought to introduce here purports to include elements of relationship, motive, and coercion and may have led to a showing of bias or interest on the part of the child.” 314 Or at 468.

The party seeking to introduce the statement does not have an affirmative burden to produce or attempt to produce the declarant and the procedural requirements of the other underlying rules similarly do not apply. Put another way, OEC 806 is its own avenue of admissibility.

OEC 806 expressly waived the procedural requirement under OEC 613 (prior inconsistent statements) that the declarant be given an opportunity to explain or deny prior inconsistent statements. Similarly, in Phillips the Oregon Supreme Court held that the foundational requirements for OEC 609-1(1) (bias evidence) were irrelevant when the moving party was seeking to introduce bias evidence under OEC 806: “We conclude that the legislature did not intend that testimony impeaching a hearsay declarant by evidence that the declarant engaged in conduct or made statements showing bias or interest be subject to the foundation requirements of [http://www.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=L&pubNum=1006369&cite=ORRREVR609-1&originatingDoc=I027af89df5a611d98ac8f235252e36df&refType=LQ&originationContext=document&vr=3.0&rs=cblt1.0&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.UserEnteredCitation) OEC 609-1(1)], and we hold that those foundation requirements do not apply to impeachment of a hearsay declarant.” 314 Or at 471.

The timing in regards to the order of the statements is generally irrelevant under OEC 806. When the inconsistent statement was made after the admitted hearsay statement, the rule allows for admission. After all, if the declarant had been testifying, it would all be prior inconsistent statements anyway. Likewise, in the normal situation, statements inconsistent to the admitted declaration that occurred prior to the declaration will normally remain admissible under the rule. The only potential exception would occur if the admitted hearsay consists of former testimony at a trial or deposition, thereby giving the opposing party a previous opportunity to cross-examine about the inconsistent statement.

The prosecutor’s impeachment back door is a danger even when the prosecutor elicits the statement first. Rule 806 creates a potential trap for a defense attorney who introduces out-of-court statements of the defendant in a case when the defendant does not take the stand. By introducing a client’s statements through another witness, defense counsel puts their client’s credibility at issue and makes admissible the client’s prior convictions to the extent they are admissible under the relevant rules. In State v. Verardo, 263 Or App 452, 453 (2014), the Court of Appeals held this was true even if the state introduced the statements the first time and the defense was simply having the witness repeat it. The state elicited defendant’s statements as those of a party opponent; thus, the statements were not hearsay and not impeachment evidence. However, when defendant elicited the statements during his case-in-chief, he proffered them for the truth of the matter asserted, i.e., that he had not been involved in the robbery. Unlike the state, defendant introduced the statements as exculpatory hearsay statements. And, as the court concluded, eliciting the statements for that purpose triggered OEC 806: “When, as here, a defendant elicits evidence of his own hearsay statement as exculpatory evidence, the defendant puts his own credibility at issue just as if he had testified and, accordingly, the defendant is subject to impeachment under OEC 806.” Id. at 92.

Confrontation Clause Analysis: For statements the state seeks to introduce under OEC 806, Crawford and Roberts will not be a potential hurdle to admissibility because impeachment evidence does not necessitate confrontation protections. In State v. Nelson, 224 Or App 193, 195-96 (2008), the Court of Appeals addressed a Crawford challenge to evidence the state sought to introduce under Rule 806. At trial, the defendant introduced a note from his former cellmate that purported to grant him the authority to begin receiving his former cellmate’s unemployment checks. Since the former cellmate refused to testify, the state used Rule 806 to introduce statements made by the cellmate to a detective to the effect that the letter was forged. The defendant objected on Crawford grounds. Noting that the statement was introduced for impeachment reasons rather than as substantive evidence, the court ruled that Crawford’s protections did not apply because nonhearsay uses of out-of-court statements are “in effect nontestimonial uses.” Id. at 197-99 (citing Kirkpatrick, 4 Federal Evidence § 8:27, 213-14 (3d ed. 2007)). Concluding that Crawford preserved the use of hearsay statements by nontestifying declarants, the court held that “[a]dmission of that statement was proper under OEC 806 as impeachment of a hearsay declarant and, because the statement was not offered for its truth, defendant’s Sixth Amendment right to confrontation was not violated.” Id.

IV. Other Common Evidence Rules in Sex Crimes Cases

A. Witness Competency – Rules 601 and 602

The Oregon Evidence Code puts forth the following requirements for witness competence: (1) the witness was able to perceive (Rule 601), (2) the witness was able to communicate the perception to others (Rule 601), (3) the witness had personal knowledge of the matter (Rule 602), and (4) the witness took an oath or affirmation to testify truthfully.

Under the old rules, a competency hearing was mandatory for children under 10 years old. With the publishing of the evidence code, the legislature dispensed with this requirement. Now the decision to evaluate is solely that of the trial court.6 The ruling of the trial court regarding competency will normally be upheld on appeal, provided an adequate record is made supporting the ruling. Laird C. Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 601.03[7] (6th ed. 2013). If the Oregon Court of Appeals is to reverse, it will almost solely be in situations when a witness was excluded.

Children or the mentally disabled will be deemed competent to testify if they are physically able to perceive events in question and possess relevant personal knowledge. Under Rule 601, limited mental capacity does not itself render a witness incompetent. See State v. Lantz, 44 Or App 695, 699-700 (1980) (“A mentally disabled person of limited intelligence or intellectual abilities may nevertheless be able to perceive events, recall them, and accurately relate the recollection from the witness stand. The evidence of [the victim’s] mental condition does not demonstrate that she is unable to perceive events or relate her perceptions as a witness”). See also State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621 (1988) (holding that two mentally impaired sex abuse victims should have been allowed to testify).

Rule 601 “establishes a liberal standard for competency of witnesses.” Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 601.03(1). A competent witness need only be able to recognize the necessity of telling the truth, to have personal knowledge and recollection of the relevant events, and be able to communicate that knowledge to the jury. State v. Lantz, 44 Or App 695, 700 (1980). Whether a person who has the ability to perceive and recount an event will do so accurately and truthfully “is to be tested by cross-examination and not by a motion to disqualify the witness as incompetent.” Id.

In State v. Kelly, 244 Or App 105, 109 (2011), the defense argued that suggestive pretrial questioning by CARES rendered the minor victim’s allegations of sexual abuse unreliable. In support for the argument, the defense analogized the victim’s testimony to other categories of evidence that may be excluded on the basis of unreliability such as coerced confession, polygraph tests, and hypnosis. On its way to rejecting that argument, the Court of Appeals held that such reliability assessments are a question of credibility for the trier of fact to weigh in its deliberations, rather than a question of admissibility for the court to determine in the first instance. Id. at 110. See also Bumgarner, 219 Or App at 633.

When a witness’s competency to testify is questioned, only counsel or the court are allowed to ask questions. State v. Doud, 190 Or 218 (1950). The defendant has a constitutional right under Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution to be present at that hearing and to allow defense counsel the opportunity to question. State v. Kitzman, 323 Or 589 (1996). The trial court will not be bound by the rules of evidence, other than privilege. Rule 104(1). There is no authority under the Oregon Evidence Code for a trial judge to order pretrial competency examinations of prospective witnesses by outside experts. Laird C. Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 601.03[3] (6th ed. 2013). In State v. Hiatt, 303 Or 60 (1987), the Oregon Supreme Court refused to overturn a trial court’s refusal to order a pretrial psychological examination of an alleged rape victim:

[Defendant] contends that if the court is responsible for determinations of competency—defined by OEC 601 as the ability to perceive and to relate those perceptions—a court must also be authorized to order a psychological examination to determine whether a psychological disorder impairs a witness’s ability to perceive reality, to recollect events or to communicate about them. We are not persuaded by these arguments. The OEC provisions that authorize a trial judge to admit expert testimony in court and to exclude incompetent witnesses does not imply a right to call on experts to evaluate the credibility or competency of witnesses in an out-of-court proceeding.

Id. at 66.

Confrontation Clause Analysis: OEC 601 is not an evidentiary rule of admissibility. Therefore when analyzing whether Crawford or Roberts confrontation applies, the defense must determine under what hearsay exception the statement is sought to be introduced. See [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=1689712124358304376&q=State+ex+rel.+v.+S.P.,+346+Or+592&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 State ex rel. Juvenile Dep’t of Multnomah Cty. v. S.P]., 346 Or 592, 607 (2009) (after child found incompetent to testify, state sought to introduce incompetent declarant’s statements to CARES under OEC 803(18)(a)(b). Affirmed Court of Appeals decision to exclude under Crawford as testimonial because agency works in tandem with law enforcement).

Unavailability under OEC 601 will constitute constitutional unavailability. See State v. Stevens, 311 Or 119, 142 (1991) (holding constitutional unavailability is present when the state made good-faith efforts coupled with extensive testimony about the children’s inability to testify).

B. Learned Treatise and the Basis for Expert Opinion – OEC 702, 703,
and 706

An expert may give an opinion about an issue in a case if the testimony “will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or a fact in issue.” OEC 702. An expert’s opinion may be based on facts or data that would be inadmissible as evidence, provided they are “of a type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field in forming opinions or inferences upon the subject.” OEC 703. Learned treatises are not admissible as substantive evidence, but they may be used for the purpose of impeaching a witness.

OEC 703 was originally a restrictive rule, intended to limit the sources of data upon which an expert may rely. However, once the rule was changed to allow experts to rely upon hearsay, the rule became permissive. Of course, an expert may rely upon their own observations as treating physicians always must. An expert may also ground their opinions upon the statements of other witnesses. Counsel may also ask the expert hypothetical questions, but the details of the question must be consistent with actual evidence in the case. Finally, experts may rely upon hearsay under the theory that this is how experts such as treating physicians come to conclusions every day. As Oregon no longer adheres to the “facts in evidence” requirement for expert opinion, the hearsay need not have been admitted nor even be admissible at trial.

Theoretically, the types of hearsay an expert is allowed to consider are limited to those types of evidence that are of “a type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field.” Thus counsel can move to exclude an expert’s conclusions under OEC 104(1) if the expert is relying upon data that is not “of a kind and manner that experts would usually rely upon.” To make this argument, counsel may need to present an independent expert hypothesizing on whether it is “reasonable” for the opinion expert to be relying upon the kinds of hearsay at issue. See Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 703.03[2].

A testifying expert may rely upon and convey the opinion of another expert as part of the basis for his or her own opinion. The opinion of the other expert need not have been admitted into evidence or be admissible so long as it is “reasonable” for the testifying expert to have relied upon it. The Commentary to OEC 703 cites to a physician basing their diagnosis on the opinions from nurses, technicians, and other doctors in support for this broad allowance.

At the time of codification, the drafters of OEC 703 indicated that they believed there
were creating another hearsay exception.7 However, ensuing case law and Laird Kirkpatrick have persuasively established that the evidence is admissible under OEC 703 because it is
not hearsay:

The statement in the Commentary that Rule 703 “in effect … represents another exception to the hearsay rule” is unsound. When an out-of-court statement relied on by an expert is admitted under Rule 703, it is not hearsay because it is not being admitted for the truth of the matter asserted, but only to explain the basis for the expert’s opinion.

Laird C. Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 703.03(5). See also McCathern v. Toyota Motor Corp, 332 Or 59, 70 (2011) ( “[P]laintiff offered the evidence of the details surrounding the 15 other rollover accidents only to provide the foundation necessary to explain Wallingford’s opinions, not for its truth. . . . Consequently, by definition, that information was not “hearsay”). See also [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=2534482676255620947&q=Oberg+v.+Honda+Motor+Co.,+316+Or+263&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 Oberg v. Honda Motor Co.], 316 Or 263, 269–70 (1993) (excerpts of documents read to jury admitted for limited purpose that defendants had notice of defective design and not for truth were not hearsay).

By comparison, in State v. Thomas, 279 Or App 98 (2016), the Court of Appeals declined to allow the introduction of specific studies underlying an expert’s testimony when the proponent of the evidence failed to establish that the expert’s opinion was specifically grounded in those studies. While acknowledging that OEC 703 does not itself authorize the admission of otherwise inadmissible evidence, the defense argued that the rule allows for the admission of studies the expert relied upon as a foundation for their expert’s opinion. Id. at 105-106. The court agreed with that proposition but disagreed that there was sufficient evidence in the record to establish that these particular studies were the foundational basis for the expert’s conclusions.

Learned treatises are not admissible as substantive evidence, but they may be used for the purpose of impeaching a witness. Travis v. Unruh, 66 Or App 562 (1984). The official legislative commentary to OEC 803(18a) provides, in relevant part:

“The Legislative Assembly did not adopt the federal hearsay exception for learned treatises which appears as subsection (18) of [http://www.westlaw.com/Link/Document/FullText?findType=L&pubNum=1004365&cite=USFRER803&originatingDoc=I2d0693c8f39611d9b386b232635db992&refType=LQ&originationContext=document&vr=3.0&rs=cblt1.0&transitionType=DocumentItem&contextData=(sc.DocLink) Fed R Evid 803]. It wishes to retain the Oregon practice of using treatises in the cross-examination of expert witnesses. Learned treatises should not be admitted as substantive evidence as they are likely to be misunderstood and misapplied in the absence of expert assistance and supervision.” Legislative Commentary to Rule 803, reprinted in Laird C. Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 803.18 (6th Ed 2013).

Although OEC 703 “recognize[s] that experts often rely on facts and data supplied by third parties,” the rule “does not give carte blanche to admitting otherwise inadmissible hearsay.” [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=16490986903285548143&q=Mission+Ins.+Co.+v.+Wallace+Security+Agy.,+Inc.,+84+Or+App+525,+&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 Mission Ins. Co. v. Wallace Security Agy., Inc.,] 84 Or App 525, 528–29 (1987). In Kahn v. Pony Express, 173 Or App 127 (2011), a civil plaintiff sought to introduce the ruminations of the plaintiff’s family’s physician through a testifying psychologist. On direct examination, the psychologist testified that in forming his opinions about the relationship between the decedent plaintiff and her daughter, he relied in part on information he received from the family physician, including that the decedent was a nurturing parent eager to learn. Because those observations and statements were outside the scope of the psychologist’s actual opinion, they were inadmissible hearsay. Kahn, 173 Or App at 154.

V. Conclusion

Hearsay almost always plays a critical role in a sex prosecution. After all, the government will go to great lengths to use prior statements from the victim and other witnesses to bolster and corroborate their case. The unfortunate reality is that the Oregon legislature has been and will continue to be all too willing to give prosecutors generous avenues of admissibility within this area of criminal practice. The good news is that a prepared counsel will be ready to fight these efforts on three distinct battle fields: Article I, section 11, the Sixth Amendment, and the individual code provisions. Knowing the factors and issues at play at each level may not always lead to exclusion, but it often will. And there is always that next appeal!
