Defending Sex Cases

Defending Sex Cases: Strategies from Allegation to Resolution, 2018 edition

Chapter 9. They Want to Introduce What?: Other Act Evidence

By David T. McDonald

I. Introduction

Until 1997, OEC 404(3) and 403 (balancing test) generally controlled the admissibility of “other act evidence.” OEC 404(3) categorically excluded “other act evidence” proffered solely to show an accused’s disposition (“propensity”) to commit the crime(s) of which they were charged. However, just as OEC 404(3) barred evidence to show that the person acted in conformity therewith,1 OEC 404(3)2 also recognized that “character” evidence might be admissible for other nonpropensity purposes. The importance of each of these two separate sentences will be discussed infra. The burden remained on the proponent of the evidence to show that the evidence was relevant under OEC 401 and was being offered for a nonpropensity purpose under 404(3). Once the proponent met those criteria, the trial court still had to undergo a “balancing test” under OEC 4033 weighing the probative value against any prejudicial impact. The trial court utilized the test from State v. Mayfield, 302 Or 631, 645, 733 P2d 438 (1987). If the trial court found that the probative value of the evidence was not substantially outweighed by its prejudicial impact, then the evidence was deemed admissible.

In 1997, the Oregon Legislature enacted 404(4) and it changed the methodology the courts used to determine the admissibility of other act evidence.4 The change was so profound that Oregon evidence guru Laird Kirkpatrick spent several pages in his treatise describing the “sharp departure from long-established doctrine governing character evidence.”5 Initially, Oregon courts weren’t certain how to handle the new provision but eventually began to hold that 404(4) eliminated the OEC 403 balancing test as a last step in evaluating the admissibility of other act evidence6 except “upon an individual and particularized due process claim.” Id.

Then in 2015, the Oregon Supreme Court issued its opinion in State v. Williams,7 which held that propensity evidence was admissible in criminal proceedings involving child sexual abuse under 404(4), but only if the trial court complied with due process by conducting a balancing test under OEC 403 (the Mayfield test).

Specifically, the Williams court held:

OEC 404(4) supersedes OEC 404(3) in a criminal case except to the extent required by the state or federal constitution. In a prosecution of child sexual abuse, the federal constitution requires that a trial court determine whether the risk of unfair prejudice posed by the evidence outweighs its probative value under OEC 403.”8

The Court of Appeals later issued a cascade of opinions attempting to interpret the new Williams rule. In a subsequent case, the Oregon Supreme Court stated that Williams left two unanswered questions:

(1) the extent to which prior bad acts evidence can be admitted solely for propensity purposes in criminal cases other than ones involving child sexual abuse; and

(2) whether, in criminal cases in which evidence is admitted for propensity purposes, due process requires traditional OEC 403 balancing or requires a greater showing of unfair prejudice than traditional OEC 403 balancing would require to exclude otherwise probative evidence.

State v. Turnidge, 359 Or 364, 432, 374 P3d 853, 897 (2016), cert. denied, (U.S. Jan. 9, 2017) (acknowledging but not addressing two questions reserved by Williams).

The Turnidge court recognized that prior to admitting nonpropensity evidence, and “upon proper motion,” a trial court must still conduct a 403/Mayfield balancing test when determining whether nonpropensity evidence that met one of the inclusionary purposes of 404(3) could be admitted.9 The Turnidge court noted that Williams, a case involving the admissibility of propensity evidence involving child sex abuse, was confined to its facts regarding that admissibility. After Turnidge, the Court of Appeals issued a series of opinions, some of which affirmed the use of the OEC 403 balancing test using the Mayfield factors.10

On April 27, 2017, the Oregon Supreme Court addressed some of the issues left undecided
by Williams in a trilogy of cases—

State v. Baughman, 361 Or 386, 393 P3d 1132 (2017).

State v. Mazziotti, 361 Or 370, 393 P3d 235 (2017).

State v Zavala, 361 Or 377, 393 P3d 230 (2017).

The court treated Baughman as the lead case. The Court of Appeals has since issued four opinions that rely on some, or all, of those decisions. A discussion of the trilogy, and the Court of Appeals’ new cases is below in Sections V and VI.

II. The Foundational Premise

Understanding the foundational premise of our rules and articulating that principle in our legal pleadings can be paramount to the trial court’s interpretation. Justice Walters, writing for the majority in Williams, went back in time and highlighted why the courts should exercise caution in determining the admissibility of other act evidence, especially as to when it is offered solely as propensity evidence:

The general rule is unquestioned that evidence of a distinct crime unconnected with that laid in the indictment cannot be given in evidence against the prisoner. Such evidence tends to mislead the jury, creates a prejudice against the prisoner, and requires him to answer a charge for the defense of which he is not supposed to have made preparation. And while, as Lord Campbell says, “it would be evidence to prove that the prisoner is a very bad man, and likely to commit such an offense” (Reg. v. Oddy, 5 Cox C.C. 210), under no enlightened system of jurisp[r]udence can a person be convicted of one crime on proof that he has committed another.”

State v. Williams, 357 Or 1, 9, 346 P3d 455, 459 (2015), quoting State v. Baker, 23 Or 441,
442-43, 32 P. 161 (1893) (emphasis supplied).

Practice Tip

Never forget that it is not just unfair prejudice that can be used to overcome admissibility. Relevant evidence may also be excluded if its probative value is substantially outweighed by confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury, or by considerations of undue delay or needless presentation of cumulative evidence''. So, work on developing specific examples of how the state’s proposed evidence will mislead the jury into convicting simply because it thinks that the defendant is a very bad person rather than on whether there is proof beyond a reasonable doubt of the charged crime(s). The state always argues “all evidence we introduce hurts the defendant.” The counter argument is that, while that may be true, the admissibility of most evidence isnot'' subject to a balancing test before admission as is other act evidence. The reason the balancing test exists as to character evidence is because other act evidence can taint the jury, more than any other type of evidence, such that the jury is willing to convict based upon the accused’s past acts rather than the evidence regarding the current and specific charge(s).

III. The Williams Basics

1. OEC 404(4) is in conflict with OEC 404(3) because the legislature specifically provided that “all” relevant character evidence specific to the accused be admitted with some exceptions delineated. OEC 404(4) does not apply to any other person, just the defendant. So the new paradigm only applies to the accused on trial.

Practice Tip

It can be argued that the plain language of the rule allows all'' propensity evidence be admitted as to a defendant, so offer as much propensity evidence as you can that isgood'' (“other good act evidence) for the accused.

OEC 404(4) was a part of SB 936 in response to the Armatta decision, which overturned measure 40. Williams makes it clear that the legislative history of OEC 404(4) shows a clear intent to make all relevant evidence admissible. The Williams court recognized that “The legislature enacted OEC 404(4) in 1997 in response to this court’s anticipated invalidation of Ballot Measure 40 (1996), which, among other provisions, provided crime victims with “the right to have all relevant evidence admitted against criminal defendants.”

State v. Williams, 357 Or 1, 14, 346 P3d 455, 462 (2015).

2. OEC 404(4) supersedes 404(3) and allows for the introduction of relevant propensity
evidence solely to establish that the defendant acted in conformity therewith.

3. OEC 404(3) still prohibits the introduction of relevant propensity evidence in criminal cases solely for the purpose of showing that any person, other than the defendant, acted in conformity therewith. OEC 404(4) only trumps 404(3) as to the defendant.

4. OEC 403 Application: Prevents Luring the Factfinders

a. The original rationale behind the use of the balancing test was to prevent the factfinder from being lured into declaring guilt on a ground different from proof specific to the offense charged. After its passage, our courts interpreted OEC 404(4) as barring the use of the OEC 403 balancing test in evaluating whether evidence was admissible under OEC 404(3). The Williams court rejected that conclusion and reinstated the use of the balancing test where character evidence (404(3) evidence) is being introduced under OEC 404(4) because due process required the use of the balancing test before allowing the admission of evidence under OEC 404(4).

b. The Williams court recognized that new Federal Rules of Evidence, FRE 413 and FRE 414, allow for broad admissibility of other acts of sexual assault committed in sexual assault cases (FRE 41311) and other acts of child molestation in child molestation cases (FRE 41412) and the Williams court found that the legislature, in passing OEC 404(4), deferred to the courts whether the federal Due Process Clause required the application of OEC 403 as a prerequisite to admitting evidence under OEC 404(4).

c. The Williams court found that the United States Supreme Court agreed that the use of a balancing test, such as under OEC 403, is sufficient to safeguard a person’s due process rights, but in the absence of a clear holding by the SCOTUS, the court needed to determine whether the federal Due Process Clause required its application. Ultimately, the Williams court found that the federal due process clause did require the application of OEC 403 in order to satisfy federal due process.13

d. The Williams court made clear that the balancing test should be used to determine admissibility of any character evidence, whether offered for a propensity or nonpropensity purpose. Yet the court attempted to offer some guidance in evaluating the spectrum of potential admissibility by stating:

At one end of the spectrum, “other acts” evidence that is offered for nonpropensity purposes—i.e., to prove motive,14 intent, identity, or lack of mistake or accident—generally will be admissible as long as the particular facts of the case do not demonstrate a risk of unfair prejudice that outweighs the probative value of the evidence. Shaw, 338 Or at 614–15, 113 P3d 898. At the other end of the spectrum, as the state recognizes, when “other acts” evidence “goes only to character and there are no permissible inferences the jury may draw from it,” it is more likely that the evidence will be excluded. Such evidence generally will have little or no cognizable probative value, and the risk that the jury may conclude improperly that the defendant had acted in accordance with past acts on the occasion of the charged crime will be substantial.

State v. Williams, 357 Or 1, 19-20, 346 P3d 455, 465 (2015) (emphasis supplied).

Practice Tip

The emphasized language in the quote above from Williams should be part of every argument. The quote highlights that it is not a bright line test but rather a continuum from not admissible to admissible depending upon the purpose for the offer of the “other act evidence.”

IV. Post Williams

Before the court issued opinions in the Baughman trilogy, the fallout from Williams broke down into pre-Turnidge and post-Turnidge cases. After Williams, courts began processing cases into two categories: 1) those cases where evidence was being offered for a nonpropensity purpose under traditional 404(3) analysis and 2) where evidence was being offered for a solely propensity purpose under 404(4).

Up until Williams, there was a presumption that 404(4) superseded 404(3) in all respects; after Williams, the courts began differentiating between whether evidence should be admitted under 404(3) for nonpropensity purposes or under 404(4) for propensity purposes. The appellate courts also seemed to scrutinize evidence being offered under 404(3) more closely.

In the pre-Turnidge category, a number of cases from the Court of Appeals struggle with preservation of error, the appropriate balancing test, and the admissibility of propensity evidence in cases not involving child sexual abuse.15 Then came Turnidge in which the court evaluated the proffered evidence under the traditional prongs of OEC 404(3) and 403 and reinstated the 403 balancing test for nonpropensity evidence.

The Turnidge court initially clarified that the test in Johns only applied to cases involving the “doctrine of chances”16 (where defendant admits to conduct but asserts mistake or inadvertence) and then evaluated the admissibility of the other act conduct as nonpropensity evidence. The doctrine of chances theory, developed fully in Leistiko,

rests on the proposition that the defendant either concedes the act that requires proof of a mental state or the trial court instructs the jury not to consider uncharged misconduct evidence offered to prove intent unless and until the jury finds the act that requires proof of intent to have been done and is proceeding to determine intent.”

State v. Turnidge, 359 Or 364, 444, 374 P3d 853, 903 (2016), cert. denied, No. 16-6738, 2017 WL 69511 (U.S. Jan. 9, 2017); see State v. Tena, 281 Or App 57, 384 P3d 521 (2016).

Subsequently, in the Tena decision reversing the Court of Appeals, Justice Landau set forth the tenets of the doctrine as follows:

The basic idea behind the doctrine of chances is the “proposition that multiple instances of similar conduct are unlikely to occur accidentally.” Baughman, 361 Or at 407, 393 P3d 1132. The more often a defendant intentionally performs an act, the less likely it is that, when the defendant performs the act again, he or she did so accidentally or innocently. As the court explained in Johns, “[t]he recurrence or repetition of the act increases the likelihood of a mens rea or mind at fault.” 301 Or at 552, 725 P2d 312.

State v. Tena, 362 Or at 524 (2018).

Justice Landau went on to emphasize that this doctrine does not only apply where an accused denies the allegation:

It is important to emphasize that the doctrine of chances applies only to explain whether or not an act that a defendant performed was performed intentionally. It does not apply when there is a dispute about whether the defendant performed the act at all. As this court explained in Turnidge, the doctrine concerns the admissibility of evidence “offered to prove a defendant’s intent or absence of mistake[;] . . . that is, evidence countering a defendant’s claim that he or she performed the act alleged but did so  by mistake.” 359 Or at 435, 374 P3d 853. It applies to evidence of other acts offered to show that “the defendant acted intentionally, rather than inadvertently.” Id. at 436, 374 P3d 853.17

Therefore, the Turnidge court refused to revisit questions left open by Williams, in part because the justices found the evidence was admissible for a nonpropensity purpose and admitted after proper balancing under 403.

After Turnidge, the Court of Appeals acknowledged that the Johns test applied only to cases involving the “doctrine of chances”18 and, unlike Turnidge, rejected the trial court’s admission of the evidence for the nonpropensity purpose and remanded for a new trial.19 The Wright court noted that the state did not raise the issue of whether or not the evidence in a case that did not involve child sexual abuse could be admitted as propensity evidence.20

Thus, the Oregon Court of Appeals and Oregon Supreme Court had still not directly addressed questions left open by Williams while beginning to develop a trend of distinguishing between propensity and nonpropensity evidence and giving greater scrutiny to the admissibility of nonpropensity evidence.

V. The Trilogy (Baughman, Mazziotti, Zavala)

Although the questions presented in the briefing for the trilogy seemed to get to the heart of all the questions left unanswered by Williams, not all were addressed. Nevertheless, it is important to analyze the trilogy of cases and recognize what is still undecided.

The Baughman court addressed three important questions:

1) What kind of OEC 403 balancing applies to evidence offered under OEC 404(4)? In other words, what, exactly, does the phrase “to the extent required by” the state or federal constitution mean?

2) What standard of review applies to a trial court’s decision under OEC 403 to admit or exclude other acts evidence offered to prove propensity under OEC 404(4)?

3) When the Court of Appeals reverses and remands a case based on a trial court’s failure to conduct proper OEC 403 balancing before admitting other acts evidence, must it reverse and remand the case for a new trial, or may it impose a “conditional remand” that requires the trial court to only hold a hearing and conduct the proper balancing?

Based on those questions, the Baughman court held:

1) In cases involving child sexual abuse where evidence of an individual’s character is offered to show that person acted in conformity therewith (propensity evidence), the trial court must utilize the “traditional” 403 balancing test to determine admissibility.

2) 404(4) supersedes the first sentence21 but not the second sentence22 of 404(3).

3) If evidence is not offered to prove that the defendant acted in conformity with a particular character trait (propensity) but rather is offered for a nonpropensity purpose (traditional 404(3) evidence), then the trial court need not reach the propensity issue.

4) The remedy on remand may be a new trial, but the court left that to the discretion of the trial court after the trial court conducted the appropriate analysis set forth in Baughman.

Note: The court deferred to another day one of the questions presented, which was the question of whether propensity evidence would ever be admissible in cases not involving child sexual abuse.23

In addressing the following question: “[W]hether the balancing under OEC 403 is “traditional” or “subconstitutional” balancing, or, as the state posits, “due process” balancing that precludes the admission of propensity evidence under OEC 404(4) only if its admission would render the trial fundamentally unfair as a matter of law.”24

The Baughman court held:

Considering the text, context, and legislative history of OEC 404(4), we are persuaded that, in enacting that rule of evidence, the legislature intended trial courts to conduct the balancing required by OEC 403 according to its terms. Under OEC 404(4) (a) and OEC 403, trial courts may exclude evidence, in the exercise of their discretion, when they determine that its probative value is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. See State v. Knight, 343 Or 469, 483, 173 P3d 1210 (2007) (explaining that court’s exclusion of evidence under OEC 403 requires exercise of discretion). Under OEC 404(4) (d), trial courts also must exclude evidence, as a matter of law, that would render a trial fundamentally unfair and violate the Due Process Clause. See State v. Rogers, 330 Or 282, 312, 4 P3d 1261 (2000) (explaining that, when only one legally correct outcome exists, decision is legal in nature and not exercise of discretion). OEC 404(4) (a) and OEC 404(4) (d) thus provide two independent bases for excluding other acts evidence.25

Practice Tip

The Baughman Court set up a two-part analysis for determining admissibility of other act (character) evidence once it is sought to be introduced:

1) Determine if there is any nonpropensity purpose that justifies admissibility and, if yes, conduct the 403 balancing test to determine admissibility. If the court determines that there is a nonpropensity purpose and the prejudicial impact does not outweigh the probative value under the 403 balancing test, then there is no need to conduct a 404(4) analysis; then, if the case involves child sexual abuse,

2) if the court finds that there are no nonpropensity purposes under which the evidence is admissible, the court can then evaluate the admissibility of the evidence under 404(4).

The Baughman court emphasized the 403 balancing test will have a due process consideration when determining admissibility and also emphasized that the prejudice prong of the balancing test would tilt more toward admissibility for a nonpropensity purpose under 404(3) than it would for a pure propensity purpose offered under 404(4):

This does not mean that [t]he balancing that a trial court conducts under OEC 403 will be devoid of due process considerations. As this court explained in Williams, it is the Due Process Clause that requires the application of OEC 403. 357 Or at 18, 346 P3d 455. The common-law underpinnings of that rule, the Supreme Court’s explanation of the meaning of the term “unfair prejudice” in Old Chief, and its description of the role that balancing plays in Dowling all remind us that OEC 403 balancing must be conducted to preclude the admission of “concededly relevant evidence” that has the capacity “to lure the factfinder into declaring guilt on a ground different from proof specific to the offense charged.” Old Chief, 519 U.S. at 180, 117 S.Ct. 644. That is why we said, in Williams, that, “when ‘other acts’ evidence ‘goes only to character and there are no permissible inferences the jury may draw from it,’ it is more likely that the evidence will be excluded. Such evidence generally will have little or no cognizable probative value, and the risk that the jury may conclude improperly that the defendant had acted in accordance with past acts on the occasion of the charged crime will be substantial.”357 Or at 20(emphasis in original).

Baughman, 361 Or at 402–03.

Practice Tip

At bottom, the Baughman court recognized that when evidence is being offered solely for propensity purposes in child sex abuse cases, it is more likely the evidence will be excluded. This is an extremely important quote and one that should be in every brief. If you can convince a court that the evidence does not meet any purpose under 404(3) and those arguments are just a ruse to get in under 404(4), then the trial court should conduct the 403 balancing test with their thumb on the scale of inadmissibility.

Practice Tip

The Baughman court, emphasizing language from Williams, has so far limited the potential of propensity evidence solely to cases involving child sexual abuse. Footnote 826 of Baughman gives a clear signal to everyone that the Oregon Supreme Court has not decided whether evidence solely being offered to show that an accused person acted in conformity therewith is ever admissible in cases other than those involving child sexual abuse. Further, it seems not to be inclined to extend inclusion of propensity evidence for cases other than those involving child sexual abuse. See cases cited under fn 17, supra.

In Mazziotti, supra, the court reaffirmed the rule that even in cases where the state seeks to admit character evidence for a nonpropensity purpose, the trial court must conduct the 403 balancing test to determine ultimate admissibility (affirming the Court of Appeals’ reversal of the trial court in a case involving the charge of failure to perform the duties of a driver).

In Zavala, supra, the court, again through Justice Walters, found that the trial court admitted evidence for a nonpropensity purpose (sexual predisposition for the alleged victim) but failed to conduct the proper balancing test under 403. However, the court found the error to balance was harmless because the defendant did not make the argument to the trial court (or on appeal) that his case was different from McKay:

Rather, this is a case, like McKay, 309 Or at 308, 787 P2d 479, in which the evidence appeared to be relevant for a nonpropensity purpose—to prove defendant’s sexual disposition for the victim. Such nonpropensity evidence is generally admissible, unless, of course, the particular facts demonstrate a risk of prejudice that substantially outweighs its probative value. Williams, 357 Or at 19-20, 346 P3d 455; Shaw, 338 Or at 612, 113 P3d 898. . . .

At trial in this case, the court cited McKay and gave defendant an opportunity to argue that his case was different. Defendant did not contend that it was or that the particular facts of his case demonstrated a risk of unfair prejudice that substantially outweighed the probative value of the evidence. See Williams, 357 Or at 19, 346 P3d 455 (permitting that demonstration). And defendant again fails to make either argument before this court.3 In the absence of a meritorious argument that could persuade a trial court to exclude the challenged evidence, we conclude that the trial court’s failure to conduct balancing under OEC 403 did not significantly affect its decision to admit that evidence. Consequently, we also conclude that there was little likelihood that the trial court’s error affected its judgment of conviction. See State v. Davis, 336 Or 19, 32, 77 P3d 1111 (2003) (error requires reversal unless reviewing court can conclude that there was little likelihood that trial court’s error affected conviction).

State v. Zavala, 361 Or 377, 385, 393 P3d 230, 235 (2017).

Recently, the Oregon Supreme Court reversed the conviction of a person convicted of domestic violence assault. State v. Tena, 362 Or 514, 412 P3d 175 (2018). In Tena, Justice Landau highlighted the holdings of [file://localhost/.https/::scholar.google.com:scholar_case%3Fcase=9903617515752744475&q=State+v.+williams&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 Williams], and the trilogy, and succinctly sets forth the analysis for how a litigant should evaluate and argue for, or against, the admissibility of other act evidence. Since the trilogy, there have also been four significant cases out of the Court of Appeals that cite to Baughman.

In State v. Jones, now Supreme Court Justice Becky Duncan, writing for the court, rejected the state’s contention that 404(4) superseded 404(3) in Williams,27 and noted that the Baughman court made clear that 404(4) only superseded the first sentence of 404(3) but did not supersede the second sentence. Id. See Tena, 362 Or at 520 (affirming the principle). The Jones court then evaluated the nonpropensity purposes the state asserted supported admission of the other act evidence it proffered. Id. The court rejected the claim that the state had satisfied its burden for admitting the character evidence for any of the nonpropensity purposes asserted.28 Finally, the court rejected the state’s request to address the unpreserved argument that the evidence (it was a robbery case) could be admitted to show propensity under OEC 404(4). In rejecting the state’s request to address the unpreserved claim, the Jones court noted that in Baughman the Oregon Supreme Court had not yet decided whether character evidence offered solely for a propensity purpose would ever be admissible in cases other than child sexual abuse.

Note: This case is a good read on what the trial courts should be doing in scrutinizing the state’s evidence by our newest Oregon Supreme Court justice. Here, the Jones court found that the stated failed to establish 1) that the prior conduct was relevant to show, using the doctrine of chances, that defendant acted intentionally and not by accident or mistake, because the state failed to establish that the prior conduct and the charged conduct were similar, as required by Johns; 2) that the prior conduct was relevant to show that defendant acted pursuant to a plan, because the state failed to show that there was a high degree of similarity between defendant’s prior conduct and the charged conduct; and 3) that the prior conduct was relevant to show defendant’s motive to commit the charged act, because the state failed to show a “substantial connecting link between the two acts,” which, in the case, required more than simply showing that defendant had been convicted of robbery in the past.29

In State v. Parker, 285 Or App 777, 398 P3d 437 (2017), the Court of Appeals again reversed the trial court. Although the Parker court found the evidence would be admissible for a nonpropensity purpose, it rejected the state’s assertion that the trial court merely needed to go through the “narrow due process balancing” rather than the traditional balancing test under 403. Relying on Baughman, the Parker court held that the trial court must go through the traditional 403 balancing
test before determining whether or not to admit the evidence, at least when requested as was in
this case.30

Note: Therefore, the courts are now strongly making two points: 1) the trial courts must use the traditional 403 balancing test whether the evidence is being offered for a nonpropensity purpose under 404(3) and for a propensity purpose under 404(4), and 2) there is a strong trend that will have to be addressed, but so far there is no definitive ruling on whether or not it will be reviewed on appeal if not requested at the trial court. See Section VIII, infra, on requesting that the court conduct the traditional 403 balancing test.

The Parker court then applied the Mayfield test and found that the probative value of admitting the evidence was substantially outweighed by prejudicial impact:

In this case, the only purpose that would be served by permitting the state to introduce the name of defendant’s crime of conviction would be to show that defendant is “a bad man and therefore probably committed the crime for which he was charged.” Zimmerlee, 261 Or at 54, 492 P2d 795; see Baughman, 361 Or at 405, 393 P3d 1132 (“[W]hen evidence is relevant only to prove a defendant’s character, more significant due process concerns are implicated, and, generally, the danger of unfair prejudice will substantially outweigh the probative value of the evidence”).

Parker, 285 Or App at 786.

This case states that if there is a less prejudical way to get the evidence admitted then it should be accepted.31 In this case, the defendant was charged with weapons and DV assault. The state introduced a prior felony conviction for delivery of a controlled substance–methamphetamine. The defense conceded that the fact of the felony conviction was relevant but the specific type of conviction (i.e., the actual name of the crime) was not and wanted the judgment to be redacted. VII. Evidence Admitted for Propensity or Nonpropensity Purpose

Propensity evidence is evidence of other acts that are proffered to prove defendant’s character and that he acted in conformity with that character at the time he committed the current alleged offense(s). OEC 404(4).

Nonpropensity evidence is evidence of a person’s prior action that is not offered to show that the person acted in conformity with his character but for another purpose such as motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, absence of mistake of fact, etc. OEC 404(3).

Each piece of evidence must be evaluated under the two step analysis: 1) whether or not it meets the requirements for admissibility as nonpropensity evidence and, if it does, is its probative value outweighed by its prejudicial impact and 2) if not admissible for a nonpropensity purpose, is it admissible as propensity evidence and is its probative value outweighed by prejudicial impact if admitted.

Also, there is a Baughman presumption that the evidence is more likely going to satisfy the 403 balancing test if it is offered for a nonpropensity purpose than solely as propensity evidence.

Practice Tip

Each piece of proffered evidence must be examined through the prism of “logical reasoning.” Make the state articulate the reason why a particular piece of proffered evidence goes to the “motive, intent, preparation.” One cannot simply say, “Defendant has an interest in child pornography. Therefore the defendant has an interest in sexually abusing children. Therefore, defendant has a motive to sexually abuse the child in this case.”

In evaluating the relevancy and admissibility of nonpropensity evidence following Williams and Turnidge, the Court of Appeals has held that:

After Williams, we observed that “the types of relevant evidence set out in OEC 404(3) (‘motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or accident’) remain viable theories for the admission of prior acts evidence.” State v. Clarke, 279 Or App 373, 382, 379 P3d 674, 680 (2016) (citing State v. Turnidge, 359 Or 364, 434, 374 P3d 853 (2016)). “Thus, in evaluating whether evidence of ‘other crimes, wrongs or acts’ is admissible for nonpropensity purposes, we may draw on the ‘settled principles’ of relevance embodied in OEC 404(3) and case law construing that provision.” Clarke, 279 Or App at 382, 379 P3d at 680 (quoting Turnidge, 359 Or at 434, 374 P3d 853).

State v. Tena, 281 Or App 57, 63, 384 P3d 521, 525–26 (2016), reversed on other grounds, State v. Tena, 362 Or 514, 412 P3d 172 (2018).

VIII. OEC 403 Balancing Test: You Must Ask For It

Baughman says “We do not decide, in this case, whether, in addition to objecting to the admission of such evidence, a party also must explicitly seek balancing under OEC 403.”33 However, the Court of Appeals decisions have almost uniformly found that the failure to ask for the test waives the requirement that the court engage in the 403 balancing test. So always ask for the court to engage in the 403 balancing test. Most recently, and just before the state supreme court issued Baughman, the Court of Appeals stated:

However, defendant did not ask the trial court to engage in OEC 403 balancing with regard to that evidence, and we have consistently held that our review of that claimed error depends on defendant . . .“mak[ing] such a challenge.” State v. Altabef, , 279 Or App 268, 379 P3d 755 (2016), review denied, 360 Or 752 but, review allowed (state’s petition), decision vacated, 361 Or 885 (2017); see Turnidge, 359 Or at 430, 374 P3d 853 (admissibility of sexual predisposition evidence “depends on a trial court determination, in response to a proper motion, that the probative value of the evidence outweighs the danger of unfair prejudice under OEC 403”); State v. Williams, 357 Or 1, 19, 346 P3d 455 (2015) (a trial court must engage in the balancing anticipated by OEC 403 “[w]hen a party objects, under OEC 403, to ‘other acts’ evidence”); State v. McMullin, 269 Or App 859, 860 n. 2, 346 P3d 611, rev. den., 357 Or 640, 360 P3d 523 (2015) (OEC 403 balancing was not preserved where the defendant neither sought a ruling nor objected to the admission of the evidence at issue on those grounds at trial).

State v. Woods, 284 Or App 559, 563–64, 393 P3d 1188, 1190–91 (2017) (emphasis supplied).

So, file a motion and ask for the court to conduct the 403 balancing test and argue it must be
done using Mayfield.

Practice Tip

Remember, Williams and Baughman appear to equate the traditional OEC 403 balancing test with the constitutionally required due process test.34 Therefore, it is arguable whether the test is constitutionally mandated. If constitutionally mandated, there should be no requirement that the defense ask the court to undergo the analysis, although it is highly recommended. However, the more interesting question is whether or not the burden remains on the state to prove that the probative value outweighs all the other factors.

OEC 403 Mayfield Test

The Mayfield test retains some flexibility for trial courts. The Court of Appeals has outlined
the Mayfield 403 balancing test analysis as follows:

[the] method includes four basic steps: (1) “analyze the quantum of probative value of the evidence and consider the weight or strength of the evidence,” (2) “determine how prejudicial the evidence is, to what extent the evidence may distract the jury from the central question whether the defendant committed the charged crime,” (3) balance those two factors, and (4) make a ruling to admit some, all, or none of the proponent’s evidence. Mayfield, 302 Or at 645, 733 P2d 438. A trial court need not “expressly follow the Mayfield analysis,” but, rather, “meets the requirements of Mayfield if ‘the record establishes that, in deciding to admit [the evidence], the trial court considered the matters prescribed in Mayfield.’ ” State v. Corbin, 275 Or App 609, 616, 365 P3d 647 (2015) (quoting Borck, 230 Or App at 637, 216 P3d 915 (citation and internal quotation marks omitted)). Appellate courts “examine whether . . . the trial court properly applied the balancing test that OEC 403 prescribes for errors of law[.]” State v. Shaw, 338 Or 586, 614–15, 113 P3d 898 (2005).

State v. Anderson, 282 Or App 24, 27, 386 P3d 154, 156–57 (2016).

In fact, in the main case prior to Anderson, the Court of Appeals reiterated a fairly “flexible” standard to interpret the record in a manner that assumes the trial court did the Mayfield analysis even if not stated on the record.35

In conducting balancing under OEC 403, the court must “make a record which reflects an exercise of discretion.” Id. That is so because we review a trial court’s decision to admit evidence over an OEC 403 objection for an abuse of discretion, State v. Brumwell, 350 Or 93, 107, 249 P3d 965 (2011), cert. den., 565 U.S. ––––, 132 S.Ct. 1028, 181 L.Ed.2d 757 (2012), and we need a sufficient record to conduct meaningful appellate review of that exercise of discretion. See, e.g., State v. Meyers, 132 Or App 585, 589, 889 P2d 374 (1995) (trial court established sufficient record for our review). In that vein, we have explained that Mayfield “is a matter of substance, not form or litany.” State v. Brown, 272 Or App 424, 433, 355 P3d 216, rev. den., 358 Or 145, 363 P3d 1288 (2015). Accordingly, a court’s OEC 403 decision meets the requirements of Mayfield even if a trial court does not expressly follow the Mayfield analysis, as long as the “record establishes that, in deciding to admit [the evidence], the trial court considered the matters prescribed in Mayfield.” State v. Borck, 230 Or App 619, 637, 216 P3d 915, *331 adh’d to as modified on recons, 232 Or App 266, 221 P3d 749 (2009), rev. den., 348 Or 291, 231 P3d 795 (2010). That is, we review whether the “totality of the attendant circumstances indicate that the court . . . engage[d] in the conscious process of balancing the costs of the evidence against its benefits that Mayfield requires.” Id. at 638, 216 P3d 915 (internal quotation marks omitted).

State v. Conrad, 280 Or App 325, 330–31, 381 P3d 880, 884 (2016) (emphasis supplied).

Practice Tip

Over the years, prosecutors and judges have struggled with this test but rarely go step by step through this process. Make them do it, because if you do not do it yourself or make them, you end up with decisions such as Conrad (compare Anderson) where the Court of Appeals affirmed Judge Kohl in Washington County where the entirety of the judge’s ruling was simply “So that motion is . . . denied.”36

To make them do it, you must file a motion requesting the court conduct a 403 analysis, cite the four factors, have your argument ready on each of the four factors and insist that the court make a finding on each of the factors. Otherwise, you may end up with an opinion like Conrad.

Also, some of the appellate decisions referenced in this chapter suggest that they can review the trial court’s decision to determine if there is some way to construe the trial court’s compliance with Mayfield. Do not let the state tell the trial court they can do a minimalist job:

The mere fact that defendant asked the trial court to conduct balancing is not enough to demonstrate that the trial court engaged in a conscious process of OEC 403 balancing. If we were to conclude that the trial court had conducted a conscious balancing process based on the circumstances we are presented with on the record here, the Mayfield requirement would be made meaningless.

Garcia-Rocio, 286 Or App at 145 (emphasis supplied).

IX. Conditional Relevancy

Although some think the idea of conditional relevancy started with Leistiko, the concept has been around for decades.

OEC 104(2) states, “When the relevancy of evidence depends upon the fulfillment of a condition of fact, the court shall admit it upon, or subject to, the introduction of evidence sufficient to support a finding of the fulfillment of the condition.”

When dealing with a matter of conditional relevancy under OEC 104(2), the judge determines whether the foundation evidence is sufficient for the jury to reasonably find that the condition on which relevance depends has been fulfilled.37 If so, the evidence is admitted; if not, the evidence is not admitted. Id. After the judge decides the foundation evidence is sufficient for the jury to reasonably find the contested fact under OEC 104(2), either party may introduce evidence before the jury relevant to the weight and credibility of the evidence. Id. at 210, 808 P2d 1002.

State v. McNeely, 330 Or 457, 462–63, 8 P3d 212, 215 cert denied, 531 U.S. 1055 (2000).

The leading case on conditional relevancy in child sexual abuse cases is State v. Pitt, 352 Or 566, 293 P3d 1002 (2012). In Pitt, the defense moved to exclude the admissibility of other uncharged acts involving the sexual touching of the child by the defendant in another county. The court held that the relevancy of the other act evidence was conditioned upon the state proving that the other touching actually occurred.

X. Conclusion

Balancing the complexities of 404(3), 404(4) and 403 takes some work and sometimes I am reminded of diagramming sentences in my 5th grade English class. One must focus like a laser on exactly what the act is that the state (or you) want to introduce, determine what rationale would—or would not—support its admissibility, make your record, provide an offer of proof, force the court to make specific findings (to paraphrase Tom Petty—Don’t back down), ride the 403 prejudice train and fight like hell. It matters. Due process demands it.
