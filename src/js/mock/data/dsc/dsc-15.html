I. Introduction

This chapter is designed to educate trial counsel on how each issue at trial might arise again in post-trial litigation.

When a court convicts a person of a sex crime, the person often receives a lengthy incarceration term. Due to the heavy consequences of such convictions, trial counsel should always assume that post-trial litigation will continue for years. For that reason, trial counsel best serves the client by thinking about post-trial matters before a conviction or plea. Counsel must ensure that they preserve issues for appellate review and that client justifiably believes that trial counsel provided effective representation (despite the negative result). From when the client first arrives at your office until final resolution at the trial court level, trial counsel must be cognizant of a myriad of issues, including how the record will assist in any post-trial court proceedings (direct appeal, post-conviction relief, and habeas corpus).

Here are some questions that trial counsel should consider during the course of defending a case:

• Is the decision I am making a “tactical decision” or one my client is constitutionally entitled to make after informed advice from me?

• If the decision is the client’s (whether to plead guilty, waive a jury, testify, or appeal), does the client understand the final decision is the client’s to make?

• Have I fully advised my client about the possible ramifications pertaining to any decision the client needs to make?

• Am I doing everything I need to do to effectively represent my client?

• Have I reviewed physical evidence in the state’s possession? If not, why not?

• Have I considered hiring experts? If not, why not?

• Have I reviewed each chapter in this manual and made a conscious decision about whether its subject matter is applicable to my case?

• Are there motions that I should file, even if I do not anticipate they will succeed at the trial level? Have I reviewed the statutes regarding motions for new trial and motions for arrest of judgment? Have I determined whether to file a demurrer that protects my client’s rights to file statutorily available post-trial motions?

• Have I reviewed the latest case law relating to my client’s charges so that I am raising any available innovative challenges?

• Am I making an informed tactical decision or a convenient one? (For example, why haven’t I sent an investigator to speak to Witness A? Why am I not objecting/moving for an immediate mistrial to an objectionable comment made during the state’s opening statement?)

• What efforts have I made to resolve the case through a plea bargain? If my client has directed me not to negotiate, does he appreciate that it is not a decision he can rethink in post-conviction? Does my client understand the role the court and prosecutor play in plea negotiations?

• If the defense loses on “this issue” (whatever it may be), have I created a clear, comprehensible record for appeal?

• If the defense is choosing to let an issue go, is the tactical reason for that decision clear from my notes in the file? Does my client understand why the defense is “keeping mum” about the issue?

• Have discussions that occurred in chambers been placed on the record?

• Have I made an explicit offer of proof?

• If there are alternate bases for admission or exclusion of evidence, have I made it clear that I am relying on all possible bases?

• Is there a state or federal constitutional dimension to an objection that I should raise?

• Am I giving a “fair opportunity” to the prosecution and the trial court to address the specific legal issue, such that it will be deemed preserved for appeal?

• Did I use an exhibit number when referencing an exhibit (objecting to it, or having a witness explain it)? If not, is there a way to clarify the reference for the record at the end of trial?

• Have I compiled a full set of exhibits (both state and defense) for appellate
counsel?

• Are my demonstrative exhibits properly labeled? If I asked multiple witnesses to draw on an exhibit, is it clear on the record which mark belongs to which person? Did I create a copy for my file/appellate counsel?

• Should I file a post-trial motion? Should I seek assistance with that effort?

• Does my client want to appeal? Does he understand the filing deadline for filing a notice of appeal? Should I send this case to the Office of Public Defense Services or refer my client to appellate counsel?

• Is my office’s client file complete? If an attorney needs to understand my thoughts and reasoning five or six years from now, will she be able to do so by reviewing my file? Will I be able to do so by reviewing my file?

If counsel defending a sex case repeatedly asks these questions and otherwise considers how each step will play on appeal (and responds appropriately), trial counsel is likely doing everything she can to “prepare” a case for appeal and to assure her client that she provided him with effective assistance of counsel. This is not to say that these questions should usurp attention from preparing for trial and making every reasonable effort to defend the client. But keeping these questions in mind will strengthen the record and improve the client’s chances of overturning a conviction or sentence.

The purpose of this chapter is not to equip trial counsel to handle the wide array of post-trial matters that can arise following a sex crime trial. Instead, it is designed to provide counsel with an overview of the types of issues that arise post-trial and promote trial counsel to think about the impacts of trial decisions on post-trial litigation. If trial counsel finds herself pursuing post-trial motions, or representing someone on appeal or in post-conviction, trial counsel should complete significant additional research.

II. Trying Your Case While Preparing It for Appeal

A. Preserve the Issues

Oregon Rule of Appellate Procedure 5.45(1) provides that, subject to the exception for plain error review, “[n]o matter claimed as error will be considered on appeal unless the claimed error was preserved in the lower court and is assigned as error in the opening brief[.]” Accordingly, one of trial counsel’s primary obligations at trial is to properly preserve legal errors for appellate review.

The “determination whether a particular issue was preserved for appeal is a ‘practical one’; it will depend on whether the policies behind the preservation requirement—judicial efficiency, full development of the record, and procedural fairness to the parties and the trial court—are met in an individual case.” Charles v. Palomo, 347 Or 695, 700, 227 P3d 737 (2010) (quoting State v. Parkins, 346 Or 333, 340-41, 211 P3d 262 (2009)).

In State v. Rumler, 199 Or App 32, 41, 110 P3d 115 (2005), the Court of Appeals explained the principles in operation:

If the trial judge were sitting here today, would he or she be surprised by your argument? And, if we were to reverse based on your argument, would the trial court feel “blindsided” by our ruling?

Oregon appellate judges who spoke at a 2017 CLE emphasized that they took this “practical” approach seriously. They gave a telling example of its use in practice. Generally, an argument presented to a trial court in writing need not be reiterated orally to qualify as preserved for appeal. See, e.g., State v. Walker, 350 Or 540, 550, 258 P3d 1228 (2011) (en banc) (“This court has never required that each and every argument that has been asserted in writing must be repeated orally in court in order for the argument to be preserved.”). But the appellate judges speaking at the CLE warned that to rely upon that general principle requires an appellant to demonstrate that the writing made the argument clear and requested a specific ruling. The judges noted that often trial memoranda outline the presence of an issue in a case without complete analysis or a requested ruling. Their concern was that a trial judge would assume from such memoranda that defendants were simply alerting the court to something that might come up at a trial. It is important to also request that the trial court rule on your written motion. Without a ruling, the appellate court may hold that there is no legal action for it to consider as error.

Accordingly, tactical decisions in making arguments to the trial court should include considerations of when to make an argument, how to ensure that the state has an opportunity to respond, and how to ensure that the trial court understands the argument and makes a ruling. Beyond those broad preservation principles, research that one does on a substantive legal issue will include specific guidance about how to preserve a similar claim for appeal. What follow are a few generally applicable concepts.

Practice Tip

Justice Michael “Mick” Gillette spoke at the OCDLA Winter Conference in 2010. His presentation, “Mastering the Vagaries of Error Preservation: Understanding the History and Anticipating the Future,” is a must listen for any trial attorney. It outlines the principles in making offers of proof and ensuring that the trial judge lets you make a complete record. Gillette reminds us that trial work can feel like hand-to-hand combat with not only the state but also the trial judge.

1. Raise Every Possible Legal Argument

Most of the time, the only legal argument that an appellate court will consider is the one expressly raised by the defendant at the trial level. See, e.g., [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=2590454681577292491&q=.,+State+v.+Strouse,+276+Or+App+392,++366+P3d+1185+(2016)&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 State v. Strouse], 276 Or App 392, 399, 366 P3d 1185 (2016) (pretrial defendant challenged via a demurrer the substantive conclusion that his charges could be joined pursuant to ORS 132.560(1); on appeal, the defendant claimed that ORS 132.560(2) “require[d] that charging instruments allege a basis for permissive joinder; given the “qualitative difference between his trial and appellate arguments” the matter was unreviewable on appeal); State v. Woods, 284 Or App 559, 563-64, 393 P3d 1188 (2017) (prior uncharged sexual conduct was offered at trial to show defendant’s sexual propensity for the victim; because defendant did not raise an OEC 403 or due process argument to the trial court, the trial court’s failure to conduct OEC 403 balancing would not be considered on appeal).

Ensuring that the appellate court has opportunity to consider the winning legal argument may require trial counsel to advance different legal or evidentiary theories. For example, when it is possible to do so without losing credibility, trial counsel should argue alternative theories of why evidence is admissible or inadmissible, because a correct theory not asserted at trial may be ignored on appeal. See State v. Ambriz-Arguello, 285 Or App 583, 588–89, 397 P3d 547 (2017) (state’s “newly raised theories of admissibility,” which were different than the arguments made for admission of evidence at the trial court level, “cannot provide a basis for affirming the trial court’s ruling”).

As this guide has discussed, trial counsel must articulate arguments and objections clearly and specifically to allow a reviewing court to spot when the issue was presented to the trial court and, thus, preserved for appellate review. See State v. Wyatt, 331 Or 335, 343, 15 P3d 22 (2000) (appellate courts will review an issue as long as trial counsel provided “the trial court with an explanation of his or her objection that [was] specific enough to ensure that the court [could] identify its alleged error with enough clarity to permit it to consider and correct the error immediately, if correction [was] warranted”).

This is especially the case with constitutional arguments, which must be presented to the trial court to be preserved for direct appeal or habeas review. See State v. Warren, 195 Or App 656, 663-64, 98 P3d 1129 (2004) (refusing to consider unpreserved Article I, section 11, argument on appeal where “the trial court was not presented with that issue with sufficient clarity to permit it to consider and correct its error”); State v. Cervantes, 130 Or App 147, 151, 881 P2d 151 (1994) (refusing to consider argument that limiting the cross-examination of victim abrogated defendant’s constitutional right to present a defense where that argument was not raised before the trial court).

2. Be Specific with Offers of Proof

The Court of Appeals has explained:

A party “ordinarily must make an offer of proof as to the content of the excluded evidence” to preserve a challenge to exclusion of that evidence. State v. Olmstead, 310 Or 455, 459-60, 800 P2d 277 (1990); see OEC 103(1)(b) (a party can assign error to a trial court’s excluding of evidence if “the substance of the evidence was made known to the court by offer or was apparent from the context within which questions were asked”). In the absence of an offer of proof, the error can be preserved “if ‘[t]he questions asked and the arguments presented to the court on the issue were adequate to inform the trial court of the substance of the evidence and its error in excluding it.’ ” State v. Lasky, 259 Or App 307, 315, 314 P3d 304 (2013) (quoting Schacher v. Dunne, 109 Or App 607, 610, 820 P2d 865 (1991), rev den, 313 Or 74, 828 P2d 457 (1992)). “The purpose of the rule requiring an offer of proof to preserve a claim of error when a trial court excludes testimony is ‘to assure that appellate courts are able to determine whether it was error to exclude the evidence and whether any error was likely to have affected the result of the case.’ ” State v. Morgan, 251 Or App 99, 105, 284 P3d 496 (2012) (quoting State v. Affeld, 307 Or 125, 128, 764 P2d 220 (1988)).

State v. Clark, 284 Or App 197, 211, 392 P3d 337 (2017).

When an offer of proof contains different types of evidence, counsel should explain the defendant’s theory of admissibility as to each specific piece of evidence. “[W]hen a party offers evidence as a whole and the evidence is rejected by the trial court, the appellate court will affirm the trial court’s ruling if any part of the evidence is inadmissible.” State v. Sarich, 352 Or 601, 618, 291 P3d 647 (2012). This is sometimes called the “Pumpelly rule”: “when a single offer of proof contains admissible and inadmissible evidence, the trial court does not err if it rejects the entire offer.” State v. Tiner, 340 Or 551, 557, 135 P3d 305 (2006), cert den, 549 U.S. 1169 (2007) (citing Pumpelly v. Reeves, 273 Or 808, 812, 543 P2d 682 (1975).

There are occasions in which the actions of the opposing party or the court will demonstrate that an issue is preserved, even if the defendant fails to make the distinction. For instance, in State v. Dye, 286 Or App 626, 636, 403 P3d 243 (2017), the defendant made an offer of proof that “did not initially distinguish between the proffer of generalized testimony on false memory and case-specific testimony.” On appeal, defendant challenged the exclusion of only the generalized testimony, and the state argued the proffer was insufficient under the Pumpelly rule. The Court of Appeals rejected the state’s argument because while the defendant had not made the distinction, the trial court had divided the offer into the appropriate types of evidence, invited argument on the separate types of evidence, and then ruled on each type. Dye, 286 Or App at 636. The Court of Appeals explained that the trial court had “the ability to make the informed ruling that the consideration of an offer of proof requires,” and it deemed the issue preserved. Id.

Practice Tip

As much as possible, divide your offers of proof into separate offers, each offer having a specified piece of evidence or line of questioning, to avoid having the trial court “throw the baby out with the bathwater” by rejecting the entire offer because it contains some inadmissible evidence.

Practice Tip

If you call a witness as part of your offer of proof (which is advisable in most circumstances to ensure preservation), make sure to divide the testimony into specific evidentiary issues if the proffered testimony involves multiple issues (this often arises with expert witnesses). Expressly inform the trial judge when you are done with an issue and moving to the next. That clarity will ensure the judge has opportunity to rule on each and every specific issue with the appropriate legal analysis.

Practice Tip

Bring letter-size manila envelopes to trial and motion hearings that contain written offers of proof or exhibits. If the trial court rejects your evidence, place it in an envelope, seal it and mark it as an exhibit. If your offer of proof is not in writing in advance, ask for a recess and handwrite on a piece of legal paper, make the court review it so they have a chance to change their mind, and then put it in the envelope.1

B. Ensure the Trial Court Record Is Clear, Comprehensible, and Available to Appellate Counsel

There is little need to elaborate on this point. Appellate counsel can only work with the record. The old adage, “what you see is what you get,” applies. If appellate counsel cannot understand the record and discern the distinct legal issues that might be available, viable
issues may be lost:

  • If there is an issue that arose during jury selection, it is critically important
    that you explicitly alert appellate counsel to that issue. Often voir dire is not transcribed unless specifically requested.

  • Make sure exhibits are entered into the record.

  • Whenever you offer or refer to an exhibit, make sure you specifically and
    clearly state the exhibit number.

  • Properly label all marks made on exhibits by witnesses.

  • When someone gestures to an exhibit or person, be sure to clarify for the re-
    cord to what or whom the person is referring.

  • At the end of trial, review the clerk’s notation about what exhibits were marked
    as admitted, excluded, withdrawn, etc.; correct any errors.

  • Make a complete set of the trial exhibits (state and defense) to be shared with appellate counsel.

  • Extra credit is awarded to the trial attorney who can end each day of trial
    making notes about the potential appellate issues that arose that day.

  • If there is an audio recording played at trial, provide a copy of it and the
    transcript you were using for the recording to appellate counsel.

Practice Tip

Bring a variety of colored sharpies to trial if you intend to have witnesses mark on the exhibits. Give each witness a different color to use if multiple witnesses are marking on the same exhibit, and then conspicuously note on the exhibit the color that applies to each witness.

C. Carefully Consider Appellate Issues if Proceeding with a Bench Trial

There is a tendency in a bench trial for the parties and the court to adopt a more short-
hand style. This can create major difficulties for appellate counsel. The difficulties compound when the issues are sensitive or complex. The perfect storm of incoherence is often found in a bench trial relating to child pornography that turns on the testimony of competing forensic experts. Trial counsel must remain diligent in those contexts to make a coherent record.

Even more importantly, counsel must not lose sight of the need to preserve issues for appeal and strategies to avoid a trial error being dismissed as harmless.

Appellate courts may engage in some effort to translate arguments made in a bench trial to the more traditional avenues of preservation. For example, the Court of Appeals has held that “[i]n a bench trial, if the defendant ‘clearly raises’ the issue of the sufficiency of the evidence in closing argument, the contention is evaluated on appeal as if the defendant had made a motion for judgment of acquittal.” State v. Gonzalez-Valenzuela, 258 Or App 263, 265, 308 P3d 1096 (2013), rev’d, 358 Or 451 (2015) (quoting State v. Forrester, 203 Or App 151, 155, 125 P3d 47 (2005), rev den, 341 Or 141, 139 P3d 259 (2006)). The Court of Appeals has also held that a defendant’s argument that the state must prove certain elements (as opposed to its failure to present sufficient evidence) is the equivalent of challenging jury instructions. State v. Schodrow, 187 Or App 224, 231, 66 P3d 547 (2003). However, trial counsel should not rely on an appellate court’s willingness to act. A defendant is far better served by trial counsel who proactively considers how she would have presented the issue at a jury trial and makes efforts to similarly preserve the issue at a bench trial.

III. Post-Trial Motions

There are two types of post-trial (or post-verdict) motions: motion in arrest of judgment, ORS 136.500, and motion for a new trial, ORS 136.535. There are numerous procedural nuances to filing these motions, most of which are beyond the scope of this overview. The Oregon State Bar criminal law manual, which was most recently published in 2013, provides a comprehensive discussion of post-trial motions. That reference is available online for free to Oregon State Bar members. See also chapter 18 in OCDLA’s Trial Notebook. Trial counsel should consider filing both in every case with an adverse jury verdict.

A. Motion for Arrest of Judgment

A motion for arrest of judgment is a challenge to the sufficiency of the indictment. It
can be based only on one of two types of claims:

(1) a claim that “the grand jury by which it was found had no legal authority to inquire into the crime charged because the same is not triable within the county,” ORS 135.630(1); or

(2) a claim that the “facts stated do not constitute an offense,” ORS 135.630(4); See ORS 136.500.

Claims alleging that the facts stated do not constitute an offense include challenges to the underlying criminal statute on vagueness grounds. State v. McKenzie, 307 Or 554, 560, 771 P2d 264 (1989). And they include challenges that the charging instrument failed to allege an element of the offense. State v. Touchstone, 188 Or App 45, 48, 71 P3d 536 (2003).

From an appellate perspective, trial counsel ideally will have raised any claim made in a motion for arrest of judgment in a pretrial demurrer to the indictment. That is true because a demurrer has a more favorable standard of review than a motion for an arrest of judgment. And the Court of Appeals has explained:

When the sufficiency of an indictment is challenged by way of a demurrer, it is strictly construed against the state, but when the sufficiency of an indictment is challenged after a verdict, the language of the indictment is held to a less exacting standard and is given a “more liberal construction.” State of Oregon v. Monk, 193 Or 450, 457, 238 P2d 1110 (1951). When the language of an indictment is challenged after the verdict, the challenge will only be successful if the indictment wholly fails to state “any crime known to the law. State v. Brewer, 7 Or App 158, 160-61, 490 P2d 202 (1971) (quoting Monk, 193 Or at 456-57, 238 P2d 1110).”

State v. Burns, 213 Or App 38, 43, 159 P3d 1208 (2007), rev dismissed as improvidently granted, 345 Or 302 (2008).

If one prevails on a motion in arrest of judgment, the client is back in the same procedural posture as before the indictment was returned. However, there are a few caveats. First, ORS 136.515:

If, from the evidence given on the trial, there is reasonable ground to believe the defendant guilty and a new accusatory instrument can be framed upon which the defendant may be convicted, the court shall order the defendant to be recommitted to custody or released and to answer the new accusatory instrument, if one is found; and if the evidence shows the defendant to be guilty of another offense than that charged in the accusatory instrument, the defendant shall in like manner be committed or held thereon. In neither case is the verdict a bar to another action for the same crime.

Second, as no jeopardy attaches if a trial court grants a motion for arrest of judgment, the state is not precluded from returning an indictment alleging more serious offenses when it reindicts. State v. Nichols, 236 Or 521, 528, 38 P2d 739 (1964).

On the upside, ORS 136.525:

If the evidence appears insufficient to charge the defendant with any offense, the defendant shall, if in custody, be discharged or, if the defendant has been released or deposited money in lieu thereof, the release agreement of the defendant is exonerated or the money of the defendant shall be refunded to the defendant; and in such case, the arrest of judgment operates as an acquittal of the charge upon which the accusatory instrument was founded.

B. Motion for a New Trial

If a court grants a motion for a new trial, it will vacate the judgment and set a new trial date. ORCP 64B (made applicable to criminal cases through ORS 136.535) sets out the grounds for a motion for new trial:

Rule 64 B Jury trial; grounds for new trial. A former judgment may be set aside and a new trial granted in an action where there has been a trial by jury on the motion of the party aggrieved for any of the following causes materially affecting the substantial rights of such party:

B(1) Irregularity in the proceedings of the court, jury or adverse party, or any order of the court, or abuse of discretion, by which such party was prevented from having fair trial.

B(2) Misconduct of the jury or prevailing party.

B(3) Accident or surprise which ordinary prudence could not have guarded against.

B(4) Newly discovered evidence, material for the party making the application, which such party could not with reasonable diligence have discovered and produced at the trial.

B(5) Insufficiency of the evidence to justify the verdict or other decision, or that it is against law.

B(6) Error in law occurring at the trial and objected to or excepted to by the party making the application.

Very specific rules apply to each type of motion for a new trial. Again, refer to Chapter 21 of the Oregon State Bar criminal law manual. For motions filed pursuant to ORCP 64 B(1) to (4), in addition to articulating the specific grounds of the motion counsel must also file supporting affidavits or declarations. ORCP 64 D.

Counsel cannot file a motion for a new trial after a bench trial. ORS 136.535; State v. Stewart, 239 Or App 217, 221, 244 P3d 816 (2010). In Stewart, the Court of Appeals held that the defendant knowingly and voluntarily made his jury waiver even though counsel had failed to advise him that his waiver included a waiver of the right to file a motion for a new trial. Best practice is for trial counsel to advise clients about that reality when clients are considering whether to waive their rights to a jury trial.

While motions for a new trial are not always applicable, they deserve trial counsel’s consideration in every case.

C. Post-Verdict Timing

For the most part, timing requirements for a motion for arrest of judgment and a motion for a new trial are the same. ORCP 64 F governs both. ORS 136.500; ORS 136.535(2). Counsel must file such motions within “10 days after the entry of the judgment sought to be set aside, or such further time as the court may allow.” ORCP 64 F; ORS 136.535(2).2 If more time is sought, the trial court must grant the motion within the statutory filing period. State v. Provonsha, 107 Or App 571, 573-74, 813 P2d 563 (1991).

Counsel should think tactically about whether to ask a trial court for additional time within which to file a post-verdict motion. Moreover, a trial court may not grant additional time to file the motion. Because the timeline for filing these types of motions is so tight, counsel will ideally think through the relevant issues before scheduling defendant’s sentencing hearing. This ensures that counsel has the largest window of opportunity possible to file any post-verdict motions.

Whether it is a motion for a new trial or a motion for arrest of judgment, the trial court must determine the motion within 55 days after entry of the judgment or the motion is deemed denied. ORCP 64 F; [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=5623951772321045788&q=McCollum+v.+Kmart+Corp.,+347+Or+707,&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 McCollum v. Kmart Corp.], 347 Or 707, 713, 226 P3d 703 (2010). The trial court’s decision is not effective unless the court enters a written order before the 55-day period lapses. McCollum, 347 Or at 713. Counsel should coordinate and follow-up with the trial court on post-verdict motions and ensure that the clerk’s office enters the written orders before the 55-day period lapses.

IV. Preparing a Case for State Post-Conviction and Federal Habeas Corpus

Trial counsel’s task is to make every effort possible to provide his or her client with effective assistance, and trial counsel would never knowingly render ineffective assistance to a client. Therefore, the title to this section is somewhat illogical—a diligent lawyer attempting to provide effective assistance to a client at trial cannot really “prepare” a case for post-conviction or habeas review.

The sad fact is that ineffective assistance occurs, however, and it is helpful for trial counsel to be aware of some of the basics of post-conviction relief. When trial counsel learns of her deficiencies, the most effective means of assisting the client is to be open and honest about mistakes and errors and to take immediate steps to rectify them. Consider the following points in navigating this
broad subject.

It is not possible to deal with all of the issues that could arise in a PCR claim. Thus, a full discussion on post-conviction issues is best left to the PCR-focused materials offered by OCDLA. However, trial counsel should be aware of some of the basics of post-conviction relief. Consider the following points in navigating this broad subject.

A. General Principles of Post-Conviction

In a post-conviction case the petitioner cannot simply relitigate. Rather, the question is whether the petitioner’s state or federal constitutional rights were violated in the proceedings in which her conviction was secured and affirmed. If so, was the petitioner prejudiced by
the violation?

Under ORS 138.530(1)(a), a petitioner is entitled to post-conviction relief when she establishes “[a] substantial denial in the proceedings resulting in petitioner’s conviction . . . of petitioner’s rights under the Constitution of the United States, or under the Constitution of the State of Oregon, or both, and which denial rendered the conviction void.” Krummacher v. Gierloff, 290 Or 867, 627 P2d 458 (1981); Trujillo v. Maass, 312 Or 431, 435, 822 P2d 703 (1991); Stevens v. State, 322 Or 101, 902 P2d 1137 (1995); Strickland v. Washington, 466 U.S. 668 (1984).

Similarly, under 18 U.S.C. § 2254, the federal post-conviction statute, a state court petitioner is entitled to post-conviction relief from a federal court when they establish a prejudicial federal constitutional violation3 and prove that the state courts had a full and fair opportunity to vindicate the petitioner’s rights and unreasonably failed to do so.4 The deference federal courts are obliged to give to state courts in this context is extraordinarily high. Virginia v. LeBlanc, 582 U.S. __, 137 S. Ct. 1726, 1728 (2017) (A “litigant must ‘show that the state court’s ruling . . . was so lacking in justification that there was an error well understood and comprehended in existing law beyond any possibility for fair-minded disagreement.’” (citations omitted)).

PCR claims are generally limited to those the defense could not have reasonably raised before the trial court. See Palmer v. State, 318 Or 352, 358, 867 P2d 1368 (1994). Counsel must raise any constitutional claim before the criminal trial court, and then appellate counsel must again raise it on direct appeal. If that occurs, Oregon appellate courts will have a full and fair opportunity to consider any constitutional claims whether they are brought pursuant to the Oregon or the United States constitutions. Having granted Oregon appellate courts a full and fair opportunity to consider a federal constitutional claim on direct appeal, a petitioner can then (generally speaking) raise that federal constitutional claim in a timely-filed petition for federal habeas corpus relief pursuant to 28 U.S.C. § 2254.

Virtually every post-conviction case relies on ineffective assistance of counsel allegations, and because of that reality, the basic standards for ineffective assistance are outlined below. However, counsel should be aware that PCR practice is not limited to IAC claims. See Urban v. Cupp, 33 Or App 453, 576 P2d 841 (1978) (PCR court addressed the merits of Brady claim); Lyons v. Pearce, 298 Or 554, 556, 694 P2d 969, 971 (1985) (voluntariness of guilty plea, in addition to IAC, addressed in post-conviction); Stone v. Cupp, 39 Or App 473, 592 P2d 1044 (1979) (prosecution’s breach of plea agreement addressed in post-conviction); Syphers v. Gladden, 230 Or 148, 156, 368 P2d 942, 946 (1962) (claim that accused tried while incompetent cognizable in post-conviction).

B. Standards for Ineffective Assistance of Counsel

1. Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution

The Court of Appeals has summarized the standard for finding ineffective assistance of counsel under Article I, section 11, as follows:

In evaluating whether a defendant’s lawyer has rendered inadequate assistance under the Oregon Constitution, our analysis ordinarily proceeds in two steps: “First, we must determine whether petitioner demonstrated by a preponderance of the evidence that [his lawyer] failed to exercise reasonable professional skill and judgment. Second, if we conclude that petitioner met that burden, we further must determine whether he proved that counsel’s failure had a tendency to affect the result of his trial.” [Montez v. Czerniak, 355 Or 1, 7, 322 P3d 487 (2014)] (quoting Lichau v. Baldwin, 333 Or 350, 359, 39 P3d 851 (2002) (internal citations omitted).

Wade v. Brockamp, 268 Or App 373, 379, 342 P3d 142 (2015).

The Court of Appeals has also framed it this way:

We will not “second-guess a lawyer’s tactical decisions in the name of the constitution unless those decisions reflect an absence or suspension of professional skill and judgment.” Gorham v. Thompson, 332 Or 560, 567, 34 P3d 161 (2001). As has often been noted “[t]he constitution gives no defendant the right to a perfect defense-seldom does a lawyer walk away from a trial without thinking of something that might have been done differently or that he would have preferred to have avoided.” Krummacher [v. Gierloff], 290 Or [867, 875, 627 P2d 458 (1984)].

Thompson v. Belleque, 268 Or App 1, 7, 341 P3d 911, 916 (2014), rev den, 357 Or 300 (2015). That court further explained:

The exercise of reasonable professional skill and judgment generally requires an investigation that is “legally and factually appropriate to the nature and complexity of the case so that the lawyer is equipped to advise and represent the client in an informed manner.” [Stevens, 322 Or at 108].

Lotches v. Premo, 257 Or App 513, 517, 306 P3d 768, rev den, 354 Or 597 (2013). Finally, the court also observed:

Thus, “tactical decisions made in the course of preparing for trial must involve ‘a conscious choice by a lawyer either to take or to omit some action on the basis of an evaluation of the nature and complexity of the case, the likely costs and potential benefits of the contemplated action, and other factors.’ ” [Lichau, 333 Or at 360] (quoting Stevens, 322 Or at 109).

Thompson, 268 Or App at 927.

2. The Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The United States Supreme Court articulated the standard for effective assistance of counsel under the Sixth Amendment in Strickland v. Washington, 466 US 668 (1984). To prevail on an ineffective assistance of counsel claim under the federal constitution, the petitioner must establish two points:

First, the defendant must show that counsel’s performance was deficient. This requires showing that counsel made errors so serious that counsel was not functioning as the “counsel” guaranteed the defendant by the Sixth Amendment. Second, the defendant must show that the deficient performance prejudiced the defense. This requires showing that counsel’s errors were so serious as to deprive the defendant of a fair trial, a trial whose result is reliable.

Strickland, 466 US at 687. A PCR court will only excuse trial counsel’s failure to complete an investigation if counsel made a “reasonable decision that makes particular investigations unnecessary.” Id. at 691.

The United States Supreme Court has further explained:

To establish ineffectiveness, a “defendant must show that counsel’s representation fell below an objective standard of reasonableness.” [Strickland, 466 US] at 688. To establish prejudice he “must show that there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s unprofessional errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different. A reasonable probability is a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome.” [Id.] at 694.

Williams v. Taylor, 529 US 362, 390-91 (2000); see also United States v. Kwan, 407 F 3d 1005, 1017 (9th Cir 2005) (same). To demonstrate prejudice, “a defendant need not show that counsel’s deficient conduct more likely than not altered the outcome in the case.” Strickland, 466 US at 693.

Practice Tip

Tactical Decision versus Trial Court Rulings—If you realize that you made a critical mistake while handling a case before the trial court, or you believe that the trial court’s rulings force you into ineffectiveness, counsel should contact the Professional Liability Fund. Under the appropriate circumstances, the PLF will immediately appoint highly qualified (and respected) “repair counsel.” Often, repair counsel can get the case back on track, and, if the mistake warrants trial counsel stepping off the case, it can help counsel accomplish that withdrawal. Oregon attorneys pay dearly for this resource and should not hesitate to use it when circumstances warrant it.

V. Preparing Your File for Post-Trial Litigation

In the rush to close a file following a case’s conclusion, most attorneys spend little time thinking about file maintenance and its impact on an appeal or post-conviction proceedings. Sometimes you are dealing with the loss, trying to assist your client and the client’s family and loved ones in dealing with a conviction. However, just as an appellate court must be able to identify that you adequately argued a legal issue to consider it, appellate and post-conviction counsel must be able to understand the trial court record to successfully handle the case on appeal or PCR.

Trial counsel can maximize a client’s chances for meaningful appellate or post-conviction relief by ensuring that a client’s file is complete, well organized, and accessible. Counsel might keep significant portions of a client’s file on pieces of paper organized in manila folders, redwells, and three-ring binders. And counsel likely maintains a significant portion of a client’s file solely in digital form, possibly saved on multiple electronic devices. Trial counsel should take time at the close of each case to assess what it is that constitutes that client’s “file.”

For example, is all of the discovery on a CD/DVD, in a Dropbox folder, or on an external hard drive? Are those files saved and preserved in a manner accessible to post-conviction counsel? Will it be lost if they are not moved to another location—that is, if I let my Dropbox membership expire in ten years, will a post-conviction attorney be able to get a copy of what is needed? Are attorney notes on paper, in a Word document, or in a cloud-based, note-taking application like Evernote? If trial counsel prepared PowerPoint presentations, timelines, or videos, are those materials saved in a way that is available for post-conviction review? While trial counsel is not expected to print thousands of pages to save in a paper file, she should save an entire copy of the client’s “file” for the client’s benefit.

To the extent trial counsel has communicated with prosecutors, expert witnesses, lay witnesses, and other people involved in the case by email, counsel should organize and save those emails—perhaps saved on a law firm’s server or converted to PDFs and burned onto a disk or saved onto a thumb drive and placed in the “paper” file. See RPC 1.15-1(d), RPC 1.16(d), and OSB Formal Ethics Opinion No 2017-192 (treating email correspondence relating to the substance of a client’s case as part of the client file that must be provided to the client upon request). Trial counsel should also attempt to preserve any correspondence or communication conducted via text messages or other messaging services. For example, if trial counsel and client exchanged text messages or Facebook messages regarding the case, taking a screen shot of the text messages, sending the image files to an email address, and saving the images to the client’s folder might assist post-conviction counsel later. Trial counsel should also ask investigators, paralegals, and any other trial team members for copies of all correspondence—by paper, email, text, or other means—and place those in the file as well.

Every attorney has her own system of organization, and no single method of preserving a complete file will work for everyone. However, effective assistance of counsel includes effective preservation of case materials. For some limited guidance, a sample “Closing a Case” checklist is provided in Appendix A.

VI. Cooperating with PCR Counsel

There is little that can be said that Dennis Balske has not already said more concisely and eloquently. That is particularly true on the issue of cooperation with post-conviction relief counsel. See Dennis Balske, “What Should You Do When PCR Comes Calling?”, The Oregon Defense Attorney, Vol. XXXVII, No. 3 at 24 (June/July 2016) (Appendix B). In a nutshell, Balske advises to check your ego at the door, admit your mistakes, be an open book with PCR counsel, and do whatever is needed to benefit your clients and provide effective assistance, even if you are doing it after the fact. See also Siegel, David M., “The Role of Trial Counsel in Ineffective Assistance of Counsel Claims: Three Questions to Keep in Mind,” The Champion, at 14 (Feb 2009); Siegel, David M., “What (Can) (Should) (Must) Defense Counsel Withhold from the Prosecution in Ineffective Assistance of Counsel Proceedings?” The Champion, at 18 (Dec 2011).

VII. Conclusion

Your work as trial counsel plays a role far beyond the trial. It affects the viability of the direct appeals, post-conviction relief, and habeas corpus claims. It is important to maintain a vision for how your arguments to the trial court will play out in future appeals. Make your case with two goals in mind—winning at trial and winning on appeal.
