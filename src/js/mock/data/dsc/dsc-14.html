Defending Sex Cases

Defending Sex Cases: Strategies from Allegation to Resolution, 2018 edition

Chapter 14. The Art of Cross-Examination

By Shaun S. McCrea

Introduction

Cross-examination is a lawyer’s opportunity to testify—by putting words in the mouth of the adverse witness. It is an opportunity to forward the defense case theory—or completely destroy it. Care is advised.

I. The Law

Cross-examination is governed by Oregon Rule of Evidence (OEC) 611(2), the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution and Article 1, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution.

OEC 611(2) provides:

[Scope of cross-examination.] Cross-examination should be limited to the subject matter of the direct examination and matters affecting the credibility of the witness. The court may, in the exercise of discretion, permit inquiry into additional matters as if on direct examination.

A defendant has the right to confront a witness at trial under the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution and Article 1, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution. Davis v. Alaska, 415 US 308, 315, 94 S Ct 1105, 39 L Ed 2d 347 (1974), held this right to confrontation “means more than being allowed to confront the witness physically.” It includes the right to engage in effective cross-examination. “Effective cross-examination” means the right to explore possible bias, interest or motive of a witness and to impeach the witness on non-collateral matters. See: State v. Herrera, 286 Or 349, 353, 594 P2d 823 (1979); State v. Jalo, 27 Or App 845, 557 P2d 1359 (1976), rev den (1977) (confrontation allowed in cases involving evidence of prior sexual conduct); and State v. Morgan, 66 Or App 675, 675 P2d 513 (1984) (confrontation allowed to explore possible bias of complainant when sex crimes were alleged).

The right to confrontation through cross-examination has some limits, however:

“[T]rial judges retain wide latitude insofar as the Confrontation Clause is concerned to impose reasonable limits on such cross-examination based on concerns about, among other things harassment, prejudice, confusion of the issues, the witness’ safety or interrogation that is repetitive or only marginally relevant.”

Delaware v. Van Arsdall, 475 US 673, 106 S Ct 1431, 89 L Ed 2d 674 (1986).

See also: Oregon Rule of Evidence 403, a trial court may limit cross-examination for impeachment where the probative value of the evidence is outweighed by the risk of prejudice, confusion embarrassment or delay. See State v. LeClair, 83 Or App 121, 730 P2d 609 (1986).

II. Why Cross-Examine?

A defense attorney cross-examines to show:

1) the witness is wrong or left out information, or

2) the witness is not believable or

3) the witness is both wrong and not believable.

The goals of cross-examination are to:

1) Obtain favorable testimony.

2) Neutralize damaging testimony from direct examination either by

• undermining or limiting the “bad” testimony, or

• challenging the witness’s facts or conclusions.

3) Challenge the witness’s credibility:

• Through impeachment

  • Lack of ability to perceive and/or remember1
  • Character for untruthfulness2
  • Prior criminal conviction3
  • Show bias or interest4
  • paid witness (e.g., expert)
  • professional bias (e.g., police officer, child services worker)
  • personal bias (e.g., dislike of defendant, desire to help someone else)
  • Prior inconsistent statements5
  • Contradiction
  • Experts by use of learned treatise.6

III. Impeachment Generally

Impeachment calls into question a witness’s veracity. Impeachment is used to “attack or discredit the witness and to attack the jury’s belief in his or her testimony.” State v. Gilbert, 282 Or 309, 311, 577 P2d 939 (1978).

A witness may be impeached for bias,7 interest,8 inability to observe,9 failure to remember,10 ineptitude,11 uncertainty,12 inability to articulate,13 mistake,14 untruthfulness,15 criminal conviction,16 lying or prior inconsistent statement,17 or by contradiction.

A witness may not be impeached based on the witness’s religious beliefs or opinions18 or by extrinsic evidence or testimony of prior bad acts of the witness even to show the witness is untruthful.19 An exception to this rule is proof of prior false accusations. Prior false accusations
by a complainant may be impeached under the confrontation clause of Article 1, section 11,
of the Oregon Constitution despite the OEC 608(2) prohibition if certain conditions are met.

Practice Tip

Be cognizant that impeachment is limited to challenging a witness’s veracity or credibility. Impeachment—by itself—is not substantive evidence. Stated another way, impeachment evidence shows only that the witness lacks credibility (should not be believed), while substantive evidence proves facts in dispute. Impeachment evidence takes away, substantive evidence adds to the case.

However, in some circumstances evidence may be admitted as both impeachment and substantive evidence if there is an independent basis for admission of the impeaching evidence. For example, in State v. Bond, 283 Or App 101, 106, 388 P3d 391 (2016) the defendant unsuccessfully attempted to show complainant’s interactions with defendant were part of a “pattern” or “lack of accident or mistake.” If the court had accepted this evidence it would have been admitted as both impeachment and substantive evidence. Also, where a prior inconsistent statement is made under oath under penalty of perjury, that statement is not hearsay and can be admitted for impeachment and as substantive evidence. See:
OEC 801(4)(a)(A).

A. Impeachment by Prior False Accusations

An exception to the prohibition against evidence of a witness’s prior bad acts under OEC 608(2) is allowed regarding prior false accusations.

Evidence of prior false accusations by a complainant may be probative on the issue of credibility. State v. LeClair, 83 Or App 121,130, 730 P2d 609, rev den 303 Or 74, 734 P2d 354 (1986). If a complainant has recanted prior accusations or they are otherwise demonstrably false, the trial court must allow the defendant to cross-examine the
complainant about them. Id.

The standard is whether at least one prong of a three-part test (set out in LeClair) is met:

1) the complainant recanted the accusations

2) the defendant demonstrates to the court that the prior accusations were
false, or

3) there is some evidence that the complainant has made prior accusations that
were false.

This rule is subject to OEC 403 balancing; if the trial court finds that the probative value of what the defendant wants on cross-examination (including the probability that false accusations were in fact made) is substantially outweighed by the risk of prejudice, confusion, embarrassment or delay, the court may deny defense cross-examination on prior accusations. Id at 130.

Recantation means “the unequivocal public withdrawal of an allegation.” State v. Wonderling, 104 Or App 204, 799 P2d 1135 (1990). See also: State v. Rose, 278 Or App 551, 377 P3d 613 (2016) (grandmother’s testimony about prior accusations was equivocal; evidence excluded).

This intended impeachment should be brought to the trial court’s attention outside the presence of the jury. If possible, defense counsel should try to raise all three grounds of the LeClair test for the court’s consideration and seek to question the complainant outside the presence of the jury.

If the trial court excludes the testimony, the defense should be prepared to make an offer of proof. An offer of proof ensures “that appellate courts are able to determine whether it was error to exclude the evidence and whether any error is likely to have affected the result of the case.” State v. Jenkins, 246 Or 280, 281, 424 P2d 894 (1967).” State v. Affeld, 307 Or 125, 128, 764 P2d 220 (1988). An offer of proof does not have to be in any special form but more is better.

Defense counsel has several options as to how to make an offer of proof:

1) Tell the judge what proposed evidence is expected during the cross-examination of the complainant, including what specific named witnesses would testify and what they would say. This is sufficient. State v. McClure, 298 Or 336, 341, 692 P2d 579 (1984), State v. Nelson, 246 Or App 91, 98, 265 P3d 8, 12-13 (2011).

Practice Tip

But be sure you do this on the record, not at a side bar at the bench or in chambers. If you are cut off, ask to finish your offer of proof at the next recess or end of the day.

2) Call the complainant and question the complainant on the record outside the
presence of the jury.

3) Call other witnesses to testify on the record outside the presence of the jury.

4) Be aware and have a response for prosecutorial objections regarding OEC 403 issues (risk of prejudice, confusion, embarrassment or delay). Also, once the offer of proof is made, insist that you have demonstrated that the defense has met at least one prong
of the LeClair test and ask the court to engage in OEC 403 balancing with regard to the offer of proof and the putative impeachment so the record is complete.

The LeClair exception to prior bad acts under OEC 608(2) has traditionally been applied only to prior false sex abuse claims. Recent appellate court decisions suggest the LeClair exception may be extended beyond prior false sex abuse claims. See: State v. Taylor, 275 Or App 962, 965-67, 365 P3d 1149 rev den 360 Or 26, 381 P3d 822 (2016) (allegations of domestic abuse); State v. Bond, 283 Or App 101, 388 P3d 391 (2016) (domestic restraining order).

B. Bias

Bias is a form of impeachment. Showing a witness is biased is a powerful tool. OEC 401 requires laying a foundation of relevance to cross-examine a witness on bias. This is a “low bar.” State v. Valle, 255 Or App 805, 810-811 298 P3d 1237 (2013). Defense counsel need only show that the evidence is “relevant,” that is, having some tendency to make the existence of any fact of consequence to the action’s determination more or less probable than it would be without the evidence. Id. at 810-811. Evidence of bias is relevant if it has a “mere tendency to show bias or interest of the witness.” State v. Hubbard, 297 Or 789, 796, 688 P2d 1311 (1984), quoted with approval in State v. Tyon, 226 Or App 428, 204 P3d 106 (2009) (trial court erred in excluding evidence of arresting officer’s bias against defendant in DUII case).

Bias may arise in a number of ways: e.g., through the witness’s connection to another person romantically, sexually or through a business relationship. Oregon courts have held a witness may be impeached for bias through evidence of a relationship with another, where bias resulting from that relationship is a matter of reasonable inference rather than mere speculation. State v. Prange, 247 Or App 254, 260-261, 268 P3d 749 (2011). See also: State v. Del Real-Galvez, 270 Or App 224, 346 P3d 1289 (2015).

Bias is never collateral. You can always cross-examine a witness on bias. A collateral issue is a matter that does not tend to prove or disprove a material proposition in the case. Conversely, a non-collateral issue is a matter that tends to prove or disprove a material proposition in the case. Bias goes to the heart of a witness’s credibility and self-interest. No matter what a witness says, no matter how true, if the jury does not believe the witness (credibility) or believes the witness to be biased, the witness’s testimony is worthless.

Recently, the Oregon Court of Appeals found a trial court erred in failing to permit the defense to introduce evidence of a defendant’s acquittal in a prior sex abuse case to show: 1) bias on the part of two allegedly unrelated complainants and 2) since the earlier acquittal defendant had taken special precautions when he was around children. State v. Lulay, 290 Or App 282, __ P3d __ (2018). The trial court prevented the defendant from making “even an initial showing of bias.” The trial court found the evidence of the prior (2008) case was “collateral,…confusing, [and] really a side issue.” The defense made an offer of proof, which the appellate court found sufficient:

See Peeples, 345 Or at 219; Shields v. Campbell, 277 Or 71, 77, 559 P2d 1275 (1977) (“A party owes the trial court the obligation of a sound, clear, and articulate motion, objection, or exception, so as to permit the trial judge a chance to consider the legal contention or to correct an error already made.”). Defendant’s offer also permitted the state, as the opposing party, to consider and respond to the contention that knowledge of the 2008 case might affect how certain witnesses view defendant and color their testimony accordingly. See Peeples, 345 Or at 220.

The Oregon Court of Appeals said that a trial court’s discretionary authority under OEC 403—when considering evidence of bias—only applies once the proponent of the evidence has first had the chance to “present sufficient facts from which the factfinder may infer bias.” Muldrew, 229 Or App at 227 (citing Hubbard, 297 Or at 800). The trial court in Lulay did not give defendant that chance. The case was reversed and remanded.

The lesson from this case is to pursue bias and make a record: an offer of proof and possibly follow up offers of proof as the case proceeds.

C. Inability to Perceive, Remember, Relate

Impeachment based on inability to perceive, remember or relate may be appropriate because of the age of the child witness, because of use of medication or controlled substances, mental limitations, age incapacity or witness uncertainty whether an act occurred or it was a dream.

A witness’s ability to perceive, remember or relate under OEC 601 or 602 should be challenged by pretrial motion if there is any question about it. A witness may not be impeached solely because the witness uses intoxicants generally, State v. Batchelor, 282 Or App 47, 50, 578 P2d 409 (1978), but a witness may be impeached if he or she was using intoxicants at the time in question, State v. McKiel, 122 Or 504, 259 P 917 (1927); see Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence, 6th Ed. Section 601.03 (8). Also, if the witness is a long-time user of intoxicants, and drugs or alcohol has affected the witness’s ability to remember or recall, impeachment may be appropriate. State v. Goodin, 8 Or App 15, 492 P2d 287 (1971).

IV. Strategies for Conducting Cross-Examination

A. Have a Theory of the Case

First and foremost, know your theory of the case; have a theory of the case (see Chapter 3). For example, if your case involves two young adults, your theory may be that any sexual contact was consensual. Complainant now contends she was raped. Your theme of the case may be “buyer’s remorse.” The defense narrative is that the complainant agreed to sex
(i.e., consented) but after the fact now regrets it and claims she did not consent, now
claiming a crime was committed against her. Your theory of the case and your themes will dictate how you approach a witness, especially the complainant on cross-examination.

Practice Tip

Before cross-examination of a complainant in a sex case it is critical to analyze why the person is making a false claim. You must delve under the surface to find “the story beneath the story.” Why is the complainant accusing the defendant of a crime: what is her motivation? Juries in sex cases need a reason to disbelieve or doubt the alleged victim. They will rarely accept that the prosecution has failed to prove the case beyond a reasonable doubt and acquit based on the presumption of innocence. Stacking the evidence against the law will not win the case.

A jury in a sex case needs a reason to doubt the complainant’s claim—especially if the complainant appears to truly believe—the complainant’s story. You must provide the missing link, the motivation for the false claim. Note: there is a difference between asking the jury to doubt the complainant’s story versus doubting the complainant. Most times you want to discount the story but not necessarily vilify the complainant. You can suggest the claimant is mistaken. She feels guilty. She is suffering from “buyer’s remorse” and tell the jury why. The jury may like the complainant. They can like her and still not believe her story and acquit your client. But, if you seek to crucify the complainant they may choose to dislike you, ignore the logic of the defense and convict your client. Better advocacy may be to have a defense explanation for why the story is wrong, misguided, and untrue without painting the complainant as a liar.

B. End With a Bang

Second, if you are going to cross-examine a witness at all, always have one “slam dunk” point for your final question. Then, no matter how much the witness goes sideways or the cross-examination is a disaster, you can end on a high note. The very best scenario is to start strong and end with a bang. You thus impress the jury with key defense points (that you will use in closing) at the beginning and end of your cross-examination. This is known as “primacy and recency,” meaning the jury is more likely to remember the first thing they hear and the last thing they hear. Of course, cross-examination is an art, and your approach will depend on your theory of the case and the sensibilities of the particular witness. Sometimes you may choose to open softly (e.g., with a younger child witness) to establish rapport with that witness before seeking to score defense points for closing.

C. Don’t Be Boring

Third, don’t be boring. Do not, do not, do not have the witness repeat what she said during direct examination. This is not looping. (Looping is when you get a good answer out of a witness and you state that answer again as the preface to your next question.) It is better to ask no questions on cross-examination then to simply rehash the direct examination and imprint it further into the jurors’ minds.

V. General Rules for Cross-Examination

The following rules will be more fully explored below:

• Ask only leading questions.

• One fact per question.

• Ask short questions.

• Use plain, simple language.

• Know the answer.

• Be able to prove up the answer.

• Listen and loop.

• Look at the witness, not your notes.

• Ask about facts and evidence, not conclusions.

• Be realistic about what you can accomplish with this witness.

• Consider your approach.

• Be prepared for objections.

A. Ask Only Leading Questions

Ask leading questions because this is your opportunity to testify. You make a statement of fact and invite the witness to accept your statement of fact by agreeing with you.

A leading question is a question that suggests an answer, such as “yes” or “no.” An open-ended question does not suggest an answer but asks Who, What, When, Where, or Why. For example, “What did you do with Mr. Smith?”

A leading question is a statement of fact. You make the statement a question with your voice inflection or by using a tag line. To make the statement a question with voice inflection, your voice goes up at the end of the statement as though it is a question. For example, “You had sex with Mr. Smith?” With a tag line, you make the statement of fact and add a tag line to either the end or the beginning: “…isn’t that right?” or “Isn’t it fair to say that… ?” For example, “You had sex with Mr. Smith, didn’t you?”

As a practical matter, quite often during cross-examination you know a witness is not going to agree with you or accept your statement of fact. The witness is not going to give you the answer you are seeking even though you know (and you can prove up) that the answer you are seeking is true. By the simple fact that you are questioning this witness on cross-examination, most likely he or she is a hostile witness. A hostile witness is not going to help you. A hostile witness will try to evade your questions. A hostile witness will try to obfuscate his or her answer. A hostile witness will try to hurt your defense case, if he or she can. Your goal is to prevent the witness hurting the defense case and to question the witness in a way that can help the defense case; otherwise don’t cross- examine this witness. You can control the witness by the questions you ask. This is why you ask only leading questions.

As a general rule, never ask a witness on cross-examination an open-ended question such as “why.” An open-ended question allows the witness to explain, to inject opinion (and bias), to confuse the issues, and to distract the jury from the point you want to make. This gives control of the evidence to the witness and takes it away from you, the questioner. This is analogous to handing a witness a stake to stab your defense case through the heart. Most witnesses on cross-examination will seize the opportunity and if they don’t verbally “kill” you, they will try very hard to hurt you badly. Don’t give them the opportunity. Control the witness by asking leading questions.

B. One Fact Per Question

Ask one fact per question because this allows you to control the flow of

information. It allows you to lead the witness fact by fact so the jury sees the progression. They can see the witness coming along with you. One fact per question makes your question simple and the witness’s answer solid and clear. One fact per question avoids a claim of confusion by the witness on redirect because you laid it out. For example, if you ask a long convoluted question, on redirect the prosecutor may give the witness an opening to claim he didn’t really understand the question and say, “Oh, gee, the answer I gave defense counsel wasn’t what I meant at all” and then the witness will explain it away. In this example the prosecutor has effectively wiped out any points you thought you made on cross and some judges will not give you an opportunity for recross.

Sometimes no matter what you ask or how you ask it, the witness will not agree with you. Assuming you know the answers and you can “prove them up,” just keep asking your questions and letting the witness disagree with you. The jury will then see (or later, when you prove it up and in closing) that you were correct and the witness was biased in refusing to admit to certain facts.

C. Ask Short Leading Questions

Ask short leading questions because this also helps you control the

witness. Short questions let you ensure the witness understands the question before you move onto the next question or topic. Short questions also help keep the rhythm of cross moving forward (remember: don’t be boring. You’ll lose the jury). Short leading questions can also encourage a witness to get into a rhythm with you. Conventional wisdom says if you can get a witness on cross to give you three “yes” answers in a row, he or she is likely to continue giving you “yes” answers because of that rhythm.

Short questions also help the witness stay with you. If at any point the witness stops or hesitates, you can rephrase the question or simply ask it again. The witness will soon understand that you are not stopping until he or she answers your questions.

Skip asking about what the witness said on direct examination or about anything that’s not important. Focus on what you need from this witness for closing argument. For example, in a consensual sex case maybe a point you want to make is that the complainant was drunk but able to consent. You want to ask short leading questions of witnesses who were with the complainant before she started drinking and while she was drinking and what they observed. Your questions can focus on demonstrating that she may have been “really drunk” but not “passed out” and not incapacitated. You want to ask short questions to show complainant knew what was going on (e.g., “Her speech wasn’t slurred?”), to show that her memory now of the events then is selective, and that she was able to consent to the sexual activity with the defendant.

D. Use Plain Simple Language

Avoid jargon. Instead of “exit the vehicle” say “got out of the car.” Use

small words. If you use “big” words a hostile witness will feign ignorance, pretend or actually misunderstand you or you may lose some jury members who don’t know what your words mean and become resentful toward you.

Minimize “tag lines” if you can. Cross-examination works best when

you can use only your voice to create the question mark: “You wanted to go home with Mr. Smith?” “Mr. Smith wanted to go home with you?” “He called Uber?” “The two of you got in the car?” “You went to his house?” “You knew you were going to his house?”

If the witness balks (police officers especially seem fond of saying, “Is that a question, Counsel?”), you can add a tag line to create the question mark and make it clear to the witness (and the jury) you are asking a question: “You wanted to go home with Mr. Smith, right?” Just try to vary the tags (e.g., “right,” “is it correct to say,” “is it fair to say,” “would you agree,” etc.) so you don’t use the same tag line each time. Then, for variety, intersperse your tag lines with inflecting your voice as the question mark.

Be conscious of your rhythm. Don’t be afraid to pause before you ask the next question. A cross-examination does not need to be one continuous inquiry. Avoid starting each question with “and” to rush through as fast as possible. Even if you’re scared or panicked, pretend you’re not. How the jury perceives you is important. Look confident. Act like you are in control. Breathe. Simple language in your questions will help with this.

E. Know the Answer

Conventional wisdom says never ask a question unless you know the

answer. This is generally wise. If you don’t know the answer, the witness, who is most likely hostile to you (or at least toward your client), is going to respond with an answer you don’t like and it is probably going to hurt the defense case. Moreover, ask only leading questions that further your defense theory to accumulate points you can weave together into conclusions you can use in your closing argument. If you don’t know the answer then consider: is that question going to further your defense? Sometimes you have to take a chance, but not often.

For a twist on this theme, see the presentation, “Dynamic Cross-Examination” by Jim McComas, OCDLA Winter Conference, 2012.

F. Be Able to Prove Up the Answer

Just because you know the answer to your question does not mean a

hostile witness is going to give it to you without a fight. You need to be able to “prove up” the answer. To do this, you need to really know your case. What facts do you need from this witness, to argue a point in closing?

In the hypothetical case about a drunken complainant who consented to sex: one point you might want to get on cross-examination is how much the complainant had to drink. To do this, investigate and analyze facts from many possible sources:

• Discovery (police reports, tape-recorded interviews of complainant and other witnesses);

• Medical records (SANE, hospital, counselor),

• Prior testimony

  • Preliminary hearing transcript
  • TRO hearing
  • Grand jury transcript
  • Motion to uppress hearing transcript
  • OEC 412 rape shield hearing transcript
  • Other pretrial hearing or trial transcript

• Photographs

• Surveillance videos from the bar or other places,

• Diagrams (layout of bar, house),

• Bank receipts (use of a debit or credit card to buy drinks).

Assemble your evidence to determine what you can show the jury about how much the complainant had to drink. For example:

• What did she tell the investigating police officer?

• What do her friends say?

• What does the bartender say?

• What about other people who observed the complainant at the time or afterward?

With this information you can make a list of the points you want to establish with any particular witness. You can group the facts chronologically or from strongest and most important to weakest (but remember to save one “slam dunk” fact for the end of your cross-examination).

When you make your cross-examination outline, list your desired factual points. Use a separate sheet of paper to cross-reference your desired fact(s) to particular evidence (e.g., page in a police report), so if you need to confront a witness about an erroneous answer you can reference the impeaching information immediately. There are many ways to organize this so you can find it quickly:

• With identifying information (e.g., police report of Eugene Police Officer Jones),

• Date (e.g., written on May 1, 2017),

• Particular notation (e.g., page 2 or transcript page number) and

• If particular language is important and it is short, write out the quote so you have the exact language.

Organize your impeaching information so you can find it fast and can confront the witness accurately and quickly. If the witness argues with you, you do not have to fumble to find the impeachment, you have it at the ready.

If you think the witness will strongly disagree with you regarding, for example, a prior inconsistent statement, be prepared to show the police report (or whatever it is) to the witness. To do that, make extra copies of the item before court (e.g., the police report) and highlight the particular evidence you want to use on your copy and on the copy for the witness. Then if the witness balks, you can provide him or her a copy of the police report with the particular statement highlighted. (You can tell the prosecutor: “Page 2, 3rd paragraph, Counsel” and let them fumble to find it.) You can ask the witness to read it silently and then confirm that what is in the police report is a prior inconsistent statement—the witness told the police officer something different than what the witness is saying now in court.

If the impeaching information is an audio or video recording, have excerpts made before trial of the segments you want to use for impeachment or have the tape cued up and ready to play for the witness.

If the witness still denies the information when confronted, you may need a separate witness to prove up the answer. For example, a witness reads the police report but says he never told Officer Smith what is written in the statement. Be sure to read the statement aloud so it is in the record. Then you need Officer Smith to testify that the witness did say what is in Officer Smith’s report. Be sure to subpoena Officer Smith before trial. If the state already has Officer Smith (or any potential contradiction witness) subpoenaed, be sure to get an agreement from the prosecutor that he or she will not release that subpoenaed witness without prior notice to you so you can subpoena that witness yourself. You want to make sure you have the witness.

If you are prepared to prove up an answer, you feel more confident. But what’s more, a recalcitrant witness will see you are prepared, the prosecutor will see you are prepared, and the jury will see you are prepared, all of which enhances your credibility at the trial or hearing.

A bonus benefit: after you go through the process of confronting a hostile witness with contrary information once or twice, the witness will often become resigned to the fact that you are going to prove up the answer on the questions you are asking. The witness will often capitulate and begin to agree, but not always. Even so, a jury may grow weary of a witness who refuses to concede when correctly confronted and then discount that witness’s credibility.

G. Listen and Loop

When you get a good answer out of a witness, restate it as the preface to your next question.

Likewise, repeat good facts from the previous answer and use them to build the next question. This way the jury hears the good facts twice.

H. Look at the Witness Not at Your Notes

Some lawyers recommend writing out all questions prior to cross-examination. There is some benefit to this. Studies show that the act of writing by hand imprints the material on the brain better than typing. See, e.g., Mueller and Oppenheimer, “A Learning Secret: Don’t Take Notes with a Laptop,” Scientific American, June 3, 2014.20 (Students who used longhand remembered more and had a deeper understanding of the material). Writing out questions can also help you visualize the flow of the cross-examination, assist your evaluation of the points you want to make, help you discover where problem areas lie and how to refine your goals for the cross (and ultimately closing argument).

But once the questions have been written out, condense them into an outline of key words or short notations to use as memory cues for the points you want to cover with a particular witness. You have a defense theory, you have written out the cross, you know what points you need for closing. You know where you are going. You know how to get there.

You don’t need to read questions to the witness. Reading questions to the witness is dull; it lacks the energy of immediacy and it will bore the jury.

An outline of key words allows you to listen, really listen, to what a witness says in response to your question. Further, if you are not looking down at your paper frantically trying to find the next question, you can actually look at the witness. Looking at the witness helps consolidate your control of the cross-examination. Eye contact allows you to gauge the witness’s reaction to your questions. For example, what kind of nonverbal cues is the witness providing that you would miss if you were looking down at your notes? Is the witness looking to the prosecutor for help? Is he looking at his mom for the answer? Is the witness fidgeting or sweating? Is a child witness (or any witness) about to cry?

Relying on an outline—not written-out questions—allows you to have a conversation with the witness, to react to what the witness says (not what you expect them to say because of the way you have written a script), and to change your questioning in response to what the witness says to best get the information you need for closing. A witness, even a friendly one, will not have your written questions and will quickly go off script. If you are flexible enough to follow the witness and rein him or her in, you won’t be lost. Maintain control via flexibility and you are able to shift your questioning focus as needed to keep the jury interested and to get the evidence you need.

I. Ask About Facts and Evidence, Don’t Ask for Conclusions

An old saw about cross-examination is “don’t ask one question too many.” Often, the only way to know what is “one question too many” is when you realize you’ve asked it and the witness has verbally stabbed you through the heart. The best approach is to focus on obtaining facts and evidence from the witness without asking for conclusions. Save conclusions for closing argument. Even a non-hostile witness can hurt you with “friendly fire.”

A hostile witness will do everything he or she can to avoid giving you a conclusion you want and may confuse or muddle the jury’s thinking such that you lose the point you made during cross examination.

J. Be Realistic About What You Can Accomplish With This Witness

Ask yourself: what do I want to be able to say in closing argument that I must get from this witness? Then ask: what do I have to gain? What do I have to lose? Do I need to question this witness? There is nothing wrong with passing on cross-examination if the witness has not hurt you with his or her testimony on direct or if the prosecution has conducted a cursory direct (or forgotten to ask something important) and you want to prevent the prosecutor from coming back and getting more information or detail from this witness on redirect. There is a powerful message to the jury when the judge says, “You may cross-examine” and you stand up and say sweetly, “I have no questions of this witness, Your Honor.” The jury will infer you believe the witness did not hurt you and they will consider that.

Sometimes you may feel you must ask a witness (such as the complainant) something or the jury will believe that you accept everything the complaining witness says. In that situation, think carefully. You can certainly question the complainant about some insignificant collateral matters to make a showing, but consider what may go wrong. If you do this, “get in and get out.” But also remember, you “open the door” for prosecution redirect and redirect may be worse than if you had asked no questions at all.

If you impeach a witness, the prosecution is entitled to rehabilitate that witness. For example, if you impeach a witness with a prior inconsistent statement, the witness is entitled to an opportunity to explain or deny the statement.21 If you impeach a witness for bias or interest, the prosecution is entitled to follow up with evidence that shows a lack of bias or interest.22 Be aware whether there are prior consistent statements the prosecution didn’t bring up on direct that may now surface on redirect (a prior statement is not hearsay if it is “consistent with the testimony of a witness and if offered to rebut an inconsistent statement or an express or implied charge against the witness of recent fabrication of improper influence or motive”).23 Also, be cautious about using part of a statement to impeach a witness, because the prosecution may then be able to use other parts of the statement for rehabilitation that you don’t want in evidence.24

On redirect, the complaining witness may repeat the most damaging evidence against the defendant because you “opened the door” by asking about insignificant non-collateral matters. The better course may be to pass on cross-examination of this witness.

K. Consider Your Approach

Generally, approach a witness on cross-examination in two basic ways: to ask for information or to confront. With informational witnesses, use a conversational tone. Ask for facts (although you are really providing the evidence through leading questions and the witness is simply affirming your statements). Try to avoid being confrontational unless you must.

The best scenario in cross-examination is “death by a thousand cuts,” where the witness does not realize he is bleeding until you put together all the evidence in closing. Don’t be confrontational or seek to impeach a witness who helps you. (This is another reason to use an outline and not scripted questions—with a script you may not even realize the witness is helping you. A corollary of this is to approach the witness with your own eyes, not with the pretrial bias or emotional reaction your client has to the witness. The key is to “be here now” during cross-examination. Be flexible. You may be surprised how a witness you thought would hurt you actually helps you and, to the contrary, how a friendly witness hurts you).

L. Be Prepared for Prosecution Objections

The prosecution may object during your cross-examination because you

are questioning beyond the scope of direct examination or because you are scoring points and she wants to break up your flow and rhythm.

Many objections the prosecutor could interpose are not specifically covered by the Oregon Evidence Code but are in the court’s discretion. For example:

• asked and answered25

• unduly embarrassing questions, collateral matters26

• undue delay27

• assuming facts not in evidence28

• confusing29

• misleading30

• ambiguous31 or compound questions32

• harassment33

• undue prejudice34

• question intended to elicit inadmissible evidence35

• or rape shield issue.36

If you see issues that may cause the prosecution to object, prepare for them ahead of time. Read Kirkpatrick’s Oregon Evidence to find rules that support you, and have case law at the ready. Make a one-page outline with the evidence rules, case cites and reference pages to Kirkpatrick. If there are particular cases that are especially helpful, make a copy for the court and the prosecutor. If the objection is made, you are prepared and you show the court (and the prosecutor) that you are prepared, which can be the tipping point for a judge to rule your way.

VI. Particular Scenarios

A. Cross of Complainants

1. Adults and Teenagers

Complainants in sex crime cases are often women. Defendants are often men. Allegations are often rape, sodomy, and sexual abuse. Defenses (leaving out special cases) are generally:

  • There was no physical contact.
  • There was physical contact but it was not for sexual gratification.
  • There was sexual contact but it was consensual.
  • There may have been sexual contact but it wasn’t me (alibi, mistake, identity).

In sex crime cases the motivation of the complaining witness is crucial for

acquittal or exoneration: the defense must be able to tell the jury why the complainant is falsely accusing the defendant of a sex crime.

Begin preparation for trial and cross-examination with careful review of discovery, analyze the factors involved in this particular case, and conduct a meticulous and vigorous investigation (see Chapter 3 for analysis and developing your themes, and Chapter 4 for discovery and investigation). For example, who is the first person the complainant contacted? Is it 911 or is it an ex-boyfriend? The latter may be a circumstance of someone needing attention or asking for rescue—either motivation can be used at trial in cross-examination). Did the complainant immediately go for help after an alleged assault or did she text with her friends about what happened (especially in an allegedly consensual circumstance)?

False Accusations

Developing a theory for trial and thus for cross-examination means exploring themes that may apply to why the complainant is making a false accusation. Theories may overlap and intertwine; more than one may apply to any single situation:

a. Jealousy

Example: defendant is single dad, he begins dating, daughter is jealous and alleges abuse against dad.

b. Need for Attention

Sometimes negative attention is better than none. Sometimes complainant feels abandoned and the accusation is a cry for help.

c. Revenge

Sometimes against a stepfather, mom’s boyfriend, or an authority figure who has disciplinary power and the ability to punish transgressions.

d. Sacrifice

Example 1: Mom gave daughter methamphetamine, mom prosecuted for that and imprisoned; mom has never liked defendant, daughter makes allegation against defendant to try and redeem herself in mom’s eyes.

Example 2: Daughter has drug and alcohol issues, boyfriend threatens to break up with her. She says dad abused her to keep boyfriend from dumping her (not my fault, I’m a mess, dad’s fault, he abused me).

Example 3: Mom undocumented alien. Daughter alleges abuse by uncle. Mom applies for U visa to stay in U.S. based on daughter’s abuse. Evidence that daughter knew mom could stay in U.S. with U visa if daughter alleged abuse against someone. State v. Del Real-Galvez, 270 Or App 224, 346 P3d 1289 (2015).

e. Manipulation

Example 1: Daughter doesn’t like mom’s boyfriend, accusation to get him out of mom’s life.

Example 2: Girl doesn’t like foster parents, accusation to get out of foster home.

f. Projection and Deflection

For example: A female college student is away from home for the first time and is pressured by parents because she is getting poor grades, she makes accusation of sex abuse to deflect parents’ attention away from her partying and not studying.

Consensual Defense Cases

For example: Sexual contact occurs between two adults, e.g., a man and a woman, alcohol and/or drugs are involved and woman regrets the contact after the fact. She may feel “buyer’s remorse” for getting sexually involved with someone. Sometimes this occurs because the man she hooked up with was her friend’s lover, partner or husband. Sometimes it is simply the result of extreme intoxication and lack of memory. Sometimes it is bad judgment, exacerbated by her friends’ negative reactions (“you didn’t hook up with him, did you?!) or the failure of the male to follow up when the woman expected sex to lead to some kind of relationship.

Approach

Apply the general rules for cross-examination and be respectful toward the complainant. Be particularly careful not to ask complainant for any conclusions or opinions, especially do not try to persuade her—on cross-examination—to recant her accusation. She probably really believes she was abused and attacks on that belief will reinforce her belief and make her more entrenched. The danger for the defense is if the complainant is likable, the jury may not care that her story doesn’t make sense and convict the defendant anyway.

Instead, show the jury inconsistencies in the complainant’s story. Ask yourself: must you do this through cross-examination of the complainant? Can you do it some other way? Possibly through statements in the police reports? Through extrinsic evidence? (For example, in a rape trial where consent was the defense the complainant was wearing black tights. Both parties were very drunk. Complainant claimed defendant pulled her tights off to have sex but then he put them back on her. Examination of the tights in evidence showed they were pristine: no runs, no snags, no holes. In closing, the defense argued that the condition of the tights supported an inference of consent. The drunk defendant could not have gotten complainant’s tights off—let alone got them back on—without her help and cooperation).

(Note: Defendants expect their lawyer to get the complainant to “break down” and confess or recant during cross-examination. They are invariably disappointed when defense counsel asks leading questions on narrow subjects without a grand denouement at the end of cross-examination. Explain to the client that this is not how it works and try to make him understand before trial).

2. Cross of the child complainant

An allegation of sex crimes involving a child complainant raises some of

the same issues as with a teenager or adult complainant: questions about whether there was any physical contact and if there was physical contact whether it was sexual (i.e., for “sexual gratification”).

Theories about the motivation of a child complainant to make a false allegation are similar as well:

a. Jealousy

Example: Jealousy of parent’s new partner, jealousy of sibling (and needing attention).

b. Attention

Examples include: “me too” syndrome—a friend or relative was abused; creating a bond (feeling of belonging and attention when another child says abused); getting positive attention (from DHS, foster parents); and reveling in victimhood.

c. Revenge

To punish a parent for a divorce (e.g., dad leaving mom).

d. Sacrifice

Custody battle: sacrifice one parent so can hang onto the other and not be abandoned by both.

e. Projection and Deflection

Child is caught touching self sexually, feels guilty, claims abused because doesn’t want to get in trouble for touching self.

f. Manipulation

Example 1: Get mom’s new boyfriend out of home

Example 2: Get attention from parent otherwise lacking.

g. Abandonment

Parent not there physically or emotionally.

h. Mistaken Identity

Child abused by someone but not the defendant.

i. Coached/Suggestibility

Example 1: Custody battle: one parent bitter and angry with the other, suggests abuse to child, child wants to please that parent.

Example 2: Young child who has vivid imagination, absolute fantasy.

Example 3: Child repeats something heard, interviewed numerous times until comes to believe story.

Approach

Your approach to cross-examination of a child complainant depends on the age of the child. With younger children there is an initial question of competence (OEC 602), specifically the child’s ability to perceive, remember and communicate. This foundation should be tested pretrial. File a motion and ask for a hearing. This permits you to have a “preview” of the child’s demeanor.

Prepare and craft your cross of a child complainant with care and try to answer the following questions in advance to know how to shape your cross: Did the child choose to make a claim of abuse? If so, what preceded it? What triggered it? Was it accidental or purposeful? Did someone else make the initial claim? Who? Was it a teacher? What prompted the initial complaint? If the child did choose to make a complaint, what was the context of the claim?

For example, was the child caught in sex play with another child? Was the child caught touching herself sexually and thought she was in trouble for that? Was the child being disciplined in school? Was the child otherwise in trouble with someone for something?

Next, if the child did make the initial claim of abuse, whom did they tell? An adult? A playmate? A teacher? Why that person? Was it situational vs. opportunistic? Did the child have something to gain by making a claim to that person?

Look at the timing of the claim of abuse—was it immediate or delayed? Were there initial denials of abuse?

Look at what the child says happened. Was there a lot of detail? Very little detail? Can the child remember the first time abuse allegedly happened and the last time? Memory experts examine a child’s ability to detail the first and last incidents of claimed abuse because the first time and last time are generally memorable. An argument can also be made that the longer the delay between a claimed event of abuse and a report of it, the greater the likelihood of suggestibility. Look to see who first interviewed the child and what types of questions were asked.

Consider how the child feels about the defendant. Does the child like the defendant? Hate the defendant? How the child feels about the accused could reflect a false accusation (but be careful, it might also cause a false denial). Also consider how the child’s parents feel about the defendant.

Once you have the answers to these questions, highlight the answers that you need for your theory of the case and how to obtain that evidence from the child complainant.

Goals of Cross-Examination of a Child Complainant

Cross-examination, especially of a child, is like sailing. You can’t get from point A to point B directly, if you can get there at all. You are unlikely to get a child complainant to say during cross-examination that the alleged abuse did not happen. You need to zig and zag with your facts like a sailor changes course with shifting winds to get your points.

a. Impeachment With Prior Inconsistent Statements

Find prior statements of the child that you can use for impeachment by careful analysis of discovery and through independent defense investigation.

Obtain education/school records to see if there is evidence of discipline at school, family conflict, special education, DHS records, juvenile records. Obtain protective orders from the judge assigned to the case, then issue subpoenas duces tecum along with the protective orders for these records to be produced to the judge assigned to the case (not to you) for in camera review. You need to know what you want from the records to avoid a prosecutorial objection that you are simply on a “fishing expedition.” Provide the court a detailed request for particular information related to your theory of the case and request specific types of exculpatory information. You may need to go through this process more than once.

b. Impeach With Inconsistencies

You can question to show there are subtle differences in the child’s story or details, such as: the child is mistaken, confused, uncertain, the child is highly suggestible or has been coached (this may be intentional or inadvertent depending on the number of interviews, the number of statements the child has made or the number of people questioning the child). You may also show that the child lacks personal knowledge of facts or, in some rare circumstances, that the accusation is simply falsified. Make note of odd word choices, for example, words or phrases that would not be natural to this child or for a child of this age. Pay attention to how the child testifies—is her delivery very deliberate? Is there a lack of detail or inclusion of weird detail? Consider whether you question about this or save your observations for closing.

c. Establish Additional Facts Left Out of Direct That Help Your Theory of Defense

For example: examine time, space, detail, and lack of detail.

d. Show the Direct Was Product of Coaching

Ask about the number of interviews, how long each lasted, and who was present to create an inference that the story is the product of numerous meetings not memory.

Watch to see if the child looks to her mom, the prosecutor or

another adult for encouragement or for the answer to a question.

Techniques for cross of a child complainant

a. General Rules for Cross-examination of Children

i. Your tone is critical. Be neutral, matter of fact, friendly and conversational. Children perceive adults as generally seeking to dominate, lecture or interrogate them, so when a child is being questioned by an adult she naturally reacts with fear that she is being questioned because she has done something wrong. The prosecution has no doubt warned the child about you so expect the child to be wary of you. Choose your words carefully. Watch how the child reacts, watch her body language, and adjust your demeanor accordingly.

ii. Treat the child with respect. Do not patronize her.

iii. Give the child assurance that telling the truth—no matter what that is—will not get the child into trouble.

iv. Use disclosure to overcome the child’s wariness of you. Talk about something the two of you have in common: pets, games, movies.

b. Apply “Match and Move” or the “Agreement Technique”

Build rapport with the child. Plan to begin by asking general questions and then eventually move to specific questions. A good way to build rapport (and to help the child relax) is to give the child something to hold, look at and talk about such as, for example, a large colorful poster with a family tree.

In a case with two (apparently unrelated) complainants, it was important to demonstrate that they knew each other. The defense theory was they had colluded in making the false accusation and each had a bias against the defendant. The poster (also projected on the wall in front of the jury) allowed defense counsel to stand close to the child and converse. At counsel table the defense lawyer is too far away from the child. Generally you want to be close to the child to give him or her focus. (Note: but, if you are a very large man, consider whether you would want to do this—you do not want to appear as though you are “looming” over the child—but if you sit near her or otherwise reduce your stature it could have a positive effect). Talking about the family tree poster became almost a game for the child. She didn’t have to look at the jury or the defense lawyer. She could look at the poster. She knew the answers to questions asked about who was on the poster because this was her family. Defense counsel gave her verbal positive reinforcement (“That’s right”) and physical positive reinforcement (nods, smiles) when complainant identified people on the poster. She responded with pleasure to this positive reinforcement. This all served to establish rapport between defense counsel and the witness. (This was also a rare exception to the rule to ask only leading questions.)

This technique can be used with photographs or almost anything that allows the child to focus on something other than you. This alternative focus allows the child to relax. It allows you to analyze her and adjust your approach with her as she answers your initial questions. Build rapport by asking the child simple questions on neutral subjects to which she will agree. Show approval for her agreement. Then move to other questions while keeping a conversational demeanor.

For example, after establishing rapport over the complainant’s family on the poster, the defense began to ask more specific questions about other people on the family tree—including the other (apparently) unrelated second complainant. These questions were more case-related but still continued toward the goal of keeping
her agreeing.

The idea is to slowly move a complainant from statements she made on direct to points you want for closing. This may require you to employ indirection in your questioning. You may need to conceal your ultimate objective by switching topics if the witness balks. You can alter questions on the sequence of events instead of proceeding chronologically. Go to the middle or start at the end. Then return to the time you want to explore. Continue until you get what information you can, again remembering to leave any conclusion for closing.

Do not get greedy. Do not cross-examine just to ask questions. Do not be mean to the child unless you feel you have permission from the jury; if you’re mean and you don’t have permission, the jury will dislike you and ultimately convict the defendant.

c. Add a Psychological Cost to the Child Disagreeing With You

If you have established rapport with the child, she will likely want to please you
with her answers. You can sometimes foreshadow for her that disagreeing with you may lead to unpleasantness (again being mindful of your jury). A child wants to avoid social embarrassment and to preserve her self-esteem. Help her preserve her dignity. Question the child in a manner that allows her testimony to be seen in a different light.

For example, if you want to say in closing that the defendant’s touching of the child’s vagina and bottom was for medicinal purposes, not for sexual gratification, ask questions about a messy bowel movement (using plain language, whatever the child would say), mom’s boyfriend wiped her, wiped her with tissues, didn’t wipe her with his hand. If she starts to balk, gently go back to the point where she agreed and try to bring her along as you can. Don’t argue with her. Be prepared to prove up your points through another witness (e.g., defendant).

d. Help the Child Recant If She Can

You can talk with a child about “truth vs. pretend.” You can confirm with her that she would get in trouble if she told a lie. You can then talk with her about how people sometimes make mistakes. Make sure she knows that if she says something different to you than what she said on direct, she is not going to get in trouble. Best approach is if you can attack the process and not the child.

e. With Very Young Children Sometimes Let Imagination Run Wild

Preschool kids say what they believe is the truth; it can be fantastical. It may have been implanted. To show claims have been implanted, ask about things that suggest hyperbole or wild flights of imagination to demonstrate the child’s perception, memory and communication are suspect.

B. Cross of the Police Officer

General rules for cross-examination apply when questioning police officers. A fruitful area for questioning is to examine the officer, especially the case agent, on things that weren’t done in a case: general failure to investigate, lack of forensic testing or examinations, failure to question witnesses, failure to follow up, etc.

You can often “make mountains out of molehills.” Ask the officer how many times the child was interviewed, who was present, who decided on the questions, how long the interview lasted, and so on. If the officer doesn’t know, she looks unprepared.

File pretrial motions and have pretrial hearings to preview a police officer’s testimony before trial. For example, if the defendant made statements to the police, file a motion to suppress the defendant’s statements and ask for a hearing. The state generally has the burden of proof (e.g., ORS 133.673(4)) and will have to call the officer to testify. This is an opportunity to preview how the officer testifies. During the pretrial hearing you can ask open-ended questions to get information. You can discover new possible bad facts (and then investigate to neutralize them). You can confirm particularly helpful facts. You may not win the motion but it will be a good “dry run” before you get in front of the jury. You may learn how better to question and neutralize this witness. This is also an opportunity to “train” the witness. If you have done your homework (looked at the scene, reviewed discovery, investigated the case), you probably know the case better than the police officer. In the pretrial hearing put the police officer on the defensive by pressing on particular issues. You can psychologically punish him
if his report or his testimony is sloppy. At trial this same officer will remember how he fared and may well “heel” during cross-examination in front of the jury and be cooperative and helpful compared to his demeanor at the pretrial hearing, because he does not want to be punished again.

C. Cross of Experts

Is the witness qualified to testify as an expert and can the prosecution lay a foundation for that qualification (knowledge, experience, training)? (See Chapter 6, “Experts and the Multidisciplinary Team in child Sex Abuse.”)

Assuming the witness qualifies as an expert, utilize the general rules of cross-examination. You can also cross-examine:

• To make the expert your own.

• To develop and demonstrate the expert’s bias. This can be as simple as showing that this expert always testifies for the state or that the expert was paid and how much.

• To show contradiction—this can be done by cross-examining the expert with learned treatises. OEC 706. Establish that a treatise is a reliable authority by getting the witness to agree to it, have another expert say so, or get the judge to take judicial notice. You can then impeach the expert with information from the treatise, but remember, it is not substantive evidence because it is hearsay unless you have some independent basis for admissibility. Cf: Federal Rule of Evidence 803 (18).

• Be sure whatever learned treatise or manual you are using is something the witness knows or has been trained with, otherwise you will not be able to impeach with it. State v. Smith, 66 Or App 703, 675 P2d 510 (1984).

• To show lack of foundation or error for expert’s opinion. But do not ask the expert to draw conclusions from the evidence—the expert will hurt you. Save the conclusions for closing.

Hypotheticals

Types of points you can make with an expert on cross-examination in a sex crime case include:

a. The protocols are just guidelines and not a standardized checklist.

An expert must use own judgment, each case is different, there are no standards, etc.

b. The number of interviews of a child:

Whether the interviews are repeated and numerous, whether recorded, the interviews can lead to suggestibility, coaching, implanted memories, etc.

c. Meetings with others (especially if on a team like MDT):

Everybody has input; decision by consensus; your opinion is in part based on the opinions of others; sets up inference for contamination because of team approach.

d. If the expert gave an opinion on direct that abused child acts in certain ways:

Nobody can determine if a child is abused because a child acts in a certain way or because of what she says; each case is different; not “one size fits all.”

e. All the things the expert didn’t do in this case.

Sometimes the best thing you can do is to let the state’s expert testify while your expert sits in the back of the courtroom and watches. Notifying the state that you have a defense expert can help keep the state’s expert “honest” because that expert knows someone is watching for errors or mistakes.

VII. The Defendant’s Direct and Preparation for Cross

Talk to the defendant about the theory of the case and her defense.

Talking about the framework of the case will also put the defendant at ease; people are fearful when they don’t know what to expect. Anything you can tell the defendant (or any witness) about what is going to happen (where the courtroom is, how it looks, administration of the oath, sitting in the chair, having water available, not taking notes up there, etc.) is going to strengthen your credibility and put the witness at ease.

Impart “the rules” of direct examination, which the defendant or witness should also use for cross-examination:

1) Listen to the question.

2) Make sure you understand the question.

3) Answer the question (just that question, nothing more).

4) Stop.

5) Tell the truth.

It seems obvious to tell the defendant to “tell the truth,” but people are easily flustered—especially if they feel they should know an answer but don’t. Emphasize to the defendant it is better to say “I don’t know” than to guess. People are punished less severely for what they do and more severely for lying about it (e.g., President Clinton, Marion Jones and Martha Stewart all come to mind).

Advise the witness or defendant to be as courteous to the prosecutor as he is to you. The prosecutor wants the defendant to get angry or to argue. If he does, or if a defendant appears disingenuous, he will lose credibility with the jury and the case is over. If a defendant can understand he does not have to win every point, that he just has to tell his truth, he will be more relaxed and more honest, and the jury will like him better and believe him more. This is true for any witness.

Just like cross-examination, if you have a written outline of points for direct, you can maintain eye contact with your defendant or witness. This helps a defendant because he or she has someone to focus on. It also makes you look interested in what the witness is saying. Your goal is to make it look almost spontaneous when it is really “improvisation” overlaid on a tight structure.

The conventional form of direct is boring. The prosecution does this best: it is called starting with “background”:

(Name stated and spelled for the record.)

Question: Where do you live?

Answer: Blah blah.

Question: How are you employed?

Answer: Blah blah.

Instead of background, start with the most important fact so it doesn’t get lost if you forget something on your outline of points. The start should grab the jury’s attention. For example: “Did you possess child pornography for sexual gratification?” If you have bad facts or something you need to explain, you can get it out there early and neutralize it. Volunteer the defendant’s prior criminal conviction to preempt the prosecution on cross-examination.37

Help the defendant or witness make the most important points to the jury. Remember primacy and recency: jurors tend to recall little but they mostly remember the first thing they hear and
the last thing they hear. People these days have short attention spans. (Goldfish have a nine second attention span, a 2015 Microsoft study says people now have an eight second attention span.38

Listen to the defendant’s answers. This helps you “loop” your questions (repeating good facts from the previous answer) and then use them to build the next question. Thus the jury hears the good facts twice. Or you can—as the story flows—hearken back to a good fact stated previously, use it in your next question (again looping) and then build on it. Like the movie Pulp Fiction, you can start smack in the middle of the story if it makes your strongest and best point. Then you can come back around so the point gets repeated more than once without drawing a pesky “asked and answered” objection.39

Your questions should be short, specific and open-ended. Catch yourself if you are saying, “Okay” after each answer by the defendant.

Practice Tip

If possible have a colleague conduct a practice cross-examination of the defendant or witness. This will give the witness a feeling for what cross-examination is like and can prepare him or her for the real thing.

VIII. Conclusion

Cross-examination is an art. As such, what works in one case may not work in another case. But if you learn the basic rules and apply those rules consistently, you have the best opportunity to provide the defendant effective assistance of counsel, to advance your defense theory of the case and to obtain an acquittal verdict.
