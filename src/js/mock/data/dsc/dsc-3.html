Chapter 3. Themes in Sex Crime Defense: Developing, Polishing and Presenting the Stories of Your Defense at Every Stage of Your Case

By Steven J. Sherlag

“Telling effective stories is not easy. The difficulty lies not in telling the story,
but in convincing everyone else to believe it.”—Yuval Noah Harari1

Introduction

You’ve tried a ton of cases—ugly domestic violence cases, maybe a robbery or two, maybe a case with a very big bag of drugs and a bunch of (allegedly) ill-gotten cash. Sometimes the client is a knight in shining armor, usually they are not. You’re ready to try a sex case, or maybe try a new approach—if you could just find a completely innocent client it’d be a lot easier. Usually they are not, or it’s not so obvious to anyone around. So now what?

This chapter is intended to give practicing lawyers things to consider when developing case stories, theories and themes, plural, which may be separate for each of three different stages of the defense—and how to tell that story in an effective fashion. The driving premise is that sex crimes defense is so difficult, so challenging, to have any chance of success a sex crime defender must confront the deepest fears of the jurors, the primitive “reptile” prejudices against those accused of sex offenses,2 and must present a cogent and compelling story appealing to each individual juror’s need to protect the defendant from the prosecutor and the accusations themselves.

This chapter’s main thrusts are to encourage practitioners to:

1) develop your theory of defense early

2) polish your themes incessantly until they speak true to you (and hopefully the finder of fact) in simple words, and

3) prepare your entire defense with your theory and themes, both direct and implied, as these will drive your case.

This chapter should be read in conjunction with Keith Belzer’s excellent article, “Practice Points: Themes and Theme-Related Defenses in Sexual Assault Cases,” The Champion (NACDL July 2016, see the appendix), as this chapter was written with a conscious attempt to avoid redundancies.

This chapter should also be used in conjunction with all other types of aids, such as OCDLA’s Trial Notebook, Gerry Spence’s excellent How to Win series, and that from many other excellent teachers of trial practice. Plus, there’s much to learn from teaching tools from the civil bar, like Rules of the Road, Ball on Damages, and the Reptilian Brain, discussed herein.

I. Storytelling in Sex Crime Defense – The Basics

Storytelling in sex crime defense is particularly hard. In almost every sex crime case the deck is stacked completely against the defendant, and the finder of fact is disposed to convict, might be shut down to any defense, and may be downright hostile. Rather than give up, it’s time to dig in. First, let’s review a couple of basic concepts.

• Story vs. Theory vs. Theme

The story of the case is what you tell in opening statement. It is a careful weaving of essential facts, good and bad, and the most important part of the applicable law. It is a narrative explanation of the crux of your defense—why your client should be acquitted. It should incorporate motivations, biases and emotions, embrace the narrative format, and be inherently persuasive while being well organized and reasoned and employ well-established storytelling techniques. It’s the counter-narrative to the state’s opening.

The story explains your defense theory and the supporting themes.

• Every Case Has at Least Two Theories

1. Legal Theory

First, there is the legal theory—that which if “accepted” results in acquittal, 3 i.e., the intellectual driving force of your opening statement:

  • The complainant is lying, she consented, or maybe didn’t make “no” clear.
  • The defendant didn’t touch the complainant.
  • The defendant touched the child, but it wasn’t sexual because client was just wrestling with the child, and didn’t mean anything.
  • The child’s memory has been contaminated.

Theories in the Alternative

Sometimes there is more than one theory.

  • He didn’t touch the child, but if he did, it was an accident.

Be careful if you raise two inconsistent or partially inconsistent defenses. The moral force of your presentation may be fatally flawed.

2. Theory of Defense

The second theory is your theory of defense, which carefully integrates your legal theory with the most important, most persuasive, theme or themes. The theory of defense is the very first thing you will say in opening, maybe in closing, too, and will be the motivation in everything you do in trial.

  • The complainant is lying because she wants revenge for defendant’s affair.
  • If the complainant couldn’t be with defendant, she was prepared to make sure no

one could.

  • The child is lying because her mom set her up to get custody.
  • The detective in his unrecorded field interview of the child improperly suggested defendant abused her, and the child adopted the detective’s story.

3. Defense Themes

The defense themes are the heart, the emotional crux that drives the defense.

The defense themes make the legal theory credible and the theory of defense more plausible than the government’s theory of prosecution. Identifying and articulating persuasive defense themes makes the legal theory and theory of defense plausible and acquittals possible.

Themes may go to credibility, bias, or things which don’t directly attack facts at issue but are designed to change the landscape. For example, this case is about:

  • Revenge—an acrimonious divorce and a mother using their child as a tool of revenge.
  • Regret—two intoxicated yet consulting adults, one just had regrets the morning after.
  • Selfish Regret—cover for an illicit affair.
  • Confirmation Bias—an officer/DHS worker who concluded the defendant was guilty before gathering all of the facts, and when they questioned the child they wouldn’t take no for an answer.
  • Innocent Mistake—something that began as a simple misunderstanding but now has taken on a dangerous life of its own.
  • Pathological Liar—sometimes people lie, and while we don’t always know why, it’s still a lie.

4. Multiple Stories and Themes

You may even have multiple stories and themes depending on different stages of your case, and they may sometimes be inconsistent:

a. Trial Defense Story

There may be lots here to choose from, including that which if “accepted” would result in full acquittal, acquittal of some counts, acquittal to lesser offenses, or acquittals of the most serious offenses; think “Differential Diagnosis”—don’t rule out anything too soon.

b. Mitigation Story

If the client did most or all of what they say client did, what is the prognosis for treatment? Opportunistic or predatory? Something exceptional in their life that makes them empathetic? Client suffered abuse themselves? Can the moral-indignation call for aggravated sentencing be tempered by presenting a risk assessment that portrays client in a positive light?

c. Negotiation Story

Where is the prosecutor most vulnerable? How do you maintain a credible acquittal defense when your client is in treatment? Can you or should you counsel client to undertake a psychosexual if they maintain innocence? When counseling a client who maintains innocence, how do you distinguish between a claim of innocence and a client that is likely guilty but “in denial”? Is the approach different if the denial is plausible or not? Do you have client undergo a psychosexual if they are in “plausible” denial? How about “implausible denial”? What do you do with that psychosexual evaluation? Is it possible the complainant is empathetic to your client, and does not want prison?

Defense theories may be simple or complicated, depending on the respective facts of your case. This chapter’s focus is on the Trial Defense Theory and the Defense Themes that are the heart of the Trial Defense Theory, but all of the stories are important and the techniques for developing and presenting the Trial Defense Theory will help one develop the other stories.

II. Storytelling – Context: Sex Cases Are Different

Sex cases are plainly different from any other kind of case. If the jurors haven’t thrown all their daggers at you when the indictment was first read in open court, and they still have a few left after voir dire, they will be grabbing them and plunging them into your chest and your client’s heart after the DDA’s opening. A mediocre prosecutor can give a killer opening in a sex case.

Some of the differences:

A. Extra Vulnerable Victim

Pick your poison:

  • Child, tween, or teen
  • Mentally, physically, or intoxicated accuser
  • An accuser with physical scars

  • An accuser suffering from deep emotional trauma in a system that feeds, perpetuates and seldom meaningfully questions people who claim trauma.

Practice Tip: Beware of the “Vulnerable Victim”

The complainant’s trauma and vulnerability are often used as a weapon against the defendant and help the state immeasurably. Child abuse assessment center (CAAC) interviewers, DHS and law enforcement are on the lookout for vulnerable victims, as they have been trained that some abusers specifically target vulnerable victims as they are known to be more susceptible to grooming and improper adult attention. Presented at trial with a vulnerable victim, such profile evidence (“abusers target vulnerable children”) can be very damaging and not necessarily with an adequate scientific basis.

Tactical Response: First, move in limine in anticipation of all profile evidence and demand a Daubert hearing. Second, when moving in limine, trial courts often lump all of the state’s proposed testimony together, ruling it all admissible. Ask for individual findings and rulings, breaking down into the smallest pieces of opinion possible. Any victories here help, and losses may create error as the appellate courts are sensitive to issues which are akin to vouching and which might not have an adequate basis in science, yet based on scientific-type evidence. See related discussion in Chapter 10.

B. Endemic Emotional Trauma

Endemic emotional trauma can be very complicated to address in court as you are unlikely to have complete and accurate information about the accuser’s emotional trauma. There is a multitude of reasons for this, including:

1. Records and Good Evidence Might Not Exist

Many individuals with serious mental health problems or personality disorders have never sought help or been properly diagnosed due to lack of access to proper care, inadequate providers, and/or a general reluctance by providers to place a “label” or diagnosis on a child or teen who may “grow out” of their problems; such individuals also may do their best to conceal the true nature of their problems as they are embarrassed, self-protective of their ego, or in denial, or their mental health or personality disorders may include features of dishonesty, manipulation, or presenting themselves in a false light.

2. Limited Access to Records and Persons Who Have Accurate Information

The “system” rallies around “victims” such that medical, mental health, and school records are tantalizingly out of reach, protected by HIPPA and confidentiality provisions, and a Cartwright motion for issuance of subpoena duces tecum and in camera inspection are notoriously unproductive (but should still be tried); add layers of laws and constitutional provisions that protect “victims” from discovery and do not allow for independent or defense expert psychological examinations of accusers, the proliferation of free “victim’s rights center” lawyers, and the “system” seems to be set up to convict, and certainly not ensure a fair trial for an accused.

3. Courts Are Still Trying to Figure Out What to Do With Mental Health Records

When records do exist, what can be done with them is still being worked out in the legal system. Psychologists who don’t see or treat the accuser cannot “diagnose” them or necessarily be able to predict the effect of given mental health problems or personality disorders on a given incident or series of reports. There is no time machine.

4. Even a Significant Diagnosis May Be of Limited Utility

As mental health problems are dynamic, one day may be a good day, the next something different, maybe more reactive or triggered. For example, if an accuser has been diagnosed with Borderline Personality Disorder—something particularly difficult to treat and frequently tied to erratic and manipulative behavior, does that necessarily undermine the credibility of a report of sexual abuse?

5. Source of Trauma May Not Be Understood or Known, or May Be Multiple

The literature and understanding around the human experience of trauma is undergoing a dramatic revision for many reasons, some listed below.

  • Repeated “minor” trauma events can be as devastating as a major event—the type

of singularly awful event previously thought necessary to cause PTSD.

  • False memories can cause severe trauma, indistinguishable from trauma caused by actual events.

  • Trauma is experienced subjectively, a personal experience. Everyone responds to trauma differently, as some are more resilient, some more vulnerable, and those with otherwise existing mental health or personality disorders may make any “predictability”
    an impossibility.

  • Trauma’s causation or effects can be transferred or misplaced. Genuine trauma might be preexisting or caused post-“event” and not necessarily related to your client at all. Some trauma sufferers just need someone to blame to help them “cope” and “recover.” The accuser may be a victim of someone else, using him or her to get back at your client. Or maybe the accuser is only such after they’ve been told they’re a victim, and they have willingly or unwillingly, intentionally or unintentionally, carried the burden of vengeance for someone else.

  • Therapy may interfere with memory. In many instances, a therapist’s role is to validate and assist someone through complicated and long-suffered events. In validating the experience of the patient, the therapist may reinforce certain events and in the process may unintentionally alter their patient’s memory or interpretation of their memory. Uncovering that may be a Sisyphean task.

Practice Tip

“Mine” the records, to the extent they exist, for inconsistencies, bias, other false reports, and history of bias; pursue the records through subpoenas duces tecum and be aware of applicable discovery case law at the right time tactically—you may need to investigate to be able to make a sufficient showing—but once you’ve sought the records, ask the judge to retain them for subsequent motions for further discovery as additional information comes to light (during trial, keep asking for in camera review), and for appeal.

C. Pro-Guilt Confirmation Bias

Find a client who will tell you, “That DHS worker really seemed to be on my side,” or “The detective is going to give me a fair shake.” It’s normal for adults to want to protect children or someone claiming to be a victim of sexual assault, even if there is no corroborating evidence or injuries. DHS workers, law enforcement and child abuse assessment workers see a lot of trauma and people who have been through hard times. So have judges and prosecutors. Defense lawyers, too. Getting your client a fair shake when all evidence seemingly points to guilt is hard. And even worse, the system is blind to systemic bias, maybe even affirms it through guilty pleas coerced through the threat of conviction and convictions after trial.

Practice Tip

Strike the word “victim” from your vocabulary before the trier of fact. If you use it, you implicitly concede the inference that comes with this charged label, unless you consciously and strategically use it to deride something about the state’s case. File a motion to prohibit use of “victim” before the jury by anyone—the judge, the lawyers, and the witnesses, especially the CAAC interviewers and the specially trained detectives. Some courts will grant this motion, others won’t. Until the appellate courts weigh in, it’s an open question. After all, “victim” invades the jury’s providence and constitutes vouching by everyone that uses it; vouching, it should be duly noted, is something with which our appellate courts consistently wrestle and generally show displeasure.

D. Prejudice – Negative Themes

Jurors who have said they’ll set aside their prejudices (but we all know some won’t) cannot. Or, maybe they will have set them aside with good intentions, but the prejudices came barreling through the door during the government’s opening. Prejudice can come in many flavors, from many different directions. The generic prejudices against defendants are intensified in sex cases (“presumed not innocent”; if charges are brought, he must have done it).

E. Undisclosed Sex Crime Victims on the Jury

Some jurors will not disclose the dark secrets of their own past—i.e., they’ve been abused, too. Accept that some have been abused themselves and have never told anyone in their entire life and they are sure as hell not going to tell you in open court.

There are many studies referencing the prevalence of sexual crimes and how they are dramatically underreported. For example, A 2005 study conducted by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention on San Diego Kaiser Permanente HMO members reported that 16 percent of males were sexually abused by the age of 18.4 Department of Justice studies suggest that three percent of American males have been victims of rape or attempted rape. Studies by David Finkelhor, director of the Crimes Against Children Research Center, show that:

  • One in five girls and one in twenty boys is a victim of child sexual abuse.

  • Self-report studies show that twenty percent of adult females and five to ten percent of adult males recall a childhood sexual assault or sexual abuse incident.

  • During a one-year period in the U.S., 16 percent of youth ages 14 to 17
    had been sexually victimized.

  • Over the course of their lifetime, 28 percent of U.S. youth ages 14 to 17
    had been sexually victimized.

  • Children are most vulnerable to child sex abuse between the ages of 7
    and 13.

Voir dire is not likely to ferret out everyone who has been affected of abuse or a sexual assault, so we must accept that we may have unqualified or problem jurors on every single panel where criminal sexual allegations are made.

F. Biased System Players

The ick factor didn’t go away just because there’s a jury trial going on. The presumption of innocence, legal fiction that many believe it to be, only applies to the trier of fact. There is considerable evidence that suggests the system responds to sex crime defendants differently.

G. A Biased Set of Laws

In State v. Williams, Justice Walters brought into the open what we all know—the rules of evidence apply differently in sex cases. Anything which tends to prove guilt seems to be admitted, end of story, irrespective of due process balancing except in the rarest cases, seemingly because our clients “deserve” it.

III. Storytelling – Discovering and Developing Your Theory and Themes

What are the good stories, the winning theories and themes?

In sex crime defense there are no magic wands, no surefire winning shots or approaches. There are many important considerations and approaches. This section is broken down into four parts:

  • Classic Criminal Defense Strategies
  • Presentation Improvement Through Conventions of Other Disciplines, i.e., media, marketing, literature, psychology
  • Unspoken Themes for Every Sex Crimes Case
  • Common Sex Defense Themes

A. Apply Classic Criminal Defense Strategies

Go Back to Square One. In sex crime defense there’s a particular need to apply all we have learned over the years in order to be effective in our presentation in opening, closing, and the whole damn trial long. A few principles can make our presentation better. More than just knowing the facts, good and bad, and presenting them for a good theory, a winning theory is one that is presented the whole trial long in a persuasive manner.

Moreover, the criminal defense bar has much to learn from the way the civil plaintiff’s bar uses techniques employed in the media (especially Fox News) and the advertising industry.
Many of the same techniques the civil bar uses are easily appropriated for a criminal defense practice.

1. Persuasive Theories and Themes Are Simple—Consider Trial a Marketing Campaign

KISS = Keep It Simple Stupid, a basic rule of politics, marketing, and trial strategy. Trial lawyers can learn a lot from politicians and advertising agencies.

As an example, like or agree with President Trump or not, Trump was able to convey quite a lot both pre- and post-election with two words: “Crooked Hillary.” This theme is simple and short, and it conveys tons of information, implicitly summarizes supporting facts, and even better (structurally, keeping politics out of this), it was really difficult for the Democratic Nominee to counter. Other major Trump themes were simple yet effective, too: “Make America Great Again” conveys there are problems to address, the Clintons/Democrats caused them, and Trump can fix them; “I’m With You” as a retort to “I’m with Hillary”; and “Drain the Swamp”—short, simple, active voice.

2. A Persuasive Theory Should Be Refined to One Simple Sentence

A persuasive theory should be refined to one simple sentence, cohesively integrating a
key fact or motivation, supporting the legal theory, and injecting an emotional component pointed to acquittal. Similarly, each theme should be articulated in a short sentence
or phrase.

This is the first thing you will say in opening, a simple powerful statement incorporating or alluding to the best fact or facts, alluding to and refuting the worst facts. Altogether it should be one very well-written paragraph that will guide your entire trial presentation. Subject sentence supported by three more phrases (best) or short sentences (second best).

3. Persuasive Themes Ring True, May Be Hard to Come By

Frame, reframe, reframe (practice and test). Can you tell it to your elderly neighbor, grandmother, etc., and will it recast the prejudice of the reading of the indictment? Will
it get them to sit up and listen with an open mind? Can you credibly recast your client
as the victim of someone’s intentional or unintentional bias, manipulation, incompetence, or lying?

4. A Persuasive Theme Is Crafted With a Pragmatic Understanding of the Challenges

A persuasive theme is crafted with a pragmatic understanding of the challenges of sex crime defense, why the state/accuser is wrong, and anticipates state themes, some implicit.

a. Lack of Physical Evidence

In the overwhelming majority of sex cases, there is no physical evidence of the offense, which cuts two ways: while nothing points to guilt, nothing points to innocence either. Most sex offenses, especially Sex Abuse in the First Degree, do not create physical evidence, absent some highly unusual trace evidence.

b. Common Prosecutor Themes Are Convincing

“He Said/She Said” recast as “He Said/Child Said—Tie Goes to the Child.” Also known as (pick as many as you like):

  • “Children don’t lie about sex abuse” (response: the child was put up to the lie; the child has a serious mental health problem; the child was abused by someone else; the child is mistaking innocent touch; or the child has a false memory which is not a lie).
  • “Why would a child lie about sex abuse?” (response: same as above).
  • “I’ll err on the side of protecting the child” (response: violates the jury’s duty).
  • “Why would the victim put themselves through this if it wasn’t true?” (response: revenge, anger, hate, compulsive liar, mental health).
  • “Our society has a serious problem with sex abuse, and it stops right here,” (response: violates the jury’s duty).

c. The Horrible Prejudice of Sex Crime Allegations

  • The defendant is a monster.
  • Protect children—subthemes:
  • Way more children are abused than report, so inherently more likely this report is true rather than false (response: violates the jury’s duty; unfair/illegal inference).
  • Children wouldn’t/couldn’t/don’t lie about abuse (response: see above).
  • A child shouldn’t have knowledge of erections, ejaculation, or sex specific details, so they must have been abused by this defendant. Four inferences that can be rebutted or attacked, the internet is always a safe place to point the finger:

• the child has knowledge

• the knowledge was obtained by victim’s abuse,

• Defendant abused victim, and

• Defendant’s abuse of this child was the source of this child’s knowledge.

  • Protect women, or #MeToo:
  • A woman wouldn’t lie about being raped (response: revenge, anger, hate, compulsive liar, mental health).
  • No woman would put themselves through this if it wasn’t true

(response: see above).

  • All men are capable of rape (response: individuation necessary here).
  • Protect the person with trauma:
  • Because Trauma = Abuse (response: trauma complicated).
  • Cross-Racial Bias:
  • A (insert race) woman wouldn’t have consensual sex with a (insert different race) man (response: maybe she’s embarrassed or does not want to be judged by family or friends; or modern world has changed).
  • A (insert race) man is more likely to commit rape then a man of my race (response: violates the jury’s duty).

5. Motive-driven Themes Can Address Strengths, Weaknesses, and Persuasive Needs

While there is an endless variety of possible themes, some are reoccurring.

Can you recast the case? Who has a motive to lie? Was there motive transference from spurned spouse to child? Was the child subject to suggestion, intentionally or unintentionally? Are law enforcement, CPS, CAAC biased, incompetent, or poorly trained? Or all three? Can you recast the case where your client is the victim? Where the only fair and just result is to return a verdict of Not Guilty?

  • Motive: Why?—it’s sometimes not enough to call it a lie.

One possible answer to all of the challenges in sex crime defense is the jury will want to know not only whether the accusations are wrong or inaccurate but why, what the motive or reason was behind the false accusation. Often it isn’t enough to simply argue the government’s case is not convincing beyond a reasonable doubt as sex crime allegations are so prejudicial you have to search for and present a reason, a motive, an explanation—the theme of the case.

  • What if you don’t know why the complainant is lying? Or whether it’s a hybrid, i.e., might be lying, might be false memory.

The theme may be, “We don’t always know why people lie, but we know they do and there are certain predictable situations in which people do lie.” When are those situations? Do what everyone does these days and ask the Google. The link here5 is a short segment from Dr. Phil. It’s a fantastic short summary of why people lie, and because it’s been massaged by Hollywood and probably vetted by social scientists (to some unknown degree), it’s really good. And if you don’t like it or want to brainstorm another approach, ask Google (or Bing, Foxfire, YouTube, etc.) as you are bound to find something that rings true to you. Of course, you cannot play this video for the jury, but it’s got the making of a great script for your defense theory and themes.

6. Convincing Themes and Theories Are Aware of and Play to the Heart

UCrJI 1009 instructs the jury that reasonable doubt is “doubt based upon common sense and reason,” and UCrJI 1004 instructs a juror: “must not allow yourself to be influenced at all by personal feelings, sympathy for, or prejudice against anyone involved in the case,” however, after years of trying cases, we are not selling cases to computers for analysis but rather to flesh and blood people who certainly are inclined to “like” the complainant, “fear” the defendant, and thus convict.

Some people profess to be logic driven, others follow their hearts. While these paradigms can be described many ways, good themes and theories appeal to emotions, encouraging jurors to reframe facts in issue in your client’s favor.

B. Themes Presentation Can Be Improved by Using Conventions of Other Disciplines

1. Use the Internet

Use a curious mind and the power of the Internet to brainstorm and polish your presentation.

The web and podcast worlds abound with interesting social science information on how people relate to various circumstances and how the brain works. This is a great place to research how to present your themes and theories. Listening to podcasts and watching online has an advantage over attending CLEs—you can go back again and again to listen to the experts, hear their descriptions, and rehearse your themes and theories—imagine how Dr. Loftus would teach the jury.

There are also very provocative podcasts on virtually every topic relevant to sex crimes defense issues. One of the author’s current favorites is NPR’s Hidden Brain, hosted by Shankar Vedantam, who recently has covered on-point topics:

2. Consider the Reptile Brain

Flip and reframe the state’s theory and cast the defendant as a vulnerable victim by using established patterns of human behavior in your theory and theme development and presentation.

Deep inside the skull of every one of us there is something like a brain of a crocodile. Surrounding the R-complex is the limbic system or mammalian brain, which evolved tens of millions of years ago in ancestors who were mammal but not yet primates. It is a major source of our moods and emotions, of our concern and care for the young. And finally, on the outside, living in uneasy truce with the more primitive brains beneath, is the cerebral cortex; civilization is a product of the cerebral cortex.

Carl Sagan, from his seminal 1980 book, Cosmos.11

Sex cases provide particular problems because in developing, polishing, and presenting our case theory, we may have to reframe it all and be tempted to try to prove innocence.12 Because a natural human instinct is to “protect vulnerable victims,” our best theory may be to flip or reframe the case to show (or raise a reasonable possibility, i.e., logical doubt) the defendant is a vulnerable victim of:

  • a spurned lover or spouse
  • a dangerously manipulative child, or one without a conscience
  • a rush to judgment by a detective, DHS worker, or DDA, which could be a result of: bias, incompetence, laziness, lack of training, failure to follow training protocols or best practices, failure to consider alternatives (differential diagnosis), etc.

Recasting may involve:

  • A Story of Injustice
  • The Story of Client’s Actual Innocence
  • A Story of a System that Overly Favors the Prosecution
  • The Story of the Client’s Life
  • The Story of the Complainant’s Broken Life

When you flip and reframe the prosecution’s case, you appeal to what the civil plaintiff’s bar calls the “Reptile Brain,” developed at length by authors Don C. Keenan (successful trial lawyer) and David Ball (influential trial consultant, author of Ball on Damages, now in its third edition). Their treatise, Reptile: The 2009 Manual of the Plaintiff’s Revolution, argues you can persuade jurors by appealing to and activating the “oldest” part of the brain that is responsible for survival instincts. Reptile posits the human brain has three different levels of functioning and that the base level, the reptilian complex, is essentially unchanged in humans from more simple animals such as reptiles.

While the “reptile” model of human brain development has come under criticism from a “brain science” perspective, there are other convincing sociological and anthropological models that support this approach,13 and irrespective of why this approach works, it does.

By framing your case as one about community safety, the case becomes about empowering the jury to improve community safety through their verdict—in a civil case by awarding buckets of cash, here an acquittal. Reptile makes a convincing argument to deemphasize theories based solely on logic and reasoning and refocus instead on danger and community safety. If you can, flip the emotional impact to:

  • A lying complainant.
  • A biased investigator or investigation (so the complainant is a victim, too).
  • A child who is being used by a vindictive ex (so the complainant is a victim, too).
  • The jury is then an ally in defense of public safety.

The jury will understand community safety calls for acquittal, not conviction.

3. Consider Integrating “Rules of the Road”

Framing and flipping the prosecutor’s case is not an easy task, but another civil plaintiff’s technique can be especially helpful to consider. In Rules of the Road: A Plaintiff Lawyer’s Guide to Proving Liability,14 the authors Rick Friedman and Patrick Malone, both trial lawyers, suggest approaching cases with a certain set of case-specific rules to which everyone can agree, which further the cause of public safety and which scream for a verdict for the plaintiff—with the unspoken theme that a plaintiff’s verdict protects everyone.

Public safety can apply equally in sex crimes defense, especially in child sex cases, as there are a lot of rules designed to make investigations and the resulting prosecutions:

  • more reliable
  • based on the impartial, unbiased gathering of high quality, accurate evidence, and
  • which are designed to carefully preserve the evidence from contamination.

The basic framework for employing Rules of the Road suggests we should:

a. Write out a simple, clear, uncontroversial list of “rules” of the case—these have been gifted to us by the Oregon Interviewing Guidelines (3d ed., 2012), even if the third edition is watered down, and Tell Me What Happened: Structured Investigative Interviews of Child Victims and Witnesses.15

b. Be prepared to explain the rationale for these rules in opening and design your defense around the rules—make sure the rules are about safety and the reliability of evidence, thereby protecting everyone.

c. Prepare a hard exhibit (foam board) with the rules on them, use it in opening, during cross of state’s experts and direct with yours, leave it so it is available the whole trial long, have experts agree to it and maybe even initial it, make it an exhibit, and send it to the jury room.

By doing this, you empower the jury to stick to the rules and by their verdict tell the complaining witness/vulnerable person, “I’m sorry, there is too much doubt around the reliability and accuracy of your testimony.”

The other convenient thing is that the state, in control of the investigation, was in control of how witnesses were interviewed (blame the detective or CAAC interviewer), or if the complaining witness’s memory was contaminated by mom, grandma, school teacher, etc., no one, including the defendant, is to blame because 1) contamination occurred by an unwitting well-meaning person and 2) there was nothing the detective, CAAC interviewer or DDA could do about it, the memory was spoiled.

C. Unspoken Themes for Every Sex Crimes Case

“Until you make the unconscious conscious, it will direct your life and you will call it fate.” ― C.G. Jung

The same thing applies to trials. Get the bad stuff out there, cultivate the positive.

1. Unspoken Theme: Be Credible

Further down, this chapter addresses credibility in the context of what the author calls “relational voir dire”—which is designed to foster openness and trust with the jury. As the defendant’s voice in the courtroom, you are producer, director, investigator, inquisitor of state witnesses, casting agent (to a certain degree), screenwriter, pitch person, etc. For your theory and themes to have any chance, you have to be in unison with your message and theme through preparation, emotional intensity, and connection. Anything you do before the finder of fact that diminishes your credibility should be cast aside.

2. Unspoken Theme: The Defendant Is Likeable (Not Dangerous)

A client who is “a child only a mother could love” is probably going to be found guilty.

Face it, this is a reoccuring problem in all sex crimes cases—clients who have done something wrong, even if a lot less than charged, are often very difficult clients to like. That’s a normal reaction. It’s the same reaction the prosecutor, judge, detective, complainant, CAAC interviewer, and probably the jury has, too. We are wired to protect children and vulnerable persons. We do it without thinking. We have reptile brains.

The completely innocent sex crimes defendants are often also difficult to like. They may have made some mistakes, even if they are not criminal mistakes. Moreover, sex crimes defendants, when completely innocent, sometimes present themselves as serious victims who cannot countenance how they could be accused of this offense because they are a good person.

The minute the jury walks into the courtroom they will judge you and your client, and they will do so based on how you look, act, and interact with your client. In a sex crimes case judgment is on steroids, and your client needs a counter-narrative. Fortunately, in most cases likeability can be established to some degree, but it has to start with the premise that you judge your client’s case, not your client. Sex cases are fraught with moral judgment. Either you confront it in opening with a compelling counter-narrative or you stand a high likelihood of losing. Hell, you stand a high likelihood of losing even if your narrative blows the jury away.

Plus, likeability is an implicit signal that your client is not a threat, not a danger. The jury wants to see and believe this.

a. Know your client and show the jury you like your client.

Ok, we are lawyers, not actors. As your client’s advocate, subtle and appropriate signals of your personal interest in the outcome go a long way. The jury will know if you care or don’t.

Find something to like in your client. In over 25 years of practice representing all kinds of people in serious cases, I can count on one hand the clients in whom I could not find anything redeeming in their life. Sometimes empathy was triggered by the client’s deep scars caused by social poverty, horrible parenting, or being victims themselves. Other times I focus on the positive nature of my client’s close relationship with someone else that, while dysfunctional, reveals a damaged, hurt but authentic person. Sometimes horribly scarred clients have revealed personal intimate details, and their brave act has triggered empathy. Or maybe they resemble an old friend in their speech or appearance. Find anything to hang your hat on.

Treat your client with respect. It’s more than being a cold, calculating professional: “How are you today?” But really ask. And really listen. Maybe reveal something about yourself. Look in their eyes and see a scared, wounded individual who is imperfect like the rest of us.

Consider that we all just might be sexual deviants—which doesn’t mean we are criminals or have acted on it. Sex is impossibly hard for almost everyone to discuss. Professor Jesse Bering wrote a fascinating book a few years ago, Perv: The Sexual Deviant in All of Us,16 which argues that we all have deep, dark secrets and desires we have probably never told anyone about or ever acted on because they live in our imagination and fantasy world. We set healthy boundaries and expectations for ourselves and keep these deep, dark secrets buried in our imaginations and fantasies, or maybe just tamp them down.

This may be why so many clients who keep and collect child pornography images show disgust and distaste for the notion they might act out in the real world. Often, these clients are good parents with partners shocked at what was found in your client’s virtual e-world. And often these clients pass full disclosure and “no hands-on offenses” polygraphs.

b. Demonstrate to the jury you like your client through:

  • Positive interactions with client before the jury, which includes your interactions and your witnesses’—let both know that the jury will be watching how each responds to the other.
  • Nonverbal communication.
  • Projecting genuine concern for your client throughout trial.
  • Humanizing and softening your client at every opportunity.

One way I do this is warning my client during trial prep that when we talk during trial, we are going to whisper because whispers don’t carry. I warn them that this means I’m going to get up close so they can hear me and that they can do the same. I warn them that I am likely to put my arm around them to get close enough. It’s natural in the courtroom, not fake at all, since the jury should expect our conversations will be private. It’s also intimate and sends a strong message that I like and do not fear my client.

Two short illustrations:

First, years ago I was called to jury duty and sat in a venire on a murder by child abuse case. I sat in the box for over an hour taking it all in, and over time began to notice the defense lawyer leaned ever so slightly away from the defendant the whole time, they never spoke, and their icy relationship began to infuse the courtroom. I knew the lawyer to be experienced and trial-tested in sex cases, but this one consistent gesture, a slight lean away and with no evidence to counter it, told me his client was unlikeable and probably guilty.

The second story takes place around the same time as I tried a difficult case—my client was charged with robbing and assaulting his wheelchair-bound former girlfriend, an amputee. During a recess the judge, very experienced as a judge and former prosecutor, cautioned me that jurors “do not like lawyers who touch their clients, put their arms around them during trial.” I liked the client, sometimes I still wonder what became of his life. The judge, who earlier said to me, “You’re going to try this case?” was shocked by my client’s acquittal.

c. Choose witnesses who support your theory of likeability.

For example, character witnesses have broader utility well beyond the very few, very limited, “magic questions” allowed (“I have formed an opinion and the opinion is…” “I am aware of defendant’s reputation and defendant’s reputation is…”).

i. “Peas in a Pod” – The Defendant

Choose witnesses, including character witnesses, that are likeable. Likeable witnesses, even if not character witnesses, convey important information to the jury. Ones that are likeable and, even better, prominent and well-liked members of the community, are nonverbal character witnesses even when not asked the limited character witness questions. A nonverbal character witness is one who, through their choice of associating with your client, gives implicit testimony.

ii. “Good Peas in a Pod” – The Complainant

Look for and call witnesses similarly situated to the complainant. If client abused a family member, what similarly situated witnesses (other nieces, nephews, cousins of similar age) can testify that client is truthful or has a reputation for sexual propriety? Can they testify as to other issues? Their in-court rapport with your client may convey an important nonverbal message that client is not a danger.

Be aware, too, of the “Cheerleader Effect”— jurors will look at who is in the gallery and how they interact with your client both in court and (if your client is out of custody) elsewhere during breaks. Your client’s support group should be well behaved, respectful of the jury’s privacy, and hopefully appear to be decent, respectable people. Tell them what you expect of them, directly, and police their behavior. You will gain kudos from the court staff and from the jury, and it will hopefully run downstream to your client.

iii. “The Sweetest Peas in the Pod” – Complainant’s Parent(s) and Your Client’s Significant Other

In a child sex case, for obvious reasons, these are probably the most important witnesses to have on your side or at least neutral. Do everything you can to have a positive relationship with them. Sometimes their opinions of your client will change over time, for better or worse. Do whatever you can to cultivate a good relationship and an open mind—the jury knows they know your client and the complainant better than anyone else in the world—they just may hold the keys to the jailhouse.

iv. “Bad Peas in a Pod” – The Complainant

Just as our client is judged by the peas in his pod, so will the complainant. Does the complainant have friends or family members that project poorly? If called?

v. Help all witnesses feel comfortable by offering pretrial preparation.

If a witness, even a nominal state witness, is nervous because of the venue (likely), the jury may mistake nervousness for nefarious reasons (don’t like the client, are put up to lie, etc.). Offering to prepare the witness serves multiple purposes.

First, it may help put the witness at ease in the courtroom. Second, it may further your rapport with the witness, something which the witness may convey in the quality of their spoken and nonverbal communication. Third, the witness may have something new to add as witnesses who prepare and think about their testimony may “jog” new facts.

vi. Be conscious of and cultivate positive nonverbal communication in pretrial preparation.

In sex cases, jurors tend to watch the personal interaction between witnesses and the defendant more intensely than others, looking for nonverbal clues such as eye contact, connection, concern—intangibles that speak volumes. They will also watch how you, the lawyer, interacts with witnesses. Both can have an outsized influence on your other implicit themes such as likeability and credibility, and may personalize and humanize your client.

vii. Prepare character witnesses to carefully explain the basis for their opinion.

While they usually cannot testify as to details about your client’s good deeds, they can testify how they know your client and have observed them in summary fashion. The tightrope is how limited your presentation will be. For a myriad of reasons, judges don’t see character evidence very often, so they can be “on guard” to make sure they don’t go beyond the “magic questions.” Loosen the rope as much as you can: “I met [client] through our church community service arm…” and have them testify as to any other issues pertinent to the case. Ushering them up to the stand for just three or four questions has limited utility. The longer they are on the stand, the more opportunity the jury has to like them, and by transference, your client.

viii. Continue to check in with witnesses and with your themes throughout trial.

Things change, especially in sex cases. Sometimes witnesses change their minds. Some witnesses, even those considered very friendly, melt down before their testimony, and it’d sure be nice to know this before they are staring down at you from the stand. If you speak with them, you might be able to reassure them or, alternatively, reconsider if you are going to call them at all. Be realistic—the witness is under pressure from your client or your client’s family (this happens in inter-familial sex crimes cases), or maybe they are simply terrified of the stand. Stage fright is real for actors, lawyers and witnesses.

3. Unspoken Theme: The Topic of Sex Is Awkward and Full of Judgment

That’s the reality of sex crimes defense. Many, if not most, people have a hard time talking about sexual topics in their lives, even with life partners, and we expect them to talk about it in a room full of people during voir dire and to listen to gruesome details all trial long. Desensitize the jury as soon as possible, address the elephant in the room, and consider confessing fears and concerns.

D. Common Sex Defense Themes and Approaches

A recent article by Keith Belzer in The Champion on themes in sexual assault cases (see the appendix, reprinted with permission) provides an excellent discussion of common themes and subthemes, which include,

  • Adult Accusation – Regret
  • Internal Regret of an Impulsive Decision – perhaps resistance lowered by intoxicants
  • Accuser: “I must have been too drunk to consent.”
  • Avoidance of Being Judged/Blamed by Others for Sexual Choices
  • Friend: “I cannot believe you had sex with Bill, he’s so gross/nerdy/creepy.”
  • Accuser: “I said no, but he didn’t stop/forced himself.”
  • Regret of Consequences for Current Relationship for Relations with Third Party
  • Accuser: “He didn’t hear me when I said ‘no’”; or “I was roofied.”
  • Avoidance of Personal Responsibility, Both Parties Drank Too Much
  • Accuser: “I didn’t consent.”
  • Accuser: “I drank too much, but he knew what he was doing.”
  • Adult Accusation – Retribution
  • Punishment for Rejection
  • Accuser “If I cannot have him, no one will. He’ll pay.”
  • Child Accusation – False Memory

Apart from Belzer’s summation of common themes, other rhetorical themes play into almost every defense.

1. Embrace Reasonable Doubt

Over the years I have heard dozens and dozens of stories and comments from colleagues that equate a reasonable doubt defense with a long-shot home run, akin to “I’ll do it if I have to, but it sends a signal that client is guilty.”

Reasonable doubt isn’t just a valid approach, it’s something we should embrace and continue to refine and rethink our approach—how we explain it to juries, especially in
sex cases.

Civil jury consultant guru David Ball has recently published a short book, David Ball on Criminal Defense (David Ball 2016) with several other authors. The first chapter, the only penned by Ball, is thought-provoking and provides a cohesive approach to dealing with reasonable doubt throughout trial. Among other things, Ball helps explain:

  • If we do not focus on reasonable doubt, the jury is likely to burden-shift and think the defense must prove innocence.
  • By focusing on reasonable doubt, we diffuse the tug-of-war over who is right.
  • Once the jury thinks we are trying to prove innocence, they will require we in fact

do that.

  • Focus on doubts which are case-determinative—i.e., any possibility that, if true, means client is acquitted.
  • Any doubt that the jury can hold without abandoning reason is a reasonable doubt.
  • The size of the doubt does not matter as long as it is case-determinative.
  • The prosecutor’s burden is to rule out every case-determinative possibility, for if they do not there is still doubt.
  • Reasonable conclusions can contradict, i.e., if the jury sees two reasonable possibilities, that is the essence of reasonable doubt.

I have never heard this explained in such a cogent and effective fashion. In Ball’s words:

If the Prosecutor does not logically rule out every reasonable doubt, the decision is made for you [the jury]: Not guilty. During deliberations, if anyone wants you to vote “guilty” even when you think there’s a logical possibility […] they are asking you to violate the law.

Ball’s chapter covers a lot of ground regarding how to use the theme of reasonable doubt throughout trial in a manner which, this author suggests, makes it a must-read.

2. Context Is Everything

All too often prosecutors’ cases are flat, lacking depth, and pulled out of the context of your client’s life, whether it is out of context about one drunken evening, a relationship developed over years, or disregards all the foibles and problems in the complaining witness’s life, which can be chronic lying, manipulation and behavior that “if only the jury knew” would mean certain acquittal.

Functionally, almost every event in life is viewed subjectively through the lens of people’s past, and when we put context before the jury it may shift the field, undermine descriptions, or create doubt. “Context is everything” is a dynamic and helpful subtheme that supports virtually every defense. It is a rule with which virtually every witness will agree if asked. And it’s easy to weave rhetorically throughout trial, reminding the jury that this is your theme.

“Let’s discuss the context of that night…”

“So, let me be sure I understand the context, you had five drinks, right?”

“Let me ask you about the context of the child’s disclosure, she said that in response to a leading question, right?”

Context is a useful buzzword, which if set up in opening can be visited and revisited throughout the trial.

3. The Jury Deserves to Hear Reliable and Accurate Evidence

This is, again, a rule that virtually every witness will accept, especially detectives and CAAC interviewers. After all, who doesn’t want the most accurate and reliable evidence? And if they don’t get that, isn’t that the road to reasonable doubt? It too is an easy theme to weave in to cross and direct, fantastic buzzwords to set up in opening and revisit throughout trial:

“There are protocols known to increase reliability?

“And protocols that are known to increase accuracy?

“And types of questions which are known to increase that we don’t get the most reliable and accurate evidence?”

Other similar attacks, independent of protocols that may not apply directly to your case, undermine reliability and accuracy of evidence, such as bias, mental health issues, intoxication. Other buzzwords to assist in this approach include “source confusion” and “confirmation bias.”

IV. Storytelling in Trial= – Begins With Credible Voir Dire

Credibility: cultivating trust and openness supports every sex crimes defense theory and theme.

Every moment in front of the jury affords opportunities to provide positive, negative, and neutral impressions.

Voir dire is not a place to tell traditional trial stories, yet it’s crucial to the storytelling process.

1) The jury probably already has a negative impression of you and your client from when the judge read the indictment.

2) The minimal voir dire typically conducted before you get up has conditioned the jury to give short answers and avoid raising their hands or they’ll risk embarrassment.

3) Voir dire is the only time in trial the defense gets to go first.

4) Voir dire is the first opportunity the defense gets to set the stage for the trial defense.

This manual has a separate chapter on voir dire—but voir dire is the one crucial place in trial that can significantly support theory and theme development and establish the defense team as the most credible voice in the courtroom. To do so, it’s worth considering whether or not traditional methods will create the atmosphere of trust and openness necessary for the defense’s themes and theories.

It may be a delicate tightrope to walk while making sufficient inquiry to establish challenges for cause and make intelligent use of peremptory challenges. A few considerations:

A. Generously Report the Length of Trial

Things often go wrong at trial when counsel under-reports the projected length of trial. Everyone gets stressed out, the judge pushes the timeframe, and you are stressed out over scheduling. Moreover, things never run on time. The first place a judge’s impatience can show is voir dire, and that’s a bad place to set an antagonistic tone for the trial if it can be avoided.

Solution? Generously estimate the length your case will last—it’s probably more realistic than what you “think,” and if you are wrong and the trial is shorter, everyone is happy. A buffer and forewarning sometimes helps to preempt impatience that can undermine your credibility as the jury is likely to credit the judge with knowing “how things are done correctly,” and raising the ire of the judge is a risk not worth taking unless absolutely necessary. When there is ample time for trial, you are more likely to be afforded ample time for voir dire and to build rapport with the jury. Similarly, good and thorough voir dire will set the jurors’ expectation that you will be thorough and respectful. A sex crimes defense generally requires more time at every stage.

B. Don’t Use Voir Dire to Improperly “Condition” the Jurors

There’s a world of difference between improper conditioning and proper conditioning, and I freely admit that for years I improperly conditioned jurors, selling my defense theory in voir dire. There are at least five meaningful problems with this.

First, if you are allowed to condition improperly, you will spend too much time talking, which in turn steals precious time from listening and letting jurors articulate their biases. Second, some jurors will know it’s part of the “pitch” and will lock down and not trust you—so you lose credibility. Third, it’s boring, so you will lose credibility for wasting the jury’s time. Fourth, if your judge clamps down, you will lose credibility with the jury because the jury will implicitly trust the judge until there is strong evidence convincing them otherwise—which rarely happens. Fifth, if you are a good listener, you will gain credibility with the jurors.

Notice there’s a theme to these points, and frankly it’s a crucial unspoken subtheme in every sex crimes defense—

DO EVERYTHING TO MAXIMIZE CREDIBILITY WITH THE JURY

C. Foster an Honest Dialogue with the Jury

Really good voir dire fosters a nonjudgmental, open, honest dialogue with the jury and may help jurors keep an open mind through the state’s opening.

Voir dire can be very entertaining and engaging, and through it you just may gain good will and “license” for the whole trial. In a sex crimes defense you will need this—especially to get through the prosecution’s opening statement and all the judgment from the jury that will come with it—to have a chance of jurors keeping open minds. If you are entertaining and engaging, the judge will give you “license” to question the venire much longer than you expect and will:

MAXIMIZE CREDIBILITY WITH THE JURY IN VOIR DIRE

D. Set Reasonable Time and Manner Expectations

Proper voir dire goes to the qualifications of a juror to sit on a case. While this isn’t much guidance, that’s maybe a good thing. The author takes it to mean counsel has a right to question (and hopefully mostly listen to) every single prospective juror on the panel. In voir dire, I set that expectation early and explicitly:

“I’ll speak with each of you, individually, and if it gets too personal, let me know and I’ll do my best to respect that, but you can ask for individual voir dire if at any time it gets too uncomfortable.”

Regarding the latter, I have never had a judge tell me no, although I now tell the judge pretrial my intent to give the jury this option. And by showing jurors respect and concern, and by conducting “relational voir dire,” the voir dire process sometimes works really, really well. And the better it works from the get-go, the more leeway the judge tends to give you.

Call on people early who are interesting, maybe funny, and/or ready to reveal personal details. Thank them when they do (“Thanks for sharing”), ask appropriate follow-up (“Would it be ok if I ask a follow-up question?” or “If it’s ok, can I ask you to tell me more about that” or “I’m curious...”), and use their answers to open other doors (“Who else feels the same way and is open to sharing their experiences?”).

IT MAXIMIZES CREDIBILITY WITH THE JURY

E. Always Be Sensitive to Casting the Defendant as Likeable

Because the jury will watch and scrutinize your interactions with your client, if the jury likes and trusts you (i.e., you are credible) and you project that you like and trust your client, your positive personal interactions with your client might or might not help but they certainly will not hurt your client. Be aware of this from the get-go.

V. Storytelling in Opening – Argue Without Arguing

The most common objection in opening is that counsel is “arguing” the case. The objection derives from the bland statutory and historical understanding of what an opening statement is.

Nonetheless, don’t have any doubt that you are arguing your case, absolutely so. You are arguing your case in opening. Repeat after me: You are arguing your case in opening. In fact, the whole trial long you are arguing your case. Every minute, everything you’re doing. It’s just that at each stage you have different constraints, different formats. In opening, you argue it by setting forth the good facts, telling them like you were actually there.

Tell a damn good story—it’s a lot more credible than anything else you can more transparently “argue.” In fact, when it comes down to it, you should be thankful for this format. It lets you build credibility with the jury so you don’t just look like another biased lawyer.

Tell a damn good story—it won’t “technically” be argument and you’ll be credible. What could help your client more than being a credible advocate?

The line between an opening statement and an opening argument may seem to be determined by little more than how willing you are to risk a sustained objection or two. Some lawyers are loathe to object to opposing counsel’s opening in light of the fact that the most common judicial
response is a rote explanation to the jury, “The statements of counsel are not evidence.
Continue.”

On the other hand, some lawyers are unwilling to risk tactics that are objectionable because they think it will hurt their credibility with the jury, or may risk disposing the judge against them. It’s up to you in your own personal litigation style, considered on a case-by-case basis, to make the judgment call.

Practice Tip: Preempt the “Argument” Objection

Be aware that even very experienced judges sometimes have a hard time with really great unobjectionable opening statements, largely because a really great story can be so persuasive the judge may start thinking they are argumentative even when they are not. That’s why opening statements can be the toughest part of a defense case. Be proactive and occasionally sprinkle your opening with “The evidence will show….” Merely saying “occasionally” may preempt the “argument” objection and give you enough to convince the judge: “I’m just telling the jury what the evidence will show, that’s the purpose of opening statements.” Surprisingly simple and effective.

  • You Can Be Persuasive in Opening Without Arguing
  • The beginning is the very most important.
  • Tell your story with active verbs.
  • Put jurors in the moment.

However, a persuasive narrative format has limitations. Although there is an expectation that the statement be persuasive, pervasive arguments are likely to be met with sustained objections and an impatient judge. Therefore, well-prepared counsel must carefully craft the opening statement’s three parts (introduction, body and conclusion) to carefully and persuasively set forth the defense theories and the facts that support those theories.

A persuasive story starts with facts, good and bad, and accommodates all of them. In every case there are facts beyond dispute and facts which can be disputed. Which category each respective fact fits into may change over time when investigation makes a (formerly) credible witness vulnerable to attack for bias, veracity problems, or other reasons. A persuasive theory, though, does not ignore the bad facts.

Approach an opening statement with the following concepts in mind:

A. Primacy

The first thing you say in opening is very important, maybe the most important thing you’ll say the whole trial. Primacy means that jurors tend to remember what was said first, thus what is said first is very important.

Spend half of your prep time dedicated to your opening statement on the very first thing you say. Make it simple and punchy, and bring it to the heart—not necessarily the intellectual core—of your case. One sentence—that’s all—giving the theme, the thrust of your defense, and do it with buzzwords to which you can return all trial long, such as:

  • Context is everything
  • Source confusion
  • Contamination
  • Biased investigation
  • Rush to judgment
  • One-sided investigation
  • Confirmation bias, or “blinders on.”

B. Recency

Your conclusion is almost as important as the first thing you say, but your introduction will write your conclusion. Recency means that jurors tend to remember what was said last, so make it good and memorable.

C. Rhetoric in Opening

A little more rhetoric, or argument, is tolerated at the very beginning and very end of your opening statement, especially if you are summarizing your theory or the main defense thrust. But for the most part plain rhetoric is objectionable so it should be avoided. That does not mean that the story told in opening cannot be effective and persuasive. In fact, it better be.

Weave the rhetoric into facts.

“We believe the evidence will show the complainant is not credible because…”
[supply facts which demonstrate bias, inconsistencies, etc., and explain who will testify as to those facts].

D. Don’t Make Promises You Cannot Keep

Be wary of over committing to specific facts, evidence, or witnesses, and whether or not your client will testify.

E. Storytelling

Tell a good story in narrative chronological form. A damn good story is persuasive, maybe more persuasive than anything else you can do.

F. Passion

You are the voice of your client! Let the jury know it, let them feel it.

VI. Storytelling and Themes in Cross – Keep Telling Your Story, Sometimes Creatively

The ability to tell stories in cross-examination is sometimes underestimated, but it shouldn’t be. It may seem strange, but an organized and well-planned cross-examination provides an excellent venue for storytelling and theme development in ways that direct really doesn’t. Because the defense lawyer can provide a clear and direct narrative in leading questions, it’s in many ways easier to tell your client’s story in cross than in direct where your witness/client is merely guided by your open-ended questions.

Two examples. First, a fantastic example of cross-examination as storytelling in support of a case theme can be found in renowned criminal lawyer Roy Black’s defense in State of Florida v. William Kennedy Smith, much of which can be found online or in other digital formats. In that case, Smith, nephew of Senator Ted Kennedy, was accused of rape, tried, and ultimately acquitted before a Palm Beach jury. The defense’s attack was multi-pronged, but in just about four minutes of powerful cross, Black demonstrated beyond any question that the accuser’s close friend did not believe the defendant was dangerous just minutes after the alleged (and obviously false18 claim of) rape.

What Roy Black did was simple, yet masterful. The complainant alleged she was assaulted deep within the Kennedy compound, which one can understand is rather large, well landscaped, and somewhat private as the “compounds” of American political royalty are accustomed. After the alleged assault, the complainant solicited her friend to help find her lost shoe—who knows her state, she may have been hysterical or theatrical, but it seemed like a really stupid request from someone who claims she was just sexually assaulted.

Irrespective of the complainant’s state, Black led the friend through a full “tour” of all the places she looked that night for her friend’s lost shoe with Smith, which included dark and what would be quite dangerous corners had the complainant’s friend believed Smith to be dangerous. The effect was, You never would have gone to these dark and secluded places with Smith if you thought him dangerous, so obviously the complainant’s bestie didn’t believe her—acquit!

Magic. Not fancy, but selling the theme one hundred percent. Acquit. The power of this cross is that Black credits the jury as being smart enough to be insiders, and it happened at the start of cross (primacy), so forever more this witness has been properly conditioned and at the same time conditioning the jury that the complainant’s bestie was not afraid of Smith.

Another example. I tried a sex case a few years ago where the detective conducted an in-depth field interview, then later conducted the forensic interview “which just couldn’t wait for a fully trained CAAC interviewer to arrive” (sarcasm), even though bio-mom was protective and dad agreed to stay away pending the investigation. When the detective made incessant excuses (“there are no perfect interviews,” “the guidelines are not rules,” and “it’s too hard to follow the guidelines,” “the child was too young to have a by-the-book interview”), the author was at first perplexed. The Oregon Interviewing Guidelines (3d ed), give so much wiggle room that all of those excuses can
be justified.

Instead of directly attacking the detective’s suppositions, counsel simply went along, made simple supportive points (“ground rules make the evidence more reliable,” “open-ended questions make the evidence more reliable,” “you are less likely to get accurate info…”) and didn’t engage in a prolonged fight. Instead, the next witness was the child “complainant.” While her direct focused on her CAAC statement, virtually verbatim, cross went a different and completely unplanned direction. She was crossed—Tell Me What Happened in hand as a visible guide and prop and in an obvious and direct fashion following the protocols—about a day at the park that had happened a few months prior. The child gave remarkably rich details in response to simple open-ended questions. I looked at the jury intermittently and when I did, smiling faces looked back at me—the risky maneuver worked. The jury got it.

You can also further the theme of a biased investigation in cross. Here’s an alt-take learned from Rich Cohen, author of Chapter 6, who reaches out to a sex crime complainant with a “victim’s rights letter,” and if the door is shut he turns the knife in cross:

“Ms. Witness, you received a letter from my licensed investigator, correct?

“My letter let you know right away I was representing Mr. Defendant, correct?

“In my letter, I respectfully asked to speak with you before trial, isn’t that true?

“In that letter I informed you that to do my job properly, it’s important that I speak with you before trial, correct?

“And because the stakes are high, it’s important for me to meet you to clear up any questions before trial?

“That it’s my responsibility to ask this one-time inconvenience of you?

“In my search for the truth?

“Incidentally, you’ve spoken with Ms. Prosecutor and Mr. Detective many, many times before trial, correct?

“You met when you made your report?

“And before grand jury?

“And after grand jury?

“And again at the DA’s office?

“And before the first time we were supposed to try this case?

“And many times on the phone?

“And sometimes when you met with the prosecutor and the detective it was in person?

“And when you did, you could see they weren’t taking notes?

“There is no way you can accurately describe all of the things that were said in those conversations?

“Precisely what questions were asked?

“Precisely what you said in response?

“And how long that meeting was? [Ask about each.]

“Now, back to my letter, I let you know we could talk at any time or place that you requested, correct?

“And I promised to be sensitive to the nature of the situation and be respectful of you, correct?

“And you could have anyone you wanted with you?

“Including the DDA?

“In fact, I told you that you had the right to have the DDA present?

“You weren’t told the same thing by the detective of the DDA were you, that I had a right to be there, or my investigator?

“I told you that you could stop the interview at any time?

“You weren’t told the same thing by the detective of the DDA, were you?

“I told you if you wanted, we could record the conversation to make sure we get the most accurate and reliable statement possible?

“You weren’t given that option by the DDA or detective, were you?

“And I told you if you wanted a copy of the recording, I’d give it to you to refresh your memory?

“And the DDA too?

“You were told by the DDA and detective they needed to prepare you to testify?

“To make your testimony more convincing?

“To clear up misconceptions?

“To make sure you get your story straight, correct?

“And they gave you feedback didn’t they?

“As to what to say?

“And how to say it?

“How to choose the right words.

“So, is it fair to say that this time I’m speaking with you in court is the first time we’ve ever spoken?

“On any topic?

“But particularly the serious allegations that have been made?

“Which can have life-long consequences for Mr. Defendant?”

This type of cross is storytelling, conveys the unfair nature of the proceeding, and sets the stage for arguments as to bias, source confusion, reliability and accuracy.

VII. Storytelling and Themes in Cross and Direct – Telling Your Story with Buzzwords

Another wonderful technique is to choose thematic buzzwords you can use again and again in your cross of the state’s witnesses. Use them frequently. In a false memory case, use the themes discussed above, “most reliable and accurate evidence”:

“There are protocols known to increase reliability?

“And protocols that are known to increase accuracy?

“And types of questions which are known to increase that we don’t get the most reliable and accurate evidence?

“And you were trained in these protocols that are designed to increase reliability and accuracy…

“And which are designed to not interfere with memory?”

When you use key thematic buzzwords in opening, and repeat them in direct and cross, you signal the jury what you are doing and you remind them of your opening.

VIII. Storytelling with Intimate and Vulnerable Direct – Especially With Your Client

The driving principal for storytelling in direct is to determine how each witness supports your themes and theories, without overtly supporting the government’s. All of the above themes and theories are crucial to review before crafting your direct strategy.

Probably the most important themes for a testifying client to support are the unspoken themes of credibility and likeability, which in turn support the theme that client is not dangerous. But how to do that in a courtroom full of judgment and terrible trauma? And a client who is terrified to get on the stand and maybe not very bright?

A well-prepared witness, especially your client, who is not defensive but is instead vulnerable and open can reach jurors and create incredible intimacy, a bond which fosters reasonable doubt. But, it’s incredibly hard to do this if you do not know your witness/client well, do not like your witness/client, or help them open up on the scary witness stand. This requires time and trust, hard work and preparation, but can be rewarded in many ways, including all of the nonverbal communication techniques the jury will be looking for and relying upon, especially in a sex crimes case.

IX. Storytelling in Closing

Closing styles and techniques very widely, but the best place to start is to review your themes and theories carefully in light of the principles discussed here, and drive home how they all fit into creating the reasonable doubt that will send your client home.
