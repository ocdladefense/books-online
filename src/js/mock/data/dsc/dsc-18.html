Chapter 18. Child Pornography

By Per Olson, Megan McVicar, and Josiah Roloff

I. Introduction

A. Scope

This chapter addresses offenses that fall under the heading, “Visual Recording of Sexual Conduct of Children” in Chapter 163 of the Oregon Criminal Code, ORS 163.665 through ORS 163.693. This class of offenses is often identified by the euphemistic phrase, “child pornography.”

The focus here is on state statutes and case law, not federal law. However, because of the very real possibility that a state prosecution will become a federal prosecution, the state practitioner should have a general understanding of what the client could face in that arena. If nothing else, the state practitioner must know enough about federal law to discern that a federal prosecution is something to be avoided, if possible. Therefore, a brief discussion of the U.S. attorney’s criteria for prosecution and an overview of the federal sentencing scheme is found in section XV.

Practice Tip

If defense counsel has any reason to suspect that his or her state child pornography case might be referred to a federal prosecutor, counsel should call the Federal Public Defender’s office at (503) 326-2123 and ask for the duty attorney. The state prosecutor’s threat to take a case federal if your client does not accept a plea offer should be taken seriously. Currently, state court cases can be resolved for less than the five-year mandatory minimum routinely imposed in federal court in the District of Oregon.

B. The Statutory Scheme

The current statutory scheme governing child pornography offenses was enacted in 1995 by which time the internet and digital devices used to facilitate these offenses were in existence. Yet, as Justice Paul De Muniz noted in his concurring opinion in State v. Barger, 349 Or 553 (2011), adh’d to as modified on recons, 350 Or 233 (2011), the Oregon legislature defined the offenses with language that assumed offenders would continue to create, obtain, and distribute unlawful images the old fashioned way:

Although the Internet was a well-established fact of life at the time that ORS 163.686 was enacted in 1995, nothing in that statutory text suggests that the legislature expressly intended to capture the kind of digital computer images that purveyors of child pornography now use as their principal means of communication and distribution. See Ty E. Howard, Don’t Cache out Your Case: Prosecuting Child Pornography Possession Laws Based On Images Located In Temporary Internet Files, 19 Berkeley Tech LJ 1227, 1228 (2004) (noting that, for defendants presently collecting and trafficking in child pornography, the media of choice are now digital images and the medium of choice is the Internet). Instead, and unfortunately, the legislature appears to have assumed that the proscribed pornographic images would appear on or in tangible objects—photographs, motion pictures, videotapes, or other visual recordings.

This “chasm” between the statutory language and modern technology is reflected in many of the appellate decisions discussed in this chapter, in which the courts have sought to analogize digital processes to traditional ways of possessing, duplicating, and distributing unlawful images. Over the years, the legislature has amended the statutes to reflect modern technology. Unfortunately for criminal defendants the effect has been to broaden the scope of criminal liability. Most notably, in 2011 the Oregon legislature, in response to the Barger decision discussed below, criminalized mere “viewing” or “accessing with the intent to view” images of sexually explicit conduct of children if done with the required sexual purpose. The state need not prove possession or control of the images if it alleges “viewing.”

The statutory scheme consists of four tiers of offenses grouped in terms of crime seriousness. In State v. Porter, 241 Or App 26, rev den, 350 Or 530 (2011), the court stated:

[T]he statutory scheme begins with the most serious offense, which involves the actual creation of child pornography or the use of a child in a sexual display for a live audience. The next level of seriousness concerns distributing child pornography or using child pornography to induce children to engage in sexual conduct. The third descending level of seriousness generally concerns possession or observation of child pornography with the knowledge that a child was abused to create it, or intending to use such materials to induce a child to engage in sexually explicit conduct. Finally, the lowest level of crimes in the group generally involves possession or observation of child pornography while failing to be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk that a child was being abused, or, if a person is involved in processing images, failing to report the existence of child pornography. To summarize, the statutory scheme punishes most harshly the creation of child pornography, next, its distribution, beyond that, its use to promote further abuse of children, and finally, its use in general.

Although the foregoing is a reasonable observation of the statutory scheme itself, this neat grouping of crimes based on level of seriousness has broken down as a result of judicial construction, most notably in the context of encouraging child sexual abuse in the first degree, ORS 163.684. That crime prohibits conduct the Oregon Supreme Court has broadly described as the “production” and “distribution” of unlawful images—i.e., the acts of developing, duplicating, publishing, printing, disseminating, exchanging, displaying, financing, or selling. According to the Porter hierarchy, this crime falls into the second most serious group of offenses, just below the actual recording of child sexual abuse and above crimes of possession. Yet several decisions have interpreted “production” (duplication and printing) in ways that have brought mere possessors into this more serious category. Thus the defendant who downloaded unlawful images from the internet solely for his own use was found to have
“duplicated” the images. State v. Pugh, 255 Or App 357, rev den 353 Or 748 (2013). And
the defendant who intended to print an image from a computer solely for his own use was found to have intended to print the image for purposes of this more serious crime. State v. Bray, 342 Or 711 (2007).

C. Evaluation and Treatment

At the outset of a child pornography case, counsel should recommend that the client enter a treatment program designed to address compulsive and addictive behaviors. The therapist should have experience or training in the treatment of sex addiction—a phenomenon not listed in the current version of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders DSM but generally recognized as a set of behaviors that can be successfully treated through traditional forms of therapy. Groups modeled on Alcoholics Anonymous and Narcotics Anonymous can be a source of support for the client struggling with compulsive online or sexual behaviors. If the client has a prior sex offense, a more robust form of sex offender treatment should be recommended.

As with any sex case, counsel should also consider obtaining a psychosexual evaluation of the client for use in settlement discussions and sentencing. Counsel should gather for the evaluator all relevant information regarding the client’s background, including any history of abuse, from the client and his or her family.

Practice Tip

Virtually all psychosexual evaluations include a polygraph concerning the client’s sexual history. In child pornography cases, prosecutors generally assume your client has engaged in “hands-on” sexual offenses but didn’t get caught. For both of these reasons, if your client denies any hands-on offending, strongly consider doing the polygraph before incurring the expense of a psychosexual examination. If the client passes, you will have some preliminary ammo for negotiations with the prosecutor, and also know that going forward with a psychosexual examination makes sense.

II. Special Forensic Issues

This section addresses the need for defense counsel to hire a computer forensic expert, and then summarizes some basic technological issues that frequently arise in child pornography cases.

A. Hiring a Computer Forensic Expert

Practice Tip

In any child pornography case involving the use of computers or mobile devices, defense counsel should, at the very least, consult with a computer forensic expert. In most cases, counsel should hire an expert to independently examine the digital evidence. Without such an examination, counsel cannot hope to fully understand the nature and strength of the state’s evidence or develop possible challenges to the manner in which law enforcement searched or seized the evidence.

The examination and review of digital evidence is unlike any other type of evidence examination. It typically involves the review of large amounts of data and often requires the use of multiple forensic tools and processes. Because computer and mobile evidence is digital, and because digital evidence is fragile, the examination requires special forensic software and hardware tools as well as the knowledge of how to use each of them correctly. Hence, digital evidence is almost always examined in a controlled laboratory environment by trained personnel using specialized investigative software, hardware and methodology.

Trained law enforcement officers will have the first crack at examining digital evidence. They will do so by obtaining a forensic extraction of the targeted hard drives or mobile devices—often described as a “mirror image” (or simply “forensic extraction” when dealing with a mobile device)—in order to preserve the integrity of the original evidence. A report noting location and other details of files containing unlawful images or videos will be produced. Both an original and a redacted version of this report should be obtained for defense review. The redacted copy will not contain alleged contraband files and, as such, can be maintained by the defense attorney, investigator, or expert outside of law enforcement facilities.

The focus of the law enforcement technician’s examination is to find evidence to support the prosecution’s theory. Rarely does the law enforcement technician examine the evidence for exculpatory data that would assist the defense. If such evidence exists, the defense team must find and document it. This is the nature and investigative process of digital forensics.

Computer or digital forensics could be more accurately called “digital investigations.” Before investigating the contents of a digital media device, the examiner and defense counsel should discuss everything potentially relevant to the case. The digital forensic process should include a thorough investigation of the media in order to determine what occurred, when it occurred, how it occurred and to the extent possible who was responsible. To answer these questions requires a working knowledge of data recovery, as well as the type of device seized, its applications, how offenses or other actions are carried out with those applications, what types of behaviors are associated with different applications, and a myriad of related issues.

The job of the defense examiner in digital evidence cases is threefold: (1) the digital evidence must be analyzed in a manner that will allow the defense attorney to evaluate the charges and the nature of the state’s case, (2) locating and documenting evidence that casts reasonable doubt on the state’s case, and (3) if it appears the client is culpable to some degree, attempting to document any evidence that might explain or mitigate
the conduct.

B. Images in Unallocated Space (Structured and Unstructured)

Unallocated file space is the area within digital device memory available for new content. This content could be files downloaded through a web browser, documents created by a user, or the installation of new software. If unallocated file space has not previously been used, it will consist of blank space. If it has been used to store files, it may contain data that has since been deleted either by a user or by automatic operation of the operating system or applications.

Data located within unallocated file space can be fully intact and thus fully recoverable with proper forensic methodologies. Data can also exist in partially overwritten states, which tends to make the recovery more challenging. Often, data located within unallocated file space will not have recoverable file system metadata, such as file name, its former logical path, and its “created” and “last written” dates and times. Without such information, a forensic examiner cannot locate additional access artifacts that may help determine whether a user knew of the file’s existence. Essentially, context for a file’s life—including how it got on the digital device, how long it resided there, and who might be responsible for it—cannot be determined when dealing with the majority of data located in unallocated file space. Thus proving elements such as knowing possession of specific files located within this area of a device’s memory can be extremely difficult.

There are caveats to the missing metadata commonly associated with unallocated file space. In certain circumstances references to deleted files can be located with the use of forensic methodology. In such cases, the deleted file’s former name, logical file path, as well as the “created” and “last written” dates and times may be recoverable. When this occurs, in order to differentiate a file recovered from unallocated file space with context versus one without, the former is often described as recovering a file from “structured unallocated file space.”

C. Temporary Internet Files

When a user browses the web, contents of the web pages, including text, images and movie files, are cached (stored) in temporary internet file folders and/or databases on the user’s local storage device. Movies and images found in these locations are not typically saved there by a willful act of the computer user but, rather, are a byproduct of the web browser.

Unless configuration settings are altered by a user, accessing such information after the automatic download would require configuring the device to view hidden files and folders, traversing lengthy file paths most users are unaware of, and ultimately accessing folders with thousands upon thousands of files which would require manual one-by-one access in order to review. Depending on the web browser, many such temporary files exist without file extensions or are further stored within databases that do not allow for simple double-click access. Short of demonstrable user access, information located within temporary internet file folders or databases is extremely difficult to use alone as evidence to prove knowing possession or receipt of a file. See United States v. Dobbs, 629 F3d 1199, 1208 (10th Cir. 2011) (government failed to prove knowing receipt of files found in temporary internet folders where defendant was unaware that computer automatically saved the images in a cache file); but see United States v. Ramos, 685 F3d 120, 132 (2nd Cir. 2013) (holding that an individual who views images on the internet exercises dominion and control over them even though they are in temporary cache files).

Operating system and application-based caching is a common occurrence in the everyday use of a digital device. Caching is commonly used to temporarily store data that will later be moved or are in the process of being modified, often automatically, by the application conducting the caching. Cache locations are typically unknown to the average user and often require a high level of technical sophistication to access. Although these areas of data storage are meant to be temporary, there are many reasons why data may not be deleted in a timely fashion; but, forensic methodologies exist that may allow deleted data to be recovered.

Note: data located within such temporary areas are often used to support allegations of knowing possession, receipt and sometimes distribution. It is important to look closely at the context for files located within such areas. The state may have a hard time proving the elements with data stored only in temporary locations.

D. Developing a Defense in the Context of Multiple Users

It is common to encounter digital devices that have more than a single user. This occurs in households where occupants share digital devices such as tablets, computers and phones. Even in such scenarios, methods to determine which user is most likely responsible for the investigated activity include an examination of the users’ different interests or hobbies and their online accounts, such as email and social media. Looking at such details can provide the digital forensic examiner with profiles of each user and the patterns of their activity. Utilizing these profiles in conjunction with relevant timeframes, context can be provided that demonstrates which profile best matches the device’s usage at or around the investigated activity. See generally Denton v. Nooth, 283 Or App 179 (2016) (counsel provided ineffective assistance by failing to call alibi witnesses to testify that defendant was not in his home at the time of the downloads of illicit images); United States v. Moreland, 665 F3d 137 (5th Cir. 2011) (reversing a conviction for possession of child pornography when defendant and two other people had joint control over the computer).

Practice Tip

The client who claims he was not aware of child pornography on his computer might insist
that someone else must have remotely accessed his device to install the contraband. That sort of foul play is possible through a malware virus often known as a Trojan horse. Again, the digital forensics expert can help develop this defense—or help identify its flaws before it becomes one.

E. Smart Phones and Other Mobile Devices

Mobile forensic examinations have similar characteristics to hard drive forensics or what is commonly called “computer forensics.” However, mobile forensics is better characterized as a specialty field within digital forensics. Commonly, “hard drive” forensic examiners possess limited knowledge and toolsets for this specialty.

One significant difference between hard drive and mobile device examinations begins with the preservation of the device. In hard drive forensics, multiple tools can create a “forensic” image—that is, a verifiable, repeatable, and ultimately defendable copy of the original tar-get media. Each tool creates the forensic image essentially in the same manner with the same output.

Likewise, in mobile forensics several tools and methodologies exist to create “forensic” images or “forensic extractions,” a more apt term for this niche. However, unlike hard drive forensics, not all forensic extractions of mobile devices are the same. In fact, in many instances each forensic tool will acquire and interpret data very differently. This is in part due to the proprietary non-standard nature in which mobile devices are programmed and thus store data. Further, this field quickly advances and changes. Within a short time span numerous updates and advances for extracting data can be found for hundreds of different devices, which in turn changes what a forensic examiner could expect to extract and analyze.

There are three general extraction methods commonly seen in mobile forensics. The first is called a logical extraction, which typically obtains only active known data structures the tool understands. In many scenarios, no deleted data can be examined and much active data goes unreported, which can include common content like text messages and call logs.

The second method is referred to as a file system or an advanced logical extraction. This process attempts to obtain a device’s entire active file structure regardless of the forensic tool’s ability to parse and interpret the data. This provides an examiner the ability to manually analyze the unreported data structures. Although not all memory has been extracted, deleted data can be found with this process, especially when looking for deleted content within database structures commonly associated with text messaging or other communication applications.

The final and what is regarded as the best method is the physical extraction. This is essentially the mobile forensic equivalent to a “bit by bit” copy created during hard drive preservation processes and will include all active and, for the most part, unallocated (unused or deleted) areas of a mobile device’s memory. When such an extraction is accomplished, the most comprehensive analysis of a device’s data can be conducted.

There are caveats to all extraction types, including the physical extraction, which typically would be considered the best possible and as such, the extraction type that would provide the most data. However, there are situations where certain extraction types are unsupported by mobile forensic tools or are unnecessary—as, for example, when encryption is an issue and obtaining a physical image would only provide a large amount of encrypted, unusable data.

In mobile forensics it is commonly recognized that obtaining all possible extractions from each device is the best practice. Doing so provides a forensic examiner with the most amount of data in the greatest number of formats possible. Adhering to these standards will give the examiner the largest amount of flexibility and possibilities during the examination process.

In certain scenarios, an advanced extraction process is required. Reasons for this requirement range from a device being pattern- or pin-locked, physically damaged, or simply unsupported by the major mobile forensic application developers. Three commonly discussed advanced extraction methods are called a Joint Test Action Group (JTAG) probe, In-System Programming (ISP), and chip-off extractions. Although advanced extraction methods have the potential to leave a mobile device in an unusable state, both the JTAG probe and ISP extractions are considered non-destructive processes where a phone is minimally disassembled and leads are connected to a device’s internal diagnostic port or memory chips. On the other hand, the chip-off process is considered destructive and typically is used in the most severe or important cases as the mobile device is completely disassembled, memory chips de-soldered, cleaned and read, which leaves the original device in fragments and unusable upon completion.

In addition to the numerous methods for data acquisition, each phone maintains privacy and configuration settings that often need to be manually reviewed to properly ascertain. As an example, some phones allow for specific items like text messages to be deleted after a set time duration or at a set interval. In various scenarios, these settings cannot be determined by simply looking at the extractions of the device. Without these settings being confirmed, it can be a challenge or even impossible to determine if the deletion of specific text messages was done automatically or intentionally.

Mobile forensic tools are prone to mistakes and require constant vigilance by the examiner in order to verify their output and interpretations. One such issue can be found in the interpretation of dates and times. Without the physical phones present during an analysis, it can be extremely difficult if not impossible for the examiner to verify that the forensic tools utilized are reporting specific datasets (e.g., the sent time of a text message) accurately. In place of
the original device, ancillary logs such as a mobile subscriber’s call detail records (CDRs) provided by the service provider can assist in corroborating dates and times noted within a phone’s memory.

It is crucial to understand that the typical “push button” extraction report provided by most major mobile forensic tool manufactures will not provide access to all evidence a device may contain. Thus it is imperative that a manual examination of the data structures be completed on the best possible extraction method. Without conducting such a manual examination, important data can be missed. Commonly observed datasets missed by such tools are communications via third party messaging applications, deleted communication within standard applications, web browsing history, email content, and search history, to name a select few.

F. Peer-to-Peer Programs

People often use peer-to-peer (P2P) programs to acquire and share unlawful images and videos of child pornography. Counsel should have a basic understanding of how such programs work. These applications and their networks allow for easy decentralized transfer of digital files around the world. Content available on such networks includes videos, games, music, books, software applications and just about every file type one could possibly imagine.

Peer-to-peer networks can be broken down into two categories: public and private. Public P2P networks allow any person desiring to join the network to simply download an application, connect it to the network, and begin sharing their own digital content and downloading files made available by other users of the network. Public networks and applications include Ares, eDonkey (eMuleOvernet, Shareaza), BitTorrent, and Gnutella (Bearshare, iMesh, Gnotella, LimeWire, Frostwire, Mactella, Shareaza), to name a few of the most popular.

A defendant accused of distributing child pornography to others over a P2P network might claim that he was not aware others could access files on his device. This defense will involve scrutiny of the particular type of P2P network and subsequent application in use and how it was configured on the defendant’s computer. Some P2P programs, by default, set users’ files to “share” mode—a fact the unsophisticated computer user may not know. Additionally, it is common for P2P applications to share pieces of files that have not fully downloaded to a defendant’s computer even if the general “sharing” of files is turned off. A forensic examination of the program might reveal whether the user changed the “share” setting or otherwise configured the software application in a manner that would tend to show that the user had knowledge of the sharing process.

Private P2P networks allow only trusted peers to participate in the sharing. Examples of private P2P networks and applications includes GigaTribe, Retroshare and Direct Connect. People who share child pornography over P2P networks typically view a private network as less likely than a public network to draw the attention of law enforcement (think setting your social media to “private” and restricting your friends and what is shared). However, private networks and their applications require more user interaction and acknowledgement of the sharing process including allowing access to specific files and folders, which ultimately can make the government’s case of “knowingly” distributing such content much easier to prove.

Law enforcement has developed its own programs to identify computer systems that share child pornography over P2P networks. These automated tools search for known images of child pornography using the “hash values” associated with the images. Hash values act like unique fingerprints or DNA of a file or set of files, thus in many cases is an extremely effective method for identifying “known” illegal files. “Known” illegal files are kept by law enforcement and also by the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC). Once law enforcement identifies a targeted file in a shared folder, it can also identify the Internet Protocol (IP) address associated with the computer network as well other identifying characteristics such as the Globally Unique Identifier (GUID), which identifies the user’s unique installation of their P2P program.

III. Defining Child Pornography

A. Statutory Definitions – ORS 163.665

The child pornography statutes target conduct relating to visual depictions and visual recordings of sexually explicit conduct involving a child. The pertinent terms are defined in ORS 163.665 as follows:

(1) Child means a person who is less than 18 years of age, and any reference to a child in relation to a visual recording of the child is a reference to a person who was less than 18 years of age at the time the original image in the visual recording was created and not the age of the person at the time of an alleged offense relating to the subsequent reproduction, use or possession of the visual recording.

(2) Child abuse means conduct that constitutes, or would constitute if committed in this state, a crime in which the victim is a child.

(3) Sexually explicit conduct means actual or simulated:

(a) Sexual intercourse or deviant sexual intercourse;

(b) Genital-genital, oral-genital, anal-genital or oral-anal contact, whether between persons of the same or opposite sex or between humans and animals;

(c) Penetration of the vagina or rectum by any object other than as part of a medical diagnosis or treatment or as part of a personal hygiene practice;

(d) Masturbation;

(e) Sadistic or masochistic abuse; or

(f) Lewd exhibition of sexual or other intimate parts.

(4) Visual depiction includes, but is not limited to, visual recordings, pictures and computer-generated images and pictures, whether made or produced by electronic, mechanical or other means.

(5) Visual recording includes, but is not limited to, photographs, films, videotapes
and computer and other digital pictures, regardless of the manner in which the recording is stored.

Of those terms the phrase “lewd exhibition” within the definition of “sexually explicit conduct” has generated the most controversy. In State v. Meyer, 120 Or App 319 (1993), the court defined it as “exhibition with the intent of stimulating the lust or desires of the person who views it.” Whether a photograph depicts a “lewd exhibition” depends upon the circumstances of the photograph’s creation. Often, the only evidence is the photograph itself. In State v. Navaie, 274 Or App 739 (2015), the photograph at issue depicted a young girl wearing a tank top and shorts. Her legs were spread and the photograph was taken from a position that allowed the viewer to see up her shorts. The girl’s genitals were partially covered and she was not wearing any underwear. Furthermore, the photograph was closely cropped and the girl was the sole subject. The Court of Appeals held that in light of these facts the trial court did not err in denying defendant’s motion for judgment of acquittal.

Similarly, State v. Mross, 274 Or App 302 (2015), rev den 358 Or 550 (2016), held that the sexually suggestive nature of photos alone is enough to prove that they were taken with the intent of arousing the sexual desire of anyone who views them. Direct evidence about the circumstances in which the photos were taken is not required. In Mross, the children in the photos were in sexually suggestive positions. The children were also wearing tight-fitting underwear that barely covered their genitals and were the sole subjects of the photos. Those facts alone supported a non-speculative inference that the photos were taken with the intention of arousing sexual desire.

B. What Is Not Child Pornography

Law enforcement officials and others who deal with child pornography offenses often use the equally euphemistic term “child erotica” to describe sexualized images of children that are not unlawful. One could imagine any number of images or depictions that would be highly exploitive or abusive of children yet fall outside the purview of prohibited conduct because they do not show the “lewd exhibition of sexual or other intimate parts.” Also, except in the context of ORS 163.688 and ORS 163.689, described below, images that depict adults posing as children, digital images that have been manipulated to make an adult look like a child (called “morphing”), and animated images of children are not prohibited. For obvious constitutional reasons, pornographic literature without visual depictions also is not prohibited.

IV. Possessing and Viewing of Unlawful Depictions

A. Encouraging Child Sexual Abuse in the Second Degree – Elements

ORS 163.686 provides:

  1. A person commits the crime of encouraging child sexual abuse in the second degree if the person:

(a) (A)(i) Knowingly possesses or controls, or knowingly accesses with the intent to view, a visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involving a child for the purpose of arousing or satisfying the sexual desires of the person or another person; or

(ii) Knowingly pays, exchanges or gives anything of value to obtain or view a visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involving a child for the purpose of arousing or satisfying the sexual desires of the person or another person; and

(B) Knows or is aware of and consciously disregards the fact that creation of the visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involved child abuse; or

(b) (A) Knowingly pays, exchanges or gives anything of value to observe sexually explicit conduct by a child or knowingly observes, for the purpose of arousing or gratifying the sexual desire of the person, sexually explicit conduct by a child; and

(B) Knows or is aware of and consciously disregards the fact that the conduct constitutes child abuse.

(2) Encouraging child sexual abuse in the second degree is a Class C felony.

B. Encouraging Child Sexual Abuse in the Third Degree – Elements

ORS 163.687 provides:

  1. A person commits the crime of encouraging child sexual abuse in the third degree if the person:

(a) (A)(i) Knowingly possesses or controls, or knowingly accesses with the intent to view, a visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involving a child for the purpose of arousing or satisfying the sexual desires of the person or another person; or

(ii) Knowingly pays, exchanges or gives anything of value to obtain or view a visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involving a child for the purpose of arousing or satisfying the sexual desires of the person or another person; and

(B) Knows or fails to be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the creation of the visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involved child abuse; or

(b) (A) Knowingly pays, exchanges or gives anything of value to observe sexually explicit conduct by a child or knowingly observes, for the purpose of arousing or gratifying the sexual desire of the person, sexually explicit conduct by a child; and

(B) Knows or fails to be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the conduct constitutes child abuse.

(2) Encouraging child sexual abuse in the third degree is a Class A misdemeanor.

C. Possession of Materials Depicting Sexually Explicit Conduct of a Child in the First Degree – Elements

ORS 163.688 provides:

(1) A person commits the crime of possession of materials depicting sexually explicit conduct of a child in the first degree if the person:

(a) Knowingly possesses, accesses or views a visual depiction of sexually explicit conduct involving a child or a visual depiction of sexually explicit conduct that appears to involve a child; and

(b) Uses the visual depiction to induce a child to participate or engage in sexually explicit conduct.

(2) Possession of materials depicting sexually explicit conduct of a child in the first degree is a Class B felony

D. Possession of Materials Depicting Sexually Explicit Conduct of a Child in the Second Degree – Elements

ORS 163.689 provides:

(1) A person commits the crime of possession of materials depicting sexually explicit conduct of a child in the second degree if the person:

(a) Knowingly possesses, accesses or views a visual depiction of sexually explicit conduct involving a child or a visual depiction of sexually explicit conduct that appears to involve a child; and

(b) Intends to use the visual depiction to induce a child to participate or engage in sexually explicit conduct.

(2) Possession of materials depicting sexually explicit conduct of a child in the second degree is a Class C felony.

E. Case Law and Potential Issues Regarding Possession or Viewing

1. Accessing and Viewing

The possession statutes listed above prohibit the “accessing with the intent to view” or “accessing or viewing” of visual depictions of sexually explicit conduct involving children. The Oregon legislature added this language in 2011 in response to State v. Barger, 349 Or 553 (2011), in which the court held that a person who uses the internet to intentionally call up an image of sexually explicit conduct involving a child, but does nothing more, does not possess or control that image. See also State v. Ritchie, 349 Or 572 (2011) (decided same day as Barger). Or Laws 2011, Ch. 515. The result is that a person now may be convicted of encouraging child sexual abuse in the second degree by merely viewing unlawful images, regardless of whether the person possessed or controlled them. Of course, the state must still prove the viewing was knowing, which would rule out the accidental or inadvertent viewing of child pornography.

2. Possession or Control

When the state relies on a theory of possession, “possess” means to have “physical possession or otherwise to exercise dominion or control over property.” ORS 161.015(9). See State v. Barger, 349 Or 553 (2011) (holding that statute defining possession “expresses the meaning that the legislature intended to convey by the phrase ‘possesses and controls’ in ORS 163.686(1)(a)(A)(i)”).

3. Sexual Purpose

The state must prove the defendant possessed, controlled, or accessed unlawful images or recordings “for the purpose of arousing or satisfying the sexual desires of the person or another person.” That formulation draws a direct link between the defendant’s actus reus of possession, control, or access and the defendant’s sexual purpose. When the state relies on the “lewd exhibition of sexual or other intimate parts” variant of “sexually explicit conduct,” it has the additional burden to prove that the person responsible for taking the photos or videos intended to arouse sexual desire. State v. Mross, 274 Or App 302 (2015), rev den, 358 Or 550 (2016). In either case, the state can meet its burden by drawing non-speculative inferences from the images or videos alone, as Mross illustrates.

In State v. Dulfu, 282 Or App 209 (2016), rev allowed, 361 Or 100 (2017), defendant testified that his possession of child pornography was not for the purpose of sexual gratification but was instead a compulsive behavior developed in response to trauma he suffered as a child sex abuse victim. In support of that defense, he intended to call Dr. Frank Colistro as an expert to testify that some individuals possess child pornography for purposes other than sexual gratification. The court upheld the exclusion of this testimony on the ground that defendant had not laid an adequate foundation for the admission of expert testimony, pursuant to the Brown/O’Key framework.

Practice Tip

Reading between the lines, the outcome in Dulfu seems to rest on the court’s dim view of Dr. Colistro’s reasoning that if a person is not diagnosed with a form of “sexual deviancy,” there may be some reason other than sexual gratification for the person to hoard child pornography, such as an anxiety disorder. In the right case, counsel should consider building upon what was attempted in Dulfu by, for example, calling the client’s treating therapist (preferably, one who had been treating the client before his legal troubles arose) to testify about the client’s anxiety disorder or other condition, so that the jury is provided with an alternative motive for the conduct. Or, consider calling a therapist who specializes in the treatment of addictive or compulsive online behaviors.

4. Unallocated Space and Temporary Cache Files

Sections II. B. and II. C., supra, describe evidence found in unallocated space or in temporary cache files. This sort of evidence creates unique issues bearing on the state’s burden of proof. For example, for images found in unallocated space, the state may not be able to prove that the defendant viewed or accessed the files, or that he “knowingly” possessed them. See, e.g., United States v. Flyer, 633 F3d 911, 918 (9th Cir. 2011) (reversing a conviction for child pornography found in unallocated space because the government presented no evidence defendant had the forensic software to see or access the files). It is generally known that files deleted from a computer folder remain on the hard drive in unallocated space until overwritten. However, not everyone knows that, and it should always remain the state’s burden to prove the defendant had that knowledge. Furthermore, not everyone has a way of knowing when any particular data becomes overwritten by new data. This line of inquiry is particularly pertinent to the defendant who claims he accidently viewed or inadvertently received unlawful images on his computer and immediately tried to remove them.

To the extent that the state relies on evidence in unallocated space or a cache file to prove a past possession or viewing of child pornography, other challenges can be made. For example, does the data show that the knowing possession or viewing occurred within the statute of limitations? Did the defendant even own the computer at that time? Were there other people besides the defendant who could have used the computer to obtain, possess, or view the unlawful images? This is a context in which a defense computer forensic expert can provide an understanding of the evidence and aid in the defense.

5. Actual Child

The statutory language prohibiting the possession of a “visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involving a child” connotes that the state must prove that an actual child is depicted in the image or recording. See State v. Dimock, 174 Or App 500 (2001), rev den, 334 Or 190 (2002) (so holding in the context of encouraging child sexual abuse in the first degree). Thus if there is any evidence to the contrary, the state must prove the image or recording depicts an actual human child as opposed to a “virtual” child. Similarly, images of adults made to look like children (a process called “morphing” if it occurs digitally) do not fall within the purview of the statute.

As a caveat to the foregoing, two statutes defining the crimes of possession of materials depicting sexually explicit conduct of a child in the first and second degree, ORS 163.688 and 163.689, prohibit the possession, accessing or viewing of a visual depiction of sexually explicit conduct involving a child or conduct that appears to involve a child. However, with these crimes the state also must prove that the defendant used or intended to use the depiction to induce a child to participate or engage in sexual activity.

V. Producing and Distributing Unlawful Depictions

A. Encouraging Child Sexual Abuse in the First Degree – Elements

ORS 163.684 provides:

(1) A person commits the crime of encouraging child sexual abuse in the first degree if the person:

(a) (A) Knowingly develops, duplicates, publishes, prints, disseminates, exchanges, displays, finances, attempts to finance or sells a visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involving a child or knowingly possesses, accesses or views such a visual recording with the intent to develop, duplicate, publish, print, disseminate, exchange, display or sell it; or

(B) Knowingly brings into this state, or causes to be brought or sent into this state, for sale or distribution, a visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involving a child; and

(b) Knows or is aware of and consciously disregards the fact that creation of the visual recording of sexually explicit conduct involved child abuse.

(2) Encouraging child sexual abuse in the first degree is a Class B felony.

B. Case Law and Potential Issues Regarding Production and Distribution

In State v. Bray, 342 Or 711 (2007), the Oregon Supreme Court noted that the crime of encouraging child sexual abuse in the first degree broadly prohibits two categories of conduct: the “producing” and “distributing” of child pornography. “That is, the statute prohibits financing, attempting to finance, developing, or duplicating child pornography—acts that go to the production of child pornography. It also prohibits disseminating, exchanging, and selling child pornography—acts that involve distributing child pornography to others.”

1. Producing

In Bray, the court held that the act of “printing” fell in the category of “producing” child pornography. One prints an image either by developing a negative or by copying an image using ink or other “analogous method.” The act of printing child pornography for the defendant’s own use violates this statute even if the defendant does not intend to show it or disseminate it further. See also State v. Betnar, 214 Or App 416 (2007) (that the evidence was found on computer floppy disks, photographs, and CDs was sufficient to prove that the images had been printed or duplicated).

In State v. JCL, 261 Or App 692 (2014), the court held there was sufficient evidence that a youth intended to share the child porn pictures in a “shared” folder on his computer via the peer-to-peer network, Limewire. This was true even though the folder was marked as hidden and could not actually have been shared via Limewire at the time of its discovery.

In State v. Pugh, 255 Or App 357, rev den 353 Or 748 (2013), the Court of Appeals held that defendant’s act of downloading child pornography from the internet amounted to an act of “duplication.” The court relied on the state’s expert testimony that downloading a file from the internet creates a copy of that image in the defendant’s computer. The fact that the defendant showed no intent to further duplicate or disseminate the image was
immaterial. See also State v. Urbina, 249 Or App 267, rev den 353 Or 103 (2012) (on plain error review, court holds that downloading an image from a peer-to-peer network
is “duplicating”).

2. Distributing

In Bray, the court interpreted the word “display” in the statute to be an example of distribution. In that sense, the state must prove that defendant showed, or intended to show, the unlawful image to other people. One cannot display child pornography only
to oneself.

VI. Creating Child Pornography

A. Using a Child in a Display of Sexually Explicit Conduct – Elements

ORS 163.670 provides:

  1. A person commits the crime of using a child in a display of sexually explicit conduct if the person employs, authorizes, permits, compels or induces a child to participate or engage in sexually explicit conduct for any person to observe or to record in a visual recording.

(2) Using a child in a display of sexually explicit conduct is a Class A felony.

B. Case Law and Potential Issues Regarding Creating

1. Permit

The most litigated aspect of ORS 163.670 surrounds the issue of whether a defendant “permitted” a child to participate in sexually explicit conduct. In State v. Porter, 241 Or App 26, rev den, 350 Or 530, (2011), the court held that by using the word “permit,” the legislature did not intend to limit liability only to persons in a legal relationship to the child. Rather, “permit” has the broader meaning of to allow or “make possible.” The defendant in Porter was married to the child’s mother. The defendant did not sexually abuse the child, but evidence that others did so in his house and that he enjoyed watching the abuse was sufficient to show he “permitted” it.

In another case turning on the meaning of “permit,” State v. Hunt, 270 Or App 206 (2015), the court held that an “attempted” use of a child in a display of sexually explicit conduct occurs when the defendant takes a “substantial step toward the criminal objective of allowing or making it possible that a child would be used in a sexually explicit display.” The defendant need not have the authority to prohibit the conduct in order to be found guilty for permitting it. The defendant had a romantic relationship with a 13-year-old child, “and in the context of that relationship asked the child to take and send to [the defendant] a sexually explicit photograph.” Because a jury could have reasonably inferred that this was a substantial step in having a child used in a sexually explicit display made possible by the defendant, the trial court was correct to deny the motion for judgment of acquittal. See also State v. Richardson, 261 Or App 95, rev den, 355 Or 880 (2014) (discussed below, holding that defendant permitted the child to participate in sexually explicit conduct).

2. Compels or Induces

In State v. Smith, 261 Or App 665, rev den, 355 Or 880 (2014) the court held that the plain meaning of “compels or induces” requires the state to prove the defendant caused the child to participate or engage in sexually explicit conduct either by force or by persuasion or influence. The photographs showed the child naked below the waist, lying on her back, with her legs spread slightly apart and displaying her vagina towards the camera. The child’s mother testified that when she was present her daughter was never naked around defendant. The court held that a factfinder could reasonably infer, based on the child’s facial expressions in the photographs, “that she would not have posed herself in that position without being persuaded or influenced to do so.”

3. Participate or Engage

In State v. Richardson, 261 Or App 95, rev den, 355 Or 880 (2014), the court addressed the extent to which a child must “participate or engage” in sexually explicit conduct of adults when that participation is not plainly obvious from the visual depiction. The state’s evidence consisted of two photographs of defendant engaging in oral-genital sex with a consenting adult female. The woman’s young son was depicted in close proximity to the sex act. The woman denied that she or defendant intended or wanted the child to participate in the sex act. The court stated that the child’s mere proximity to or observation of sexually explicit activity is not enough to prove that the child is participating in the sexually explicit activity. However, the court held that the evidence was sufficient to survive a motion for judgment of acquittal. The court held that “participation” can occur through the actions of another participant without any volition of the child. Also, the court agreed with the state that evidence of the child’s participation in the sex act was shown by the defendant’s conduct of taking two photographs when the child was so close, indicating that the defendant himself viewed the child as being a party to the act.

4. Observe

The offense of using a child in a display of sexually explicit conduct may be committed when the sexually explicit conduct is not recorded, but rather is observed “by any person.” In State v. Tyson, 243 Or App 94, rev den, 351 Or 401 (2011), the Court of Appeals held that an observer of sexually explicit conduct can also be a participant. Thus, the court upheld a denial of a motion for judgment of acquittal when defendant engaged in sexually explicit conduct with a child while her husband watched and instructed the child.

5. Lewdness Is Determined From Perspective of Person Charged

As discussed above, sexually explicit conduct includes the lewd exhibition of sexual or other intimate body parts. “Lewd exhibition” means “exhibition with the intent of stimulating the lust or desires of the person who views it.” State v. Meyer, 120 Or App 319 (1993). In the context of the offense of using a child in the display of sexually explicit conduct, the court has held that the lewdness of the exhibition depends on the intent of the person charged with the offense. State v. Evans, 178 Or App 439, 445 n3 (2001), rev den, 334 Or 76 (2002). The court rejected the dissent’s view that the sexual nature of the photograph or display should be determined from the perspective of a reasonable person. See also State v. Smith, 261 Or App 665, rev den, 355 Or 880 (2014) (rejecting argument that Evans was wrongly decided).

VII. Defenses Surrounding the Defendant’s Mistake of the Victim’s Age

A. Affirmative Defense

The defendant may assert that he or she did not know that the victim in a child pornography case was a child. ORS 163.690 establishes an affirmative defense when the defendant, at the time of engaging in the prohibited conduct, “did not know and did not have reason to know that the relevant sexually explicit conduct involved a child.” ORS 163.690. The affirmative defense may be raised in prosecutions for encouraging child sexual abuse in the first degree, ORS 163.684, encouraging child sexual abuse in the second degree, ORS 163.686, encouraging child sexual abuse in the third degree, ORS 163.687, and failure to report child pornography, ORS 163.693.

The offense of using a child in the display of sexually explicit conduct, ORS 163.670, is not among the list of crimes in ORS 163.690 to which the affirmative defense applies. However, with such an offense, counsel should consider arguing that the mistake of age defense set forth in ORS 163.325(2) applies. The state will argue that the mistake of age defense set forth in ORS 163.325 applies only to sex offenses listed in ORS 163.355 to 163.445, and not to any of the child pornography crimes. However, subsection (2) of ORS 163.325, by its terms, is not limited only to certain types of offenses.

As with any affirmative defense, the burden is on the defendant to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that he did not know, and had no reason to know, that the sexually explicit conduct involved a child. ORS 161.055(2).

B. Putting the Burden on the State to Prove Knowledge of Age

Before relying on an affirmative defense, counsel should argue that it is the state’s burden to prove beyond a reasonable doubt a defendant in a child pornography case knew that the victim was a child under the age of 18. Although the child pornography statutes do not contain an express mens rea requirement as to the victim’s age, ORS 161.115(2) provides that “if a statute defining an offense does not prescribe a culpable mental state, culpability is nonetheless required and is established only if a person acts intentionally, knowingly, recklessly or with criminal negligence.” Further, in State v. Olive, 259 Or App 104, 113 n2 (2013), the court held that “unless the legislature expressly provides otherwise, a culpable mental state is required for all facts that the state must prove beyond a reasonable doubt to convict a defendant except those that relate solely to the statute of limitations, jurisdiction, venue, or other procedural prerequisites to conviction.”

In State v. Nelson, 241 Or App 681, 688 (2011), the court held that the mental state of “knowingly” applied to the material element of subjecting the victim to physical compulsion as described in the first-degree sexual abuse statute because the forcible compulsion element was the “harm or evil that the legislature specifically sought to prevent” in enacting the statute. Likewise, the child’s age is a material element of the child pornography offenses, as the victim’s status as a child goes to the core harm or evil that the legislature sought to prevent.

The question then arises as to which mental state applies. In State v. Simonov, 358 Or 531 (2016), the Oregon Supreme Court ruled that when a material element is silent as to culpable mental state, the answer depends on whether the element is classified as “conduct,” a “circumstance,” or a “result.” The minimum culpable mental state for conduct elements is knowledge. Id. at 540; ORS 161.085(7) and (8). The minimum culpable mental state for a result or circumstance element is criminal negligence. ORS 161.085(10). In Simonov, the issue was how to define the element of “without consent” in the crime of unlawful use of a vehicle. The state argued that “without consent” is a circumstance and, therefore, the lowest applicable mental state of criminal negligence applied. However, the court held that “without consent” was inseparable from the conduct element of “using” a vehicle, as it is the non-consensual use of the vehicle that is the essence of the crime. It held:

[W]hen an element of an offense within the Criminal Code describes the nature, that is, the essential character, of a proscribed act or omission, it generally is a conduct element, and (unless different mental states are specified in the statute defining the offense), the minimum culpable mental state is knowledge.

Simonov, 358 Or 519. Applying Simonov to the child pornography statutes which are in the Oregon Criminal Code, see ORS 161.005, counsel should argue that the victim’s status as a child similarly is the inseparable and essential aspect of the prohibited act, whether it be viewing, possession, distributing, or producing child pornography. All of those acts are legal if the person is an adult; it is the depicted person’s age that is the gravamen of the crime. Therefore, as the age is inseparable from the conduct element of the offense, the applicable mental state should be knowledge. That means the state must prove beyond a reasonable doubt that defendant acted with an “awareness” the person depicted was a child. ORS 161.085(8).

VIII. Statutory Exceptions

ORS 163.682 lists a number of activities to which the child pornography statutes do not apply. They include:

(1) Any legitimate medical procedure performed by or under the direction of a person licensed to provide medical services for the purpose of medical diagnosis or treatment, including the recording of medical procedures;

(2) Any activity undertaken in the course of bona fide law enforcement activity or necessary to the proper functioning of the criminal justice system, except that this exception shall not apply to any activity prohibited by ORS 163.670 (Using child in display of sexually explicit conduct);

(3) Any bona fide educational activity, including studies and lectures, in the fields of medicine, psychotherapy, sociology or criminology, except that this exception shall not apply to any activity prohibited by ORS 163.670 (Using child in display of sexually explicit conduct);

(4) Obtaining, viewing or possessing a visual recording as part of a bona fide treatment program for sexual offenders; or

(5) A public library, as defined in ORS 357.400 (Definitions for ORS 357.400 to 357.621), or a library exempt from taxation under ORS 307.090 (Property of the state, counties and other municipal corporations) or 307.130 (Property of art museums, volunteer fire departments or literary, benevolent, charitable and scientific institutions), except that these exceptions do not apply to any activity prohibited by ORS 163.670 (using child in display of sexually explicit conduct).

Also, ORS 163.676 exempts employees of “motion picture theaters” from prosecution under ORS 163.684 or under “any city or home rule county ordinance for exhibiting or possessing with intent to exhibit any obscene matter or performance provided the employee is acting within the scope of regular employment at a showing open to the public.” The statute likely is an artifact of an earlier statutory scheme, as evidenced by its irrational scope: It exempts employees from a prosecution for encouraging child sexual abuse in the first degree, but not from the second and third degree crimes.

IX. Constitutional Challenges to the Statutes

A. Vagueness

The phrase “lewd exhibition” has withstood a void for vagueness challenge. It means an exhibition with the intent of stimulating lust or sexual desire of the person who views the exhibition. State v. Meyer, 120 Or App 319 (1993).

The phrase “visual recording” in ORS 163.684 also is not unconstitutionally vague because “[t]he specific types of recordings that the statute prohibits make clear that the general reference to ‘other visual recording[s]’ is limited to visual recordings that involve real children and real abuse, not images that are ‘virtual’ or ‘invented.’ ” State v. Dimock, 174 Or App 500 (2001), rev den, 334 Or 190 (2002).

B. Free Expression

A statute is unconstitutional under Article I, section 8, of the Oregon Constitution when it is directed at expression rather than conduct. Beginning with the seminal case of State v. Stoneman, 323 Or 536 (1996), Oregon appellate courts have upheld Oregon statutes prohibiting conduct involving child pornography against challenges under the free expression clause of Article I, section 8, of the Oregon Constitution.

In Stoneman, the Oregon Supreme Court found that a former statute criminalizing acts by the “producers, purveyors, and purchasers” of child pornography, ORS 163.680 (1987), reflected “the state’s determination to deter the harm that arises from participation of children in sexually explicit conduct for the purpose of visual recording.” In light of this purpose, the court found that “the legislative focus was not on the message or ‘substance’ of the photographs and videotapes but, rather, on their undeniable relationship to the underlying acts of sexual exploitation of children.” The court concluded that the statute:

prohibited the purchase of certain communicative materials, not in terms of their communicative substance, but in terms of their status as the products of acts that necessarily have harmed the child participants. So understood, it will be seen that the statute punished sexual exploitation by commerce that is a continuation and an integral part of the underlying harmful acts.”

Id. at 548 (emphasis in original). The court went on to find that the legislature could lawfully regulate commerce in communicative materials derived from actual sexual exploitation of children “where the manufacture of those products for distribution and purchase provides the very raison d’etre for engaging in the underlying sexual abuse,” as the state had the authority to both forbid direct harm and “destroy the incentives for causing that harm, at least when that incentive is to sell others a visual reproduction of the harmful act itself.” Id. at 542-43. Based on these findings, the court held that the statute “was narrowly tailored to reach only forbidden effects” and therefore did not violate Article I, section 8.” It emphasized that “the permissible reach of this statute extended only so far as is required for the protection of children from the harm inherent in sexual exploitation.” Id. at 543-44, n 21.

The Court of Appeals subsequently upheld later versions of the statute against Article I, section 8, challenges relying on language from Stoneman indicating that child pornography is a “continuation and integral part” of the underlying harm. See State v. Ready, 148 Or App 149, 160, rev den, 326 Or 68 (1997) (finding a former statute was not overbroad under Article I, section 8, if interpreted to apply to images that were produced or acquired prior to the enactment of the statute because, under the “rationale” from Stoneman, it could lawfully address not only the harmful effects caused by the production of child pornography, but also the continuation of that harm via the possession and use of those images which “exists regardless of when the videos were produced and acquired”); State v. Dimock, 174 Or App 500, 504, (2001), rev den, 334 Or 190 (2002) (upholding under Article I, section 8, the prohibitions on duplicating and distributing child pornography under ORS 163.684 and noting: “If the state may constitutionally prohibit the mere possession of visual recordings of sexually explicit conduct involving a child, as we held in Ready, it may also prohibit their duplication and distribution.”).

In State v. Betnar, 214 Or App 416, 423 (2007), the court affirmed its decision in Dimock that ORS 163.684 does not violate Article I, section 8, emphasizing that the aim of the statute was not the underlying sexual abuse, the circumstances of which “are not within the ambit of the statute’s proscription,” but rather the “further exploitative conduct that occurs after the abuse takes place” via the dissemination of images depicting that abuse.

Notably, the court in Stoneman relied in part on a statutory affirmative defense to former ORS 163.680 when the production of the image did not involve illegal conduct. This meant that, for example, a defendant would not be found guilty if he could prove the image at issue depicted consensual sexual conduct involving a 17-year-old in a state where the age of consent was 17. As a result, in context, the unlawful sexual exploitation of children was a necessary element of the crime. See State v. Ciancanelli, 339 Or 282, 298 (2005) (explaining that the statute in Stoneman was upheld because “it made such exploitation a necessary element of any violation”).

The statute setting out that affirmative defense was subsequently repealed. Moreover, the current definition of “child abuse” brings within the scope of prohibited conduct images that depict sexual conduct of a 17-year old, even when that conduct is legal in the state in which it occurred. See ORS 163.665(2) (defining “child abuse” as “conduct that constitutes, or would constitute if committed in this state, a crime in which the victim is a child.”) (Emphasis added.) The encouraging child sexual abuse statutes are violated regardless of whether the depicted conduct was lawful where it occurred as long as it would have been unlawful had it occurred in Oregon. Thus, the adverse effect relied upon by the Court of Appeals in upholding the current statutory scheme (unlawful sexual exploitation) is not, in fact, a necessary element of the offense. This leaves open the argument that these statutes violate Article I, section 8, of the Oregon Constitution, at least as applied to images that depict older teens under 18.

X. Search and Seizure Issues

A. Statutory Provisions Regarding Electronic Privacy

Several state and federal statutes pertain to law enforcement’s ability to investigate crimes involving the transmission of child pornography over the internet. The federal Stored Communications Act (SCA), 18 USC § 2701 et seq., applies to two types of service providers: “electronic communication service providers”—entities that allow users to send and receive wire or electronic communications—and “remote computing service providers,” which are entities that provide computer storage or processing services by means of an electronic communications system.

Law enforcement must obtain a warrant to require “a provider of electronic communication service” to disclose the “contents of a wire or electronic communication” that is in electronic storage for 180 days or less. If the contents of communications are in storage for more than 180 days, they may be obtained with a warrant, administrative subpoena and notice to the subscriber, or a court order. 18 USC § 2703. See also In re U.S., 665 F. Supp. 2d 1210, 1212-14 (D. Or 2014) (describing statute).

A violation of any of the statutory provisions set forth in the SCA regarding the process for obtaining electronic communications from service providers does not provide the affected person with the remedy of exclusion of unlawfully obtained evidence. 18 USC § 2708; United States v. Ferguson, 508 F Supp 2d 7 (D.D.C. 2007). Therefore, defendant must allege a constitutional violation in the search or seizure of electronic communications to have a shot at suppression.

In 2009 the Oregon legislature enacted ORS 136.583, the purpose of which has been described as allowing “Oregon courts to issue warrants for electronic communications in accordance with the Stored Communications Act (SCA), 18 USC sections 2701 to 2712.” State v. Rose, 264 Or App 95, rev den, 356 Or 400 (2014). The statute authorizes search warrants and other court process for obtaining “papers, documents, records or other things” from third parties located both inside and out of state. Thus, the statute gives law enforcement the ability to gather relevant electronic data from companies in other states, such as Google and Yahoo that provide email service and other online communication and storage services. See Rose, supra (ORS 136.583 is a long-arm statute that gives Oregon courts the authority to order the production of documents in the possession of entities in other states, as long as Oregon has personal jurisdiction over the entity, which can be established by evidence of doing business in the state).

Practice Tip

Another feature of ORS 136.583 is that it allows the proponent of evidence obtained by search warrant or other process to offer the material at trial without having to call the custodian of records, as long as certain conditions are met. ORS 136.583(7). One condition is that the proponent must provide advance written notice to the other side of the proponent’s intent to offer the material, so that the other side has an opportunity to object. ORS 136.583(7). If the state fails to provide the requisite notice of intent to use this material, the defendant should object to the admissibility of this evidence on foundational grounds. See OEC 901.

An argument can be made that ORS 136.583 does not authorize the state to subpoena third parties for documents before a charging instrument has been filed. The opening paragraph authorizing “criminal process” for the seizure or production of papers, documents, records or other things, provides that this authorization is “subject to ORS 136.580(2).” That statute requires a party desiring the production of documents before trial to obtain a court order authorizing the issuance of such a subpoena, and therefore contemplates a filed case and a pending trial or hearing date. Thus, it would appear that the state may not use ORS 136.583 to issue a subpoena to an out-of-state entity for documents until after an indictment has been obtained. That said, ORS 136.583 also applies to other “criminal process,” defined to include search warrants, the issuance of which is not controlled by ORS 136.580(2).

B. Challenging Search Warrants

In child pornography investigations, law enforcement employs search warrants in the usual way to search the suspect’s home and to seize and search computer equipment, storage devices, and other electronic media. Law enforcement also obtains search warrants to serve upon providers of electronic communication services such as Google and Yahoo as required by the Stored Communications Act.

Issues of probable cause, overbreadth, particularity (see sidebar) and other warrant requirements present fertile ground for litigation in the context of digital searches. For background, counsel should read the Ninth Circuit decision in [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=11100745107771394348&q=United+States+v.+Comprehensive+Drug+Testing,+Inc.,+621+F3d+1162&hl=en&as_sdt=1ffffffffffffffffffffffffffffffffe000000000000001f000001ffffffecfff87fe3fffffff00108000400000000003 United States v. Comprehensive Drug Testing, Inc]., 621 F3d 1162 (9th Cir. 2010), in which the court described the constitutional problem that arises from a general search of an entire computer system when probable cause pertains only to limited data within that system. See also United States v. Galpin, 720 F3d 436 (2nd Cir 2013) (child pornography case where court held that warrant failed to meet particularity requirement and constituted a “general” warrant); State v. Mansor, 279 Or App 778 (2016), review allowed, 360 Or 752 (2017) (warrants authorizing the forensic examination of devices must specifically identify and carefully circumscribe the information to be examined and the “place” to be searched—which in turn requires that the “items” within that “place” (data and files on the computer) be identified with constitutionally sufficient particularity); State v. Friddle, 281 Or App 130 (2016) (court held the affidavit was insufficient to support probable cause to search digital storage devices and therefore search for such devices unlawful); State v. Keodara, 191 Wash App 305, 313, 364 P3d 777, 781 (2015), review denied, 185 Wash. 2d 1028, 377 P3d 718 (2016) (search warrant affidavit failed to demonstrate with sufficient particularity that evidence of a shooting would be found and therefore warrant was overbroad); State v. Higgs, 177 Wash App 414, 426, 311 P3d 1266 (2013), as amended (Nov. 5 2013)
(affidavit insufficient and warrant partially overbroad thus requiring suppression of some of the items seized).

Practice Tip

The argument in support of probable cause laid out in the search warrant affidavit requires close scrutiny and perhaps the assistance of a computer forensic expert because it may be built upon technical matters beyond the ken of the average lawyer. An expert could advise counsel whether the affiant’s inferences or conclusions relating to probable cause are subject to attack. As with any search warrant affidavit, counsel should closely examine each step law enforcement took in its investigation to reach probable cause to determine whether any step amounted to a “search” that itself required a warrant. (See examples discussed in next section).

1. Probable Cause

In State v. Williams, 270 Or App 721, 725 (2015), the Court of Appeals summarized
the probable cause standard as follows:

When evaluating the sufficiency of a search warrant affidavit, our task is to determine “whether the affidavit alleged sufficient facts to permit a neutral and detached magistrate to determine that seizable evidence probably would be found at the place to be searched.” State v. Castilleja, 345 Or 255, 269, 192 P3d 1283, adh’d to on recons., 345 Or 473, 198 P3d 937 (2008). That standard requires an affidavit to do more than allege facts that support a mere suspicion that evidence will be found; even a “well-warranted suspicion does not suffice.” State v. Tropeano, 238 Or App 16, 18–19, 241 P3d 1184 (2010), rev. den., 349 Or 654, 249 P3d 543 (2011). Rather, the standard of probability requires the conclusion that “it is more likely than not that the objects of the search will be found at the specified location.” State v. Castro, 194 Or App 109, 115, 93 P3d 815 (2004).” (Emphasis added.)

Defining probability as “more likely than not” is helpful and provides a clear standard on which to build an argument for suppression. See also State v. Friddle, 281 Or App 130, 138 (2016) (in which the Court of Appeals highlighted that standard).

One recurring issue is whether, and to what extent, a defendant’s history of sex or child pornography offenses may factor into the affiant’s argument for probable cause to search for child pornography. It is fair to say that history alone, or even a current allegation of sex abuse, will be insufficient to establish probable cause to search for child pornography. See Dougherty v. City of Covina, 654 F3d 892 (9th Cir. 2011) (officer’s experience that child molesters possess child pornography did not provide probable cause for search for child pornography).

But the cases suggest that not much more is required to reach the probable cause threshold. For example, in State v. Tropeano, 238 Or App 16 (2010), rev den, 349 Or 654 (2011), the court held that (1) the defendant’s status as a registered sex offender based on a conviction for possession of child pornography, (2) his statement to the officer that he had obtained adult pornography from a country in which child pornography could be legally obtained, (3) the presence of a laptop in his hotel room, and (4) his direction to hotel staff to give him fifteen minutes’ notice before entering his room all gave rise to a reasonable inference that child pornography would be found on defendant’s computer. See also State v. Daniels, 234 Or App 533, rev den, 349 Or 171(2010) (probable cause to search for videotapes was found to exist based on evidence of defendant’s past sexual abuse of minors in his care, his unsuccessful “attempt” at video-recording one incident 20 years before the warrant, and, most critically, the affiant’s opinion, based on his training and experience, that pedophiles “often retain movies in reference to their deviance” and they “rarely dispose of their sexually explicit material”).

2. Overbreadth and Particularity

A warrant to search a digital device should be closely examined for an overbreadth challenge and for violating the requirement that the warrant “particularly describe the place to be searched.” Or Const., Art. I, § 9; ORS 133.565(2); see generally State v. Reid, 319 Or 65 (1994) (statutory warrant provision requires that a warrant “may not authorize a search that is broader than the supporting affidavit supplies probable cause to justify” and it “must be definite enough to identify with a reasonable degree of certainty what is to be searched”).

The Oregon Court of Appeals addressed the problem of overbroad search warrants targeting digital devices in State v. Mansor, 279 Or App 778 (2016), rev. allowed, 360 Or 752 (2017). It is not a child pornography case, but its exhaustive discussion of overbreadth and particularity make it a good foundation for challenging any search warrant that targets computer equipment, regardless of the type of investigation.

The defendant was the father of an infant who died of head trauma. The medical evidence suggested death by “shaken baby syndrome.” The defendant told the officer that, before calling 911 to report concerning symptoms, he spent fifteen minutes on the internet trying to research what to do. Based on that statement, the officer sought and obtained a search warrant to search all computer devices found in the home. The warrant was not limited to a search only for what defendant Googled in that fifteen-minute timeframe.

The court held that the warrant lacked particularity and was overbroad by authorizing a wholesale search of the entire contents of the computer equipment, when probable cause was limited to fifteen minutes of internet searching. Treating the matter as one of first impression, the court held that a personal electronic device is akin to a “place” to be searched rather than a “thing” to be seized for purposes of Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution. Mansor, 279 Or App at 801. “Concomitantly, that requires that the search of that ‘place’ be limited to the ‘thing(s)’ – the digital data – for which there is probable cause to search.” Id. at 802. The court recognized the difficulty in striking the right balance between “protecting against generality and overbreadth” and the “special challenges” law enforcement faces in the digital world. Id. 801-802. The guiding principle is that the search of a digital device “be as precise as the circumstances allow[.]” Id. at 802 (emphasis in original; internal citation omitted). In other words, if the officer believes it is necessary to search an entire device to find evidence for which there is probable cause, he or she must explain that necessity in the search warrant affidavit. See also State v. Friddle, 281 Or App 130 (2016) (warrant that authorized wholesale search and seizure of all of defendant’s electronic devices was overbroad because probable cause pertained only to one phone and the home security system; affiant’s statement that based on his “training and experience,” persons involved in criminal activity regularly record their crimes on cell phones was insufficient to warrant a broader search of all devices in the home).

To reach that conclusion in Mansor, the Court of Appeals had to distinguish its earlier opinion in State v. Rose, 264 Or App 95, rev den, 356 Or 400 (2014), in which it rejected an overbreadth challenge. Rose involved a search warrant issued to Yahoo!, Inc., for the defendant’s Yahoo account. The warrant authorized the seizure of all emails in the account, despite the fact that the target of the investigation was emails within a two-month timeframe that would contain illicit images of a 16-year-old. Defendant argued that the warrant was insufficiently particular because it did not limit the search to the pertinent time period or to the relevant subject matter. But the court held that because the warrant stated police could search for evidence of the crimes of using a child in a display of sexually explicit conduct and encouraging child sexual abuse located in the Yahoo account, it was sufficiently particular, “and the description of the items to be seized left the officers with no discretion in the matter.” Rose, 264 Or App at 109. The court also concluded that the scope of probable cause included not just the photographs but also other email communications between the defendant and the minor.

The court in Mansor distinguished Rose by limiting it to its circumstances and the precise framing of the issue on appeal. Specifically, the Mansor court held that the result in Rose boiled down to a rejection of the defendant’s fact-specific claim that probable cause was limited only to the two illicit photographs contained in two emails sent in a narrow timeframe. Mansor, 279 Or App at 800-801. Importantly, the court held that its decision in Rose cannot be read as a categorical rejection of overbreadth arguments based on warrants that fail to limit their scope to the pertinent timeframe or to the relevant subject matter. Mansor, 279 Or App at 800.

Another helpful takeaway from Rose is that the court regards online social media accounts like Yahoo, Google, and Facebook as “places,” just as the court in Mansor considered a computer a “place” for purposes of Article I, section 9. See Rose, 264 Or App at 108 (referring to defendant’s Yahoo account as a “place” to be searched). Therefore, as with a search of a computer device, the search warrant must target only those parts of a person’s online account for which there is probable cause; or, the affidavit must explain why a broader search and seizure is necessary under the circumstances.

Ninth Circuit cases involving computer searches are helpful to build arguments around overbreadth or particularity. See United States v. Cotterman, 709 F3d 952 (9th Cir. 2013) (“papers” protected by the Fourth Amendment include those in digital form); United States v. Hill, 459 F3d 966, 973 (9th Cir. 2006) (describing the prohibition on general warrants in the context of a computer search; finding a warrant overbroad but declining
to suppress).

An example of an overly broad warrant targeting Google accounts occurred in In the Matter of the Search of Google Email Accounts, 2015 WL 926619 (D. Alaska, March 3, 2015). Agents had probable cause to believe that Google emails within a specific date range contained evidence of child pornography, yet rather than seek a warrant for only emails within that date range, the agents sought the entire contents of the relevant accounts. The magistrate refused to issue the warrant on the ground that it was overbroad. The court recognized that investigations into computers inherently involve the risk of “over-seizing” and “over-searching,” and that, in the right case, law enforcement could justify a blanket search and seizure in order to obtain the targeted evidence. However, “the government must still demonstrate to the magistrate factually why such a broad search and seizure authority is reasonable in the case at hand. * * * Thus, there must be some threshold showing before the government may ‘seize the haystack to look for the needle.’ “ In re Search of Google Email Accounts, at *5, quoting United States v. Hill, 459 F3d 966, 975 (9th Cir. 2006) (emphasis in Hill).

This sort of challenge can be made not only to warrants that are temporally over-broad—i.e., those that go beyond a date range for which there is probable cause—but also to warrants that target “apps” within an online account for which there is no probable cause. For example, a Google account has separate apps for email communications; calendar entries; personal contact information of friends and associates; personal documents which could include health records, diaries, and other correspondence; spreadsheets which could include financial information; personal and family photographs and videos; social networking applications; and internet search history. If law enforcement has probable cause to search only emails, there should be no basis for it to also search the contents of all other apps. A warrant that allows it to search and seize an entire Google account should be challenged as a prohibited general warrant.

C. Challenging Warrantless Searches

Practice Tip

Compulsive consumers of child pornography perpetually devise and discover new technologies to secretly obtain, store and trade unlawful digital images and videos over the internet. Their spirit of innovation is driven by law enforcement officials who just as quickly detect the new technologies and find their own new ways to track down and identify consumers. Likewise, defense counsel must closely examine new law enforcement tools and look to compare them to other techniques found to violate a person’s right of privacy protected by Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution or the Fourth Amendment.

The concern over new law enforcement tools that affect an individual’s protected privacy interest within the home was discussed in State v. Smith, 327 Or 366 (1998). The court stated that high-powered telescopes, parabolic sound gathering devices, and infrared cameras that are used from outside the home but can gather otherwise imperceptible information about what is occurring on the interior of the home would infringe upon a person’s privacy interest. See also Kyllo v. United States, 533 US 27, 40 (2001) (“Where . . . the government uses a device that is not in general public use to explore details of the home that would previously have been unknowable without physical intrusion, the surveillance is a ‘search’ and is presumptively unreasonable without a warrant”).

1. The Shadow

One such tool used in child pornography investigations is the Shadow, a device that allows law enforcement to locate and track a suspicious device. By receiving radio signals traveling from the suspicious device to the router, the Shadow allows the officer to discover information that he would not be able to perceive without the use of the device.

In United States v. Broadhurst, 2012 WL 5985615 (D. Or Nov. 28, 2012), the court ruled that the use of a Shadow to detect radio signals emanating from defendant’s home did not violate the defendant’s reasonable expectation of privacy. The outcome might have turned on the fact that the defendant was using his neighbors’ routers to access the internet, which resulted in radio signals—and thus the scope of his activity—extending outside the confines of his own home. The court might have found a privacy invasion had the defendant been using his own router. In any event, despite the result in Broadhurst counsel should challenge the use of such a device when it discovers activity occurring within the confines of a home—i.e., computer use and internet activity—that could not otherwise be perceived, using Smith and Kyllo as establishing the protected privacy interest at issue. Counsel should also compare the Shadow to a tracking device and cite State v. Campbell, 306 Or 157 (1988), for the proposition that the use of such devices that allow continuous monitoring of a suspect’s activity amounts to a search under Article I, section 9.

2. NCMEC—State Actor

Another law enforcement tool is a mechanism for online communication services such as Google or Yahoo to report suspicious images to law enforcement through the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC). Indeed, internet companies are required to report the existence of unlawful images to NCMEC, along with subscriber information that allows law enforcement to find the account holder. 18 USC § 2258A. So far, the argument that internet companies are acting as government agents in this regard has failed. See United States v. Stevenson, 727 F3d 826 (8th Cir. 2013) (internet service provider reporting apparent violation of child pornography laws to NCMEC is not an agent of the government); United States v. Cameron, 699 F3d 621 (1st Cir. 2012) (same).

However, NCMEC has been found to be an agent of the government for Fourth Amendment purposes. United States v. Keith, 980 F. Supp. 2d 33 (D. Mass. 2013); but see United States v. Ackerman, 2014 WL 2968164 (D. Kansas, July 1, 2014) (finding that NCMEC is not a government agent). In any case involving NCMEC, counsel in a state case should consult Oregon cases discussing when a private party can be deemed a state actor for purposes of Article I, section 9. Assuming you have a solid claim that NCMEC is a state actor, pay close attention to what it did with the information provided by the internet company. Specifically, did NCMEC (or for that matter, the officer who ultimately received the NCMEC Cybertip) open a file of the targeted account that had not previously been opened by the internet company? If an employee of the company had opened an image or video file to discern that it contained child pornography, it is likely that any reopening of the same file by law enforcement will be found to be permissible as it merely replicated a previous private search. However, it is possible for a NCMEC report to be generated without an employee opening a suspected file. This is what occurred in Keith, supra, in a report generated by AOL. Known images of child pornography were identified through an automated process that compares the hash values of digital files to a database of images previously determined to be unlawful depictions of child sexual abuse. In those situations as in Keith in which NCMEC or another law enforcement person opens the image for the first time, it can be argued that such an opening is a search, analogous to the opening of a closed container, and that the officer should have first obtained a warrant based on probable cause.

3. Smart Phones and Mobile Devices

In any case involving the seizure of a smart phone believed to be involved in the receipt, possession or distribution of child pornography, law enforcement must obtain a warrant to search the phone, thanks to the U.S. Supreme Court decision in Riley v. California, 134 S Ct 2473 (2014); see also United States v. DiTomasso, 56 F. Supp. 3d 584 (S.D. N.Y. 2014) (defendant had reasonable expectation of privacy in texts and online chats).

4. Undercover Operations

Law enforcement personnel often engage in undercover operations by posing as fellow collectors or distributors of child pornography in internet chat rooms or through peer-to-peer networks. Generally speaking, the defendant who makes his child pornography available to others through the internet will have a difficult time arguing that law enforcement’s capture of those images and associated data invaded his protected right of privacy. For example, in State v. Combest, 271 Or App 38, rev den, 358 Or 70 (2015), law enforcement used specially designed software to search for child pornography with known hash values available on a peer-to-peer network. This allowed officials to identify an IP address associated with the distribution, which in turn led them to the defendant. The court rejected defendant’s claim that the police conduct amounted to a search, relying on two factors: that the IP address obtained by law enforcement was available to any other user of the network, and the police conduct did not sweep “so broadly that it amounted to pervasive surveillance of the defendant’s daily life.”

Despite the result in Combest, counsel should scrutinize the nature and extent of the state’s conduct in exploring the contents of the client’s network, and should obtain discovery regarding the technical abilities of the software program the police used to discern defendant’s online activities. Not all programs may be as limited or restrained as the one involved in Combest. For inspiration and ideas, counsel should look into the “Operation Pacifier” prosecutions that occurred nationwide in 2015 arising from an FBI sting operation that involved the government disseminating actual child pornography to unsuspecting targets through a “dark web” bulletin board and using a software program to gather personal information from users’ computers. In one such prosecution, a federal judge in the Western District of Washington suppressed evidence as a sanction for the government’s refusal to provide discovery regarding the software program and the scope of the government’s undercover activities. (United States v. Jay Michaud, US District Court for the Western District of Washington, Case Number 15-CR-05351-RJB).

5. Use of Subpoenas on Internet Service Providers

In Combest, the defendant’s IP address allowed the authorities to identify the associated internet service provider, who they subpoenaed for account information. This practice of using only a subpoena, rather than a search warrant, to obtain a person’s account information from the ISP was upheld in State v. Delp, 218 Or App 17, rev den, 345 Or 317 (2008). The court held that the defendant did not have a protected privacy interest in records independently maintained by his ISP, and thus a warrant was not required. The court reasoned that the account information was “non-content” data relating to the account, rather than substantive and inherently private communications; the data was maintained by a third party for use by the third party; and no source of law indicated the data should be treated as private.

At least part of the rationale for the result in Delp was dealt a severe blow in State v. Ghim, 360 Or 425 (2016). In Ghim, the court held that the fact that data is kept by a third party does not rule out the existence of a privacy interest in the data. Instead, the existence of a privacy interest depends on contractual restrictions that govern the third party’s use and dissemination of the data; general societal norms; and the level of generality with which the state analyzes the data. In the context of an internet service provider, the inquiry should include an examination of the ISP’s “terms and conditions” and any other policy statement regarding privacy to determine whether the contractual terms give rise to an argument in support of a privacy interest in account information.

6. Work Computers and Phones

Suspects who use a work computer or internet service provider to obtain or share child pornography may have a difficult time asserting a privacy interest in the contents of the device. Depending on the particular employment relationship, the state may claim that the employer’s ownership and control over the device and its contents sufficiently diminished the employee’s expectation of privacy with regard to any communications or internet search history discovered pursuant to a search. See State v. Meredith, 337 Or 299 (2004) (search did not occur for purposes of Article I, section 9, when law enforcement attached a radio transmitter to a pickup truck owned by defendant’s employer and used for work activities, distinguishing Campbell). Under Fourth Amendment analysis, the court might find that an employee has a protected privacy interest in work-related text messages, but that government intrusion into those messages can nevertheless be upheld as reasonable. See generally [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=1102310652013119778&q=Quon+v.+Arch+Wireless+Operating+Co.,+Inc.,+529+F3d+892&hl=en&as_sdt=1ffffffffffffffffffffffffffffffffe000000000000001f000001ffffffecfff87fe3fffffff00108000400000000004 Quon v. Arch Wireless Operating Co., Inc.], 529 F3d 892 (9th Cir. 2008) (employees had reasonable expectation of privacy in content of text messages), rev’d and remanded on other grounds, City of Ontario v. Quon, 560 US 746 (2010) (court assumes employees had reasonable expectation of privacy in texts, but concluding that employer review of texts was reasonable). See also In re Grand Jury Subpoena (Kitzhaber), 828 F3d 1083 (9th Cir. 2016) (subpoena for former governor’s emails maintained on state server was overbroad because its scope included non-government–related emails in which governor had a privacy interest under the Fourth Amendment).

XI. Discovery Issues

In any child pornography prosecution, the state will not produce copies of the alleged illicit images or videos in discovery. As discussed above, in the case of seized computers or other electronic devices and storage media, law enforcement will create a mirror image of the suspect device for purposes of its investigation and analysis. Because defense counsel will not receive his or her own copy of the cloned device, the state must provide reasonable access to the seized digital evidence sufficient to allow counsel and the defense expert to fully evaluate the evidence for purposes of developing suppression issues and defenses to the allegations.

Prosecutors and law enforcement personnel generally understand the defense function in this regard. Counsel and the defense expert should be able to schedule a block of time sufficient to perform a thorough review. Depending on the nature of the inquiry, counsel should insist that a law enforcement person not be present in the room during the evaluation so counsel and the expert can speak freely and not be forced to share work product information with the state. For example, the Oregon Department of Justice Internet Crimes Against Children Taskforce will allow attorneys and experts to examine digital evidence in an office in their Salem office building without a detective being present.

Practice Tip

If the state unreasonably restricts the defense team’s access to the digital evidence, counsel should file a motion to compel production of discovery that describes with specificity counsel’s need for greater access. To avoid revealing work product to the prosecutor, counsel should seek permission of the court to submit an affidavit ex parte that sets forth counsel’s concerns with particularity.

XIII. Sentencing Issues

A. Merger of Convictions

Merger in an encouraging child sexual abuse case is almost always an issue at sentencing, as it is the rare defendant charged with only one count for downloading or possessing a single illicit image. To determine whether multiple counts merge into a single conviction, courts apply ORS 161.067. ORS 161.067(2) provides that charges violating the same statutory provision and stemming from “the same conduct or criminal episode” merge into a single conviction unless the conduct involves “two or more victims,” in which case “there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are victims.” ORS 161.067(3) provides that in a single criminal episode violating a single statutory provision and involving a single victim, “there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are violations…separated from other such violations by a sufficient pause.…” Thus far, merger questions in ECSA cases have focused on the identity of the victims and the concept of “sufficient pause.”

In State v. Reeves, 250 Or App 294, rev den, 352 Or 565 (2012) (Reeves I), the Court of Appeals held that the “victim” for purposes of ORS 163.684 is the child or children depicted in the images. In State v. Reeves, 273 Or App 37, rev den, 358 Or 249 (2015) (Reeves II), defendant argued that the trial court erred in finding that each count involved a separate victim because the state did not establish the identity of each child depicted and did not prove that the children depicted were alive when defendant downloaded the images. The Court of Appeals rejected these arguments based on its decision in Reeves I, stating, “(1) a child’s anonymity does not make the child less than a victim; (2) the child’s victim status is determined based on whether the child was less than 18 years of age when the image was created; and (3) that the victims in this case, for purposes of merger analysis, are the children ‘depicted in the downloaded images that are the basis of the 15 counts on which the court rendered guilty verdicts.’ “

In Reeves I, the trial court determined that the counts did not merge based on its finding of a “sufficient pause” between each instance that the defendant doubled-clicked on the file name to open, view, and download an individual image. However, the Court of Appeals determined that this finding of a “pause” was unsupported by the record because the only testimony offered on the issue showed that the files could have been downloaded concurrently and that it was not possible to determine when the images were downloaded or deleted. Reeves I, 250 Or App at 306.

In State v. Cale, 263 Or App 635 (2014), defendant produced and transferred to his computer three images of a minor in his care engaging in sexual conduct and was convicted of three counts of encouraging child sex abuse and three counts of using a child in a display of sexually explicit conduct. The court held that the three counts of ECSA merged, because “the only evidence of a pause between the transfer of the three photos at issue [was] an invariable two-second gap between each transfer time stamp,” which alone was insufficient to support a finding that the images were not transferred concurrently or that there was a sufficient pause between each download for defendant to renounce his criminal intent. Similarly, the court determined that the charges for using a child in a display of sexual conduct merged because all the images were taken within one minute and 16 seconds, and there was no evidence of a pause in the abusive conduct.

B. Reconstituting the Criminal History Score

In State v. Dulfu, 282 Or App 209 (2016), rev allowed, 361 Or 100 (2017), defendant was convicted of fifteen counts of encouraging child sexual abuse in the first degree and fifteen counts of encouraging child sexual abuse in the second degree based on evidence that he had downloaded the prohibited images on separate days. The sentencing court determined that defendant’s criminal history score was “I” for the first count. For the second count, the court reconstituted defendant’s criminal history score to “D” based on the first conviction, and similarly increased the criminal history score for all subsequent counts. See State v. Witherspoon, 250 Or App 316 (2012) (a “court may use a defendant’s convictions arising from earlier criminal episodes to calculate the defendant’s criminal history score with respect to a crime arising from a later criminal episode).

On appeal, defendant argued that the court erred in concluding that the separate counts arose from separate criminal episodes, pointing to the fact that defendant was found to be in possession of all images on one day, when his computer was seized and searched by law enforcement. The court rejected that argument, reasoning that each download, occurring on a different day, was its own criminal episode of possession. Id. at 222-226. As of the writing of this chapter, this case is pending review by the Oregon Supreme Court.

C. Proportionality

The foregoing discussion of merger in the context of child pornography cases gives rise to a significant issue of proportionality in sentencing where the district attorney decides to pile on multiple counts of possession or distribution, with each count involving only a single image or video. Unfortunately, in State v. Parker, 259 Or App 547 (2013), rev den, 355 Or 380 (2014), the Court of Appeals held that the proportionality analysis from State v. Rodriguez/Buck, 347 Or 46 (2009), only applies to an individual sentence imposed for a single conviction and that it was “not appropriate” to consider whether an “aggregate or cumulative sentence” imposed for ten separate convictions of ECSA I was disproportionate to those ten offenses. The court then determined that a sentence of 45 months, the highest presumptive sentence allowed for a single ECSA I conviction, was not disproportionate to the offense.

Another type of proportionality argument may arise from recognizing that ECSA I carries a crime seriousness ranking of 8, while possession of materials depicting sexually explicit conduct of a child in the first degree, ORS 163.688, which requires possession of child pornography and use of that child pornography to induce a child to participate or engage in sexually explicit conduct, carries a crime seriousness ranking of 6.1 This means that a first-time offender that downloads an image of child pornography is facing a presumptive sentence of 16–18 months (with eligibility for optional probation), while the offender who engages in the more serious conduct of possessing child pornography to induce a child to engage in sexual conduct faces only a presumptive probationary sentence. This “vertical proportionality” scenario may be grounds to argue that an ECSA I sentence for downloading an image is unconstitutionally disproportionate. See Rodriguez/Buck, 343 Or at 58 (identifying “the penalties imposed for other, related crimes” as an appropriate factor for courts to consider under Article I, section 16).

D. Restitution Issues

ORS 137.106 requires that a court order restitution to a victim for any economic damages that “resulted from” or “would not have been incurred ‘but for’ the defendant’s criminal conduct,” but only when those damages were reasonably foreseeable. State v. Ramos, 358 Or 581 (2016). In other words, the state must establish both a “but-for” and a proximate cause connection between the defendant’s criminal conduct and the victim’s losses.

Oregon case law has yet to develop with regard to restitution claims by child victims in ECSA cases, i.e., where the defendant did not cause or engage in the underlying abuse. One case that may be helpful to resist a claim is State v. Sigman, 141 Or App 479, 484 (1996). The court held that a defendant is not liable for damages caused by another, and therefore the state must establish the particular harm caused by the particular defendant to support a restitution award.

In federal court, attorneys for child porn victims file restitution claims in cases in which the defendant possessed, received, or distributed previously identified or known images of the child. The victims and their attorneys become aware of the criminal case through the NCMEC reporting scheme. The lead case discussing the standards of proof in such claims is Paroline v. United States, 134 S Ct 1710 (2014). The Supreme Court held that under the federal restitution statute a defendant found guilty of possessing and looking at images of a child being sexually abused was the proximate cause of the victim’s losses and must pay the victim restitution for losses attributable to his conduct, but is only responsible for the losses proportional to his own individual conduct and cannot be held liable for the victim’s entire losses. See also United States v. Galan, 804 F3d 1287 (2015) (Ninth Circuit vacated restitution order in child pornography case because the trial court failed to disaggregate the losses caused by the original abuse of the victim from the losses caused by the ongoing distribution and possession of the images of the original abuse). As of the writing of this chapter, the “Amy, Vicky, and Andy Pornography Victim Assistance Act of 2017” is pending before Congress. (S2152). If passed, it would amend the federal mandatory restitution statute, 18 U.S.C. § 2259, and modify the procedures for determining restitution in child pornography cases.

XIV. Ethical Issues Involving the Client Presently Possessing Child Pornography

The client who currently possesses child pornography might present an ethical conundrum for the attorney. On the one hand, the attorney would want to advise the person that possession is a continuing crime and destroying or wiping the device clean is warranted. On the other hand, if the client presently is under investigation for possession of child pornography, such advice may run afoul of Rule 3.4 of the Oregon Rules of Professional Conduct and put the client and attorney in jeopardy for a prosecution for tampering with physical evidence, ORS 162.295.

The offense of tampering with physical evidence occurs when the defendant, among other possible actions, “destroys, mutilates, alters, conceals or removes physical evidence impairing its verity or availability.” ORS 162.295(1)(a). Key to the present discussion, the person must intend that the evidence “be…unavailable in an official proceeding which is then pending or to the knowledge of such person is about to be instituted.” ORS 162.295(1). In State v. Austin, 265 Or App 140 (2014), the court emphasized that, in the absence of a “then pending” proceeding, the statute requires actual knowledge of an imminent proceeding. See State v. Bailey, 346 Or 551, 561, 213 P3d 1240 (2009) (excellent discussion on “official proceeding”). “A ‘belief’ about a proceeding
that ‘might’ occur is not the same as ‘knowledge’ of an actual proceeding that is ‘about to be instituted.’ ” Austin, 265 Or App at 144.

Because the possession of child pornography is prosecuted at the federal level, counsel should also be aware of 18 USC § 1519, which, in relevant part, prohibits one from knowingly altering or destroying any record, document, or tangible object “with the intent to impede, obstruct or influence the investigation or proper administration of any matter within the jurisdiction of any department or agency of the United States…or in relation to or contemplation of any such matter or case.…” The statute is different from ORS 162.295 in several respects, including the absence of a requirement of a pending or imminent proceeding. However, the government must prove that defendant’s conduct had a “nexus” (a relationship in time, causation, or logic) with a federal proceeding or investigation. United States v. Russell, 639 F Supp 2d 226 (D. Conn. 2007). Also, the government must prove that the defendant acted with the specific intent to “impede, obstruct, or influence” an investigation or other matter. In United States v. Stevens, 771 F Supp 2d 556 (D. Md. 2011), the court emphasized that without the necessary element of wrongful intent, the statute would reach inherently innocent conduct, such as a lawyer’s instruction to his client to withhold documents the lawyer in good faith believed were privileged.

Also relevant to this discussion is 18 USC § 2252(c), which creates an affirmative defense to the federal offense of possession of child pornography if the possession involved less than three images and the person promptly and in good faith either took reasonable steps to destroy each image or reported the matter to law enforcement. (In the unusual case involving less than three images, destruction would appear to be the better of those two options.)

Rule 3.4 of the Oregon Rules of Professional Conduct provides that a lawyer “shall not…knowingly and unlawfully obstruct another party’s access to evidence or unlawfully alter, destroy or conceal a document or other material having potential evidentiary value. A lawyer shall not counsel or assist another person to do any such act[.]” The phrase “potential evidentiary value” is quite broad, and, at least on the surface, would seem to severely restrict counsel’s ability to advise a client to delete unlawful images or destroy a device holding contraband. But the phrase “unlawfully obstruct” suggests that there must be an external legal standard that would make the destruction unlawful, such as the tampering with physical evidence statute. Also, the phrase “knowingly and unlawfully obstruct” establishes that a lawyer does not violate this rule if he or she is guided by the intent to stop the client from continuing to commit a crime, as opposed to the intent to obstruct.

The foregoing is not intended to cover all possible scenarios that may arise when the lawyer is confronted with a client presently in possession of contraband. In general, the attorney whose sole intent is help the client comply with the law should feel confident in advising the client of the continuing nature of the crime and that he or she should delete, destroy, or otherwise lose possession and control of the contraband in a responsible manner. When in doubt, counsel should seek the advice of an attorney specializing in legal ethics.

For further reading, see ABA Criminal Justice Standards for the Defense Function, Standard 4-4.7 (Handling Physical Evidence With Incriminating Implications); see also, Gregory C. Sisk, “The Legal Ethics of Real Evidence: Of Child Porn on the Choirmaster’s Computer and Bloody Knives Under the Stairs,” 89 Wash. L. Rev. 819 (October 2014).

XV. Federal Prosecutions

As mentioned at the beginning of this chapter, the focus here is on state law, not federal offenses. However, because a state prosecution could “go federal” for any number of reasons, the state practitioner should have a general understanding of what a client would face in federal court.

According to the US Attorney’s Manual, the prosecutor considers the following nonexclusive list of factors in deciding whether to seek an indictment in federal court:

[C]haracteristics of the material, such as the age of the involved child, the type of conduct depicted, the number of involved children, the quantity of the involved material, whether violence or force is used against the child, and characteristics of the target, such as whether the target currently is engaging in sexual activity with children, whether the target is the parent or guardian, or is in a position of trust with the depicted children, whether the target has shown the material to children, whether the target is making money from the conduct, whether the target has committed any prior offenses involving child sexual abuse or exploitation, and the safety of the community.

United States Attorney Manual 9-75.020. Over the last two decades, Congress has significantly increased the statutory penalties for child pornography offenses and has provided the government with a number of tools to strengthen its effort to investigate and prosecute this type of offense. See generally PROTECT Act of 2003, Pub. L. 108-21, and Adam Walsh Child Protection and Safety Act of 2006, Pub. L. 109-248. The main child pornography statutes are codified at 18 USC §§ 2251(a), 2252, and 2252A. Section 2251(a) defines the crime of using a minor to engage in sexually explicit conduct to produce a visual depiction and covers the production or creation of child pornography. Sections 2252 and 2252A, which are nearly identical, define four separate offenses: (1) transporting or shipping child pornography, (2) receiving child pornography, (3) distributing child pornography, and (4) possessing child pornography. The government must prove the jurisdictional element of “in or affecting interstate commerce,” and in practice that is not difficult. For example, the government does not have to prove that images crossed state lines but simply that the images were produced or stored using a product that affects interstate commerce. United States v. Brown, 785 F3d 1337, 1351 (9th Cir. 2015).

For a person with no prior sex offense, simple possession carries no mandatory minimum sentence and a maximum of either 10 or 20 years, depending on the age of the minors depicted in the images. Section 2252(b)(2). Transportation, receipt, and distribution carry mandatory minimum sentences of five years and a maximum sentence of 20 years. 18 USC § 2252(b)(1). The production crimes carry a mandatory minimum sentence of 15 years and a maximum of 30 years. Section 2251(e).

For a person with a qualifying prior sex offense, the statutory penalties increase dramatically. Possession carries a mandatory minimum sentence of 10 years, and the minimum penalties for transportation, receipt, and distribution increase to 15 years. Section 2252(b)(1) & (2). The production crimes go up to 25 years with one prior qualifying offense, and to life imprisonment with two prior offenses. Section 2251(e).

Practice Tip

When evaluating a client’s exposure in a federal case, counsel should not automatically assume that a prior state sex offense qualifies as a predicate sex offense under the federal sentencing scheme. Federal courts apply the “categorical approach,” which involves an exacting comparison of the elements of the state crime and the federal definition. See United States v. Sullivan, 797 F3d 623, 635 (9th Cir. 2015). The facts of the previous crime are not considered. If the state statute prohibits a broader swath of conduct than what is embraced by the federal definition, it is not a predicate offense that increases the statutory penalties. See, e.g., United States v. McElroy, 2014 WL 1877409 (D. Or 2014) (Judge Hernandez ruled that because sexual abuse in the first degree, ORS 163.427, can be committed by touching the vagina over clothing, it is broader than the federal definition of aggravated sexual abuse which requires direct contact, and, therefore, it is not a qualifying sex offense that increases the mandatory minimum sentence). The Federal Public Defender’s office is a great resource to assist in determining whether a prior state offense will expose the client to increased statutory penalties in a federal case.

Projecting a likely federal sentence also requires counsel to consult the United States Sentencing Guidelines, which establishes an advisory guideline range after assigning numerical values to the “total offense level” and the person’s criminal history category. The federal sentencing guideline for child pornography is quite draconian; it counts as aggravating factors phenomena that exist in the vast majority of cases and, therefore, should not count as circumstances that enhance a particular defendant’s sentence. For example, there is an enhancement if the offense involved the “use of a computer,” which occurs in just about every case. The number of images, the age of the children depicted in the images, and the nature of the abuse depicted are also factors that enhance the sentence.

There are other factors that render federal court a hostile environment for defending a child pornography case, including the greater likelihood of pretrial detention, a resource-rich adversary, the erosion of the exclusionary rule under the Constitution, and the possibility of civil commitment at the end of a prison sentence for persons the government deems “sexually dangerous.” See 18 USC §§ 4247, 4248 (codification of Title III of the Adam Walsh Child Safety and Protection Act of 2006 which created the civil commitment program). As mentioned at the outset of this chapter, the defendant in a state prosecution who has reason to believe his case could be referred to the US Attorney’s office should give serious thought to striking a deal with the state prosecutor in order to stop that from happening.
