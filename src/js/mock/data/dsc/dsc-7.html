Chapter 7. Physical Findings

By Bob Moon

I. Child Sexual Abuse Cases

A. Introduction

Almost all child sexual abuse case are swearing matches between the child and an adult with no physical findings. Only in a rare case will the state to be able to produce evidence that the alleged victim in a child sexual abuse case had physical findings that are diagnostic for penetrating trauma. However, defense counsel facing such a testimony has an extremely difficult and important challenge ahead. It is difficult enough to win a child sexual abuse case these days when it is simply a child’s word against an adult’s with no physical findings. But when the state can produce medical testimony that the child has physical findings, more likely than not caused by penetrating trauma, this evidence corroborating the child’s statements can be devastating. It must be a main focal point of the defense attorney’s attack on the state’s case.

In cases with no physical findings, child assessment center medical providers often offer testimony that it is common for a child that complains of penetrating trauma to have no physical findings. In cases with physical findings, they will offer testimony that certain hymeneal damage is diagnostic for penetrating trauma. In other words, physical findings or not, you are likely to be faced with a medical provider that is a dedicated child advocate.

B. Anticipating Physical Evidence Testimony

1. Prepubescent Children

• Prepubescent hymens are red in color, thin and translucent. They are much more delicate and less flexible than the estrogenized hymen of girls who have entered puberty.

• As such, they are very painful when touched and they rip much more easily.

• If there are physical findings, it is said that this is “consistent with” blunt force penetrating trauma.

• If the child had a normal exam, blunt force penetrating trauma cannot be ruled out. The contact may have been penetrating and the vaginal tissues either stretched without being injured or the contact resulted in injuries that healed by the time the child was examined. (Injuries heal within 24–72 hours.)

• When the child alleges touching only, you would expect the findings to be normal.

• Studies of more than 2000 prepubertal children have shown that there is less than a 5% chance that a physical exam for sexual abuse will show evidence of abuse.

2. Postpubescent Children

• Once a female is postpubescent, her hymen becomes estrogenized. The color changes to lighter pink/whitish.

• An estrogenized hymen is much thicker and much more elastic, stretchable, and redundant than hymens before puberty. They are much less likely to tear with penetration.

• There are physical findings in only four to five percent of cases. If there are physical findings, this is “consistent with” blunt force penetrating trauma.

• If the adolescent had a normal exam, blunt force penetrating trauma cannot be ruled out. Evidence of acute injury disappears in an estrogenized hymen, which heals very quickly after trauma. The contact may have penetrated vaginal tissues, but the tissues stretched without being injured.

• Hymeneal and vaginal tissues change when estrogenized. These changes allow the hymen to stretch and move so that penetration of the vagina can occur without injury to the hymen and vagina.

• After puberty, the hymen and vagina are capable of stretching, enlarging, and returning to their original shape and appearance without injury.

• An estrogenized hymen becomes so elastic that women can have babies and have intact hymens.

• The anogenital tissues heal very rapidly and often completely without any evidence of injury within a few days, much like the mucus membranes in the mouth.

3. All Children

• Straddles and falls do not cause hymenia-vaginal injury. Straddles only cause anterior (3 o’clock to 9 o’clock line) and unilateral injuries. The hymen is protected from injury by the fatty padding of the labia.

C. What To Do First

1. Get Discovery

Ask for all physical records of the examination, including handwritten notes taken by the evaluator. Get copies of colposcopic videos or photographs. Also, ask for and make sure you receive before trial all studies relied upon by the state’s medical evaluator.

b. Learn the Terms

• Cleft: An angular defect on the edge of the hymen whose edges are closely approximated. The defect may extend to the muscular attachment of the hymen.

• Concavity: A curved or hollowed U-shaped depression of the edge of the hymeneal membrane.

• Notch: A V-shaped indentation or defect on the edge of the hymen membrane that may extend to the muscular attachment of the hymen.

• Hymeneal tear/laceration: A defect (injury) in the hymeneal membrane caused by a blunt object that has ripped or pulled apart (rendered) the tissue.

• Superficial partial tear of the hymeneal membrane: A laceration or tear of the hymeneal membrane that extends less than halfway through the width of the membrane.

• Intermediate partial tear of the hymeneal membrane: A laceration or tear of the hymeneal membrane that extends halfway through the width of the membrane.

• Deep partial tear of the hymeneal membrane: A laceration or tear of the hymeneal membrane that extends more than halfway through the width of the membrane.

• Complete tear or transection of the hymen: A laceration or tear of the hymeneal membrane that extends through the entire width of the membrane to its attachment.

• Transection of the hymen with an extension: A laceration or tear of the hymeneal membrane that extends through the attachment and into the surrounding tissues.

• Laceration: A defect of the tissues caused by a ripping or pulling apart (rendering). The wound may contain bridging structures.

• Incision: A wound created by a sharp instrument whose edges are well defined. The wound contains no bridging structures.3

• Abrasion: The rubbing or scraping of the surface layer of cells or tissue from an area of the skin or mucous membrane.

• Contusion: Bruise, an injury transmitted through unbroken skin to underlying tissue causing rupture of small blood vessels and escape of blood into the tissue with resulting discoloration.

• Cut: A wound made by something sharp.

• Erythema: Abnormal redness of the skin due to capillary congestion (as in inflammation).

• Hematoma: A mass of usually clotted blood that forms in a tissue, organ, or body space as a result of a broken blood vessel.

• Petechiae: A minute reddish or purplish spot containing blood that appears in skin or mucous membrane as a result of localized hemorrhage.

• Lateral: Toward the side.

• Medial: Toward the middle.

• Anterior: Toward the front of the body.

• Posterior: Toward the back of the body.

• Superior: Toward the top of the body.

• Inferior: Toward the bottom of the body.

• Dorsal: Along or toward the vertebral surface of the body.

• Ventral: Along or toward the belly surface of the body.

• Caudal: Toward the tail.

• Cephalad: Toward the head.

• Proximal: Toward the trunk.

• Distal: Away from the trunk or point of attachment.

• Visceral: Toward an internal organ; away from the outer wall.

• Parietal: Toward the wall; away from the internal structures.

• Medullary: Refers to an inner region.

• Cortical: Refers to an outer region.

• Examination positions: supine, sims’, prone, knee-chest, dorsal recumbent, lithotomy, standing, squatting, and sitting.

c. Study the Manuals and Guidelines

i. The Oregon Medical Guidelines for Evaluation in Children and Adolescents

This is the manual in which all Oregon assessment centers’ medical providers are trained (2nd ed., 2004). It is “the Bible” in determining whether the examination that was conducted is in accordance with the evaluator’s training (much like the NHTSA manual is “the Bible” for DUII cases and the Oregon Interview Guidelines is “the Bible” when assessing the adequacy of an assessment center “forensic” interview). An expert can be cross examined with “their” manual. State v Hites-Clabaugh, 251 Or App 255, 265 (2012).

ii. Joyce Adams

In 2007, Joyce Adams and others did a comprehensive study of the research at that time and developed the Adams Chart that describes physical findings that are “normal variants, findings commonly caused by other medical conditions, findings that are indeterminate due to insufficient or conflicting data from research studies, or findings that are diagnostic of trauma and/or sexual contact.” Assessment center medical providers will likely agree that the Adams study is authoritative, enabling you to cross examine the medical provider with the Adams Chart under Oregon Evidence Code Rule 706. Her guidelines were revised in 2016. The 2007 study can be found online here and the 2016 study is included as Appendix A.

(e) Is the study relied upon by the state’s evaluator flawed?

For example, the state’s evaluator will often testify, “We know that studies of more than two thousand prepubertal kids have shown that there is less than a five percent chance that a physical exam for sexual abuse will show evidence of abuse.”

Where does that come from? It comes from this study:

Hegner, Ticson, Valasquez, “Children referred for possible sexual abuse: Medical findings in 2384 children,” Child Abuse and Neglect, 2002.

Let’s take a closer look:

This is a study of 2384 children of all ages.

This study concluded that 96.3 percent of all children referred for evaluation had no physical findings for abuse and 95.6 percent of children reporting abuse had no physical findings.

But on closer look:

• The study sample was originally 2742 kids and the evaluators excluded the 358 children that were seen in the emergency room (and likely had physical findings).

• 421 of these children were boys (not likely to have physical findings).

• 550 of these children were referred because of an abusive environment (wouldn’t expect physical findings).

• 182 of these children were referred for medical complaints.

• The 2384 were of all ages, mixed acute and remote exams and any allegation of abuse or neglect were included.

• Those who conducted the study narrowly defined “abnormal.”

• Penetrating abuse was disclosed by mostly post-pubescent children when often there are no physical findings.7

(f) Is the study relied on by the state applicable to your case?

Does the sample group in the study relied upon by the state’s evaluator evaluate children that are the age of the alleged victim in your case?

When were the physical exams done in the study? When was the physical exam done in your case? Are they close in time? If not then the study is irrelevant to your case.

What was being used as “ground truth” as to whether abuse occurred? Confessions? Convictions? Simply that the child said it happened? If so, was the child prepubertal (and likely to say just about anything)?

Did the study combine penile and digital penetration?

Did the study combine all allegations? In other words, if the allegations in your case only involve touching, is the study applicable if it evaluated some children who alleged touching and others who alleged penetration?

Did the researcher use scales that were validated scientifically? The Berenson, 2000 study, for example, did not.8

II. Forcible Rape Cases

A. Sexual Assault Nurse Examiner (SANE) History

The first sexual assault nurse examiner (SANE) programs were established in Memphis, Tennessee, in 1976. The goal was to collaborate with law enforcement, shorten examination times, improve forensic evidence collection, provide more complete examination documentation and improve the ability to prosecute offenders. In 1992 the guidelines of the Joint Commission on the Accreditation of Health Care Organizations (JCAHO) first required emergency and ambulatory care facilities to have protocols on rape, sexual molestation, and domestic abuse.10

B. State Protocols

In 1987, California became the first state to standardize their sexual assault protocol statewide.

There is still significant variation in what evidence is collected and how it is collected in different locations even within the same state. A number of protocols explain the specific components of the evidentiary exam, some step by step. All protocols include the following components:

1. Obtain written consent.

2. Get an assault history, including orifices where violence was used or penetration occurred and by what, forms of violence used and where.

3. Obtain pertinent medical information including allergies, current pregnancy status and menstrual cycle.

4. Conduct physical exam for trauma and areas of tenderness, examine orifices for trauma and collect sperm and seminal fluid.

5. Collect any foreign matter present.

6. Comb the pubic hair for foreign hair and matter.

7. Complete finger nail scrapings.

8. Collect the alleged victim’s blood for type, DNA screen.

9. Collect saliva for secretor status.

10. Collect torn or stained clothing.

The areas of variation in these protocols include:

1. The amount of documentation.

2. Prophylactic treatment for STDs versus culturing.

3. What clothing is saved as evidence (all versus only torn or soiled clothing).

4. If head and pubic hairs are plucked and how many.

5. The collections of additional blood specimens for drug and alcohol analysis.11

In 2016, the Oregon legislature passed Senate Bill 1571. This is an omnibus bill relating to the testing of sexual assault forensic evidence kits. The law went into effect March 29, 2016, and, among other things, directs law enforcement agencies to adopt written policies concerning the collection, testing, retention and destruction of sexual assault forensic evidence kits. It further requires these policies to be available to the public. It established a task force on the testing of sexual assault forensic evidence kits. The task force is directed to examine the testing processes in Oregon and report their findings to an interim committee of the legislative assembly related to the judiciary. Accordingly, it appears that Oregon is about to standardize the sexual assault protocol in a document that should be available to defense attorneys once the policies are adopted.

C. Anticipated Testimony

1. Masters and Johnson’s Theory of Human Sexual Response

Some SANE nurses have adopted the Masters and Johnson theory of human sexuality when evaluating sexual assault cases.12 This theory states that a woman’s natural sexual responses safeguard her from injury during consensual intercourse. The physiologic changes associated with human sexual response (e.g., lubrication, increased muscle tension and vaginal outlet lengthening) in combination with the cooperation of sexual partners, was hypothesized to protect women from injury. Conversely, during sexual assault, the lack of these responses would contribute to the occurrence of genital injury. Based on this long held theory, any genital injury detectable by gross visualization was considered evidence of sexual assault.13

A review of legal cases involving sexual assault noted a higher rate of convictions when injuries were documented and there was “expert” testimony that these injuries must have come from nonconsensual intercourse based on the Masters and Johnson theory.14

In Commonwealth of Virginia v. Steven David Johnston, 2000WL33177221 (2000), the court ruled that the scientific literature submitted to them established that a SANE nurse cannot distinguish between consensual and nonconsensual intercourse by the presence of genital injury detected by gross visualization. The court further ruled that a nurse acting in a SANE role could not testify regarding Masters and Johnson’s theory of human sexual response because it was not consistent with the relevant scientific research.

Since this decision, other studies have also concluded that the injuries are present at the same rate whether the intercourse was consensual or nonconsensual.15 In light of these studies, defense lawyers should consider filing a motion to exclude the SANE’s findings. If injury occurs at the same rate in both consensual and nonconsensual intercourse, how can evidence of injury be relevant? Further, if injury occurs at the same rate in both consensual and nonconsensual intercourse, wouldn’t the inference that the intercourse was nonconsensual have a fifty percent error rate?

2. The Victim’s Injuries Were “Consistent With” Nonconsensual Intercourse

In 2002, the Virginia Supreme Court in Velazquez v. Commonwealth, 263 Va. 95, 557 S.E.2d 213 (2002), reversed the conviction which concluded that the SANE’s testimony that the victim’s injuries were “consistent with” nonconsensual intercourse was improp-
erly admitted. The court reasoned that to allow such testimony would, in essence, allow the SANE nurse to give an opinion on the ultimate issue, thus “invading the province of the jury.”

3. The Color of Bruises

A SANE nurse may also attempt to testify about the age of a bruise by its color. “The literature cautions the forensic examiner against trying to date the age of a bruise by its color. While we know that in people with light skin recent bruising is red or dark blue in color, and older bruising maybe green-blue or yellow-blue and older still bruising maybe barely visible, people vary greatly in their rates of healing. Medications may affect bleeding and healing response as well. Experts suggest that the size and color should be documented, e.g. ‘2 cm x 3cm, deep blue-purple bruising’ without further interpretation (Ladray: 1999). It is also important to remember that it can be very difficult to even identify bruising in individuals with dark skin if alternative light sources are not available. Unfortunately, since these light sources are very expensive, most medical facilities do not have them available.”16

III. Anal Findings

“The interpretation of anal signs of abuse (sodomy) in boys and girls is far more controversial than genital signs of trauma. The frequency and significance of finding are subject to substantial disagreement in the literature.17 The ability of the external sphincter to dilate considerably when passing large bolus of fecal matter without any injury to the anal tissues is a major contributing factor to the controversy. Variables include the size of the object introduced, the amount of force used, the age of the victim, the use of lubricants, frequency of episodes and time elapsed since the last episode.”18

In 1986 and 1987 researchers Hobbs and Wynne claimed that a pathognomonic sign of child sex abuse was a “reflex dilation and alternate contraction and relaxation of the anal sphincter or ‘twitchiness’ without dilation.” As a result of using this criterion, hundreds of children in England were diagnosed as having been sexually abused when they were not.19

McCainn, Vorez, Simon and Wells (1989) found anal dilation in 49% of the children evaluated and the mean time of the initial dilation was 65 seconds. The anus opened and closed intermittently in 62% of the subjects in which dilation occurred. Accordingly, about 30% of all the children studied exhibited the intermittent dilation and relaxation of the anal sphincter, which Hobbs and Wynne considered a sign of sex abuse.

McCainn, et al (1989) described other anal findings in normal children that are often considered signs of sex abuse. They found 41% of their group exhibited erythema. There is no question that children who have been sexually molested per anus will exhibit erythema. But in this study 41% of normal children exhibited erythema as well. McCainn, et al found increasing pigmentation in 30%, another finding that is often considered a sign of sex abuse. They found venous engorgement in 52% after two minutes in the knee-chest position. Again, venous engorgement has also been considered a sign of sex abuse. Anal tags and folds are also considered by some to be indicative of sex abuse. These are found anterior to the anus in 11% of the children studied. No abrasions, hematomas or fissures (common findings in sex abuse) were found.20

D.M. Paul observed that even with the use of a lubricant, penal penetration of the anus will almost invariably result in some injury to the anal verge. He stresses the importance of the history, from the child, of severe pain — not only during the abuse but when the child next attempts to have a bowel movement. He stated, “This exacerbation of pain on defecation is almost an invariable ‘story’ and is so impressed on the child’s mind that it is rarely forgotten.”21

Gardner concluded, “This research indicated that many of the physical findings often claimed to indicate probable sexual abuse are found frequently in non-abused children. This research must be taken into account when evaluating reports of medical examinations of children in cases of suspected sex abuse.”22

IV. DNA Evidence

[Note: The following section is provided by Jennifer Nash, Albany, Oregon.] In most sex crimes there is DNA evidence. Juries will give great weight to this evidence and it is very important that you fully understand the evidence being presented by the prosecutor. There have also been recent changes to the manner in which DNA evidence is analyzed at the crime lab that will have a significant impact on cases.

A. Considerations and Suggestions When Dealing With a DNA Case

1. Read Up

DNA evidence is complex and can be difficult to understand. Familiarize yourself with the subject by reading as much information as possible.

The following publications/treatises should be on every sex crimes practitioner’s shelf:

  1. National Institute of Justice’s June 2012 report, “DNA for the Defense Bar.” For example, the report contains a sample cross-examination of a state’s DNA expert (Appendix B) that discusses:

• DNA comes from cells in the body from hair follicles, saliva, epithelial cells, blood, sperm, seminal fluid and vaginal fluid.

• The crime lab has testing available to determine the source of the DNA (for example, they can run a test to determine that the source of the DNA was saliva).

• DNA can transfer from one person to another.

• “Rich” sources of DNA.

• You can expect to find the DNA of all of the people who live in the home throughout the home and if a curious alleged victim rummaged through the accused’s drawer and touched a sex toy there, her DNA could be present on that sex toy (i.e., they didn’t have to be sexually involved to have DNA on the sex toy).

• Scientists can detect (based on calculations) that a particular person’s DNA is on an object but they have no way of knowing how the DNA got on the object.

• That there are no tests to determine how long the DNA had been on the object.

• The ease with which a lab technician can contaminate a DNA sample.

b. Fundamentals of Forensic DNA Typing, John Butler. Original edition published 2009 (look for the newest edition).

c. The Oregon State Police Crime Lab policy and protocol manuals will soon be available on their website.

2. Hire an Expert

Do this especially in cases where there is more than one DNA contributor to a sample (mixture cases).

You don’t have to use the expert as a witness, but have them evaluate your evidence and explain any problems with the physical evidence or methodology used for analysis. They can suggest what to ask for from the crime lab and help you with cross-examination questions.

3. Request the Complete Crime Lab File

The National Institute of Justice’s report “DNA for the Defense Bar” has a comprehensive list of items you should request.

4. Read the Crime Lab File, Especially the Conversation Log

The crime lab takes detailed notes of every conversation they have with anyone about the case. If the detective or prosecutor called them, the details for the conversation will be documented. You can find Brady and prosecutorial misconduct issues here if, for example, the prosecutor asked the crime lab not to test certain materials.

5. Familiarize Yourself With the Terminology

You should understand every word of the crime lab report and be able to explain it easily to a middle schooler. Your expert can help. At a minimum, you should know the terms STR, PCR, Y-STR, “likelihood ratio,” “match,” “cannot be excluded,” “major profile,” “minor profile,” and “discrete contributor.”

a. “Major” v. “minor” contributor: A “major” contributor only means that that person left more DNA than the minor contributor. It bears absolutely no correlation to culpability.

b. Y-STR analysis is discretionary and will only be used in mixture cases when there is too much female DNA to locate a male DNA profile.

6. Carefully Examine the Assumptions Used in the Likelihood Ratio (LR)

Do they fit the facts of your case? Should you ask the crime lab to recalculate the LR? Note: Remember that the crime lab keeps detailed notes of all conversations they have with others about the case and will likely provide those notes to the prosecutor (once a request is made for their file, they will provide both sides with any updates to the file as needed). You cannot ask the crime lab to perform confidential work on your case.

B. 2017 Oregon State Crime Lab Changes

To be in compliance with the FBI requirements for access to CODIS, the crime lab implemented broad changes to their DNA testing in June 2017. A summary of some of the changes are below.

1. Testing Kit

The GlobalFiler DNA Testing Kit is now used. It examines 24 total DNA markers (locations). For comparison purposes, the total number of DNA markers examined before the change between 1998 and 2008 was 13 and from 2008 to 2017 was 16. The more markers used, the more accurate the results. The current FBI standards require 21 locations.

a. The GlobalFiler kit contains additional gender markers to assist in the identification of male DNA within a sample.

b. The kit has also been formulated to maximize information from degraded samples.

c. You cannot run old standards through the new kit. New standards must be provided to run them through the new kit.

d. Changes to profile interpretation guidelines are included in the change to the new kit.

2. Probabilistic Genotyping

In conjunction with the launch of the new dna kit, the crime lab has incorporated “probabilistic genotyping” using strmix software in mixture cases.

a. Probabilistic genotyping uses computer-based modeling to break down DNA mixtures into all the possible genotyping combinations of the contributors then it ranks them from “not likely, but possible” to “highly likely.”

b. This is the interpretation method recommended by President Obama’s Council of Advisors and Technology (disbanded under President Trump).

3. The “Likelihood Ratio”

Along with STR mix, a new method of providing weight to evidence determined to be probative in the case being examined has been implemented. This is the “likelihood ratio.”

a. The “likelihood ratio” doesn’t compare tested results to the occurrence in the random population (as the former method did), it compares the tested results to the evidence in the case.

b. It is a comparison between two conditional probabilities. The numerator of the ratio (top number) is the probability of observing the evidentiary profile if the person of interest donated the DNA to the profile (this assumes the POI is the contributor). The denominator (bottom number) is the probability of observing the evidentiary profile if someone who is not the person of interest donated the DNA to the profile (assumes the POI is not the contributor).

c. The number used for the probabilistic genotyping is derived from the STRmix computer modeling analysis. You will not be given the ranking list unless you ask for it from the crime lab. You should examine the ranking list generated by the STRmix and compare it to the numbers used in the likelihood ratio. The DNA analyst is who decided what numbers to use for the LR, chosen from the ranking list generated by the STRmix software. A simple substitution of another number from STRmix ranking list will change the likelihood ratio.

Practice Tip

There is more variability in other aspects of the DNA testing process than in the STRmix deconvolutions. You should have them all reviewed by your own expert.

d. The NIJ’s report states that the significance of the ikelihood ratio (LR) calculations may generally be interpreted as follows:

i. If the LR value is 1 to 10 times more likely, there is limited support for the prosecution hypothesis.

ii. If the LR value is 10 to 100 times more likely, there is moderate support for the prosecution hypothesis.

iii. If the LR value is 100 to 1,000 times more likely, there is strong support for the prosecution hypothesis.

iv. If the LR value is 1,000 or more times more likely, there is very strong support for the prosecution hypothesis.

4. Assumption of Contributors

The STRmix software will also make assumptions about the number of contributors based upon the total DNA provided.

5. Format Changes in the Report

The crime lab report formats have changed as well. The LR will be expressed in any report with a mixture. The number of contributors assumed will also be indicated. A further comparison might be included as well.

a. The further comparison will state the person either is a “match,” “cannot be excluded” or is “excluded.” Those three options are the only options now being used by the lab.

b. “Match” is only used for a single source profile. You will not see that term in mixture cases.

c. “Cannot be excluded” is used only in relation to mixtures. You will not see that in single source profile cases.

d. The likelihood ratios will sometimes yield very large numbers. They will be expressed in scientific notation.

i. X.XX x 10yy.

ii. The YY number reflects how many zeros occur after the first X number.

iii. 4.10 x 1025=41,000,000,000,000,000,000,000,000.

6. The Crime Lab Will Treat the Evidence Provided to Them As Neutral

This means that they don’t expect the exclusionary hypothesis to concede anything. They will provide the LR for multiple hypothesis in their report when appropriate.

a. They will run neutral propositions.

b. They will assume the victim’s contribution on items recovered from their person or upon which they can be reasonably assumed.

c. They will assume the suspect’s contribution on items recovered upon their person or upon which they can be reasonably assumed only after establishing a positive association through a LR.

7. Other States’ Use of STRmix

As of July 2017, only ten1states are using STRmix. New York has ruled it inadmissible. There have been no challenges yet in Oregon.

V. What To Do Next: Make Legal Challenges to the State’s Evidence

A. Admissibility of Expert Testimony

1. OEC 702 Testimony by Experts23

OEC 702 says, “If scientific, technical or other specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue, a witness qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training or education may testify thereto in the form of an opinion or otherwise.”

a. Scientific Evidence

i. What Is Scientific Evidence?

In State v. Brown, 297 Or 404 (1984), the Oregon Supreme Court stated that the term “scientific” refers to “evidence that draws its convincing force from some principle of science, mathematics and the like.” 297 Or at 407.

In State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995), the court defined scientific evidence as evidence “proffered expert scientific testimony that a court finds possesses significantly increased potential to influence the trier of fact as ‘scientific’ assertions.” 321 Or at 293. Thus, if the court thinks that a juror could think that the witness is asserting scientific principles, it should be deemed important “scientific evidence.”

ii. Federal Standard for Admissibility of Scientific Evidence — Daubert

The United States Supreme Court in Daubert v. Merrill Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579, 113 S.Ct. 2786 (1993), indicated that a court determining whether evidence is scientifically reliable should consider the following four factors (although none is decisive and the list is not exhaustive):

• Whether the theory or technique in question “can be (and has been) tested.”

• Whether the theory or technique has been subject to peer review and publication.

• The “known or potential rate of error” and the existence of operational standards controlling the technique’s operation.

• The degree of acceptance in the relevant scientific community.

Under Daubert, evidence must be both reliable and valid.

The advisory committee’s note to FRE 702 contains the following additional criteria:

• Whether experts are “proposing to testify about matters growing naturally and directly out of research they have conducted independent of the litigation, or whether they have developed their opinions for purposes of testifying.

• Whether the expert has unjustifiably extrapolated from an accepted premise to an unfounded conclusion.

• Whether the expert has adequately accounted for obvious alternate explanations.

• Whether the expert is being as careful as he would be in his regularly professional work outside his paid litigation consulting.”

• Whether the field of expertise claimed by the expert is known to reach reliable results for the type of opinion the expert would give.”

Reliability is the measure of how stable, dependable, trustworthy, and consistent a test is in measuring the same thing each time.

Validity is the degree to which a test of instrument accomplishes the purpose for which it is being used.24

iii. Oregon Standard for Admissibility of Scientific Evidence — Brown/O’Key

In State v. Brown, the Oregon Supreme Court adopted the standard for admissibility of scientific evidence. The court suggested that the following seven factors should be considered by the trial court in ruling on the admissibility of scientific evidence:

• The technique’s general acceptance in the field.

• The expert’s qualifications and stature.

• The use which has been made technique.

• The potential rate of error.

• The existence of specialized literature.

• Novelty of the invention.

• The extent to which the technique relies on the subjective interpretation of the expert.

In footnote 5 on page 418, Brown set forth additional factors to consider:

• The potential error rate in using the technique.

• The existence and maintenance of standards governing its use.

• Presence of safeguards in the characteristics of the technique.

• Analogy to other scientific techniques whose results are admissible.

• The extent to which the technique has been accepted by scientists in the field involved.

• The nature and breath of the inference adduced.

• The clarity and simplicity with which the technique can be described and its results explained.

• The extent to which the basic data are verifiable by the court and jury.

• The availability of other experts to testify about the evaluated technique.

• The probative significance of the evidence and circumstances of the case.

• The care with which the technique was employed in the case.

In State v. O’Key, the court reaffirmed the Brown standard but considered that standard in light of Daubert v. Merrill Dow Pharmaceuticals, 509 U.S. 579, 113 S.Ct. 2786 (1993). In O’Key, the Oregon Supreme Court adopted the additional criteria set forth above under Daubert. The O’Key court also approved additional criteria. These criteria are “the nonjudicial uses and experience with the process or technique and the extent to which other courts have permitted expert testimony based on the process or technique.”25

To illustrate, in State v. Dulfu, 282 Or App 209 (2016), defendant was accused of encouraging child sexual abuse after the discovery of child pornography on his computer. His defense was that he used child pornography to cope with his abuse as a child and not for sexual purposes. He offered expert testimony from a psychologist on this issue; the court ruled that the evidence “drew its convincing force from science” and concluded that it was “scientific evidence,” weighed the Brown/O’Key factors, and found that the evidence was not admissible. They found that the expert was qualified and used a differential diagnosis but that his technique of applying that diagnosis was “novel” and a “qualitative not quantitative analysis” not supported by scientific literature or studied, and that he relied heavily on self-reports from people he treated. Though the defense lost this case, the analysis can be used to attack state’s experts offering “scientific evidence.” The Oregon Supreme Court allowed review of this case in February of 2017 (361 Or 100).

See also State v. Beltran-Chavez, 266 Or App 590 (2017) (police officer’s conclusion that a driver “passed” or “failed” walk and turn and one leg stand field sobriety tests draws its convincing force from an external scoring rubric and is not a mere expression of the officer’s opinion that a defendant was impaired. It thus draws its convincing force from science so foundation for scientific evidence is required).

b. Expert Testimony Based on “Technical or Other Specialized Knowledge”

i. What Is Technical or Specialized Knowledge?

Evidence that is based on a person’s observations and does not draw its convincing force from a principal of science but requires expert proof from a person with specialized knowledge is “technical or other specialized knowledge.” State v. Stafford, 157 Or App 445, 456 (1998). It is expert testimony derived from personal observation made in light of the expert’s education, training and experience.26 See United States v. Johnson, 575 F2d 1347 (5th Cir. 1978) (witness qualified by extensive prior use of marijuana as sufficient expert to identify marijuana as coming from Columbia); State v. Briner, 178 NEB. 766, 255 N.W. 2d 422 (1997) (“retired” burglar with five burglary convictions qualified as an expert to identify burglary tools under Nebraska evidence rule 702); United States v. Anderson, 813 F2d 1450 (9th Cir. 1987) (experienced drug dealer qualified as an expert concerning techniques and methods used during unlawful drug transactions.)27

ii. Federal Legal Standard for Admissibility

The leading case is [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=11404623739922196630&q=Kumho+Tire+Co.,+Ltd.+vs.+Carmichael,+526+U.S.+137+(1999&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38 Kumho Tire Co., Ltd. v. Carmichael], 526 U.S. 137 (1999). In Kumho Tire, the expert’s foundation of expertise was derived from his knowledge and experience obtained while working in the tire industry. His experience was not founded on scientific methodology but from inspecting failed tires. The Kumho court held that FRE 702 required that the rule of Daubert be applied to all types of expert opinions, whether the opinion is derived from scientific, special knowledge, or skill and experience expertise. Thus a nonscientific expert, to survive a Daubert-based challenge of admissibility, must demonstrate the empirical reliability of their expertise and methodology. The court found that not all of the Daubert criteria would apply in a case involving the admissibility from a person with special knowledge, skill, or experience, but the trial court must act as a “gatekeeper” to determine the admissibility of all expert testimony. The goal is to ensure that “an expert, whether basing testimony on professional studies or personal experience, employs the same level of intellectual rigor that characterizes the practice of an expert in the relevant field.”

There is no “bright line” test but courts have considered a number of factors including but not limited to: (1) whether the theory or technique has been tested, (2) whether it has been subject to peer review or publication, (3) whether its rate or potential rate of error is known, and (4) whether it has general acceptance in the relevant field.

Some additional criteria have been considered and noted by the advisory committee note to the Federal Rule of Evidence 702. They include: (1) whether experts are “proposing to testify about matters growing naturally and directly out of research they have conducted independent of the litigation, or whether they have developed their opinions expressly for the purposes of testifying,” see Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals, Inc.;(2) whether the expert has unjustifiably extrapolated from an unaccepted premise to an unfounded conclusion, see General Electric Co. v. Joiner, 522 U.S. 136, 146, 118 S.Ct. 512 (1997) (noting that in some cases a trial court “may conclude that there is simply to great an analytical gap between the data and the opinion proffered”); (3) whether the expert has adequately counted for obvious alternative solutions, see [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=1700867040112396994&q=See+Clar+vs.+Burlington+N.R.R.,+Co.,+29+F.3d+499+(9th+Cir.+1994&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38 Clear v. Burlington N.R.R., Co.], 29 F.3d 499 (9th Cir. 1994); (4) whether the expert is “being as careful as he would be in his regular professional work outside his paid litigation consulting,” see [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=18204418469370025237&q=Sheehan+vs.+Daily+Racing+Form,+Inc.,+104+F.3d+940,+942+(7th+Cir.+1997&hl=en&as_sdt=3,38 Sheehan v. Daily Racing Form, Inc.], 104 F.3d 940, 942 (7th Cir. 1997); and (5) whether the field of expertise claimed is known to reach reliable results for the type of opinion the expert would give.

iii. Oregon Legal Standard for Admissibility

The legal standard for the admissibility of evidence offered by one with “technical or other specialized knowledge” for nonscientific expert testimony is not yet clearly established in Oregon. State v. Sanchez-Cruz, 177 Or App 332, N.4 (2001) (“it is by no means clear…that the courts are not to exercise a gatekeeping role with respect to all expert testimony”); State v. Leach, 169 Or App 530, 537 (2000) (the court indicated that the issue of whether a scientific foundation is needed before the state can offer “grooming” evidence was “unresolved.” However, it appears that a full “scientific evidence” foundation need not be laid in a case involving nonscientific specialized knowledge); State v. Stafford, 157 Or App 445, 456–58 (1998) (expert testimony about “grooming” was based on experience and specialized knowledge—not scientific evidence and was thus not subject to the Brown test for scientific evidence.) See also State v Henley, 281 Or App 825 (2016) (court held that “grooming” evidence was not “scientific evidence” that draws its convincing force from science. The “grooming” evidence in Henley was based on the witness’s “training and experience.” The court found that the state had met its foundational requirements for the admissibility of “grooming” testimony when the witness testified that she’d received a bachelor’s degree and a master’s degree in social work.
She had also received special training in forensic interviewing, which included training on the concept of grooming. In addition to her training, she had over ten years of experience working in child protection and she had completed over six hundred forensic interviews of children; State v. Woodbury, 289 Or App 109 (2017) (error for the trial court to refuse to allow a registered nurse who had cared for or supervised the care of approximately 20 patients with traumatic brain injury over her ten years as a nurse and who regularly observed people with TBI to testify whether a DUII defendant’s behavior in a video was consistent with the behavior of a person with TBI).

Again, these courts have ruled that the foundation laid by the state was sufficient, but the courts have never articulated exactly what that standard is. We should urge courts to adopt the reasoning used in the federal courts articulated in Kumho Tire and urge our courts to consider factors considered by federal courts that include but are not limited to: (1) whether the theory or technique has been tested, (2) whether it has been subject to peer review or publication, (3) whether its rate or potential rate of error is known, and (4) whether it has general acceptance in the relevant field.

Also, opposing the admission of expert opinions based on specialized knowledge can be done with the standards set forth in OEC 702. The testimony must be something that “will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue.” (In other words, the opinion must be something that will enlighten jurors beyond what is commonly known to jurors.) Also, the witness must be “qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training or education.”

To use the Henley case as an example, the court concluded that the interviewer was qualified based on her “training and experience.” This interviewer explained that grooming included actions like “spending time with a child, allowing a child to do things that the child’s parents would not allow, giving a child money, tickling a child, or massaging a child (sounds like something grandparents do all the time). This interviewer testified that any of these actions done “with ‘evil’ intent” could very well be “grooming.” Who doesn’t know that? How is that helpful to a jury?

See also State v. Clemens, 208 Or App 632 (2006) (police officer permitted to testify that sex abuse victim’s statement was generally consistent with the non-chronological reporting patterns of child abuse victims, based on his own experience. This was not deemed to be scientific evidence subject to the Brown/O’Key standard).

Another case discussing “helpfulness to a jury” is State v. Jesse, 360 Or 584 (2016). The defendant was convicted of Sexual Abuse I after he touched the front of his daughter’s diaper, then confessed that the touching had a sexual purpose. This defendant had adjustment disorder. His expert psychologist offered testimony that this condition caused him to ruminate and become obsessive and may have caused him to confess falsely. The Oregon Supreme Court noted that the psychologist testified only that it was within the realm of possibility that defendant confessed out of stress and not that the disorder can cause false confessions. It thus left the jury to speculate about a connection between the disorder and whether defendant touched the child for a sexual purpose. The court concluded that the testimony was not helpful to the jury and excluded it. See also State v. Dye, 286 Or App 626 (2017) (state concedes that memory expert Dr. Daniel Reisberg’s generalized testimony on the phenomenon of false memory would be helpful to a jury), and State v. Mendoza-Sanchez, 291 Or App 299 (2018) (the state, citing State v. Jesse above, challenged Dr. Wendy Bourg’s testimony about memory source confusion. The court concluded that Dr. Bourg’s testimony would have assisted the jury and that the trial court’s exclusion of her testimony was reversible error).

A case excluding “junk science” in a defendant’s favor is State v. Dunning, 245 Or App 582 (2011). The state called a police officer to testify, based on his training and experience, that one’s memory improves over time after a traumatic event. The officer testified that he had read a lot of materials concerning what an officer can expect following a deadly force encounter in an officer-involved shooting. This officer was a firearms instructor and he taught students what they could expect to experience psychologically after a deadly force encounter. However, this officer had no formal training as a memory expert. He had written no books or articles and he passed no qualifying exam. The court concluded, “That is not the stuff of expertise; if it were, any literate person with access to a library or an internet connection could become an expert in anything over one long weekend. Our standards are higher.”28

2. Challenging Proposed Expert Testimony

a. Timing of Challenge

The determination of an expert’s qualifications is a preliminary question for the trial judge under Rule 104 (1). See State v. Evans, 236 Or App 467, 470 (2010) (error to fail to hold a hearing under OEC 104 to determine admissibility of CARES physician’s diagnosis of sexual abuse where defendant was not afforded the opportunity to challenge the admissibility of the testimony). Thus, you can either file a pretrial motion in limine or you can wait until trial to challenge the evidence. Both approaches have advantages and disadvantages.

Check local practice. Judges in some counties consider motions in limine to be akin to motions to suppress and require counsel to file their motion 21 days before trial pursuant to UTCR 4.010. Do you want to give the state advance notice of your challenge? Do you have a better chance of winning if the judge has more time to think about it or if the judge has to make a decision mid trial? If you win pretrial, what are the chances that the state will appeal the pretrial ruling (which they could not do if evidence is excluded mid trial)? Would it be more advantageous to get under oath testimony from the state’s expert pretrial (where you will have the ability to get a transcript for trial)? Or will your pretrial questioning give the state and expert advance notice (more time to prepare) for your cross-examination at trial?29

b. Burden of Proof

The burden is on the proponent of the expert opinion evidence to make a sufficient showing that the test meets the standards for admissibility by a preponderance of the evidence. State v. O’Key, 321 Or at 307 n. 29; State v. Bevan, 235 Or App 533, 539–41 (2010).

B. OEC 401 (Relevance) and 403 (Probative Value Outweighed By Prejudicial Effect)

To use State v Henley, 281 Or App 825 (2016), cited above as an example, the forensic interviewer testified that grooming included activities such as spending time with a child, allowing a child to do things that the child’s parents would not allow, giving a child money, tickling a child, or massaging a child. What do you suppose the error rate is for any person who engages in such activity for the conclusion that this person was “grooming” a child—99.9 percent? So how is it relevant? Even if you add that when coupled with “evil intent” the action is grooming doesn’t that make the evidence, at best, conditionally relevant? (i.e., it doesn’t come in unless there is proof of “evil intent”).

If the evidence has some probative value, what is the danger that a trier of fact will use the evidence improperly in reaching their decision (i.e., prejudice)? Wouldn’t a jury likely conclude that if a forensic interviewer with over six hundred cases investigated found that adults who spend time with a child, give a child money or presents or tickles a child constitutes grooming, isn’t that highly prejudicial?

C. Other Challenges to Consider

1. What Does “Consistent With” Really Mean?

When a medical expert testifies that something is “consistent with” child sexual abuse isn’t that expert conceding that though their finding may be “consistent with” child sexual abuse, they can’t prove that child sexual abuse really happened? If child sexual abuse can’t be ruled out, aren’t there conclusions other than child sexual abuse that the findings can support?

2. Requisite Certainty of Expert’s Opinion

A medical expert’s testimony is not admissible unless the expert can verify that their opinion is based on a reasonable medical probability. [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=5379908112794544071&q=Feist+vs.+Sears+Roebuck+%26+Co.,+267+Or+402&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 Feist v. Sears Roebuck & Co]., 267 Or 402, 407 (1973). “For medical opinion testimony to have any probative value, it must at least advise the jury that the inference drawn by the doctor is more probably correct than incorrect. If the probabilities are in balance, the matter is left to speculation.” Crawford v. Seufert, 236 Or 369, 375 (1964). “The question here, however, is not one of the admissibility, but of the sufficiency of evidence; and medical testimony in terms of mere possibility will not lift the case out of the area of uncertainty if the other evidence leaves the question speculative.” Henderson v. Union Pacific Railroad Company, 189 Or 145,
160 (1950).

3. Cross-examining the State’s Expert

To prepare to cross-examine the state’s evidence you must review that expert’s curriculum vitae, all published articles by this expert, any textbook contributions, and any transcripts you can find from this expert’s prior testimony and you must understand the medical issues.

Talk to your expert. Find friendly doctors that can help you with the medical issues. Have them explain the medicine to you. Read the literature and the studies. Find out what treatises are authoritative. See if there are any explanations consistent with abuse not occurring. Review the expert’s complete file.

Expert witnesses may be impeached to the same extent and by the same methods as any other witness. See OEC 607. Impeachment methods include evidence showing the witness’s character for untruthfulness (Rule 608), evidence of bias or interest (Rule 609(1)), and prior inconsistent statements (Rule 613).

In addition, under Rule 706 an expert witness may be impeached with “statements contained in a published treatise, periodical or pamphlet on a subject of history, medicine or other science or art if the treatise, periodical or pamphlet is established as reliable authority. A treatise, periodical or pamphlet may be established as reliable authority by the testimony or admission of the witness, by other expert testimony or by judicial notice.”

The statements in the treatise may be read to the jury and the expert cross-examined regarding them. However, Rule 706 provides that the statements can be admitted only for purposes of impeachment, not as substantive evidence.

4. Profile Evidence

In State v. Lawson, 127 Or App 392 (1994), “profile evidence” is scientific evidence that must meet the Brown standard for admissibility. One way to view “profile evidence” is how the Oregon Supreme Court evaluated “grooming” evidence in State v. Hansen, 304 Or 169 (1987), when they observed, “For example, it is probably accurate to say that the vast majority of persons who abuse children sexually are male. This says little, if anything, however, about whether a particular male defendant has sexually abused a child.”30

5. Syndrome Evidence

State v. Moore, 72 Or App 454 (1985) (witness did not have requisite qualifications to testify regarding battered woman syndrome). State v. Butterfield, 128 Or App 1 (1994) (expert testimony admissible that “a caretaker’s changing explanations of the causes of a child’s injuries is a textbook symptom of battered child syndrome”).

In State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621, 631 (1988), the court held that psychological syndrome evidence is scientific evidence and can only be admitted into evidence if it satisfies the Brown foundational requirements. The court stated that, “We suggest that in future cases involving ‘syndrome’ testimony full foundations be established, if indeed it can be shown that the so-called ‘typical’ reactions can be demonstrated to be either typical or reliable.” 305 Or at 631.

In State v. Bockorny, 124 Or App 585, 592 (1993), an expert was permitted to testify that the defendant suffered battered woman syndrome and post-traumatic stress disorder.

In State v. Ogden, 168 Or App 249, 247 (2000), the court concluded that before a person’s behavior can be attributed to battered woman syndrome an expert diagnosis of BWS is required. In Tush v. Palmateer, 179 Or App 434, 441 (2002) the court concluded that a diagnosis of PTSD is required before evidence is admissible concerning characteristics of those who suffer from PTSD.

VI. Consider Challenging Non-Physical Evidence Testimony

Examples of such testimony:

Social workers offering testimony regarding the impact of child abuse on families or the impact of trauma and the ability to recall.

Child forensic interviewers who offer “memory expert” testimony regarding how children perceive and disclose abuse. For example, testimony that delayed or “progressive” disclosure is common (sometimes it happens, sometimes is doesn’t, so how is that helpful?). Testimony that recantation is common after the child has been abused (again, sometimes it happens, sometimes it doesn’t). Testimony regarding underreporting of sexual assaults and /or the low percentage of false reporting (how is that relevant to whether abuse happened in this case?).

Child advocate assessment center’s medical expert who testifies about the vital role they play in the community; getting referrals from doctors, talking to kids about “things that should have never happened to them,” discussing how some kids don’t get regular medical care, and this may be the only time they get to see a doctor.
