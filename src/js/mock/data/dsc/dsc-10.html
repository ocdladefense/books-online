Defending Sex Cases

Defending Sex Cases: Strategies from Allegation to Resolution, 2018 edition

Chapter 10. “Vouching”: Comments on Witness Credibility

By Susan Elizabeth Reese

I. What Is Vouching?

A strong legal principle dating back to the beginning of our legal system places the determination of a witness’s credibility solely within the province of the jury. In Marbury v. Madison, the United States Supreme Court set that boundary which was recently reaffirmed by the Ninth Circuit:

Just as “[i]t is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is,” Marbury v. Madison, 5 US (1 Cranch) 137, 177, 2 L Ed 60 (1803), it is emphatically the “province and duty [of the jury] to determine...the weight and the credibility of the testimony of the witnesses....” Allis v. United States, 155 US 117, 121, 15 S Ct 36, 39 L Ed 91 (1894); United States v. Bonds, 784 F3d 582, 603 (9th Cir 2015) (“[W]e must respect the exclusive province of the jury to determine the credibility of witnesses....”) (citation omitted). Accordingly, “testimony regarding a witness’s credibility is prohibited unless it is admissible as character evidence.” United States v. Sanchez-Lima, 161 F3d 545, 548 (9th Cir 1998).

United States v. Preston, 873 F3d at 837 (9th Cir 2017).

In Oregon, Justice Richard Baldwin authored the Oregon Supreme Court’s most recent analysis of the problem of vouching for a witness’s credibility in State v. Chandler, 360 Or 323, 380 P3d 323 (2016). The court stated that vouching is the “rule prohibiting one witness from commenting on the credibility of another witness.” He noted that the rule applies both to comments that bolster and to those that may undermine a witness’s credibility. See Chandler, at 329 and fn 3.

The rule is a judicially created rule of evidence, which is not included in the Oregon Evidence Code. The court expressly adopted the rule in State v. Middleton, 294 Or 427, 437-438, 657 P2d 1215 (1983):

We expressly hold that in Oregon a witness, expert or otherwise, may not give an opinion on whether he believes a witness is telling the truth. We reject testimony from a witness about the credibility of another witness, although we recognize some jurisdictions accept it.

We hold that if a witness is accepted as an expert by the trial court, it is not error to allow testimony describing the reaction of the typical child victim of familial sexual abuse and whether a testifying victim impeached by her prior inconsistent statement reacted in the typical manner when she made that inconsistent statement.

Middleton involved a 14-year-old girl who reported that her father raped her, later claimed that she had lied about the allegation, and then at trial testified that the rape had occurred as she originally alleged. The trial court admitted testimony from two social workers who had interviewed the girl. They were allowed to discuss the behavior “typical” of children who report rape by a family member and whether the complainant’s actions were “consistent with typical” behavior in such circumstances. The testimony also included statements that the recantation was “a very common kind of thing to happen.” 294 Or at 433-434.

II. Milbradt: “We really mean it…no vouching!”

The prohibition against vouching developed largely in response to the use of expert psychiatric testimony to attack a witness’s character. Courts have been concerned that such testimony would undermine the role of the jury in assessing credibility and the ultimate facts of the case.

Using an expert’s testimony to challenge a witness’s character “would create a class of cases in which opinion evidence would, in fact, determine the credibility of witnesses. Unless the function of a jury is to find the truth, its role is devoid of substance.” State v. Walgraeve, 243 Or 328, 333, 413 P2d 609 (1966). Thus, the rule prohibiting vouching testimony serves the policy goals of ensuring that the jury remains the only arbiter of credibility and that such a role is not usurped by another witness’s opinion testimony which could empower the jury to give ‘special credence’ to such testimony. Chandler, at 331, other citations omitted.

This concern regarding expert testimony led the Oregon Supreme Court nearly three decades ago to articulate more clearly its simple but emphatic rule against vouching:

We have said before, and we will say it again, but this time with emphasis—we really mean it—no psychotherapist may render an opinion on whether a witness is credible in any trial conducted in this state. The assessment of credibility is for the trier of fact and not for psychotherapists. In the case at bar there was a direct disregard of the specific prohibitive statement set forth in Middleton, 294 Or at 438, 657 P2d 1215: “We expressly hold that in Oregon a witness, expert or otherwise, may not give an opinion on whether he believes a witness is telling the truth.” An opinion that a person is not deceptive, could not lie without being tripped up, and would not betray a friend (to wit: the defendant) is tantamount to the same thing.

State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621, 624,756 P 2d 620, 630 (1988) (emphasis in original).

Milbradt has been the guiding star in assessing whether an expert’s opinion consists of improper vouching and thus invades the province of the jury. The case involved two mentally retarded women who complained that the defendant, a school bus driver, had sexually assaulted them. Trial testimony included that of a psychologist and a psychiatrist who discussed the alleged victims’ competence and their credibility. The psychologist, Dr. Farrenkopf, declared that he saw “no indications of deception” in his assessment of the two victims. In ordering a new trial, the court found that even the court’s limiting instruction was insufficient to cure the impact this expert’s testimony had on the jury:

The proffered evidence directly reflected upon the credibility of the testimony of the state’s key witnesses, a matter to be assessed by the jury…the inadmissible testimony was received and the jury heard it. The trial judge did not tell the jury to disregard the expert’s opinion. Its admission and the trial judge’s failure to instruct the jury to disregard the testimony concerning…credibility…were reversible errors.

305 Or at 626.

III. Experts and Scientific Evidence

Justice Robert Jones’ opinion in Milbradt included a lengthy appendix of decisions across the nation which “addressed the twin issues of the admissibility of evidence and the extent to which an expert can testify concerning the credibility of other witnesses.” See Milbradt, fn 5.

Since that opinion, concerns about vouching have dovetailed with those about admissibility of expert testimony in numerous cases. The descriptions below reflect a partial list. This area is ever-changing, and the careful practitioner is advised to remain current by regular involvement in OCDLA’s Pond listserve and the Library of Defense.

A. State v. Keller, 315 Or 273, 844 P 2d 195 (1993)

A doctor testified that during her evaluation of an alleged child victim of sex abuse she found no evidence that the child had been led or coached or had fantasized the incident. This was an impermissible comment on the child’s credibility, because the statements were, in effect, testimony that the child was credible. See also State v. Pergande, 270 Or 280, 348 P3d 245 (2015): a social worker’s testimony that she saw no evidence that the complainants were coached was “plain error.” In State v. Ross, 271 Or App 1 (2015), the court stated that “In many cases where credibility is critical to the outcome, even a single ‘vouching’ statement by a witness…with years of experience and training in the field of child abuse prevention, can be given considerable weight by the jury.” In fact, the trial judge may have an obligation to intercede and exclude testimony sua sponte. B.A. v. Webb, 253 Or App 1 (2012) (where trial judge did not sua sponte exclude testimony of mental-health counselor and a psychosexual evaluator who “emphatically and repeatedly offered their…opinions that [the] plaintiff was truthful in her accounts of the alleged abuse).

B. State v. Remme, 173 Or App 546, 23 P3d 374 (2001)

Testimony from Kroeger, a social worker, and Stiles, an SCF (DHS) caseworker was permitted in a case charging criminal mistreatment. Each discussed reasons the children might have given inconsistent reports, that recanting is common, and that the process of “disclosing” abuse often involves variances in detail. The court found that “neither expert rendered an explicit or direct opinion” on credibility or comment on the witness’s demeanor and ruled that, “The jury’s function is not impinged upon when expert testimony does no more than provide jurors with useful, non-conclusive information from which inferences as to credibility may be drawn.” 173 Or App 563. (Emphasis in original)1

Whether a witness’s testimony is admissible as “scientific” is a question distinct from but interrelated to the question of whether the testimony is impermissible vouching. Milbradt implied that the testimony concerning “typical” reactions of sex abuse victims was scientific and suggested that, for it to be admissible, a foundation as set out in State v. Brown, 297 Or 404, 687 P2d 751 (1984) would be required. 305 Or at 630-631.

C. State v. Trager, 158 Or App 399, 974 P2d 750 (1999)

A pediatrician from CARES testified that a child had been sexually abused. The Court of Appeals found, curiously, that her testimony was not “scientific” but, rather, drawn from her personal observations in light of her experience.

D. State v. Logan, 105 Or App 556, 806 P2d 137 (1991)

A doctor and an interviewer at CARES evaluated a child complainant. The doctor testified about the child’s statements to her and her conclusion that the child whom she examined [and who was “unavailable” at trial because she was unable to remember events]2 had been sexually abused. The interviewer, Butler, videotaped her interview, and the tape was admitted; there, the child identified the defendant as the abuser. The evidence was allowed as a “medical diagnosis” under the hearsay exception https://www.oregonlaws.org/ors/40.460 OEC 803(4) permitting such testimony. The court was not asked to address whether the testimony was “scientific.”

Judge Edmonds’ thoughtful dissent pointed out the dangers inherent in allowing this diagnosis, providing a hint that it might be impermissible “vouching:”

The record in this case is absolutely clear that the child was taken to Bays, not for medical treatment, but only for the purpose of obtaining a diagnosis that she was a sexually abused child and obtaining evidence that defendant was the one who had abused her. Although Bays may have told her that she was being examined for medical purposes, purportedly to qualify her statements under OEC 803(4), I am concerned about the implications that flow from the use of the hearsay exception in this manner. I expect that we now will see many hearsay statements admitted in evidence under OEC 803(4), just because the physician, at the instance of inventive counsel, said the magic words to the declarant as a predicate for the examination. OEC 803(4) was never intended to be a vehicle by which to manufacture evidence. See Louisell and Mueller, Federal Evidence 596, § 444 (1980).”

105 Or App 564.

E. State v. Wilson, 121 Or App 460, 855 P2d 657 (1993)

A CARES pediatrician testified to her diagnosis that the child witness had been sexually abused, although there was no physical evidence. The court allowed the testimony, despite a defense claim that it was impermissible vouching, because the doctor had included in her conclusions a complete examination and also because she did not express a “specific opinion” about the child’s believablity or lack of coaching. But see State v. Southard, 347 Or 127, 218 P3d 104 (2009) and State v. Lupoli, 348 Or 346, 243 P3d 117 (2010).

F. State v. Sanchez-Cruz, 177 Or App 332, 33 P3d 1037 (2001)

A conviction of rape and sex abuse was affirmed. A CARES physician, Bays, testified that a diagnosis of child sexual abuse is not novel and is a generally accepted medical diagnosis. The court held that the diagnosis of sexual abuse was scientific evidence, that it was sufficiently probative under OEC 401 and 702, and that the diagnosis was not substantially outweighed by any of the reasons against admission in OEC 403.

G. State v. Harrison, 267 Or App 571, 340 P3d 777 (2014)

A counselor at Liberty House testified as follows:

A: I believe the statistic at Liberty House is 96 to 98 percent of the time when a child makes a disclosure about abuse, there’s truth to it. The exact numbers, from my experience, are unclear. Excuse me. What is clear is that children rarely lie about these things. And over—I believe it’s over 95 percent of the time when child—when a child discloses information about being sexually abused, it’s true.

Q: Over 95 percent.

Counsel failed to object to the testimony, and the defendant argued on appeal that this was plain error and should be reversed, sua sponte. The Court of Appeals distinguished this testimony from “true vouching,” that is, “one witness’ testimony that he or she believes that another witness is or is not credible, which a party offers to bolster or undermine the veracity of that other witness.” The court said that this stopped short of saying the complainant was like the 96 to 98 percent of Liberty House complainants whose reports were truthful. Cf. State v. Remme, 173 Or App 546, 558, 23 P3d 374 (2001) (it is impermissible for a witness to “ ‘connect the dots’ explicitly by [giving] testimony relating [a general] phenomenon’s specific application to a particular witness’ testimony”).

The Harrison court ruled,

It is not apparent that Mills even indirectly connected the statistic that he cited to A’s report of abuse or its purported veracity. Instead, the testimony by Mills more closely resembles the kind of “general information…indicat [ing] that [a general class of witnesses] is or is not” credible that we approved in State v. Preuitt, 255 Or App 215, 221, 296 P3d 648, rev. den., 353 Or 868, 306 P3d 640 (2013).

In short, “Because the evidence in question here was not clearly vouching for [A’s] credibility…but was more akin to permissible testimony,” State v. Wilson, 266 Or App 481, 495, 337 P3d 990 (2014), “we agree with the state that there is a reasonable dispute as to whether Mills impermissibly vouched for A.” Thus, the trial court did not plainly err by not sua sponte striking the disputed testimony at issue here.

H. State v. Marrington, 335 Or 555, 73 P3d 911 (2003)

Psychologist’s claim that “delayed reporting” is a prominent feature in a child’s claim of abuse is a scientific assertion that requires a proper foundation under State v. Brown. Because this issue was a central fact in the case and the expert’s testimony had an increased potential to influence the jury, a reversal was proper.

I. State v. Henley, 281 Or App 825, 386 P3d 126 (2016)

Palfreyman, an interviewer at CARES, described defendant’s acts of massaging the child and other behavior she labelled “grooming.” The court affirmed, ruling this testimony permissible, because Palfreyman was not an expert and her testimony did not seek “to draw its convincing force from principles of science, but rather” from her training and experience in forensic interviews of child victims of sexual abuse. Significantly, however, the court noted that objections to relevance https://www.oregonlaws.org/ors/40.150 OEC 401 and prejudicial impact https://www.oregonlaws.org/ors/40.160 OEC 403 were not properly preserved. 281 Or App 834.

J. State v. Southard, 347 Or 127, 218 P3d 104 (2009)

Here, the court finally barred the testimony of a CARES doctor who, in the absence of physical evidence, made a diagnosis that the child had been sexually abused. The court found that such testimony was scientific, but found that the probative value of the evidence was outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice under OEC 403. The court cautioned, however, that “expert testimony with which a lay person ordinarily would not be familiar may be admissible.” 347 Or 143.

K. State v. Lupoli, 348 Or 346, 234 P3d 117 (2010)

The Oregon Supreme Court found that testimony by a nurse, a CARES social worker, a physician and a therapist who diagnosed the complainants as having been sexually abused constituted impermissible vouching. The court again cautioned that some aspects of the testimony might be admissible, but only if it was not based on an assessment of the complainant’s believability.

L. State v. Black, 289 Or App 256, 407 P3d 992 (2017)

Relying on the Oregon Supreme Court decisions in Keller, Middleton, Milbradt and Beauvais, the Black court affirmed the trial court’s exclusion of the defense expert’s proposed testimony that the particular interviews conducted of the child victims contained leading, suggestive, and emotionally coercive questions because allowing such testimony would amount to improper comment on the credibility of child victim. Black, 289 Or App at 260.

The Black court explained that even though the testimony did not go to the “ultimate question” of the child victim’s credibility, it went to the “penultimate” question—i.e., the expression of an opinion as to whether the specific complainant’s account comports with more general phenomena or dynamics bearing on credibility and therefore was an improper comment on the credibility of the witness. Id. at 263. The court concluded that the “proposed expert testimony was a “commonly understood way [ ] of signaling [the expert’s] belief [regarding whether] a witness is telling the truth” and was not relevant for an independent reason. Id. at 264.

On May 24, 2018, the Oregon Supreme Court granted review. Allowed a petition for review in State of Oregon v. Johnathan Richard Black, 289 Or App 256, 407 P3d 992 (2017) (A158879) (S065729) (from the Washington County Circuit Court):

Defendant Johnathan Richard Black has been granted review of a Court of Appeals decision that affirmed a trial court ruling excluding expert witness testimony proferred by him with respect to whether certain established protocols for interviews of children who have reported sexual abuse – touching on issues of credibility – were violated in the interviews of the children in this case.

On review, the issue is:

Does expert testimony from a child psychologist concerning whether forensic child interviews were conducted according to appropriate standards and protocols constitute impermissible vouching?

IV. Significant Cases Reversed Under the Holdings of Southard and Lupoli:

A. State v. Lovern, 234 Or App 502, 228 P3d 688 (2010)

ABC House doctor’s diagnosis of sexual abuse, in a case tried before Southard was decided, nevertheless constituted “error on the face of the record” and required reversal.

B. State v. Merrimon, 234 Or App 515, 228 P 3d 666 (2010)

“Plain error” for pediatric nurse practitioner to testify, in the absence of physical evidence, that she could make a diagnosis of “highly concerning” for abuse.

C. State v. Bainbridge, 238 Or App 56, 241 P 3d 1186 (2010)

Reversible error for pediatric nurse practitioner to testify to diagnosis of sexual abuse in the absence of physical evidence.

D. State v. Davilia, 239 Or App 468, 244 P3d 855 (2010)

Guilty finding after bench trial was reversed after CARES nurse practitioner testified to a diagnosis of “highly concerning for sexual abuse.” Appellate court declares this impermissible vouching in a close case which rested on credibility.

E. State v. Cordova-Contreras, 239 Or App 279, 245 P3d 147 (2010)

Juliette House doctor’s testimony that he diagnosed sexual abuse based on examination of child and observation of her interview was an improper comment on complainant’s credibility in the absence of any physical findings.

F. State v. Kelly, 244 Or App 105, 260 P3d 551 (2010)

Testimony from physician at The Lighthouse that he diagnosed sexual abuse in the absence of physical evidence was an improper comment on the complainant’s credibility. Court does not reach question of whether physician’s testimony was unduly prejudicial under OEC 403. Appellate court also upheld the trial court’s refusal to allow a “taint” hearing and refusal to prohibit child’s testimony as “unreliable.”

G. State v. Beauvais, 261 Or App 837, 322 P3d 1116 (2014); 357 Or 524, 354 P3d 680 (2015)

Following the holdings in Southard and Lupoli, prosecutors continued to seek ways to admit expert testimony to buttress that of a complainant, particularly in a close case. State v. Beauvais gave both the Oregon Court of Appeals and, eventually, the Oregon Supreme Court an opportunity to articulate what type of diagnostic testimony was permissible when there are some physical findings.

In Beauvais, the defendant was charged in a single count of sexual abuse and two counts of attempted sexual abuse arising from contact with two complainants. The state dismissed charges involving one of the children midway through the trial. Testimony in the case included that of a physician at the KIDS Center, Kyriakos, who reviewed an early report from a sexual assault nurse describing some “acute injury” in the genital area. Kyriakos examined the child six weeks later and found no physical symptoms. Nevertheless, she offered her diagnosis that the child had been sexually abused.

The Court of Appeals affirmed, noting that the admissibility of this diagnosis continues to be subject to a balancing test under OEC 403, even when there are physical findings. The court found that the significance of the physical findings was complex, and they corroborated the observations Kyriakos had made and thus supported her diagnosis.

The Oregon Supreme Court then addressed the issue that Southard foreshadowed but did not resolve: whether and under what circumstances a diagnosis of child sexual abuse is admissible under OEC 403 when physical evidence of abuse is present.

The court held that when proper foundational requirements are satisfied, the probative force of the diagnosis is based more likely on the strength of the causal connection between the physical findings and the diagnosis than the expert’s reliance on the child’s statements. The court ruled,

We therefore hold that, when a jury otherwise reasonably could perceive a medical diagnosis of child sexual abuse to be based on a credibility determination, the diagnosis nevertheless tells the jury something that it is not equally capable of determining on its own if (1) physical evidence meaningfully corroborates the alleged type of abuse; (2) the expert significantly relies on that physical evidence in making the diagnosis of sexual abuse; and (3) the causal relationship between the physical evidence and the diagnosis is sufficiently complex such that a lay trier of fact cannot assess the connection as well as an expert.

357 Or 539, 354 P3d 690.

Here, the trial court could have concluded that the diagnosis told the trier of fact something that it could not determine as well on its own.

Further, because the diagnosis was based in meaningful part on corroborative physical findings, the trial court could have concluded that the danger of unfair prejudice described in Southard—that the jury improperly would defer to the expert’s assessment of the complaining witness’s credibility—was correspondingly reduced. On balance, therefore, we conclude that the trial court did not abuse its discretion in determining that the probative value of [the] diagnosis of sexual abuse was not substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice.

357 Or 540, 354 P3d 691.

The court set out a three-part test to establish “sufficient physical evidence of abuse so that a medical diagnosis of sexual abuse ordinarily is admissible under OEC 403” if these factors are present:

(1) physical evidence meaningfully corroborates the alleged type of abuse;

(2) the expert significantly relies on that physical evidence in making the diagnosis of sexual abuse; and

(3) the causal relationship between the physical evidence and the diagnosis is sufficiently complex such that a lay trier of fact cannot assess the connection as well as an expert.

When those factors are present, the diagnosis tells the jury something that it could not determine as well on its own; thus, the probative value of the diagnosis ordinarily will not be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. In this case, because those factors were present, the trial court did not err in admitting the diagnosis.

357 Or 549.

Practice Tip

In challenging this type of evidence, defense counsel should look carefully at the proffered evidence to challenge the three factors:

1. What is “meaningful corroboration”?

2. What is “significant reliance” and is it independent of the complainant’s statements
or dependent on them?

3. Is there a “complex causal relationship” between the physical evidence and the diagnosis that is not simply a recitation of the complainant’s behavior or allegations?

H. United States v. Preston, 873 F3d 829, (2017)

The Ninth Circuit has ruled that testimony by a therapist concerning her treatment of an alleged sexual abuse victim was improper vouching during the following exchange:

“[I]s it your opinion that Tim Rosenberg was truthful to you about the sexual abuse?”

She replied, “[i]t is, yes.”

873 F3d at 837.

In addition, the Preston court noted that testimony of a treatment provider whom the court has not found to be an expert should be considered that of a lay witness. Thus, the admissibility of her statements should be decided on that basis, and the following exchange should have been stricken:

Q: “To the best of your knowledge, have you treated males for sexual abuse only to find the abuse to be false?” Bussart replied, “I have not experienced that even when I have done—treated men and women or girls or boys, yes, that the allegation has normally been true.”)

873 F3d at 838.

The district court also was held to have abused its discretion by allowing a juror question as follows:

“Based off of your sessions with Tim and his emotions, did his emotions demonstrate similarity to those of a person that had been a victim of sexual abuse?”

Bussart replied, “Yes. Very much so.”

873 F3d at 839.

V. Non-Scientific Evidence

Lay witnesses, frequently law enforcement officers, talk about credibility when questioning other witnesses. In determining whether testimony about these techniques constitutes impermissible vouching, courts have examined two criteria: first, is there a basis for the witness’s observation in specialized “training and experience,” and second, is the comment made in court or out of court.

If the witness is describing demeanor, behavior and statements based on “training and experience,” the courts generally have allowed the testimony. Likewise, if the comments are made out of court rather than in court and related to a specific witness’s testimony, the statements are often allowed.

A. Law Enforcement Vouching

State v. Leahy, 190 Or App 147, 78 P3d 132 (2003)

Reversible error in sodomy trial for state trooper specialized in child abuse investigation to testify, “I believe that it most definitely happened exactly the way the victim described it
to me.”

State v. Charboneau, 323 Or 38, 913 P2d 308 (1996)

Reversible error to allow a prosecutor to introduce portions of a plea agreement which included the state’s “beliefs” about the role of the witness, because they were analogous to a comment on the credibility of the witness.

State v. Viranond, 346 Or 451, 212 P 3d 1252 (2009)

Police officer in a home invasion robbery case could testify that two witnesses’ trial testimony was “consistent” with their prior statements to officers. Justice Gillette denied a claim that this was an improper comment on the witnesses’ credibility. He noted that the testimony was allowed as prior consistent statements of the witnesses after the defense cross-examination had challenged the statements. He declared that the officer was simply testifying that the witnesses told the same story, regardless of whether it was true or false, on the occasion of the interviews.

State v. Salas-Juarez, 264 Or App 57, 329 P3d 805 (2014)

Lead detective in a murder/attempted murder prosecution could testify that he did not find a witness evasive and that his out-of-court statement “appeared to be truthful to me.” The appellate court noted:

Young’s comment that he found Russell’s statement “truthful” and not “evasive” is a straightforward instance of vouching. That the comment related to his decision-making process for the investigation is of no consequence; investigators routinely make credibility determinations out of court, but that does not create an exception to the prohibition against relaying those credibility determinations in their in-court testimony.

329 P3d at 810.

The court held, however, that the objection had not been preserved at the trial level; in addition, the court found a strategic reason for trial counsel’s failure to object at the time of the testimony and affirmed the conviction.

State v. Marquez-Vela, 266 Or App 738, 338 P3d 813 (2014)

In a murder prosecution police detective Ober interviewed the defendant, claiming he was “nonchalant” during the interview and also testified as follows:

[Ober:] I think he was dishonest. I think he was untruthful. I think he was lying about the whole situation and he was selectively leaving out details that he thought was harmful to him.” (emphasis in original).

The state acknowledged that this testimony was inadmissible but argued that defense
counsel had “opened the door” to the remarks by cross-examining Ober about the defen-
dant being “nonchalant.” The Court of Appeals rejected this suggestion and reversed.

State v. Thomas, 270 Or App 195 (2015)

In DUII prosecution, Officer Douglas testified that he did not see a particular action attributed to the defendant, but he heard remarks about it from Officer Burke which informed his actions because, “I trust Officer Burke.” The court held this was not an improper comment on Burke’s credibility, but even if it was the error was harmless.

State v. Althof, 273 Or App 342, 359 P3d 399(2015)

Police officer’s testimony about delay in reporting child abuse was sufficient to qualify him as an expert under OEC 702, but his testimony was not scientific evidence:

A witness is not assumed to be disqualified merely because the person lacks a particular educational or professional degree. To be an expert, a person simply must have the “knowledge, skill, experience, training or education” to provide testimony “in the form of an opinion or otherwise” regarding the “particular topic” on which the person claims expertise. OEC 702; Rogers, 330 Or at 315. “ ‘The capacity [to testify] in every case is a relative one, i.e., relative to the topic about which the person is asked to make his statement.’ ” (Additional citations omitted.)

The court found that the investigating detective’s training and experience enabled him to testify as an expert about different reasons why victims of sexual abuse or domestic abuse might not report such abuse immediately. The court noted that his testimony was narrow: he did not describe the delay as if it were a scientific or psychological phenomenon, as in Marrington, supra. Instead, he simply gave concrete reasons why victims of physical or sexual abuse might not report abuse to authorities immediately.

Practice Tip

Counsel should look carefully at the “training and experience” offered to allow these officers to be “experts,” pointing out the requirements of basic and continuing training and officer experiences which are routine, not specific to the type of case involved. The substance of the testimony may also be subject to challenge as in Dunning, below.

State v. Dunning, 245 Or App 582, 263 P3d 372 (2011)

In a trial for attempted murder and assault, Officer Kozowski testified that a pivotal witness’s testimony had changed because the memories of trauma victims improve over time. The appellate court refused to accept him as an expert, pointing out,

His expertise derived from reading some material by one author and one institute and from familiarity with one or two public documents. That is not the stuff of expertise; if it were, any literate person with access to a library or an Internet connection could become an expert in anything over one long weekend. Our standards are higher. Allowing Kozowski to testify as an expert on the subject of memory after traumatic events was error.

The court noted that the pivotal witness’ account of events had changed from one which mentioned defendant only in passing to one which strongly implicated her. Because Kozowski’s testimony gave an “expert’s” imprimatur to the counterintuitive proposition that a person’s memory improves over time, the conviction was reversed. 245 Or App 592.

State v. Higgins, 258 Or App 117, 308 P3d 352 (2013)

Complainant’s mother testified at trial that she waited a period of time before asking her daughter to repeat her claims that the defendant raped her so that she “could be sure that [the complainant] wasn’t lying.” This was a direct comment that the girl was truthful and that mother believed the accusations. As her mother, she was aware of the girl’s behaviors, characteristics, and past experiences. “That fact significantly increases the risk that the jury’s credibility determination was affected by [the mother’s] comments.” The issue of force depended on the girl’s credibility, and the ends of justice required that the convictions be reversed.

State v. Wilson, 266 Or App 481, 337 P3d 990 (2014)

The court allowed testimony from an EMT worker that the complainant’s distressed demeanor when telling her father about her attack “wasn’t fake” and that “I did not think she was faking it.” Defense counsel did not object. The appellate court ruled the testimony not plain error. The evidence in question was not clearly vouching for the young woman’s credibility but was more like permissible testimony about her demeanor. In addition, the court found plausible reasons that the evidence could support the defense of the case, so the trial court did not plainly err in failing to strike the testimony sua sponte.

State v. Ferguson, 247 Or App 747, 271 P3d 150 (2012)

The prosecutor asked an alleged rape victim’s father about his call to police:

“So, if you…had thought she had just made a mistake, got drunk, but had sex with somebody knowingly, would you have let the police be called?

“A—oh, no, sir.” [emphasis in opinion].

In reversing the conviction, the Court of Appeals held that this testimony vouched for the woman’s credibility on the issues of whether sex occurred, whether she was drunk and whether it was a mistake. The suggestion to the jury that they should believe complainant because her father, with whom she was “very close,” believed her was improper vouching.

State v. Inman, 275 Or App 920, 366 P3d 721 (2015)

Police detective testified that he spoke with a complainant a week before trial to be sure she had not lied; this was not “plain error.” Without an objection from trial counsel, the “vouching” argument was not preserved.

Practice Tip – Object! Object!

Object to any testimony that hints at support for the credibility of another witness’s statements.

State v. Chandler, 360 Or 323, 380 P3d 982 (2016)

Officer Gates’ pretrial videotaped interview of the defendant was admitted at trial. In it, Gates expressed her opinion that the victims were truthful and the defendant was lying:

The girl had no history of…lying, making accusations…that have turned out to be lies….I can see you act a certain way when you say something that’s truthful and… a different way when you are saying something…I already know is a lie.

The court distinguished Charboneau, supra, from State v. Odoms, 313 Or App 76, which allowed an officer’s testimony recounting his statements while questioning the defendant and which indicated his belief that the victim was truthful. The court ruled that the distinction between in-court and out-of-court statements was not decisive. The court disavowed the reasoning in Odoms and expressly ruled that the bounds of the vouching rule are not defined by the setting in which the comments are uttered, but rather whether the statement is offered for the “truth of the credibility opinion” that it expresses.

The court carefully ruled that a vouching statement is “not categorically inadmissible” if it is offered for a relevant, non-opinion purpose; but the evidence must still be relevant under OEC 401, may not be unduly prejudicial under OEC 403, and in appropriate circumstances may be subject to a limiting instruction under OEC 105. 360 Or 335. On the record before it, the court noted that trial counsel had not objected to the “non-vouching” purpose [context] for which Gates’ statements were offered.

Justice Baldwin, however, noted that “We do not disagree that such statements are troubling. Indeed, Gates’s claim of expertise in determining truthfulness posed the risk…that jurors might place undue weight on the ‘aura of reliability’ created by such a claim.” But the proper procedure for challenging such statements was to raise an objection under OEC 403 which triggers the trial court’s duty to weigh the statements. Here, trial counsel not only developed no argument under OEC 403, but also failed to cite Southard, “the primary case on which his appellate argument relied.”

Since Chandler, several cases have relied on the portion of the opinion that allowed the statement into evidence by finding it was not vouching. See State v. Garcia-Rocio, 287 Or App 745, 749-750, 403 P3d 470 (2017); State v. Codon, 282 Or App 165, 386 P3d 45 (2016); State v. Davis, 291 Or App 146, ___ P3d ____ (April 2018). In both Codon and Garcia-Rocio, the Court of Appeals let stand a trial court ruling allowing the recording and interrogation of the defendant in its entirety where the interrogating detective constantly confronted the defendant with statements that the defendant was lying and the victim was telling the truth. The court’s reasoning set forth in Garcia-Rocio is that the detective’s statements made during the interview were not vouching because they were offered “to show [defendant’s] reaction to being confronted with a particular statement,” and the jury was instructed that the detective’s statements were “not to be considered for their truth” or as “comments upon the credibility of any person or witness.” Because the detective’s statements were not admitted for the truth of the credibility opinions they contained, they were not categorically inadmissible. State v. Garcia-Rocio, 287 Or App 745, 750, 403 P3d 470, 472 (2017), review denied, 362 Or 389, 411 P3d 385 (2018).

In Davis, the trial court allowed a text exchange between the alleged victim and the accused. The Davis court hammered home the new Chandler rule that the categorical prohibition only applies when the statements are offered “for the truth of the credibility opinion that it expresses” and went further to emphasize that statements by a witness (law enforcement, victim, etc.) regarding his opinion as to whether an accused is being truthful with them are admissible as nonhearsay context for defendant’s admissible statements. State v. Davis, 291 Or App 146, 159 (2018).

Unlike in Chandler, Condon and Garcia-Rocio, Davis preserved his 403 argument, but the Davis court summarily rejected the position that the alleged victim/witness pronouncements regarding the veracity of the accused, even in light of Mayfield, were more probative than prejudicial because of the importance of putting the accused’s statements “into context.”

United States v. Preston, 873 F3d 829 (2017)

The Court found the following exchange to be improper vouching:

Q: [...]Did you ask the defendant, Did you put your penis in [Rosenberg’s] mouth at your house?

A: Yes.

Q: And what was the defendant’s response to [the] question[ ]?

A: No.

Q: Did you indicate to the defendant that you did not believe him?

A. Yes.

Q: What was the defendant’s response when you told him you didn’t believe him?

A: Preston never denied receiving blow jobs from [Rosenberg] ... [he] stated that he
could not remember receiving blow jobs from [Rosenberg] because that is when he was a meth addict.”

United States v. Preston, supra.

B. Non-Law Enforcement Lay Opinion Vouching Prohibited

United States v. Preston

The Court held the following was impermissible vouching by father of alleged victim:

Q: “What was your response when you learned about the allegations of sexual abuse by the defendant?”

A: “[T]here wasn’t any questioning my brother about what he was saying. There was just understanding. I can understand. I can see. Things make sense to me.”

Id. at *6 (9th Cir Oct. 17, 2017).

VI. Indirect Vouching

Prosecutors often refer to the complaining witness, particularly a child, as a “victim.” This term is both a legal conclusion and a signal to the jury that the complainant has been wronged and that the defendant is the person responsible. Defense counsel should ask the court to prevent the prosecutor from using this term. Also see Chapter 12, “Demurrers and Motions” by Matthew McHenry.

ORS 131.007 defines a “victim” as follows:

As used in ORS 40.385, 135.230, 135.406, 135.970, 147.417, 147.419 and 147.421 and in ORS chapters 136, 137 and 144, except as otherwise specifically provided or unless the context requires otherwise, “victim” means the person or persons who have suffered financial, social, psychological or physical harm as a result of a crime and includes, in the case of a homicide or abuse of corpse in any degree, a member of the immediate family of the decedent and, in the case of a minor victim, the legal guardian of the minor. In no event shall the criminal defendant be considered a victim.
(Emphasis added).

In a sex case, the issue will be whether a crime or crimes occurred at all. Until that decision is made, there is no objective basis to accord the complainant the label “victim” in front of the jury.

The statutes providing complaining witnesses the status of “victim” and providing “victim’s rights” have limited, specific attributes. Those individuals designated as “victims” under the statutes are given that designation so that they may exercise certain statutory rights: (1) consultation with the district attorney about plea negotiations, ORS 135.406, (2) the right not to have his or her name and address provided to the accused, ORS 135.970, (3) the right to be notified of his or her constitutional rights, ORS 147.417, (4) the right to obtain a transcript or tape of a criminal proceeding, ORS 147.419, and (5) the right to information about an accused that a public body is required to provide, ORS 147.421.

The foregoing rules do not, and cannot, diminish an accused’s constitutional rights. ORS 147.540(2)(c). The statutes do not change the evidentiary rules concerning procedures for a trial or shift the burden of proof to a defendant to disprove his guilt. Those rights bestowed upon designated “victims” do not give the complainant the privilege of being labeled a “victim” in front of the jury. The definition of “victim” in ORS 131.007 makes use of that label inappropriate until after a verdict or finding of guilt (i.e., determination of injury “as the result of a crime”). To determine the verdict is precisely the reason for a jury trial.

The constitutional provisions concerning designated victims are similar.

Article 1, sections 42 and 43, set out “the rights of victims in criminal prosecutions and juvenile court delinquency proceedings.” The specific rights enumerated include: (1) the right to be present at any critical stage, Article 1, section 42 (1) (a); (2) the right to obtain information about conviction, etc., Article 1, section 42 (1) (b), (3) the right to refuse an interview to the defense, Article 1, section 42 (1) (c), (4) the right to prompt restitution, Article 1, section 42 (1) (d), (5) the right to a copy of the transcript, Article 1, section 42 (1) (e), (6) the right to be consulted regarding plea negotiations involving any violent felony, Article 1, section 42 (1) (f), and (7) the right to be informed of the rights as soon as practicable, Article 1, section 42 (1) (g). There is no right to be called “the victim” in front of the jury.

The Oregon Constitution does leave the designation of who is a “victim” in certain contexts to the discretion of the district attorney. See, e.g., Article 1, sections 42 and 43 (3) (c); see also ORS 147.500 (statute leaves designation of victim to discretion of district attorney or court).

Even though the prosecution has discretion to determine the complainant’s status as a “victim,” that status is still limited by the requirement that the person has suffered “harm as a result of a crime.” The limitation is the same as in the statute. There must be a final determination that a “crime” occurred, and this determination must be made by an objective source (e.g., not the complainant, not the prosecuting attorney) before the label can be used without limitation. The Oregon constitutional sections are consistent with the foregoing analysis because those sections recognize that a criminal defendant’s rights are unchanged. Article 1, sections 42 and 43 (2) specify, “Nothing in this section reduces a criminal defendant’s rights under the Constitution of the United States.”

Under both state and federal law, a defendant is entitled to the presumption of innocence and has no burden of proof. He is also entitled to require the state to prove each and every element of each charge beyond a reasonable doubt. Use of the term “victim” for the complainant before the jury would unfairly and improperly dilute the state’s burden of proof. It would say to the jury that the prosecution has already shown—by filing an indictment—that a crime has been committed and this defendant committed it. Clearly that is not the law.

By referring to a complainant as a “victim” before that fact has been determined by a jury, the state and its other witnesses would be, in effect, personally vouching for the credibility of the complainant and the justness of the state’s case in front of the jury. A prosecutor may not offer his or her personal opinions on an issue as a factor for the jury to consider in its deliberations without violating federal due process rights. Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution; Article 1, sections 11 and 12, of the Oregon Constitution; Brady v. Maryland, 373 US 83, 83 S Ct 1194, 110 L Ed2d 215 (1963).

The Oregon Supreme Court has held that a trial lawyer may not assert the lawyer’s personal opinion about an issue at trial. State v. Charboneau, 323 Or 38, 47-48, 913 P2d 308 (1996). See also DR 7-106(C)(4), which prohibits a lawyer from asserting “the lawyer’s personal opinion as to the justness of a cause.” While the trial court has broad discretion to control argument, it cannot permit argument that is unfair to a defendant. State v. Bolt, 108 Or 746, 817 P2d 1322 (1991); State v. Blodgett, 50 Or 329, 343, 92 P 820 (1907).

Courts have been inconsistent in their responses to a motion in limine seeking to preclude the use of the label “victim” at trial. One judge in Benton County ruled that he would preclude “excessive” use of the term. A judge in Umatilla County ruled that, like the label “defendant,” the term simply designated a participant in the trial. But presiding Judge Thomas Branford in Lincoln County has held for more than 30 years that use of the term “victim” as a label for the complainant is fundamentally unfair to the accused in a criminal trial.

VII. Prosecutors

A. United States v. Garcia, 522 F3d 597 (5th Cir 2008)

In United States v. Garcia, 522 F3d 597 (5th Cir 2008), the government concedes error, personal assurances are improper.

In Garcia, the Court first quoted the ABA standard:

The prosecutor should not express his or her personal belief or opinion as to the truth or falsity of any testimony or evidence or the guilt of the defendant or “cloak a witness in its protective mantle.”

The Court then set out the rule as follows:

As the government concedes, it is impermissible per se for a prosecutor to offer personal assurances to the jury that government witnesses are telling the truth, or to tell the jury that law enforcement witnesses should be believed simply because they were doing their job.

522 F3d at 602.

The court also found it improper and impermissible for a prosecutor to ask a rhetorical question about whether federal agents would risk their careers to commit perjury and whether they had gotten up that morning determined to engage in a conspiracy wrongfully to convict Mr. Garcia. 522 F3d 600.

See also United States v. McCann, 613 F3d 486, 495 (5th Cir. 2010) (“The test for improper vouching for the credibility of a witness is whether the prosecutor’s expression might reasonably lead the jury to believe that there is other evidence, unknown or unavailable to the jury, on which the prosecutor was convinced of the accused’s guilt”), other citations omitted.

B. United States v. Preston

The Court held the following comments by the prosecutor were all improper vouching for the witness:

1. In opening, the government stated, “The truth always comes out no matter how many years have passed.... And the truth in this case is that the defendant, Christopher Preston, put his penis in ten-year-old Timothy Rosenberg’s mouth[.]”

2. In summation, the government reiterated, “The truth will always come out.... And in this case the truth has come out.” The government further noted, “In the end...ladies and gentlemen, the truth came out of the mouth of Tim Rosenberg.

3. In rebuttal, referring to Rosenberg’s testimony, the government stated, “And that is the truth in this case. That is the evidence in this case.” Later in rebuttal the government added, “[Rosenberg] has come here to court...to testify to you as to the truth of what happened...the uncontroverted truth as to what happened.” The government also stated, “The crux of this case, the core of the truth of what Timothy Rosenberg testified to in detail on the stand under oath is that the defendant sexually molested him as a child....” Finally, the government declared, “[Rosenberg] probably wishes that the drinking and the drugs...could erase this memory of what happened. But it can’t. And it didn’t. Because it is the truth of what happened.”

873 F3d at 844.

VIII. Post-Conviction Relief

Trial counsel’s failure to object to vouching testimony may be constitutionally ineffective assistance, which justifies post-conviction relief.

A. Simpson v. Coursey, 224 Or App 145, 197 P3d 68 (2008)

Trial counsel was constitutionally defective for failing to move for a mistrial, to strike, or for a curative instruction after a uniformed police officer testified that he found the complainant “very honest, very straightforward.” Although counsel had objected to the testimony and the objection was sustained, this was insufficient without asking for additional remedies from the court.

In granting post conviction relief, the appellate court observed that the possible effect of this vouching on the verdict was magnified because the trial seemed extremely close for the jury. It returned a verdict of not guilty on three of the four charged counts, and the single guilty verdict rested on a vote of 10-2. The charges involved just one victim and depended on her credibility. “Thus, the possibility that [the] testimony vouching for the credibility of the victim affected the verdict is very real.” 224 Or App 155.

B. Berg v. Nooth, 258 Or App 286, 309 P3d 164 (2013)

A DHS caseworker testified on cross-examination, “I did believe the children in this case.” The prosecutor objected, and the court asked defense counsel if he wanted a curative instruction. The attorney did not answer. In addition, the prosecutor in his closing arguments referred to the expertise of the state’s witnesses, claiming that they would not have been fooled if the children were lying, that the girls had disincentives to lie, and that the defendant should “not be allowed to get away” with the crimes.

The Court of Appeals had “little difficulty concluding that trial counsel failed to exercise reasonable professional skill and judgment” in the case in light of the Oregon Supreme Court’s “emphatic condemnation” of vouching testimony. The court also reaffirmed the court’s instruction that if counsel tried to elicit such testimony, “the trial judge, sua sponte, should summarily cut off the inquiry before a jury is contaminated by it.” 258 Or App 298.

C. Alne v. Nooth, 288 Or App 307, 309, 406 P3d 109, 112 (2017)

As in the Berg case, supra, the Alne court found deficient the failure of trial counsel to object to the following testimony:

[STATE]: In your training and experience in dealing with children, is that something that a child who has not been abused would ordinarily come up with or burst out with?

[FINDLAY]: Not generally. I think, again, I look at the statements that she made about that and the way in which she described it. We do try to discern to the best of our ability is a child describing something that was an experience, or by the way that they’re describing the event, does it appear as if that’s an experienced event, or is that something that they could have seen or something like that.

“From my training and experience, I thought again that she provided information that was from a child’s point of view, and it just seemed like a genuine statement.

The court affirmed post-conviction relief, emphasizing that no reasonable attorney would have failed to object to such a statement and, where there is no physical evidence, the failure of the trial counsel to object is ineffective assistance of counsel.

D. Heroff v. Coursey, 280 Or App 177, 380 P3d 1032 (2016)

In Heroff v. Coursey, the prosecutor made the following arguments:

  • “Well, to a young, undeveloped child, apparently, it really didn’t feel like anything. Was that an accurate description? Probably. I think so.”

  • “Your first experience as a little child being sodomized by an adult, your body is going to be kind of shaky, and that’s what she described, and that’s how it was, and that’s credible, and that’s honest, and that’s the truth.”

  • “She came forward, and she told the truth. She told the truth here this week. She was nervous. She didn’t want to be up there, but she came up here. She told the truth, and she got it out, and she told her story.”

380 P3d at 1042.

The court held that it is permissible for a prosecutor to argue that the jury should infer a witness is credible based on the evidence in the record so long as the prosecutor does not vouch for the witness by interjecting his or her personal opinion of the witness’s credibility.

IX. Conclusion

Defense counsel should challenge testimony by any witness—whether doctor, police office or ordinary citizen—which addresses statements made by another witness—whether in court or out of court—on several grounds as Justice Baldwin clearly set out in Chandler.

• First, make a vouching objection. The prosecutor should demonstrate specifically and in detail, on the record, any “non-vouching” purpose for which she is offering the statement.

• Second, object that the statement is not relevant under OEC 401.

• Finally, ask the trial court to engage in the balancing test under OEC 403, weighing whether the probative value of the evidence is outweighed by its prejudice.

Ultimately, if the court allows the statement, trial counsel should insist that the court provide a limiting instruction, specifically telling the jury that the statement is not to be considered for its truth and the jury alone determines the credibility of all the witnesses. Without such a record, any supportive reviewing court has no legal basis for offering relief to a client who is defeated in the trial arena.
