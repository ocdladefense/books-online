Chapter 11. Other Evidence Matters

Brian Schmonsees1

I. Impeachment for Bias

A. “Bias Is Never Collateral”

In Oregon’s seminal case, the Oregon Supreme Court quoted the United States Supreme Court on the admissibility of bias evidence:

It is the essence of a fair trial that reasonable latitude be given the cross-examiner, even though he is unable to state to the court what facts a reasonable cross-examination might develop. Prejudice ensues from a denial of the opportunity to place the witness in his proper setting and put the weight of his testimony and his credibility to a test, without which the jury can’t fairly appraise them. Alford v. United States, 282 U.S. 687, 692, 51 S.Ct. 218, 219, 75 L.Ed. 624, 628 (1931).

See State v. Hubbard, 297 Or 789, 800, 688 P2d 1311 (1984).

The Hubbard court then held that:

a decision to exclude evidence relevant to bias or interest which is error, is reversible if it denies the jury an adequate opportunity to assess the credibility of a witness whose credibility is important to the outcome of the trial…where the impeached witness is the sole witness on a given issue and there is no corroborating evidence, the interests of a fair trial require that the adverse party be given ample opportunity to establish the witness’s bias or interest. To fail to allow cross-examination sufficient to establish facts from which bias or interest may be inferred in such a situation is reversible error.

Id at 800-801.

Thus, impeachment for bias is one of the most powerful cross-examination tools a practitioner can utilize with minimal judicial interference or objections from opposing counsel. This chapter explores the constitutional and statutory provisions relating to impeachment for bias. This chapter also includes a discussion of federal and state case law on impeachment for bias in sex cases.

B. Defining Bias and Interest

Bias or interest seems to be a little bit of “I know it when I see it,” but the Oregon courts have defined bias as friendly or hostile feelings a witness holds towards a party and “interest” meaning to have a stake in the outcome of litigation. See State v. Neal, 143 Or App 188 (1996).

C. The Rule: OEC 609-1

One of the starting points for using bias to impeach a witness is Oregon Evidence Code provision 609-1 (codified as ORS 40.36) which provides:

(1) The credibility of a witness may be attacked by evidence that the witness engaged in conduct or made statements showing bias or interest. In examining a witness concerning a prior statement made by the witness, whether written or not, the statement need not be shown nor its contents disclosed to the witness at that time, but on request the statement shall be shown or disclosed to the opposing party.

(2) If a witness fully admits the facts claimed to show the bias or interest of the witness, additional evidence of that bias or interest shall not be admitted. If the witness denies or does not fully admit the facts claimed to show bias or interest, the party attacking the credibility of the witness may then offer evidence to prove those facts.

(3) Evidence to support or rehabilitate a witness whose credibility has been attacked by evidence of bias or interest shall be limited to evidence showing a lack of bias or interest.

The Commentary to OEC 609-1 explains that “bias” takes the form of friendly feeling or hostility toward a party, while “interest” commonly takes the form of having a stake in the outcome of the litigation. Professor McCormick writes that “[p]artiality, or any acts, relationships or motives reasonably likely to produce it, may be proved to impeach credibility. While the Federal Rules of Evidence does not in terms refer to attacking the witness by showing bias, interest, corruption, or coercion, it clearly contemplates the use of [those] methods of impeachment.” McCormick, Evidence, 130-131 (4th ed 1992) (footnotes omitted). See also Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence, 377 (2d ed 1989) (bias may be shown by family relationship or by a showing of a motive to curry favor with a party).

State v. Phillips, 314 Or 460, 840 P2d 666 (1992).

D. Constitutional Right to Explore a Witness’s Credibility

The United State Supreme Court has emphasized that impeachment for the purposes of attacking a witness’s credibility is a constitutional right. Davis v. Alaska, 415 US 308 (1974). In Davis, the Court held that effective cross-examination requires more than just “gazing at the witness” and that the Confrontation Clause requires that the defendant be allowed to cross-examine the government witness by directly and personally questioning the witness and obtaining immediate answers. The partiality of a witness (bias, prejudice, ulterior motives) at trial is always relevant, or, put another way, bias is never collateral. Id. at 316.

There are different ways to challenge credibility than due to bias. For example, a challenge to the credibility of a child witness who has previously made allegations and then recanted them, is not necessarily relevant to show bias, although it may still be helpful to challenge the child witness’s credibility. See State v. LeClair, 83 Or App 121 (1986) (OEC 412 evidence sought to be introduced regarding two separate incidents where the victim had made sexual abuse allegations and then recanted them were asserted to challenge the child witness’s credibility). It is important to note that there was no claim re: bias in LeClair. Thus, once the evidence was analyzed and found to be inadmissible under other evidence code provisions, there was no analysis as to bias as there was no claim that the evidence could also be admitted to show bias or interest.

1. The Nacoste Rule

There is a limited right for the trial court to engage in balancing under 403 for evidence that challenges the credibility of the witness. In State v. Nacoste, 272 Or 460 (2015), the court reversed a trial court’s exclusion of evidence and limitation on the cross-examination of the witness. The defendant’s cross-examination would have established the victim had a warrant for her arrest due to an allegation that she had violated her juvenile probation at the time she accused defendant of assault and sexual abuse. Id. at 464-465. The court held that the defendant had the right to cross-examine the alleged victim in order to show she was fabricating the allegations and that the balancing test the trial court imposed was inappropriate as OEC 403 allows the court to prevent cumulative evidence, not the initial showing of bias. See State v. Calderon, 237 Or App at 615. Id. at 468. The court affirmed that one well-recognized area of bias or interest evidence is when a witness has a “reason to curry favor with the prosecution, or is under the influence of the prosecution, because of the witness’ss own criminal conduct or custody status.” A defendant is able to explore these biases even when such evidence dips into confidential or otherwise inadmissible evidence. Id. at 467-468.

2. Limitations

The right to cross-examine does not extend to extrinsic evidence. See State v. Driver, 192 Or App 395 (2004), citing the rule from State v. LeClair. Driver limited LeClair’s extension by holding a defendant could not use extrinsic evidence to contradict the testimony of the victim. Thus, there is a question as to whether one could use extrinsic evidence to show bias.

It can be difficult to allege bias when one really wants to offer 412 evidence. In State v. Bender, 91 Or App 420 (1988), the state appealed a pretrial order allowing testimony regarding other sexual conduct to be presented under Oregon’s Rape Shield law, OEC 412. The Court of Appeals reversed the trial court, noting that 412 generally bars this evidence unless it rebuts scientific or medical evidence presented or relates to the victim’s motive or bias, or is constitutionally required. The defendant maintained on appeal the evidence related to the victim’s bias to accuse him falsely, but the court found the defendant failed to draw any rational connection between the ex-wife’s possible affair and her motive to falsely accuse him of rape. Id. at 423-424. Thus, although the evidence arguably shows a bias by the victim, the Court of Appeals held “there was other
evidence to show that bias.” Id.

Defendant argued that OEC 412, Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution and the Sixth Amendment and Confrontation Clause required defendant be allowed to cross-examine his ex-wife. The court noted that the right to cross-examine is not absolute and the evidence must be relevant and have some “probative value in establishing or rebutting a material issue.” Id.

As with LeClair, supra, some 412 evidence may not be admissible to show prior sexual conduct. However, if the otherwise inadmissible evidence can be sufficiently connected to bias or interest, it will be admissible under OEC 609-1, even as to a hearsay declarant.

Practice Tip

Hubbard, Driver, Bender and Nacoste teach us that in to impeach a witness based upon the witness’s bias or interest, the evidence need only have a “mere tendency” to show bias or interest of the witness and it need not be relevant to another matter of consequence to determination of the case. In fact, even if inadmissible for another purpose (such as under OEC 412), evidence can still be admitted for bias if the lawyer can tie it directly to the bias or interest. Therefore, where the evidence as in LeClair may not be admissible under OEC 412, it could still have a “mere tendency” to show bias or interest and so the defense lawyer should try and frame the evidence to show bias or interest under Hubbard.

Practice Tip

Make an offer of proof if a court restricts you from offering impeachment evidence for bias or interest. If the impeachment evidence consists of another person’s statements, call that person outside the presence of the jury and conduct the questioning that you would have conducted had the court found the information to be admissible. If the impeachment evidence consists of extrinsic evidence in the form of documents, label and submit them for the appellate file with an explanation as to how they show the bias and/or interest of a witness.

E. Relevancy

Bias is always relevant, unlike other forms of impeachment. Other forms of impeachment may be held either collateral or too far removed from the witnesses’ interests in testifying to be relevant. State v. Guerrero, 11 Or App 284 (1972); State v. Hernandez, 269 Or App 327 (2015). In Hernandez, the defendant sought to impeach the victim in a sex abuse prosecution by cross-examining her on her intentions to obtain a U-Visa, which, as a crime victim, would allow her to remain in the United States even though she was an illegal alien. The trial court sustained the state’s objections to this line of questioning and the defendant was convicted. The Court of Appeals, citing Hubbard, reversed, holding that it is “always permissible” to show bias or interest when that goes to show a witness’s credibility. “Wide latitude must be given to the cross-examiner to ask for and receive answers to questions sufficient to demonstrate to the jury the nature of the bias or interest of the witness.” Hubbard at 798.

F. Impeaching the Hearsay Declarant

In State v. Phillips, 314 Or 460 (1992), the alleged victim was unavailable but her statements were offered by the state. The defense sought to introduce child’s statement to her mother that “her father wanted her to say bad things about the defendant.” The defense cited rule 806, which states that when a hearsay statement has been introduced, the credibility of the declarant may be attacked as if the witness testified at trial. The court held that 806 applied and then admitted the prior statement of the child under OEC 609-1 because the evidence was “a recognized type of impeachment evidence (bias or interest) under OEC 609-1(1) and it was offered by defendant for the sole purpose of casting doubt on the credibility of the hearsay declarant—the unavailable child witness.

Practice Tip

There are two steps to getting bias evidence admitted against a hearsay declarant. Step one is to get the court to agree OEC 806 applies. Step two: cite to the rules and cases allowing the court to admit evidence of bias under 609-1. Remember, you also do not have to meet the normal foundational requirements under 609-1.

G. Finding the Bias—U Visa Trilogy

These recent sex abuse cases all discuss the framework for admitting evidence for bias, and the low bar for relevancy, that Due Process and the Confrontation Clause demand. State v. Valle, 255 Or App 805 (2013), State v. Del Real-Galvez, 270 Or App 224 (2014), and State v. Hernandez, 269 Or App 327, (2015).

In Valle, the defendant alleged the victim had a motive to fabricate her allegations against him because she could obtain a U visa and, ultimately, citizenship. The Court of Appeals reversed the trial court and held that the defendant had shown a reasonable inference of bias or interest regarding the motive to fabricate and allowed for the victim to be impeached with evidence of the U visa and potential for citizenship. Id. at 8102. The court held that:

party lays a sufficient foundation for the admission of evidence to impeach a witness if the party shows that the evidence could support a reasonable inference that the witness is biased or self-interested. Evidence from which a reasonable jury could infer that a witness has a motive to testify in a certain manner is relevant impeachment evidence. The inference need not be the only one that could be drawn, or even the most probable. At the admissibility stage, the only question is whether a jury could find that the witness has a motive to testify in a certain manner. Whether the witness actually has a motive and, if so, whether the motive has influenced the witness’s testimony, are separate and subsequent questions for the jury.

Id. at 814.

In Del Real-Galvez, the mother of a sex abuse complainant applied for a U visa based on the minor’s report of abuse. The mother also assisted the state with their investigation by bringing the victim to meet with law enforcement at various points. Id. at 226. The Court of Appeals reversed that trial court’s erroneous ruling that evidence of the mother’s immigration status was irrelevant. The court also found error for the trial court to exclude the testimony of a witness who heard mother and minor conspiring to accuse another person of abuse in order to be eligible to obtain citizenship. Id. at 228. The court held that the evidence was admissible because it inferred that the complainant had a motive to testify falsely. Id. at 230.

In Hernandez, the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court, holding it had erred by barring the defense from inquiring about whether the victim sought a U visa in a sexual abuse/harassment prosecution. The court held that evidence the victim obtained a U visa was relevant because it impeached the victim and that the evidence gave rise to an inference that a witness had a motive to testify in a certain manner. Id. at 332.

H. Persuasive Authority: How Other Jurisdictions Allow Questions for Bias

State v. Guenther, 181 NJ 129 (2004): Similar to Oregon’s LeClair case, supra, this case involved a prior false accusation of sexual abuse against another person by the alleged victim. The New Jersey Supreme Court reversed the trial court and held: a prior false accusation is admissible, despite evidentiary restrictions on admissions of prior specific acts, when it goes to credibility, and credibility is a central issue in the case.

In State v. Ross, 249 NJ Super 246 (App Div. 1991), the New Jersey Court of Appeals held that prior false accusations would be admissible to impeach a victim’s credibility where there is a judicial determination that the accusations were likely false.

I. Examples of Bias

It is error for a trial court to exclude evidence from which a jury could reasonably infer that the witness has a motive to testify in a certain manner. For example, it is error for a trial court to exclude evidence from which a jury could reasonably infer that a prosecution witness has a motive to curry favor with the state. Such evidence includes evidence  the witness is on probation, has pending charges, or is the subject of a criminal investigation. See, e.g., Davis, 415 U.S. at 318, 94 S.Ct. 1105 (trial court erred by refusing to allow the youth to impeach a prosecution witness with evidence that the witness was on probation); State v. Shelly, 212 Or App 65, 68, 157 P3d 234 (2007) (trial court erred by refusing to allow the defendant to impeach the victim with evidence the victim was on probation and had been the subject of a recent criminal investigation); State v. Weinstein, 108 Or App 486, 487, 814 P2d 565 (1991) (trial court erred by refusing to allow the defendant to impeach an assault victim by showing that the victim was on probation and therefore had a motive to testify about the fight in a manner that would protect against the risk his probation would be revoked); State v. Presley, 84 Or App 1, 5, 733 P2d 452 (1987) (trial court erred by prohibiting the defendant from cross-examining the state’s informant about his theft charge).

Several other cases provide additional, useful illustrations of the rule that a party is entitled to present evidence from which a jury can draw inferences relating to a witness’s potential bias or self-interest; these rely, in part, on Hubbard, supra: State v. Crum, 287 Or App 541, 403 P3d 405 (2017); State v. Muldrew, 229 Or App 219, 210 P3d 936 (2009), and State v. Knobel, 97 Or App 559, 777 P2d 985 (1989), rev. den., 309 Or 522, 789 P2d 1387 (1990).

In Crum, an en banc Court of Appeals reversed a conviction for menacing which held that evidence supporting an inference that a witness has a motive to make certain statements is relevant to show the witness’s bias or self-interest. The Crum court specifically found that law enforcement’s use of force policy should have been admitted to show whether officers had a personal interest in providing a particular version of events such that their version would be in line with the policy. The court also held that the exclusion was not harmless. See also State v. Titus, 328 Or 475, 482, 982 P2d 1133 (1999) (error in excluding bias evidence was not harmless where the excluded evidence was qualitatively different from other bias evidence that had been admitted, and thus the jury did not have an adequate opportunity to assess the witness’s credibility).

In Muldrew, the defendant sought to impeach the officer who had arrested him by showing that the officer had a personal interest in ensuring the defendant’s conviction because a conviction could help protect the officer from civil liability for injuries that the defendant had sustained during his arrest. Specifically, the defendant sought to impeach the officer by presenting evidence that (1) the officer had been trained that “the typical situation in which police find themselves civilly liable [for injuries to arrestees is] when there is a use of force, and no conviction for Resist[ing] Arrest,” 229 Or App at 224, 210 P3d 936 (second brackets in original), and (2) the officer’s report included the statement, “I recommend that the DA’s office charge [the defendant] with Resist[ing] Arrest, based on the injuries he received,” Id. at 222–23, 210 P3d 936 (second brackets in original).

The trial court excluded the evidence, and the defendant was convicted. He appealed, and the Court of Appeals reversed, concluding that the trial court erred in excluding evidence of the officer’s recommendation that the district attorney charge the defendant with resisting arrest, which the court described as “the critical piece of evidence necessary to create an issue about [the officer’s] credibility.” Id. at 229, 210 P3d 936. The court further concluded the error required reversal because it deprived the jury of information it needed to properly evaluate the officer’s credibility. Id. at 230, 210 P3d 936.

In Knobel, the defendant sought to impeach a witness with evidence that the witness’s employer was hostile toward the defendant. The defendant was a newspaper reporter and the witness was employed by the sheriff. The defendant wanted to impeach the witness with evidence the witness knew that the sheriff—who had been the subject of negative news stories written by the defendant—did not like the defendant and thought it was a good idea to press charges against him. The trial court prohibited the questioning and the court, again relying on Hubbard, reversed and held, “In view of [the witness’s] employee-employer relationship with [the sheriff], the evidence that [the] defendant sought to elicit was relevant” and “required reversal because it “den[ied] the jury an adequate opportunity to assess [the witness’s] credibility.” Id. at 567, 777 P2d 985.

II. Character Evidence: Putting Defendant’s Character for Sexual Propriety at Issue

A. OEC 404(2)

OEC 404(2) provides in pertinent part:

Evidence of a person’s character is not admissible for the purpose of proving that the person acted in conformity therewith on a particular occasion, except:

(a) Evidence of a pertinent trait of character offered by an accused, or by the prosecution to rebut the same;

(b) Evidence of a pertinent trait of character of the victim of the crime offered by an accused, or by the prosecution to rebut the same or evidence of a character trait of peacefulness of the victim offered by the prosecution to rebut evidence that the victim was the first aggressor;

(c) Evidence of the character of a witness, as provided in ORS 40.345 (Rule 607, Who may impeach) to 40.355 (Rule 609, Impeachment by evidence of conviction of crime); or

(d) Evidence of the character of a party for violent behavior offered in a civil assault and battery case when self-defense is pleaded and there is evidence to support such defense.

Thus the defendant may put on evidence of his or her character for good sexual propriety or nondeviant sexual interests so long as it complies with OEC 405 restrictions on the methods of proving character. State v. Enakiev, 175 Or App 589 (2001), reversed a trial court’s ruling not to allow defendant to put on character for sexual propriety.

Note that by putting on evidence of character, the defendant “opens the door” to the state rebutting the character evidence. In State v. Guritz, 134 Or App 262 (1995), defendant’s testimony that he was a good father who wouldn’t have sodomized his daughter opened the door to evidence the defendant purchased marijuana while taking his daughter to the park.
Id. at 266.

Character evidence must be in the form of opinion or reputation evidence. Prior specific acts of conduct to prove character are expressly forbidden by OEC 405. This includes, as in State v. Reeder, 137 Or App 421, 423-425 (1995), the absence of prior sexual abuse complaints.

Practice Tip

The Oregon Supreme Court’s recent ruling in State v. Williams, 357 Or 1 (2015), allows the state to present other acts evidence of a defendant’s “sexual purpose.” This evidence comes close to character evidence and counsel should object to its admission under the 6th amendment Due Process Clause as well as request a limiting instruction. [Note: Also see Chapter 9, “They Want to Introduce What?: Other Act Evidence,” by David McDonald.]

B. Expert Witnesses

1. OEC 701 and 702

OEC 701, opinion testimony by lay witnesses, provides:

If the witness is not testifying as an expert, testimony of the witness in the form of opinions or inferences is limited to those opinions or inferences which are:

(1) Rationally based on the perception of the witness; and

(2) Helpful to a clear understanding of testimony of the witness or the determination of a fact in issue.

OEC 702, testimony by experts, provides:

If scientific, technical or other specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue, a witness qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training or education may testify thereto in the form of an opinion or otherwise.

2. Vouching

The Southard/Lupoli line of cases bar the state from introducing evidence that essentially vouches for the credibility of another witness. However, a body of cases still exists that allows state witnesses, specifically detectives, to testify to their training and experience regarding behavior by a defendant or victim. These cases find that because officers testify from a concrete base of experience and training, and their expertise isn’t couched as a psychological or scientific theory, the evidence is admissible to help rebut part of a defendant’s case.

In State v. Clemens, 208 Or App 632, 145 P3d 294 (2006), the Court of Appeals affirmed a trial court’s admission of a police officer’s opinion, that the victim’s statement was generally consistent with the nonchronological reporting patterns of child abuse victims. The appellate court agreed the statement admitted by the trial court was not scientific evidence but rather lay opinion based on the witness’s personal experience.

The appellate court expressed concern that the police witness’s generalization (about nonchronological reporting patterns of child abuse victims) did not respond to any question or issue before the court at the time he testified and also recognized that the disputed statement may have served to bolster the victim’s credibility.

But the Oregon Court of Appeals contrasted the facts in Clemens with “child abuse syndrome” evidence cases (e.g., State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621, 631, 756 P2d 620 (1988)), and found this case distinguishable. The court analyzed whether the disputed testimony constituted scientific evidence, i.e., “whether the expert’s assertions possess significantly increased potential to influence the trier of fact as scientific assertions,” State v. Marrington, 335 Or 555, 562, 73 P3d 911 (2003). The appellate court found the police officer’s testimony was based on personal experience and did not involve “the vocabulary of scientific research.” Id. On that basis the court found the disputed statement did not have an “increased potential” to influence the trier of fact as a scientific assertion.

Appellate courts classify evidence from experts in sex cases as either corroboration or impermissible vouching. Corroboration, if relevant, is generally admissible per Clemens. Impermissible vouching, as seen in Lupoli and Milbradt, is inadmissible. Thus, in State v. Althof, the Court of Appeals rejected the defendant’s contention that the lead detective had no basis under OEC 702 to testify about why victims of child abuse often delay their report. The Court of appeals found the officer had ample experience with child abuse investigations and had received numerous trainings specific to child sexual abuse investigations. 273 Or App 342, 345 (2015).

[Note: Look to Chapter 10, “ ‘Vouching’: Comments on Witness Credibility,” by Susan Reese for an expanded discussion.]

3. Grooming Evidence

State v. Swinney, 269 Or App 548 (2015), allows for grooming evidence from an expert witness. In Swinney, the state introduced testimony by a therapist characterizing defendant’s attempts to gain the victim’s trust as grooming. The Court of Appeals held such evidence does not require a foundation, and a properly trained therapist can testify to grooming behaviors.

However, State v. McCarthy, 251 Or App 231 (2012), reversed a case allowing grooming evidence since that evidence crossed into vouching. The state presented evidence regarding “delayed child sex abuse syndrome” to explain why a victim would delay reporting abuse. The Court of Appeals held that such evidence is vouching. For a further discussion on vouching, see Southard, supra.

4. Scientific Evidence in Sex Cases

Whether offered by the state or the defense, scientific evidence admissibility is subject to the requirements of Brown/O’Key to be admissible. Research regarding the treatment of sex offenders has led to the development of forensic tools that parties try to introduce. Some of the evidence would be admissible under Brown/O’Key, while some of the other methods are likely inadmissible.

The state was able to introduce a sexual consent capacity test through a psychologist in State v. Reed, 268 Or App 734 (2015). The defendant was accused of impregnating his incapacitated daughter, who suffered from serious developmental disabilities. The state’s psychologist testified that the victim was administered the Sexual Consent and Education Assessment test which showed she was unable to consent to sexual activity. The defense moved to strike the test from evidence under the Brown/O’Key test. The Court of Appeals examined the seven factors for admissibility:

1. The general acceptance in the field.

2. The expert’s qualifications and stature.

3. The use of the technique.

4. The operational standards controlling the technique and the potential rate of error.

5. The existence of specialized literature on the technique.

6. Novelty.

7. The extent the technique relies upon subjective interpretation.

The Court of Appeals found the test did pass the O’Key analysis and was admissible.

Practice Tip

It is an open question in Oregon if similar forensic testing is admissible under O’Key. For example, a defendant should attempt to rebut Williams “sexual purpose” evidence with an ABEL assessment, Static-99 or other assessment tool used in psychosexual evaluations to demonstrate that the defendant does not have a sexual interest in children.

5. Impeachment of State Expert Witnesses

OEC 706 (ORS 40.430) allows for impeachment of an expert witness by learned treatise. Child forensic interviewers and the medical doctor conducting the child’s physical examination are experts in their field. Successful cross-examination hinges on impeaching experts who fail to follow their own materials.

OEC 706 states:

Upon cross-examination, an expert witness may be questioned concerning statements contained in a published treatise, periodical or pamphlet on a subject of history, medicine or other science or art if the treatise, periodical or pamphlet is established as a reliable authority. A treatise, periodical or pamphlet may be established as a reliable authority by the testimony or admission of the witness, by other expert testimony or by judicial notice. Statements contained in a treatise, periodical or pamphlet established as a reliable authority may be used for purposes of impeachment but may not be introduced as substantive evidence.

In State v. Morgan, the Court of Appeals noted that the witness need not be familiar with the treatise in question to be cross-examined on the treatise (so long as the treatise is established by judicial notice or other expert testimony as an authority). 251 Or App 99 (2012).

6. Use of Specific Treatises in Child Sex Cases

Challenging the forensic child interview is often a key component to defending sex abuse cases. Three concepts that can be raised as part of a defense are contamination, source monitoring and alternative hypothesis.

Contamination studies show that children can be susceptible to influence, whether deliberate or unintentional, from a caregiver to the forensic interviewer.

Source monitoring is a concept regarding how a child knows what she or he knows. An evaluator who doesn’t properly discover the source of a child’s information could unwittingly sanction a false memory.

Alternative hypothesis is the concept that a child interviewer should keep an open mind to explain the claims of abuse. Interviewers must test a hypothesis rather than look to confirm it.

Practice Tip

The Oregon Interviewing Guidelines establishes the methodology that forensic child interviewers should follow when interviewing a child. The guidelines are a must-have for any practitioner to cross-examine child interviewers. Be aware that there have been several renditions and the practitioner should be aware of all.

In addition, the following books by leading researchers in the field of child memory and forensic interviewing are helpful to anyone who intends to cross-examine a child witness or challenge the interviews of a child witness:

Jeopardy in the Courtroom by Ceci and Bruck

Investigative Interviews of Children by Poole and Lamb

A Child Interviewer’s Guidebook by Bourg et al.

Tell Me What Happened by Lamb et al.

These books discuss various challenges to interviewing child witnesses, the reliability of the various interviews of child witnesses by both lay people (i.e., a family member) and alleged professionals (i.e., Child Abuse Center “forensic interviewers”).

7. Testimony Rebutting Forensic Children’s Interview

In State v. Warren, 224 Or App 204 (2008), the Court of Appeals found error when the trial court excluded a psychologist from testifying for the defense to rebut the state’s witness, a physician who diagnosed the victim was sexually abused. Id. at 206.

The defense sought to call Robert Stanulis to testify that in the absence of physical findings, diagnosing a child comes down to psychological or psychiatric science. Dr. Stanulis would have testified regarding not only interviewing techniques but that the absence of psychological problems in the victim was evidence she wasn’t suffering from sexual abuse. Id. at 209.

Citing the ruling from State v. Rogers, 330 Or 282, 316 (2000), the court noted that OEC 702 does not bar disqualification of an expert because they lack a particular degree and the court must engage in a case-by-case assessment. Thus, despite Dr. Stanulis lacking a medical degree, he was qualified to testify about the lack of psychological evidence that supported a diagnosis of sexual abuse. Warren, 224 Or App at 212.

Note: In the absence of physical findings, the state can no longer introduce evidence of a diagnosis of sexual abuse. However, as per State v. Beauvais, 357 Or 524 (2015), a diagnosis of sexual abuse is relevant and admissible if physical evidence supports it. Furthermore, child forensic interviewers will introduce evidence supporting the inference the child was diagnosed as sexually abused, which should open the door for the defense to introduce evidence in opposition to that inference. [Fore more on this topic, see Chapter 10.]

III. Right to Present a Defense

Practitioners should cite to the Due Process Clause of the Sixth Amendment to overcome claims of statutory privileges that the state seeks to use as a shield against the defendant’s right to present a defense. In State v. LaJoie, 316 Or 63 (1993), the Oregon Supreme Court held that the Due Process Clause does not allow a defendant to trump the statutory restriction imposed by OEC Rule 412.

In LaJoie, the defendant sought to cross-examine the victim with numerous prior instances of sexual conduct that purported to show her bias. However, the defense’s offer of proof did not comply with Rule 412, which requires notice “not later than 15 days before the trial date.” Id. at 481. The trial court granted the state’s motion to bar the evidence, and after a jury convicted the defendant, the defendant appealed on the basis that OEC 412 does not apply to cross-examination and OEC 412’s notice requirements are unconstitutional under both a state and federal analysis.

The Oregon Supreme Court summarily rejected the argument that cross-examination is not evidence under Rule 412. Id. at 68. Regarding the constitutionality of the notice, the court noted that a similar rape shield law requiring a 15-day notice prior to trial of defendant’s intent to offer past sexual behavior was upheld by the United States Supreme Court in Michigan v. Lucas, 500 US 145 (1991): “The notice and hearing requirement serves legitimate state interests in protecting against surprise, harassment, and undue delay.” The court noted “a notice requirement . . . protects the alleged victim against surprise and needless anxiety by ensuring adequate warning of the extent of the ordeal that he or she will face at trial.” Id. at 71.

The court disposed on the defendant’s challenge to the preclusion of evidence by noting that neither Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution nor the Sixth Amendment of the US Constitution mandate admission of the evidence. Noting that the third goal of Rule 412 was protecting the victim (the other two interests of unfair surprise and avoiding undue delay seek to prevent prejudice to the state), the court held preclusion was appropriate when the Rule 412 notice requirements were violated. Id. at 76.

The court rejected the Sixth Amendment challenge on the basis that “a defendant’s Sixth amendment rights are not absolute.” Id. at 79. The court found procedural requirements are not “arbitrary or disproportionate” to the purposes they are designed to serve and are a reasonable condition on the defendant’s exercise of the right to present evidence. Id. at 81.

Interestingly, the conviction of Mr. LaJoie was overturned on post-conviction under LaJoie v. Thompson. 217 F.3d 663 (2000). In LaJoie, the Ninth Circuit criticized the Oregon Supreme Court’s analysis of Michigan v. Lucas, holding that they incorrectly interpreted the United States Supreme Court’s ruling. Specifically, the state supreme court failed to engage in case-by-case balancing in denying LaJoie’s claim and that failure to balance led to the wrong decision. Id. at 669-670. The court went on to state that since some of the evidence could reasonably have shown how the victim had a prior source of sexual knowledge, the Sixth Amendment required admission of the evidence. Id. at 672-673. Note that this holding is merely persuasive authority and not precedent; however, it can be cited in 412 motions as justification for admitting evidence that rebuts the state’s case.

IV. Conclusion

The above cited cases and statutes will allow you to introduce impeachment for bias in a sex abuse case. Knowing foundational cases like Leclair, Davis v. Alaska and Nacoste will help you fend off any attempts by the government to limit your impeachment of state witnesses.

Understanding the Rule 412 bar on past sexual behavior of the victim and exactly what evidence you can and cannot get in will also play a significant factor in your ability to present a complete defense.
