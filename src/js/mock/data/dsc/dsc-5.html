I. Introduction to an Introduction

That’s not a typo. That’s all one can hope to do in some forty-odd pages, creating a leaping point, a springboard into the vast and complicated field of literature which tries to make sense of what we know about memory and perception.

Much of what is described you may find completely congruent with common sense and experience, i.e., our brains do a pretty good job most of the time organizing and evaluating our everyday experiences, storing the important information we need to live our lives with a reasonable degree of predictability and rationality. However, once you travel down Wonderland’s rabbit hole and into the scientific literature that explains how memory and perception work, some may find it plainly shocking, maybe bewildering, that Alice’s memory is sometimes quite jumbled, evolving from one day to the next, and might not be very accurate at all, even though Alice thinks her memory is quite good, thank you very much. Memory errors plainly exist, and they can create really, really big mistakes with enormous consequences.

And, unlike the title suggestion of “confabulation,” most memory errors are not indicative of memory disorders, because they can and do occur in perfectly healthy, happy, and cognitively normal children, teens, and adults, not just in people with memory disorders.

Perhaps most troubling, it isn’t necessarily a big mistake that makes an enormous difference in a criminal case. Sometimes a subtle twist reinterpreting a seemingly innocent fact can send a client to years of incarceration and possibly a lifetime of registration and stigma of being a sex offender.

Practice Tip— Choose Your Words for Maximum Advocacy.

This chapter uses “memory errors” interchangeably with the more commonly used term, “false memories.” It’s really a matter of what works best for you, what rings truest to you in your own personal style and approach, and what you feel you can “sell” to the people that really matter (JURORS). The author struggles with “false memories” as it seems like snake oil, mumbo-jumbo concocted by nerdy professors in tweed and sold by a fast-talking car salesman. “False” seems to invite debate, invoking “is it a mirage or not?” It also carries an implied sense of blame, the false reporter is somehow at fault, like a liar who makes a “false accusation” resulting in a (incorrectly named) “false conviction”—the latter not being false at all, but a wrongful conviction. “Memory error” carries no judgment of anyone other than the fact the memory is inaccurate, thus no one is judged but the memory itself.

Still, “memory error” is not entirely accurate either, as “information recall mistakes” are simply a byproduct of a process that generally serves us well. Jurors may resist the notion that their memories, and specifically the complainant’s, are somehow flawed. We can both praise memory but then note that positive functioning comes at a cost: the type of memory the legal system relies upon to make a decision is different from the sort of memory that we all rely on in our day-to-day lives. The legal system needs to know about exact episodes. In our day-to-day lives, we rely on sweeping patterns of knowledge.

But, Warning: If you call a memory expert, carefully prepare them to use, define, and validate your terminology long before trial so you can speak freely in opening and on direct without concern that you might undermine your own credibility with your expert taking issue with your word choices.

II. What Is Memory? It’s a Locked Filing Cabinet, Sometimes

In its popular construct, memory is a filing cabinet where we put our objective experiences into neat folders, some of which we can locate cleanly and relate back coherently “like it happened yesterday.”

Other folders cannot be found because they’ve not been opened in a while; we just need the right prompt to locate them. When we do, these folders are in the same shape as when we put them there, whether a week ago, a year ago, or back when we were thirteen years old. Once we find that folder, our memory is untainted and pristine, ready to go, ready to prove “the truth,” although maybe it’s suffered some mild deterioration with age, though sometimes not at all—especially in the minds of sex crimes complainants when they are on the stand (Sarcasm.)

This construct, however, over years of research and study has proven to be a really nice and neat half-truth, one that allows us to make sense of the world relatively cohesively, but is not necessarily tied to the reality of human memory. Instead, what we now know is memory at its essence is a subjective construct, potentially but not always a dynamic process which allows us to make sense of our present experience with due consideration of our past experiences, but which might have been altered by intervening events (contamination) and in ways that are not discernable (source confusion).

Still, as a practical matter, human memory is pretty darn effective at perceiving and storing “events” that we think are really important at the time we perceive them. But it’s not perfect and things can go wrong.

A. Memory Is a Dynamic, Ongoing, Information Processing System

As a chemical reaction occurring and imprinting in your brain, memory is a dynamic, ongoing, information processing system.

As the scientific community has tried to understand and explain this process, models have been developed—each example of which has its limitations—to explain the dynamic process we have come to understand as memory. What are the primary limitations? First, our understanding of memory continues to evolve as we develop new tests, through cognitive experiments and imaging. Second, these models tend to oversimplify how memory works; at its root level, memory is an incredibly complicated and dynamic information processing system.

In spite of the limitations, two different but similar diagrams of these models are included because

• Most people, especially the people that matter (JURORS), are unlikely to bring to the courtroom a root knowledge of how memory works.

• The information jurors really need to know is kind of wonky and counterintuitive to life’s lived experience—i.e., memory works so seamlessly that we don’t “see” or “witness” the changes, and there are no remnants of the changes we can later document or identify—think no “chain of custody” of memory.

• Until you plunge into the literature—or are prepared with an expert to teach the people that matter (JURORS) this information—we need to get up to speed; visuals, frankly, are good teaching tools for us and for the people that matter (JURORS).

• If we are going to use the science of memory in our practice effectively, we have to find the words or teaching tools that best allow us to convey this wonky and counterintuitive science to the people that matter (JURORS) in ways we are comfortable teaching.

Crucially, these models help convey the notion that memory is:

• Dynamic—ever changing.

• Ongoing—yes, redundant of dynamic, but helps emphasize that memory formation and modification is a never-ending process, even if most changes are trivial.

• Multi-staged—there are many times and occurrences when memory can be influenced, modified and changed, unconsciously.

• Information processing system—memory acts relatively seamlessly we don’t hit “Control + S” to make it work, it just does its stuff.

At each step in this seamless process errors can and do occur, even after highly accurate perceptions are recorded and stored, because the recall and re-storage gives ample opportunity for the memory to be unintentionally and unconsciously altered.

B. Memory Has a Potentially Endless Number of Points Where Errors Can Occur

As a dynamic, ongoing, information processing system, memory has a potentially endless number of points where errors can occur.

In life’s most simple example, an event occurs, and if you are questioned about it right away, there are few opportunities for that static event to get out of the sensory memory stage and move to a stage with outside influences. In this way, current thinking about memory is in line with the traditional hearsay exception for an excited utterance—there is little to no time for someone under the influence of a startling event to modify or change their perception; we accept such statements possessing increased reliability and they are allowed in court.3 Seldom does life give us simple examples in the courtroom. Instead, what we often see is testimony about situations “perceived” many months if not, especially in the case of child sex abuse, years prior, with many stages for memory to be subtly reformed and subject to unconscious influences, including

• The original perception, which can be influenced by things like attention, past experiences (does the event fit a known pattern?), biases, stereotypes, drugs, alcohol, etc.

• The manner in which the memory went from the perception stage to “storage” in the working, or short-term, memory. There is a reason law enforcement personnel are trained to segregate witnesses—attempting to remove potential outside influences both in the short-term and long-term storage.

• The manner in which the memory went from working or short-term memory to long-
term memory.

• The manner in which the memory was retrieved each time, because each time—that’s right, each time—the original memory is subject to being rewritten and influenced by any number of subsequent experiences and outside influences, intentionally and unintentionally, such as attention, past experiences (does the event fit a known pattern), biases, stereotypes, and outside information that confirms or contradicts other information.

• How many times the memory was retrieved and re-stored, and what was occurring during each retrieval and re-storage because each time, yes, each time the original memory is re-stored it may be rewritten and influenced by any number of subsequent experiences and outside influences, intentionally and unintentionally, such as attention, past experiences, biases, stereotypes, and outside information that confirms or contradicts other information, which can include simple questions that provide new information or suggest alternatives.

• Go back to the third bullet above and loop endlessly, but don’t keep track of that loop because it’s downright impossible. Source confusion is seamless and not contemporaneously recorded—how would it be recorded? How could your mind register each time your memory changed, with each event or factor undermining your personal “record” of events? In other words, when storing physical evidence we record the chain of custody to make sure the physical evidence has not changed. But in memory storage, there is no chain of custody.

III. False Memories|Memory Errors: What are they and how does this dynamic, ongoing, information processing system go wrong?

No one’s memory is infallible, although some people think theirs is better than others and project that explicitly (“my memory’s like a vault”), and sometimes implicitly through demeanor, confidence, and “looking you right in the eye.” Studies that have tried to determine the correlation between confidence and accuracy are all over the board, almost as radically as the “debate” over global warming.4

Still, several characteristics define the types of memory errors most likely at issue in criminal cases, but before we go down the rabbit hole of how these memory errors occur, we must examine just what a false memory is and is not.

A. Characteristics of False Memories/Memory Errors

1. Psychologists Define a False Memory as an Unconscious Memory Error, Indistinguishable in the Holder from Memories of Real Events

A false memory is a mental experience that is mistakenly taken to be a veridical5 representation of an event from one’s personal past. Memories can be false in relatively minor ways (e.g., believing one last saw the keys in the kitchen when they were in the living room) and in major ways that have profound implications for oneself and others (e.g., mistakenly believing one is the originator of an idea or that one was sexually abused as a child).

(Johnson, M. K., 2001).

2. A False Memory Is a Memory Error, Not A Lie

Lord Tennyson may have been talking about the role of the defense attorney when he said,

That a lie which is half a truth is ever the blackest of lies. That a lie which is all a lie may be met and fought with outright. But a lie which is part a truth is a harder matter to fight.

The Grandmother, st. 8 (1864).

He may as well have been talking about our task, to defend against the darkest of lies, of a sexual assault or abuse of a vulnerable victim, often a child. But often when wrong the witness isn’t lying, they are simply mistaken. And a memory error isn’t a lie, it’s a memory mistake. Or maybe a perception mistake, but irrespective of what it’s called it’s an honest mistake. If Lord Tennyson taught a psychology course today he might say: “A small memory error is ever the worst of memory errors, because a small one can send an innocent man to a life behind bars.…”

Functionally, there is a world of difference between a memory error and a lie. A lie is a conscious decision to say something the speaker subjectively believes is untrue. A false memory is sincerely held and believed. Thus it becomes a memory error, or a mistake that can be innocently and unconsciously made by well-meaning people, sometimes unintentionally, unconsciously encouraged by equally well-intentioned people. The sincerity of the belief in the false memory is a serious problem in our work.

When suggesting something is a lie, you need to ask: what motivates it? Motivation is largely irrelevant for false memories.

  • Lies can be created quickly and abruptly; false memories generally emerge slowly.
  • Lies can concern very recent events; false memories generally involve events further into the past.
  • Someone who’s more articulate will be better at lying; that person may be somewhat less vulnerable to false memories.
  • Lies may be more inconsistent—because the liar cannot remember what they said the last time; false memories are neither more nor less consistent than any other memory, because they are a memory.
  • Liars may appear insincere (because they are); not so for false memories.

But for both lies and false memories, the content must come from somewhere, even if just a dream or inference. If a five-year-old starts talking about sexual practices, whether a lie or a false memory you still need to ask from where that information came.

In both lies and false memories there are almost always some true elements. That does not necessarily validate the overall truth of either. Made-up stories and false memories often incorporate real locations and truthful aspects; “true in part” does not mean “true overall.”

3. False Memories Are a Different Concept from Memories that Have Simply Faded, or Events that Were Not Perceived or Noted, So No Memory Was Imprinted

It is essential, at this early stage, to distinguish false memory from the more familiar idea of memory fallibility. Memory, as everyone knows, is an imperfect archive of our experience.... In its most general sense, false memory refers to circumstances in which we are possessed of positive, definite memories of events—although the degree of definiteness might vary—that did not actually happen to us.

(Brainerd & Reyna, 2005).

Still, these memory gaps provide the opportunity for false memories to be suggested, woven into other veridical memories.

4. False Memories May Cause Real Trauma, Which Is Indistinguishable from Trauma Caused by Real Events Still Remembered

Just as the field of psychology around memory has exploded, so has our understanding of trauma. Trauma is one of the great wildcards in our practice. Crying witnesses naturally elicit empathy from the people that matter (JURORS) and the gatekeepers (JUDGES). Moreover, lawyers and judges see so many guilty pleas and are exposed to so much trauma in our clients’ lives that discerning the “real trauma caused by veridical memories” as opposed to the “real trauma caused by false memories or memory errors” may be an impossible task for us, let alone jurors and judges. So, when a DDA argues in closing that “those were no crocodile tears…” to support the premise that tears = truth, the jury might miss this crucial alternative explanation: tears = trauma, trauma ≠ truth. Be prepared to address this.

5. Trauma and Other Mental Health Issues May Interfere with Accurate Memory Formation

Complicating matters further, unrelated trauma may interfere with memory formation. It may contribute to the formation of false memories. And, seeking therapy to ameliorate trauma may itself undermine the accuracy of memory.

There is a great deal of literature which may inform or deeply confuse the issues as relate to trauma. Some of it makes sense, i.e., therapeutic goals may not have anything to do with establishing the objective validity of what occurred, and may in fact undermine the fact-finding goal of the criminal justice system.6 Again, something to be prepared to address.

6. Which Is the Chicken, Which the Egg?

A significant challenge in sex crimes defense is determining whether the report is a memory error, a lie, or maybe a bleeding of the two. It’s not always apparent, and it might have been a lie one day and a sincerely held and believed memory error the next.

There is extensive scientific literature as relates to children lying where it has been established:

a. Children are more likely to lie if they are motivated to lie, such as if they are encouraged by an adult or confirming facts otherwise “known.”

b. Any limits to the types of lie are not in the telling but in the creation of the lie. If someone supplies the child with a lie, the likelihood goes way up it will be adopted by the child.

Once the lie is told and the child is validated, has the lie supplanted the true memory? Is the child strong enough to stand up in court and recant? How does one know?7 What if part is a lie, part a false memory? Moreover, does a complainant’s trauma prove the crime, or undermine the validity of the report? Does the apparently suffered trauma, on full display in the courtroom (or on the CAAC recording), relate to the reported event, or is it triggered by other trauma, which may not be real at all? Moreover, because prior trauma can interfere with perception and memory formation, how do we unravel and find the “true memory”? Can we?

B. How Do False Memories Form/Memory Errors Occur?

At its essence, through extensive study psychologists have established that memory errors, or false memories, can be induced by suggestion, and that when such errors occur, they are so seamlessly intertwined with ordinary, “true” or veridical memories, the source of suggestion is not consciously known, i.e., source confusion. An apt analogy is that our internal dynamic information processing system weaves a coherent narrative from whole cloth. Each time we pull the narrative back into our working memory, there is an opportunity for the memory itself to change, or be re-coded when stored.

Moreover, source confusion and the discarding of earlier formed memories is an intuitive process, which can be grasped once one understands the dynamic nature of memory. If a person could keep track of each time their memory evolved in one fashion or another, think of all the memory storage that would be required to accurately record all of the factors and subfactors leading someone to remember something differently, while still remembering the originally stored memory. That’s quite a chain of information to command, requiring an incredible amount of memory and a very complicated processing system to analyze it all.

How are false memories suggested? Think of the parent confronted with a child who says, “My pee pee hurts!” What parent isn’t going to want to get to the bottom of it right away? What happened? Who did it? How can I make my child safe? Who touched you?

If that parent had been abused by their uncle, or stepfather, to whom are they going to look? Or what if their ex has a penchant for “age-inappropriate” sexual partners or an online “teen porn” habit, even if those images are legal 18-year-olds with relatively early stage 2 Tanner development? Or what if this magically springs up after visitation with an ex with whom the parent has a volatile relationship? The normal response is to get to the bottom of it, and it’s not likely the parent, aunt, uncle, teacher, or grandparent has been trained in child interviewing best practices. Because bad questioning can cause contamination, memory can be contaminated merely from a well-meaning source neutrally asking about information.

Still, it is important to keep in mind that sources of suggestion are not necessarily overt, intentional or malevolent and can be innocent, well-meaning, unintentional (a conversation overheard), built upon inferences, or even sourced from imagination or dreams.

C. What Kind of Memory Errors Are Possible?

There is no clear proportionality test, no measure of how large, how small, how recent, or how far in the past a false memory can be created. Memory errors can be large (complete events) or subtle (accidental bump turns into frottage or a grope). Still, case-specific factors may influence the scope of possible errors (small influences tend to create smaller errors, large errors may require a more significant influence or particularly vulnerable person, e.g., very young child, or both).

An initial report that is a lie, ambiguous, or acquiesces to another’s version can turn into a false memory. Memory errors can be caused unintentionally, unconsciously, and by otherwise well-meaning, well-intentioned people. All we can do is be on guard for the warning signs.

And there is no reason to think that just children are susceptible to suggestion. In a recent study, college-aged participants were interviewed over time and were subjected to aggressive suggestion techniques such as encouragement to recall specific fictitious events, affirmation that similar details were provided by others, positive and negative feedback, and social pressures. The researchers were astonishingly successful in planting false memories: 70 percent of their participants “remembered” a serious juvenile event which led to their police detention along with vivid, rich details, in most ways indistinguishable memories of “actual” events.8 While this study has been criticized for the aggressive suggestion techniques employed and possibly overstating of results,9 it still informs how far suggestibility can go.

D. What Are the Warning Signs of Memory Errors? Is There a Memory Error in My Case?

The following series of questions are illustrative of the task at hand, undertaking the painstaking archaeology of memory evaluation.

Practice Tip

Memory errors and sources of contamination may have occurred long before the child abuse assessment center interview. One of the most challenging aspects of our inquiry is that once the child makes it to the assessment center, they may (likely) have already been subjected to outside influences and suggestion, so we will never be able to “recover” and adequately evaluate the memory-chain satisfactorily. Therefore, while the first thing we do as lawyers is usually to review the discovery and watch the CAAC recording, a really great interview and compelling story by the child is only the beginning. It’s akin to watching the last ten minutes of a movie and then trying to use only those ten minutes to determine what happened earlier.

For this reason, we should not overly focus on the CAAC interview as the only “source” of possible contamination—in fact, contamination may be more likely to occur upstream, before the CAAC interview, since presumably the CAAC interviewer has been trained in how to conduct an interview in accordance with protocols and best practices.

While the questions below refer to a “questioner,” it doesn’t have to be a parent, therapist, detective or CAAC evaluator. You should consider the questioner to be anyone or any other competing source of potential contamination—a conversation with a parent, grandparent, stepmother, friend, classmate, or anyone else who comes up as the complainant “remembers” the original source event. And, keep in mind a “source event” doesn’t even have to be an actual event of any sort—it could just be an initial suggestion, a dream, or from an overall well-meaning, innocent question:

1. Who questioned the complainant/child first?

In many circumstances the first questioning occurs long before the first “official report,” and by someone who may have an axe to grind or may themselves be flooded by their own emotions.

2. When and where did the questioning occur?

Certain locations can be inherently more coercive than others.

3. Was the questioner properly trained to follow the rules?

a. Did the questioner provide ground rules?

b. Did the questioner ask neutral questions?

c. Did the questioner ask repeated questions?

d. Did the repeated questions in any fashion infer that earlier answers were incorrect?—which may occur just by asking the same question over

e. Did the questioner insert information into questions?

f. Did the questioner attribute other sources for information placed into questions?

g. Did the questioner ask neutral follow up questions?

h. Did the complainant/child initially fabricate anything?

i. Did the complainant/child’s story evolve in a fashion inconsistent with
earlier responses?

4. Did the questioner objectively document the interview (video/audio tape)?

If the answer is no, an altogether separate source of inquiry relates to the questioner’s memory for how the conversation unfolded, as people are terrible at remembering the exact words that they heard. After just 30 or 40 seconds, people tend to remember their own (sometimes rough) paraphrase of what they heard, not the exact words. Even worse, parents are particularly poor at recalling conversations with
their children.

a. If not recorded, did the questioner accurately record what was asked?

b. Did the questioner accurately record what was said?

c. Can the questioner accurately describe feedback provided?10

5. Was the questioner biased? Or did the questioner have a stake in the outcome—even if not expressed?

6. Is the questioner in a position of authority or trust in society or vis-à-vis the complainant/child?

7. Did the questioner provide any feedback/validation/negative feedback in questions or in response to answers?

8. Does the recording preserve objective signs of feedback from the questioner?

9. Did the questioner ask questions building on known historical facts which are a short stretch from the allegations (kernel of truth)?

10. What was the questioner’s emotional state during the questioning?

11. Did the questioner unintentionally provide feedback including nonverbal reactions?

12. Did the questioner have any prior information about the alleged perpetrator?

13. Is the questioner also the investigator? (Best practice is for questioner to have no preconceived understanding as to what occurred.)

14. What information did the questioner have about the allegations prior to starting
the interview?

15. Repeat the above for all subsequent questioners.

16. How long was the delay between each questioning?

17. How many times was the complainant/child questioned prior to the “official”
statement?

18. Did the story change or evolve over time, and if so, how and why?

19. If the story changed, was the complainant exposed to other potential sources of contamination?

Practice Tip

Asking and getting answers to all of these questions may seem tedious and risks alienating the people that matter (JURORS) and the gatekeeper (COURT). The tediousness of this task may work against you, trying the patience of jurors and judge, so counsel may need to do extra ground work in opening to set the stage for a deep inquiry, or instead target the expected best points. Every case is different. When examining an experienced detective or CAAC interviewer, expect contemptuous looks and responses, exasperation, and endless excuses, which, unfortunately, they are often skilled at providing (“I was trained that there is no perfect interview”) and which the Oregon Interviewing Guidelines, 3d Edition, provides in bulk. As described in Chapter 6, the third edition is vastly inferior to the previous edition, and provides significant ammunition to equivocate and explain away poor compliance with accepted protocols.

If you plan to go in-depth, you may be able to accomplish this at a pretrial hearing, which will allow you to “discover” a more pointed approach or hot topics to hit on in a pithier fashion when you are before the people that matter (JURORS).

The pretrial hearing also may allow you to condition the state’s expert as to your approach (i.e., heavy science, aggressive attitude, snarky questioning), so that when you are before the people that matter (JURORS) the state’s expert may react to the pretrial approach in a manner where they have been “conditioned” to be more helpful, i.e., expecting to get hammered they may be more prepared at trial, or alternatively they might be more cautious or even very hostile or defensive even when you toss easy and polite questions at them, which may allow the people that matter (JURORS) to draw inferences of bias and hostility to fairness.

E. Lure and Traps of a Memory-Based Defense—Strategy Concerns

The lure of a memory case is like the attraction to a fancy new car. It brings so many
benefits as you:

• Don’t have to call the complaining witness a liar, be it a child or the crying victim of a horrible assault, but instead present a cogent story why the complaining witness is telling their truth, just not the truth.

• Don’t have to build the expectation of the brilliant “ah ha” moment when the witness breaks down and gets caught in a dramatic lie, a la Perry Mason.

• Get to call “fancy pants” experts who can wax eloquently about all kinds of interesting studies where ordinary people have made huge mistakes, thus “revealing” the nature of our flawed human experience to previously blissfully happy jurors (cue the drum roll…).

• Get to take the state’s interviewing protocols and shove them down the throat of the state’s experts, including those who have no idea what they mean or say, but will nonetheless agree and admit they have been trained that witnesses should be segregated.

• Get a sneak peek, possibly, at the state’s witnesses under oath at a pretrial hearing, including the lead detectives, the complainant, the complainant’s family and friends, and maybe someone from a CAAC (see Taint Hearing, below), and have the chance at trial with transcripts to nitpick.

And so on. Then that brand new fancy car gets a door ding, or two, and you realize it’s
not always what it’s cracked up to be.

Maybe instead of a lure, it’s a trap. Memory cases are difficult because:

• The way memory works is contrary to “common sense” and experience.

• Explaining the way memory works can be very complicated and confusing to jurors, and fraught with things that alternatively sound like junk science and a lame excuse made up by a defense lawyer who cannot come up with a better explanation, except by calling a fancy pants expert to divert the jury’s attention from the truth.

• The law as to what comes in, below, is inconsistent, frustrating, and the common thread seems to be the state’s witnesses get to say just about anything they want except direct vouching, and the defense witnesses are closely scrutinized and are stopped well short of the correlating, more forgiving “limits” put on the state.

F. Anticipate the Government’s Responses and Arguments

1. Expect attacks on your expert:

  • If your expert is not a psychologist or former child interviewer:

  • Isn’t it true you are not an active psychologist or therapist?
  • Isn’t it true you have never seen a patient?
  • Isn’t it true you have never worked at a CAAC? Never conducted a training at a CAAC? Been to a training at a CAAC?
  • Isn’t it true you have never conducted a forensic interview of a child? Been formally trained how to interview a child?
  • Isn’t it true you have only worked in the classroom, lab performing studies, or as a teacher?11

  • How much has the expert been paid on this case? Paid on other defense cases in the past year?

  • Has the expert ever testified for the government? Consulted with the government? Trained CAAC or detectives?

  • Attacks using their former testimony under oath in other cases, including pulling excerpts out of context; just like we have a members’ email listserv, the DA’s communicate too and discuss attacks at their conferences.

  • Attacks using the expert’s former writings including pulling excerpts out of context—be ready for this in redirect.

  • Expect the state to attempt to use your expert against you:

  • Wouldn’t you agree memory is, generally, very accurate? (Rebuttal: “We’re not addressing most of the time, we are discussing the circumstances of this case. So the right question is whether memory is generally very accurate in the circumstances presented in this case, and what factors presented here might inform the jury in their decision-making process?”)
  • Wouldn’t you agree memory is accurate most of the time?
  • Wouldn’t you agree memories of traumatic events are recalled more clearly, in more detail, and last longer than non-traumatic memories? (see Science of Perception at 88 for rebuttal).
  • Wouldn’t you agree that there is no test to establish what is a false memory?
  • You cannot say the complainant’s claim is a result of a false memory/memory error? (Objectionable question, but be prepared for it.)
  • You cannot point to any objective facts that support the defense theory? (dripping with sarcasm and emphasis).
  • In other words, there is no objective evidence of a memory error here? (Objectionable question, but be prepared for it.)
  • Expect objections that the defense expert’s discussion of statistics is misleading, the studies are hearsay, and the studies don’t support the proposition for which they are being offered (potential rebuttals below).
  • Expect objections that the defense expert’s testimony is “tantamount” to vouching or “connecting the dots.”

2. Expect Attacks on the Science

  • The studies are in the lab only, not the real world (not true as a general rule).
  • The participants have been compensated (paid) for their participation (not true as a general rule).
  • The studies are not on point and/or do not study child sex abuse (not true as a general rule).
  • Aspects of the studies hurt your case too.
  • Contrary to the “real world”: The case detective, CAAC interviewer, DHS worker, and/or therapist will come chock full of explanations and excuses for their work and impressions, so consider pretrial motions and evidentiary hearings to get them under oath and to force production of materials upon which they rely in their testimony. If they won’t produce in discovery, subpoena them to trial.
  • Expect state witnesses to use their “training and experience” to avoid science attacks, with lots of impressive but vague raw numbers (“I’ve been doing this for X years, and have been involved with hundreds of prosecutions”), years of trainings and “peer review” of their interviewing.
  • Counter: These may seem impressive to you (and the jury), but when pressed you might find it much less so as often the witnesses have little specific recall of the trainings, have little idea of what is in the literature, their “training” may well bleed into areas which should be subject to Daubert scrutiny, have little idea what “peer review” of the literature actually means, and “peer review” of their interviewing techniques may be exceedingly limited and superficial.12

3. Expect Rhetorical Attacks

  • “Of course people lie, but there is no proof of that here…” The DDA may confuse the issues, but so might the jurors. (Response: burden shifting, false memories are not lies.)
  • “Of course memory isn’t perfect, did you need a fancy pants expert to tell you that…?” (Response: Yes, because much of memory function is contrary to common understanding.)
  • “Look, the complainant is crying, that was real and so was the trauma they suffered at the hands of Bad Defendant.” (Response: False memories can cause trauma indistinguishable from real memories.)
  • “If the complainant is lying, they deserve an academy award for those crocodile tear.s…” I.e., tears = truth. (Response: False memories can cause trauma indistinguishable from real memories.)
  • “Why would someone subject themselves to all of this humiliation, being retraumatized in court and aggressive attacks on their character if it wasn’t true?” (Response: False memories are not a lie, but “true” to the person who has them.)
  • “Children don’t lie.” (Response: Not a lie, but a false memory, and children do lie, especially when encouraged by an adult.)
  • “Children don’t lie about sex abuse.” (Response: Not a lie, a false memory.)
  • “Did you notice the de-fense lawyer does not have any evidence of any single claimed memory error or a single claimed false memory? (dripping with sarcasm). (Response: Burden shifting, further response is case specific but generally not our burden, state has to prove beyond a reasonable doubt the factual basis and reliability; beyond a reasonable doubt is undermined by lack of consistent story and state cannot disprove contamination.)
  • When client is on the stand, the prosecutor may go through every event in numbing detail, looping, and get them to agree to as many accuracies in the report as possible; rhetorically, this may appear to make the complainant’s statement overall—and what you deem memory errors—to be accurate.

Practice Tip

Review these and other lines of attacks with your own expert to anticipate and prepare responses. Make sure any studies discussed directly support opinions relevant to the issues in your case.

G. Do I Have a Memory Error–Based Defense? If so, what do I do?

Every case to some degree is a memory case unless the entire event is captured from multiple angles, with no obstructions, by high-quality recordings, both audio and visual. The people that matter (JURORS) at some point will need to rely to some degree on someone else’s perception, memory, and recounting of same. Our work as defense lawyers is almost always to attack those same perceptions, memories and recounting. There are so many decision points and opportunities to re-record, reinterpret and then alter our dynamic, ongoing, information processing system.

What’s the case about: Lies? Biases and vengeance? False memories?

Seldom is there a completely clean case where it is entirely one or the other, more likely there is a blend, the bump that becomes a touch or grope because the complainant hears the defendant is a creeper or groped someone else; the lie that gets repeated so much the witness starts to believe it, or has to believe it because so much has now been lost, “Because mom is counting on me.”

Seldom can we expect the testimony to come out exactly the way we anticipate. Maybe the duration of the grope gets extended, or there are now many “other acts” which were not in the discovery. Or there was undocumented earlier questioning of the child that we learn of for the first time at trial. Or the state’s expert starts slipping in profile evidence or their personal experience evidence, which the trial judge is loath to exclude and, and if it is excluded, now is in front of the jury with an empty instruction to “disregard…”—another legal fiction.

A reasonable approach is to treat every sex case as a potential memory case and to have an expert on board who has read the discovery, reviewed all recorded evidence, was consulted pretrial and is ready to be called, consulted and available to testify at trial as the evidence unfolds.

1. Prepare, Prepare, Prepare

Sex cases are very arguably the hardest of defense cases, and of the sex crime defenses false memory or memory error cases may be the most difficult of this subset. Think vulnerable child, a world hostile to sex crimes defendants, and a defense that does not directly answer “why would a child lie?” but instead supplies a wonky “it’s not a lie, it’s a false memory…” “Right. Just lock ’em up.” (Sarcasm.)

Preparation means digging hard into the literature, using your expert as a devil’s advocate (ask them how they’d respond to your attack or to put themselves into the state’s expert’s position), and determining how you are going to explain this “memory junk science“ to the people that matter (JURORS), who may be disposed to thinking this is all a bunch of nonsense, just poor excuses from a defense lawyer.

Appendix A is a series of PowerPoint slides the author has used in trial to help convey a series of conversations that occurred before the police investigator ever got involved, and how difficult it is to reconstruct the specific language that was used in each—we just don’t know. Each slide was a separate conversation, the blank bubbles convey that we cannot know just what was said.

The next set of PowerPoint slides, Appendix B, has been used to explain the differences between approved and less-approved interview styles. Think back to law school: How long did it take you to figure out what a leading or forced answer question is? And to adopt it in your practice? Jurors are not lawyers. They don’t understand that an open but very bland style of questioning leads to the best, most accurate responses and that life’s ordinary interview styles can be highly suggestive.

2. Study and Practice the Science of Memory

Use it in your everyday life in everyday language. Practice telling people about how fascinating the stuff you are learning is, how memory works really well sometimes and really poorly other times. Use the web to find other people talking about memory—
there’s tons of fascinating materials on memory, a few links are included in the bibliography where Professor Elizabeth Loftus explains her groundbreaking work in user-friendly language.

The more you familiarize yourself with the science of memory and practice by telling others about it, the easier it will be when standing in front of a very skeptical and sometimes hostile jury, and easier to keep going in a convincing manner. For this author, there have been really difficult times in the courtroom, but the more you practice, especially with friends, the more neurological pathways get built up so you can tap, in your own words, ways in which you can most effectively tell your memory-based defense.

Practice with stories from real life, use them as intros to your practice with friends. Just Google “false memories” and you’ll come up with articles such as this one,13 in which Neil deGrasse Tyson, Brian Williams, Mitt Romney, and Tony Blair “remembered” events they did not witness, for easily provable reasons. For example, Romney remembered14 a parade honoring his father, former Governor George Romney, that took place before Mitt was born.

3. Hire a Memory Expert, Early

Although it’s a legitimate tactic to study up and cross the heck out of the state experts, this is an area where your own expert might be able to help—considerably. Apart from helping get you up to speed on the research, and there is lots of it, an expert can help discern which is the most important research, whether the research on which you want to rely can pass the Daubert test, help guide your examination of the state’s expert(s), and offer expert testimony—one of the Lawson remedies.

Even if you believe that yours is not a direct memory attack case, once you’re in trial and the state’s expert begins to pontificate about their “training and experience,” you’ll be relieved to have someone you can call during a break to ask for advice and maybe to rebut subtle but potentially damaging testimony.

4. Memory and the Law

The starting place for all memory litigation under Oregon law is the nationally recognized landmark case State v. Lawson, 352 Or 724 (2012), which both comprehensively summarizes research in the field of memory and resets the admissibility standard as it relates to eyewitness identification, requiring the state to demonstrate sufficient reliability as a precursor to admissibility. The case is a must read, must study, as it integrates concepts such as estimator and system variables, and comprehensively explains the interaction between the science, the Due Process Clause, and the Oregon Evidence Code. Estimator and system variables are, essentially, the factors that may influence the accuracy of perception and memory storage—in essence, the quality control of this dynamic, ongoing, information processing system.

a. Estimator variables are factors external to the criminal justice system that influence memory and the accuracy of the recollections. They cannot be controlled by others, especially law enforcement and investigators. They include how good the witness’s vision is, the opportunity to perceive an event, and, especially in eyewitness identifications, errors that unconsciously occur in cross-racial indemnifications.

b. System variables are factors that can influence memory and accuracy which can be controlled by criminal justice system players (such as police, DDAs, DHS, CAAC investigators). They include how a lineup or photo array is arranged, instructions given to witnesses, and other contamination to which the witness is subjected.

In Lawson, the court recognized that under OEC 403, the probative value of evidence may be undermined by both estimator and system variables. This, in turn, can mislead the jury’s evaluation of the accuracy and result in unfair prejudice. The Lawson remedy is inherently case specific, such that if the government cannot meet its burden, the evidence may be inadmissible, or the court may impose intermediate remedies such as (1) admission of only the most reliable testimony, (2) allow expert testimony to address problems, or (3) case-specific special jury instructions.

c. Lawson in Sex Crimes Defense There is no separate memory function in the human condition that applies to eyewitness identification and not to every other type of memory function. The exact same memory science analysis that relates to eyewitness identification applies to memory for every other type of case. Memory Science = Memory Science; Suggestibility = Suggestibility.

So the analysis is identical—where a sex crime complainant has been subjected to both estimator and system variables, their testimony may be inherently unreliable,
so much so that jurors cannot effectively evaluate the evidence. Remedies which
may include:

  • Complete exclusion; State v. Lawson, 352 Or 724, 756 (2012) (state that as the proponent of evidence succeeds in establishing that the evidence is not barred

by OEC 402, the defendant, as the opponent of the evidence, assumes the burden of proving that OEC 403 nevertheless requires its exclusion).

  • Admission of only the most reliable testimony; id., at 759.
  • Admission of expert testimony; id., at 759-61.
  • Case specific juror instructions. Id. at 763.

After Lawson, the defense has the option of using the science of memory to attack the government’s case, generally, to go on the offensive and seek dismissal of government’s case (not likely), or to seek another remedy suggested by Lawson. The remedy is within the discretion of the trial court. Lawson at 762, (citing State v. Cunningham, 337 Or 528, 536 (2004)). Still, the Lawson court explicitly recognized that generalized jury instructions are not adequate to inform the people that matter (JURORS). Id. at 760.

Practice Tip

Expect the state to say over and over, “Lawson is an eyewitness identification case” and doesn’t apply to child abuse cases or in other contexts. Expect many judges to agree, initially. It’s a great rhetorical technique, but it’s inaccurate. Similarly, you can repeat again and again, “Lawson is a memory case, and the exact same evidence code and constitutional provisions apply to every case.” And, our rhetoric is accurate, because nothing in Lawson suggests that Lawson is limited to eyewitness identifications. In reality, the science of memory is identical across the board—there is, after all, only one memory system, not a separate one for eyewitness identification vs. memory for words or conduct. You may wish, however, to call your pretrial motion something other than a Lawson motion, but instead a “taint” or “competency” hearing.

How should the court remedy the taint in your case? Lawson suggests it may depend on the variable type, with estimator variables, cross, experts and case-specific jury instructions. Id. at 763. However, here’s the nuclear option:

If the state’s administration of one or more of the system variables (either alone or combined with estimator variables) results in suggestive police procedures, that fact can, in turn, give rise to an inference of unreliability that is sufficient to undermine the perceived accuracy and truthfulness of an eyewitness identification—only then may a trial court exclude the eyewitness identification under OEC 403.

State v. Lawson, 352 Or 724, 763 (2012) (emphasis supplied). Good Stuff!!!

5. Pretrial Motion Practice

Pretrial motion practice is the first step in a false memory/memory error litigation and may set the stage to more fully understand the state’s case and just what the complainant’s claims are. All too often discovery is incomplete, there are questions not asked by investigators, and avenues left unexplored because to a large extent, especially in child sex cases, the prosecutor will rely on generalities of an uncountable number of events of alleged abuse which will increase the horror for the people that matter (JURORS), prevent the defense from knowing enough details to undertake a sufficiently in-depth evaluation of the evolution of the complainant’s statements and prior inconsistent statements, and prevent the defense from establishing other sources of contamination. There are, however, several pretrial motions that may help.

a. “Other Acts” Motion

It’s crucial for counsel to be up to speed on the nuances of the Williams/Baughman cases and their progeny as relates to “other acts” evidence, which is addressed in Chapter 9. Besides an initial admissibility determination of particularly damaging evidence, “other acts” motions can serve other important purposes such as filling in discovery holes as to the number of incidents and how the “disclosure process”15 occurred. This may also support making memory contamination arguments as memory does not improve over time, and the literature is replete with evidence that the “gold standard” is to conduct a single, comprehensive interview as soon as possible. The more the state embraces the “disclosure process,” the more the state might prove the factual underpinning for your contamination arguments—so use their “good facts” against them.

Practice Tip

“Disclosure process” seems to be one of the terms du jour of law enforcement investigators and CAAC interviewers to explain when complainants, usually children, make progressively more detailed disclosures, both in the number of incidents and in vastly greater detail over time. It’s very convenient for them to have an explanation, i.e., excuse, why the complainant’s initial disclosure is not, at the first setting, complete and comprehensive. Problem for them is that a continually evolving narrative may also be a prime indication of a complainant’s memory evolving inaccurately through the creation of false memories via outside information, i.e., contamination.

b. Antoine Motion

Similarly, a pretrial motion to elect, pursuant to State v. Antoine, 269 Or App 66 (2015), should always be considered and may be used in conjunction with an “other acts” motion. In Antoine, Judge Lynn Nakamoto (now a justice on the Oregon Supreme Court) dealt with the all-too-common scenario of a complainant making a vague number of allegations that changed over time, and the DDA’s representation that the grand jury indicted a “representative sample” of the alleged sexual misconduct which the state argued it did not have to elect until the close of their case. Defense counsel was understandably hamstrung and demurred, alleging insufficient notice as to which events needed to be defended and which events correlated with indicted counts; there were extras, as usual. While Antoine affirmed the trial court’s denial of the defendant’s demurrer, then-Judge Nakamoto explained that the proper vehicle was a pretrial motion to elect, forcing the DDA’s hand, and that the non-elected events are subject to “other acts” analysis. Id. at 78-79.

c. Taint or Competency Hearing/Motion to Dismiss

One of the very best passages from Lawson may, in the right case, provide the basis for a motion to dismiss under the Due Process Clause, see State v. Zinsli, 156 Or App 245 (1998) (state’s bad faith destruction of evidence may violate due process). In Lawson the Oregon Supreme Court did a remarkable thing—it equated memory with trace evidence:

Because of the alterations to memory that suggestiveness can cause, it is incumbent on courts and law enforcement personnel to treat eyewitness memory just as carefully as they would other forms of trace evidence, like DNA, bloodstains, or fingerprints, the evidentiary value of which can be impaired or destroyed by contamination. Like those forms of evidence, once contaminated, a witness’s original memory is very difficult to retrieve; it is, however, only the original memory that has any forensic or evidentiary value. 

State v. Lawson, 352 Or 724, 748 (2012) (emphasis supplied).

Certainly other remedies are more likely to be granted. Either way, ask for specific rulings relating to each “aspect” of memory testimony and expert evidence—each event, each stage of the “disclosure process.” Even if you don’t win, you may well set the case up for appeal (unless the trial judge painstakingly weighs the evidence), and may help with a later request for a case-specific jury instruction.

Based on the work of lawyers in other jurisdictions, a motion for a “taint” hearing may well be the right approach, applying the same construct suggested by Lawson to child interviews. A couple of examples of this approach from other jurisdictions can be found here and here.16

6. Attacking the State’s Experts

Tactically, you will need to decide how far you want to go in attacking the government’s case directly, as opposed to providing and relying on the strength of a counter-narrative. Pretrial motion practice may give you a “sneak peek,” but will also give the government and its witnesses a sneak peek at your trial attack. If you go on the offensive, there is an excellent chance the government’s witness will not be versed in the literature, will rely heavily on “training and experience,” and will defend themselves using all the wiggle room provided by the Oregon Interviewing Guidelines (3d ed. 2012 or OIG 3d). The guidelines purport to “provide a general framework for how to go about conducting a child forensic interview in Oregon” and to “promote consistency in the quality of care provided to those Oregon children who are interviewed for possible abuse.” Id., at 3-4.

It is worth noting that OIG 3d is an enormous step backwards from the second edition, and the reasons for this are contrary to the science and frankly inexplicable unless you are a prosecutor or child abuse assessment investigator who has had to defend themselves against a well-planned attack based on the OIG 2d.

Here are some of the changes from the second to the third edition of the Oregon Interviewing Guidelines, among many others:

  • an interviewer should use extra caution in situations tied to divorce or custody battles
  • precautions in the use of puppets
  • cautions in OIG 2d that the “field interview” should be kept to a minimum and

the interviewer should move rapidly to the forensic interview, to OIG 3d’s much more permissive approach to field interviews, which may even obviate the need
for a forensic interview(?!?).

Lots more wiggle room can be found when comparing the two.

Practice Tip

If you have a delayed reporting case with an original interview prior to 2012, use the OIG 2d as much as possible and consider a pretrial motion or obtaining a pretrial concession from the prosecutor that the OIG 2d applies to your case.

7. Calling Your Own Memory Expert

In most memory cases, the best practice is to call your own expert. But how far can
the expert go and what can they say?

Case law is developing rapidly and is generally consistent with Oregon’s overall approach that witnesses should not be commenting on the credibility of witnesses, see, e.g., State v. Lupoli, 348 Or 346, 357 (2010); State v. Middleton, 294 Or 427, 438 (1983) (“We expressly hold that in Oregon a witness, expert or otherwise, may not give an opinion on whether he believes a witness is telling the truth”); State v. Chandler, 360 Or 323, 335 (2016) (out-of-court “vouching” statements in interrogation may be excluded if not offered for other relevant purpose). Still, what constitutes permissible testimony is nuanced.

First, the burden of proving admissibility is on the proponent to demonstrate that the evidence is scientifically valid and possesses sufficient indicia to be helpful for the jury. State v. Clark, 284 Or App 197, 203, rev den, 361 Or 803 (2017). Validity is determined pursuant to State v. Brown, 297 Or 404 (1984), and State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995). Clark, at 199.

Second, while it may be reversible error to exclude generalized qualified expert testimony relating to false memories, State v. Dye, 286 Or App 626, 639-40 (2017)17, expect careful scrutiny of your expert and be careful that the studies and science directly support the purposes for which the testimony is offered and are not overly broad or conclusory.

In Clark, Dr. Robert Stanulis’ OEC 104 hearing testimony was subjected to relatively high levels of scrutiny after the trial court “admonished Stanulis for making conclusory statements and failing to make sure that his words ‘mean what [he is] trying to say.’ ” Id., at 204. The Court of Appeals critically cited problems at the Brown hearing at length in a fashion both instructive and cautionary that experts should avoid both highly generalized and “conclusory” statements,

For example,…Stanulis testified that if an interviewer repeats a question, the interviewer is communicating that he or she did not like the answer, and that children “will change their answer to meet…what they think the interviewer wants.” The court stopped Stanulis and admonished him for saying that children “will” change their answer, explaining that the “words you use…particularly in a courtroom setting, are very important,…and so I want you to watch the words you use.”

Elsewhere, during the Brown hearing, the court stated:

“All right. One other matter, Dr. Stanulis yesterday appeared to have a tendency to continue talking or talk about matters that were nonresponsive in relation to the questions that he was asked. He should be instructed, [defense counsel], that he may respond to questions with responsive answers. He may not offer additional information that is not responsive to the questions. In other words, listen to the question and answer the questions specifically and then wait for another question.

“I also don’t want him to attempt to advocate or be the lawyer from the witness stand. If he, in front of the jury is doing these things, he will be interrupted by me, and I’m going to attempt to prevent that from occurring, which won’t be difficult, but I’m going to attempt to do that, and this should serve as a warning that will come, and I will not be pleased if I see that there appears to be some intentional attempt to get around this instruction from the Court.”

The court further cautioned Stanulis,

“to not use language which doesn’t communicate exactly what he’s trying to communicate. So absolutes probably aren’t what you intend generally, and so if you don’t intend an absolute, don’t use an absolute.”

Clark, 284 Or App at 204. At issue in that passage was Stanulis’ testimony that in children you “see confusion between fantasy and reality,” where had he testified with the qualification “prone,” the testimony may have otherwise been allowed. Id., at 205-06.

Functionally, the expert’s testimony,

must assist, not supplant, the jury’s assessment of credibility. The jury’s function is not impinged upon when expert testimony does no more than provide jurors with useful, nonconclusive information from which inferences as to credibility may be drawn.

State v. Remme, 173 Or App 546, 562 (2001) (observing that it was permissible to allow an expert witness to provide the jury with information or possible reasons from which it could draw inferences as to credibility). Moreover, a carefully mapped out approach may also forestall preservation challenges that the proffer is too general. See State v. Dye, 286 Or App 626, 401 P3d 243 (2017).

Third, the line between vouching and permissible testimony isn’t always obvious, but a fair guide is do not connect the dots.

The prohibition against vouching “applies both to ‘direct comments on the credibility of another witness, as well as to statements that are ‘tantamount’ to stating that another witness is credible.’ [Chandler], at 331, 380 P3d 932 (quoting State v. Beauvais, 357 Or 524, 543 [ ***] (2015)).” State v. Black, 289 Or App 256, 261 (2017).

Black’s discussion comprehensively addresses and takes a stab at making sense of the “tantamount” vouching case law, as guided by the explanation in State v. Beauvais, 357 Or 524, 543 (2015); of State v. Keller, 315 Or 273, 285 (1993) (expert testimony “there was no evidence of leading or coaching or fantasizing” during a child interview was impermissible); State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621, 629-30 (1988) (expert testimony that a witness was “not deceptive,” was incapable of lying without getting “tripped up,” and would not betray a friend was impermissible); and State v. Middleton, 294 Or 427, 543-44 (1983) (expert testimony that child raped by her father changed her reports was “typical” and “very much in keeping” with home molestation, shed light on “superficially bizarre behavior” not within the normal juror experience and may assist in credibility determination and not impermissible).

The distinctions were focused on two factors: (1) whether the testimony is commonly understood as signaling the declarant’s belief as to the witness’s truthfulness and (2) sufficiently beyond common understanding as to be helpful to assist and not undermine the jury’s own credibility determination. Black, at 263. The line is fine, but only in Middleton could the experts “connect the dots.” Id.

This area of law is particularly complicated. It’s important to consider the holdings in State v. Southard, 347 Or 127 (2009); State v. Lupoli, 348 Or 346, (2010); and State v. Beauvais, 357 Or 524 (2015), and there are no assurances how trial courts will rule on these issues. We’re likely to continue to see developments in the appellate courts, including in Black, which as of this writing is pending petition for review.

In Black, the defendant offered expert testimony on the general principles of suggestibility and how a child witness should be offered (uncontested), but also on the expert’s analysis of the interview and whether it met interviewing protocols; the testimony was deemed inadmissible by the Black court, which seems to be inconsistent with Middleton.

There seem to be three types of evidence that are consistently considered in this area. The first, the view from 10,000 feet, i.e., general informational testimony concerning pertinent psychological issues such as false memories, including source confusion, contamination, and suggestibility; recantation of reports of abuse; inconsistent or delayed reporting of abuse; and child interviewing protocols are generally deemed admissible.

The second, on the opposite end, are things not admissible—case-specific application of the principles and analysis to the instant case, i.e., absent physical findings and no medical diagnosis of abuse, complainant did or did not show signs of deception, complainant was not capable of deception, the memory was false, or true.

The third is the middle ground, which is a case-specific analysis of how the general concepts might be applied to a given case. The latter was at issue in Middleton (allowed testimony that recantation and inconsistent statements are typical of persons suffering abuse) and Black (analysis of interview in light of protocols not allowed), and this writer suggests that this area will continue to confound the trial courts, the lawyers, and the experts on the stand, and Black should be reversed.

Part of the problem in Black is the manner in which the court viewed “connecting the dots” and did not draw a very nuanced distinction between the ultimate and penultimate questions. In Black, the expert, Dr. C. Kirk Johnson, testified about specific questioning and how they comport with protocols, not that, e.g., “this child testified as to false memories,” or that such questions would necessarily lead to unreliable evidence.

There is a second problem in Black that also seems inconsistent with Middleton: specifically, the notion that Dr. Johnson’s testimony would not provide information sufficiently beyond the ordinary experiences of jurors. Id. at 264. How can it be that general testimony, i.e., the view from 10,000 feet, is admissible and helpful, but the application of these principles by an experienced psychologist’s analysis is not? This author suggests that if deemed inadmissible in your case, a detailed offer of proof is warranted.18 It is possible that a Supreme Court decision in Black will provide further guidance.

Practice Tip

Strategically, you may wish to consider posing “hypotheticals” to your expert that integrate key case facts—a tactic that may implicitly get key expert opinions to the jury but, if cautiously structured, avoid “connecting the dots.” Be cautious, as judges vary considerably in their rulings depending on a variety of factors, including how close and how transparently the hypothetical is posed.

Fourth, you may encounter objections to your memory expert discussing specific studies or statistical information from which inferences may be drawn as “unfairly influential” or as inadmissible hearsay. The key to admission is finding the right “fit” and articulating the “fit” carefully in light of the proposition, opinion, or conclusion for which it is offered.

In State v Clark, the state moved to prevent defendant’s memory expert from testifying as to studies’ statistics, arguing on appeal that “statistical evidence is precisely the sort of evidence that can unfairly influence a factfinder.” 284 Or App at 211, n8. While the Court of Appeals did not reach the merits as defendant’s arguments were unpreserved, in dicta it did reject the state’s proposed approach:

[O]ur case law indicates that there is no such categorical rule that excludes statistical evidence as unduly prejudicial under OEC 403. See, e.g., State v. Futch, 123 Or App 176, 190, 860 P2d 264 (1993), aff’d, 324 Or 297, 924 P2d 832 (1996) (statistical evidence for DNA testing was not unduly prejudicial and, thus, admissible); [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=15776145213761132552&q=Plemel+v.+Walter,+303+Or+262&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38' Plemel v. Walter], 303 Or 262, 735 P2d 1209 (1987) (setting out how statistical evidence and probabilities should be presented in paternity disputes).

Clark, at 211, n8.

In State v. Thomas, 279 Or App 98, 107 (2016), the state argued that Professor Daniel Reisberg’s testimony relating the specific details of a memory study constituted impermissible hearsay, the trial court agreed and the Court of Appeals affirmed on other grounds as there was a framing problem. In Thomas the defense asked Reisberg to opine on how “traumatic events are clearly remembered by children,” id. at 107. The defense argued that details of a study where children very accurately recalled a particularly invasive medical procedure supported this proposition, thus was admissible under OEC 703 as the basis of his opinion. The Court of Appeals carefully noted that at the hearing Reisberg was not asked to draw any conclusions based on the study and defense counsel failed to identify any opinions based on the medical procedure, thus the trial court did not err in excluding details “under those circumstances.” Id. at 108. Still, Thomas is helpful as it suggests that an expert is allowed to discuss studies as long as they are not offered for their truth and for the limited purpose of explaining how the expert has arrived at their conclusions. See Thomas, at 107 n2. Potentially, the studies too could be offered to allow the jury to assess the expert’s opinion. Id.

Prosecutors routinely make this argument based upon Oregon Evidence, Kirkpatrick, L. (6th ed. 2017), at 677 (“Evidence should not be admitted for its truth merely because it was reasonably relied upon as basis for an expert’s opinion”), dropping the crucial “for their truth,” so this is an important argument to anticipate.

8. Jury Instructions

Remember, Lawson suggested one remedy is a case-specific jury instruction and invited the use of the instructions crafted in State v. Henderson, 27 A3d 872 (2011). There is growing legal scholarship on this topic and, sadly, an accumulation of research suggesting those instructions really don’t help much. Still, worth considering.

While many judges show some reluctance to deviate from the UCrJIs, pragmatically the less controversial the instruction is, the more likely you may get it, or perhaps build error into the case. In addition, at least one of the UCrJIs reflects considerations that can be characterized at a minimum as sensitive to concerns raised in the memory research, namely UCrJI No. 1024, Defendant’s Statements.19

U.S. District Judge Michael Bennett of the Northern District of Iowa, in an essay20 appearing in Duke Law School’s Judicature publication [a summary version of his 2015 law review article, “Unspringing the Witness Memory and Demeanor Trap: What Every Judge and Juror Needs to Know About Cognitive Psychology and Witness Credibility,” 64 Am. U.L. Rev. 1331 (2015) can be found here] suggests our current jury instructions are woefully out of date and all too often cases turn on the credibility and demeanor of witnesses. He argues that the jury should be instructed in light of current science-based understanding of credibility, and thus, deals with memory research in a thoughtful fashion. His suggested instruction is attached as Appendix E, and might be a great launching point.
