Defending Sex Cases

Defending Sex Cases: Strategies from Allegation to Resolution, 2018 edition

Chapter 12. Demurrers and Motions

By Matthew McHenry

I. Introduction

Motion practice is a crucial component of any vigorous defense. While several of the concepts and arguments herein apply across a broad array of cases, defense practitioners have found them particularly useful in litigating pretrial motions in sex crime cases. That these motions are not always (or even often) granted should not deter defense counsel from filing and litigating them.

Information gathered through investigation and a thorough review of discovery, in consultation with appropriate experts, can be used to file motions which frame and press the defense theory, block unfavorable evidence, and admit favorable evidence.

The defense bar should push trial judges to think critically about the importance of the defense function, their role as gatekeeper, and their responsibility to ensure the jury is not impermissibly or unfairly prejudiced. Be creative—favorable appellate law is grounded in innovative arguments made at the trial level.

II. Discovery Motions

Do not rely on the state meeting its discovery obligations to provide you with all relevant discovery. Police reports and child abuse center assessments may not reveal critical information about the complaining witness, such as a history of lying, mental health issues, or a pattern of sexual behavior relevant to your defense. Be proactive in filing motions for discovery and subpoenaing evidence under Oregon’s statutory scheme as well as your client’s rights under Brady v. Maryland, 373 US 83 (1963). Defense counsel should, for example, seek copies of text messages and other forensic data downloaded from the complaining witness’s digital devices that may contain information relevant to the witness’s background or the allegations. All discovery motions made under Brady should be factual and specific, and can be supported through proffered testimony of investigators, family members, etc.

Defense counsel should seek in camera review of a complaining witness’s counseling records, if any. Records of counseling sessions pertinent to the alleged conduct can be particularly valuable. Such records contain information and details about the allegations not provided elsewhere. They may reveal inconsistencies, or show how a complaining witness’s account has developed over
time, perhaps due to suggestive interviewing techniques or other outside influence. Such records may contain information about mental health issues, developmental disabilities, or other traits
that could affect a person’s ability to accurately perceive, recall, and report events. Counseling records, when viewed by an expert, can provide a better understanding of the complaining wit-ness, both as a person and as a witness.

Counseling records are generally protected by privilege under the Oregon Evidence Code (Rule 504). The privilege can be overridden, however, by your client’s constitutional right to produce evidence and present a defense, and at the very least an in camera review by a judge for information relevant to the defense will be obtainable in many cases. See, e.g., State v. Lammi, 278 Or App 690, 375 P3d 547 (Lammi I), adh’d to as clarified, 281 Or App 96, 380 P3d 1257 (2016) (Lammi II ), rev. den., 360 Or 697, 388 P3d 710 (2017) (defendant entitled to in camera review of complaining witness’s otherwise privileged counseling records upon demonstration of reasonable basis to believe records contain exculpatory evidence); see State v. Covington, 291 Or App 514, ___ P3d ___ (2018). In crafting motions for and proffering evidence in support of a request for in camera review, focus on why the records you seek are material and helpful to the defense. Under Lammi and Covington, you have a two-step threshold: “the defendant must first ‘demonstrate that the items of which he sought review would have been material and favorable to his defense.’ ” State v. Cockrell, 284 Or App 674, 689, 395 P3d 612, rev. den., 361 Or 886, 403 P3d 772 (2017) (alterations omitted). Once the defendant establishes that the evidence he seeks in the record meets that standard, he must show it is reasonable to believe the documents records sought contain that evidence. Covington, 291 Or App at 517.

Practice Tip: Concepts to Obtain Sensitive/Confidential Records

When arguing to obtain such records, important considerations include whether the records contain:

  • information that the complaining witness has a mental illness that can affect perception, memory, or accurate recounting

  • information regarding other assaults or sexual activity alleged by the complaining witness, which could support a motion to introduce prior false accusations (see below) or, alternatively, could be the source of the complaining witness’s knowledge of sexual behavior

  • the identity of other possible perpetrators who may be responsible for the abuse

  • information about suggestive interviewing techniques, inconsistent statements, or witness coaching.

Obtaining an in camera inspection from the court is only the first step. Equally important is educating the judge (who in all likelihood has never been a criminal defense attorney) about the materials you seek and why they are critical to your defense—be prepared to articulate your theory of relevance and admissibility for the materials. If you are concerned about prematurely disclosing defense strategy to the other side, seek an ex parte hearing on your need for the records. For additional procedural guidance on obtaining in camera review of such records, review State v. Reed, 173 Or App 185, 197-201 (2001). Under Reed, a defendant is entitled to in camera review of records if the defendant demonstrates that such a review “might yield” evidence subject to disclosure. Id. at 197. To satisfy the “might yield” standard, the defendant must provide the court with evidence that would support a reasonable belief that something in the records might be subject to disclosure (such as exculpatory evidence). See also Lammi, 278 Or App at 694 (noting that Reed “explains what standard a defendant . . . must satisfy to obtain an in camera review of the counseling records); See Covington, supra setting forth the two step test from Lammi.

School records are an important investigative lead as well, as they can lead to evidence of motive, reactions to peer pressure, disciplinary actions related to dishonesty, and potential witnesses. They can also expose whether there had been recent behavioral changes (or the absence of any behavioral changes, which could also be helpful to the defense).

III. Severance

Severing counts can provide multiple benefits. Severing counts means severing trials, which leads to more opportunities for problems to arise in the state’s case(s). In addition, damaging evidence relevant only to severed counts can be excluded from a trial on the non-severed counts. Severed counts also allow a defendant to present inconsistent defenses in separate trials without alienating the jury. Of course, even if the motion is unsuccessful, the defense has created an issue for appeal.

A. Law of Severance

The process for severing counts is governed by ORS 132.560:

Joinder of counts and charges; consolidation of charging instruments.

(1) A charging instrument must charge but one offense, and in one form only, except that:

(a) Where the offense may be committed by the use of different means, the charging instrument may allege the means in the alternative.

(b) Two or more offenses may be charged in the same charging instrument in a separate count for each offense if the offenses charged are alleged to have been committed by the same person or persons and are:

(A) Of the same or similar character;

(B) Based on the same act or transaction; or

(C) Based on two or more acts or transactions connected together or constituting parts of a common scheme or plan.

(2) If two or more charging instruments are found in circumstances described in subsection (1)(b) of this section, the court may order them to be consolidated.

(3) If it appears, upon motion, that the state or defendant is substantially prejudiced by a joinder of offenses under subsection (1) or (2) of this section, the court may order an election or separate trials of counts or provide whatever other relief justice requires.

Note that a defendant wishing to sever counts only needs to show “substantial prejudice” if the counts are already joined properly under subsections (1) or (2). If the counts are improperly joined to begin with, as has historically been true in many charging instruments, “substantial prejudice” is not required. State v. Crummett, 274 Or App 618, 620 (2015) (“Once charges are properly joined in a single charging instrument, either party may move to sever the charges if the joinder will ‘substantially prejudice[]’ the party”) (citing ORS 132.560(3)) (emphasis added).

To avoid the high bar of the “substantial prejudice” standard, defendants should argue for severance on the grounds that joinder of the charges is impermissible under the statutory criteria. While a traditional motion to sever may be filed, severance can also be accomplished through a demurrer based on State v. Poston, 277 Or App 137 (2016), adhered to on recons. 285 Or App 750 (2017); see also State v. Page, 290 Or App 562, WL 1083431 (2018). A demurrer may be a better vehicle than a traditional motion to sever because it is aimed at “the face” of the charging instrument, thus the state is not permitted to introduce extrinsic evidence in an attempt to save the deficiencies. ORS 135.630. The court cannot examine anything outside the four corners of the indictment. State v. Cervantes, 232 Or App 567, 573 (2009) (citing cases).

A defendant may demur to an indictment if it does not “substantially conform to the requirements of ORS 132.510 to ORS 132.560 . . .” ORS 135.630(2) (emphasis added). ORS 132.560 is the joinder statute discussed above. Thus, if the charges are impermissibly joined under ORS 132.560, the demurrer should be allowed and the indictment dismissed—the equivalent of a successful motion to sever counts, and if the state chooses to reissue the charges, it must do so under separate charging instruments.

When joining charges, the state must allege in the indictment the basis for the joinder. Poston, 277 Or App at 144–45 (error to deny demurrer where state failed to “allege in the charging instrument the basis for the joinder of the crimes that are charged in it, whether by alleging the basis for joinder in the language of the joinder statute or by alleging facts sufficient to establish compliance with the joinder statute”). Under the joinder statute if any of the charges are not of the “same” or “similar” character to others, those charges are improperly joined. Likewise, the charges are improperly joined if the indictment fails to allege either that the crimes occurred as part of the same criminal episode, or that the charges are part of the same scheme or plan. ORS 132.560(1)(b).

1. Crimes of the Same or Similar Character

Because the Oregon legislature and appellate courts have offered little guidance in determining whether crimes are of the “same or similar character,” defense counsel can and should be creative in arguing they are not. “Similar character” means “[n]early corresponding; resembling in many respects; somewhat alike; having a general likeness.” State v. Meyer, 109 Or App 598, 602 (1991). In arguing crimes are not of similar character, focus on the differences in the elements, including the required mental state for each charge. Other areas ripe for argument against joinder are differences in possible or likely sentences, or differences in the nature of the victims (age, or images vs. contact offenses, for example). Urge trial judges not to accept vague arguments by the state. “Similar” must mean something—it is a restrictive term. Not all crimes can be joined—they must be of similar character. A prosecution argument, for example, that “all sex crimes” are of similar character divests the phrase “similar character” of any meaning. See, e.g., State v. Johnson, 199 Or App 305 (2005) (rejecting state’s argument that joinder of marijuana and methamphetamine charges was proper because both involved “drug trade” as too generalized). Unless the charges allege the same crime and the same victim, arguments can be made that the charges are not of the “same” or “similar” character.

2. Two or More Acts or Transactions Connected Together or Constituting Parts of a Common Scheme or Plan

The legislature and the courts have similarly provided vague guidance on the meanings of “same act or transaction” and “two or more acts or transactions connected together constituting a common scheme or plan.” State v. Witherspoon restated and summarized the existing case law on the subject. 250 Or App 316 (2012). A key component of the analysis is “whether a complete account of one crime necessarily includes details of the other, or, framed another way, whether the crimes are ‘cross-related.’” Id. at 322 (quoting State v. Potter, 236 Or App 74, 82-83 (2010)). Crimes are not cross-related where one of the crimes may be proven without evidence of the other. Id.

The Oregon Supreme Court has held that “same criminal episode” from ORS 131.505(4) has the same meaning as “same transaction” in ORS 132.560(1)(b). State v. Boyd, 271 Or 558, 565-66 (1975) (en banc). ORS 131.505(4) defines “criminal episode” as “continuous and uninterrupted conduct that establishes at least one offense and is so joined in time, place and circumstances that such conduct is directed to the accomplishment of a single criminal objective.” Defense counsel should review Justice Walters’s concurrence in State v. Campbell, 354 Or 375 (2013). Writing in support of the court’s decision to dismiss a petition for review, and after reviewing the Commentary to the criminal code regarding “single criminal objective,” Justice Walter wrote:

What I take from those examples is that, to be directed to the accomplishment of a single criminal objective, the two offenses need not be similar, need not have the same elements, need not be proved by the same evidence, and need not share the same statutory intent. Rather, they may be as dissimilar as robbery and murder or as robbery and rape. It also appears that a “single criminal objective” is not a narrow concept: Two or more offenses may be directed toward more than one criminal objective and still be part of the same criminal episode, as long as they reasonably can be seen to be directed toward a single overarching criminal objective. Those examples from the Commentary are helpful in assessing how closely distinct criminal events must be connected in time and place before they can be said to share a criminal objective.

Id. at 379.

State v. Johnson, 199 Or App 305 (2005), is also useful. Johnson confirmed that to be part of the same act or transaction, or common scheme or plan, the crimes must be both logically related and there must be large amounts of “overlapping proof.” Id. at 317-18. Too often, trial courts focus on the “logically related” prong while overlooking the “large amount of overlapping proof” requirement.

The Johnson defendant was involved in marijuana manufacturing, which led to his affiliation with methamphetamine manufacturers, which led to a methamphetamine-related robbery. Id. at 316. The trial court allowed the joinder of the methamphetamine manufacturing, robbery, and murder charges with a marijuana manufacturing charge because both involved manufacture of “controlled substances.” Id. at 315. The Court of Appeals rejected the state’s argument that joinder was proper because the charges were both related to the “drug trade.” Id. at 316. That generalized argument “is best appreciated at high altitudes” and “would stretch the meaning of the phrases ‘connected together’ or ‘common scheme or plan’ to a level of generality that equates the expansive standard for relevance in OEC 401 . . . with the connectedness between offenses required in ORS 132.560(1)(b)(C), rendering almost useless the tests in subparagraphs (1)(b)(A) and (1)(b)(B).” Id. Rather, the overlapping evidence for the two charges was “very slight,” and “aside from the attenuated relationship between defendant’s drug manufacturing and the state’s theory concerning his motive to rob . . . there were no interlocking facts that would suggest a logical relationship between the two offenses.” Id. at 317-18. See also State v. Dewhitt, 276 Or App 373, 380-87 (2016) (discussing joinder statute and analysis under Johnson). As with the “same” or “similar” character standard, be thoughtful—if your client faces different charges with different victims, press your arguments that the charges do not stem from the same criminal episode.

IV. Grand Jury Recordings and Motions to Produce Grand Jury Notes and Records

Governor Kate Brown signed Senate Bill 505 into law in September of 2017, which directs certain counties (Multnomah, Deschutes, Jackson) to record grand jury testimony and proceedings. Under the new law, if the grand jury returns a true bill, the district attorney must provide the defense with audio or written transcripts of the proceedings 10 days after the defendant is arraigned. The district attorney may move for a protective order to redact, restrict, or deny access based on various factors such as witness security and secrecy of informants.

It remains to be seen how often prosecutors will move for protective orders or how courts will respond to such motions. In addition, the grand jury recording requirement will not apply statewide until 2019. As such, defense counsel should be prepared to respond to prosecutorial motions to restrict and move for production of grand jury notes and records (insofar as they exist) under the current scheme. If the motion is denied, the defense attorney should move to have the grand jury notes sealed and made part of the record for appellate purposes. Filing the motion can have other beneficial results as well. In one sex abuse case with multiple alleged victims, though the motion was denied, the state responded to it with a letter admitting that at grand jury, one of the complaining witnesses recanted the statements detectives attributed to her during the investigation. Though the state was obligated to produce this information under Brady in any event, it was not until the motion for grand jury notes was filed that the state acted on its obligation.

While ORS 135.855 exempts from discovery “[t]ranscripts, recordings, or memoranda of testimony of witnesses before the grand jury,” due process dictates that in certain situations, records of a grand jury witness’s testimony should be disclosed to the defense. ORS 132.220 expressly provides that a court may require disclosure of “[t]he testimony of a witness examined before the grand jury, for the purpose of ascertaining whether it is consistent with that given by the witness before the court,” and also “[t]he testimony given before such grand jury by any person, upon a charge against such person for perjury or false swearing or upon trial therefore.”

Brady and the United States Constitution present the strongest arguments to obtain grand jury records. Disclosure of grand jury testimony may be constitutionally compelled. State v. Cockrell, 284 Or App 674 (2017). The state must disclose grand jury testimony that is favorable to the defendant. Brady v. Maryland, 373 US 83 (1963). ORS 153.055(2) defines “material” as that which can affect the course or outcome of any proceeding or transaction. A false statement need not bear on an ultimate fact in dispute to be material. State v. Ray, 36 Or App 375, 378-79 (1978). See also Brady, 375 US at 87 (“suppression by the prosecution of evidence favorable to an accused upon request violates due process where the evidence is material either to guilt or to punishment, irrespective of the good faith or bad faith of the prosecution”); State v. McDonnell, 313 Or 478, 500 (1992) (en banc) (state has affirmative due process obligation to produce exculpatory evidence to defense).

The most common ground for production of grand jury records is inconsistent statements by grand jury witnesses. If, for example, defense counsel learns a witness testified before grand jury in a manner inconsistent with statements made to law enforcement, the defendant is entitled to the grand jury records. Likewise, if the complaining witness’s statements to law enforcement are inconsistent with statements made during a child abuse assessment interview (such as CARES), it necessarily follows that any testimony given to the grand jury is inconsistent with at least some of their prior statements, thus entitling the defendant to the materials.

A credible motion for grand jury records should at least result in a judge conducting an in camera review of any grand jury notes and records, looking for exculpatory information, including impeachment material. As with a request for in camera review of counseling records, the defense must produce “evidence sufficient to support a reasonable belief that in camera review might yield” relevant evidence. State v. Bray, 281 Or App 584, 616 (2016). Defense counsel should be prepared to proffer testimonial and documentary evidence where available to support counsel’s belief that grand jury notes “might yield” exculpatory evidence.

V. Carbon Copy Counts and Forcing the State to Elect Differentiating Theories

In many sex crime cases, particularly those involving minors as complaining witnesses, the state charges our clients with multiple identical counts—same alleged act, same time period—against the same victim. At times the state differentiates counts by designating them as a “separate act or transaction” from the other counts, but even in that situation they are still otherwise exact copies of each other. For example, an indictment may contain multiple charges stating:

That as a separate act and transaction from [each of the other counts]: The defendant, on or between September 1, 2013 and October 1, 2015, in Washington County, Oregon, did unlawfully and knowingly subject [the victim], a child under 14 years of age, to sexual contact by touching the victim’s genitalia, a sexual and intimate part of the child.

Federal case law supports arguments that carbon-copy indictments violate due process by failing to provide sufficient notice of the alleged offense and also implicating double-jeopardy concerns. The grand jury’s role is compromised when it is unclear whether conduct a grand jury found to support a charge is the same conduct a petit jury considers when deciding on convictions. Until recently, upon defense motion a court would either require the state to “elect” a theory on each count at the close of its case in chief (assigning specific differentiating conduct or circumstances to each carbon-copy count), or would deliver a “concurrence” instruction to the jury (instructing the jury that in order to convict on any count, the jurors must all agree on the same act or transaction underlying the count). See State v. Houston, 147 Or App 285, 292 (1997).

The election process clearly provided an unfair advantage for prosecutors—it allowed the state to tailor the charges to conform to the evidence as it came in at trial. This could lead to the presentation of evidence that might otherwise be inadmissible as irrelevant or improper character evidence. It also creates enormous difficulties in formulating and presenting a defense theory if the prosecutor’s theory is not fully developed until the close of the state’s case.

In 2015, however, the Oregon Court of Appeals provided a way to attack these indictments and force the state to provide differentiating details for each count before the trial begins. State v. Antoine, 269 Or App 66 (2015). Antoine recognizes that carbon copy counts do not provide sufficient notice of the charges under the federal and state constitutions. Id. at 75-76. The operative facts in Antoine are familiar. The defendant was charged with four counts of sodomy and four counts of first-degree sexual abuse. Each count alleged the crimes occurred during the same two-year period using the wording of the relevant statute. Each count was indistinguishable from the other counts of the same crime. The defendant demurred to the charging instrument, challenging the lack of specificity in the charges. Id. at 68-70. Specifically, the defendant argued the indictment lacked the notice necessary to defend himself, created a risk of double jeopardy, and failed to ensure he was being tried only for those acts for which the grand jury indicted. Id. at 68.

In response to the demurrer, the state agreed to elect what specific acts constituted each count after the close of the state’s case in chief. The defendant did not object to the prosecutor’s proposal, and the trial court overruled the demurrer. At the close of the state’s case in chief, the prosecutor ascribed specific acts to each of the carbon-copy counts, and the judge tailored the jury instructions accordingly. The defendant was convicted.

On appeal, Antoine held that carbon-copy indictments were impermissible. Such indictments fail to provide defendants “with sufficient notice, and the problem with inadequate notice, which arose before trial, was not cured during trial.” Id. at 76. By waiting until the close of its case in chief before electing the specific criminal acts it was prosecuting, “the state’s charging method effectively allowed the state to adduce evidence of multiple criminal acts in each count of the indictment, without defendant knowing which of the acts would be specified and argued to the jury for convictions. Such a charging process failed to provide defendant with proper notice of the charges before trial.” Id. at 77.

The court also raised concerns that the mid-trial election process undermines efforts by Oregon courts to prevent “other acts” evidence from being introduced in contravention of OEC 404(3), which instructs that certain evidence is “not admissible to prove the character of a person in order to show the person acted in conformity therewith.” Antoine noted that if carbon-copy counts are allowed, a trial court “is powerless to sustain objections to ‘other acts’ evidence under 404(3) because the court does not know what charges are being prosecuted.” Id. at 78.

Practice Tip: The Defendant Must Object to the State’s Offer to Elect After Its Case In Chief

Despite the lack of notice, Antoine upheld the defendant’s convictions. This was because though the defendant filed a demurrer, he did not specifically object to the prosecutor’s offer to elect at the close of the state’s case in chief. Antoine noted the burden is on the defense to attempt to obtain notice of the charges. The defense “could have moved to discover the state’s election of the specific criminal acts . . . in time for defendant to tailor his defense to those specific incidents.” Id. at 79. The state “put defendant on notice at the hearing on the demurrer that it would make its election after it presented its case in chief. Defendant did not object to the timing of the election.” Id. at 71 (emphasis in original).

The upshot of Antoine is clear. First, carbon-copy indictments fail to provide the defense
with adequate notice necessary to defend against the charges and are subject to demurrer. Second, a prosecutor’s election of facts to assign each charge after its case in chief is impermissible. Third, however, is that the defendant must take affirmative steps to obtain adequate notice if possible.

The defendant should move for election of counts early—way before trial—and must object to any attempt by the state to elect its theories after trial has begun.

Practice Tip: Use Antoine to Obtain Grand Jury Records

Antoine also provides support for obtaining grand jury records. Recall that under Antoine, the burden is on the defendant to attempt to procure adequate and timely notice of charges even when the indictment does not. Upon the state’s pretrial election of its theories, the defense should move, or move again, for grand jury notes, arguing that in light of the election, the testimony presented to grand jury must be inconsistent with the election and/or prior statements of the complaining witness and thus constitutes impeachment evidence, or that the election was inconsistent with the charges the grand jury intended.

Practice Tip: Use Antoine to Obtain Judgments of Acquittal

Forcing the state to elect prior to trial should also lead to more judgments of acquittal. In sex cases, the testimony of the complaining witnesses—especially young complaining witnesses—is nearly always at some odds with the police reports. Prior to Antoine, judgments of acquittal were particularly difficult to obtain in these cases, because the state was free to shift the theory of any individual count to conform to the testimony. Post-Antoine, however, pretrial election by the state precludes the state from doing so. Now, those variances between police reports, charges, and trial testimony should lead to more judgments of acquittal on specific counts as the state is required to provide more detail.

VI. Motion to Introduce Evidence of the Complaining Witness’s Prior Sexual Behavior

Oregon Evidence Rule 412 prohibits evidence on a complaining witness’s past sexual behavior, except in certain situations. Where available and appropriate, defense counsel should focus on those exceptions and move to admit evidence of a complaining witness’s past sexual conduct under OEC 412(b). These motions do more than allow the introduction of critical defense evidence at the trial. A properly litigated motion under Rule 412 can put enormous pressure on the state and the complaining witness. Often, when it becomes apparent that a complaining witness’s sexual history will be covered extensively at trial through cross-examination of the complaining witness as well as extrinsic evidence, pretrial negotiations turn favorably for the defense.

Practice Tip: File at Least 15 Days Before Trial, and Include a Written Offer of Proof

In moving to introduce such evidence under Rule 412, be aware of the rule’s filing deadline of 15 days before the trial date (“not later than 15 days before the date on which the trial in which the evidence offered is scheduled to begin”), and the requirement that such a motion be accompanied by a written offer of proof. OEC 412(4).

OEC 412(2)(b) provides that, in a prosecution for certain sex offenses, “evidence of a victim’s past sexual behavior other than reputation or opinion evidence” is admissible if the evidence “[r]elates to the motive or bias of the alleged victim, is necessary to rebut or explain scientific or medical evidence offered by the state, or is otherwise constitutionally required to be admitted.”

While the need to rebut or explain scientific or medical evidence offered by the state is clearly case-specific, the other two options—evidence that “relates to the motive or bias of the alleged victim” or evidence that is “otherwise constitutionally required”—should be at play in most, if not all, cases where the defense can obtain information about past sexual behavior. For example, a teenager’s sexual relationship with another person could explain why the allegations against your client are false or mistaken. Other possible theories of admissibility include:

  • prior sexual experiences or viewing of sexual activity in person or through pornography that would explain a complaining witness’s knowledge of sexual practices and behaviors

  • prior consensual sexual conduct for which a complaining witness was punished or embarrassed, to explain the motive for false accusations of non-consensual assault

  • prior sexual abuse at the hands of another individual

Practice Tip

Be expansive with your required written offer of proof in support of your motion under OEC 412. This is an opportunity to frame your case and press your theory of defense on the court, which can lead to favorable rulings on other evidentiary issues. Use investigation reports and witness statements to support and articulate why the evidence of prior sexual behavior must be admitted.

VII. Motion to Pre-Admit Exhibits

In many cases it makes sense to move the court to pre-admit your exhibits (and the state’s exhibits as quid pro quo). Preadmission of exhibits allows the defense to reference and display these exhibits during opening statements, and also saves time and interruptions to the flow of a direct or cross-examination by avoiding the need to establish foundation or move to have the exhibits admitted then.

However, the defense must be vigilant about potential challenges to preadmission of state exhibits. The relevance and admissibility of exhibits can be established through proffer, but be sure that each exhibit in the state’s requested list for preadmission is addressed individually. In one post-conviction case, for example, defense counsel’s competency was challenged due to his failure to examine the state’s packet of exhibits for which it sought preadmission. One exhibit (a pamphlet aimed at children discussing the danger of child predators) was pre-admitted without objection, but the state never established relevance or otherwise referred to the pamphlet at trial. Nevertheless, this pamphlet went into the jury room and, with no frame of reference for the jury, likely infected the deliberations unfairly.

VIII. Motion to Prohibit Use of the Term “Victim”

Defense attorneys should be conscious of any conduct by the state or the court that impermissibly skews the jury’s view of the case or the defendant. For example, prosecutors and judges often refer to the complaining witness as a “victim” throughout trial proceedings before a jury. The defense should argue that referring to the complaining witness as a “victim” violates due process and should move the court to prohibit the state from doing so.

The term “victim” refers to a person who has been injured as the result of criminal act. References to the accuser as a “victim” necessarily conveys the speaker’s opinion that a crime in fact occurred, and evinces a bias against the defendant in violation of his presumption of innocence. See, e.g., Jackson v. State, 600 A2d 21, 24 (Del. 1991) (“the word ‘victim’ should not be used in a case where the commission of a crime is in dispute”).

Referring to an accuser as a “victim” also violates the proscription against a prosecutor expressing personal beliefs about the credibility of a witness. This is particularly dangerous in classic “swearing contest” cases where the credibility of a complaining witness is crucial to the jury’s deliberations. A prosecutor calling a complaining witness a “victim” equates to an assertion that the complaining witness is truthful, and is thus an impermissible comment
on credibility.

Defense counsel should file similar motions to prohibit the government from referring to the complaining witness’s statements as “disclosures.” A “disclosure” refers to a revelation of fact. As such, labeling a statement as a “disclosure” conveys that the statement is not an assertion of opinion, but rather an assertion of fact. Labeling a statement as a “disclosure” insinuates that the statement has already been established as fact.

Other motions can be made on the same general principles of impermissibly infecting the jury’s deliberations. If, for example, a prosecutor has a habit of placing a copy of the defendant’s mug shot on the outside of her trial notebook, a motion should be made. Certain posters, art, and décor in a courtroom, courthouse, or curtilage of a courthouse could be subject to a motion—an informational poster regarding reporting child abuse, for example. The juror assembly room in Multnomah County used to play reruns of Law and Order on the televisions until a defense attorney pointed out the negative view that the show presents of defendants and defense attorneys. Impermissible intrusions into a jury’s deliberations can be everywhere—be creative in your motions.

IX. Motion for Juror Questionnaires

Trials can be won or lost during jury selection. More than any other type of charge, allegations of sex crimes invite significant biases and prejudices, and can likewise evoke deeply personal and visceral reactions in the members of the jury pool. Because a fair and unbiased jury is critical in all cases, every effort should be made to obtain as much information as possible about the prospective jurors. Jury questionnaires and sequestration for questioning if necessary are two ways to obtain that information. Though not a right of the defendant, persuasive case law supports
the procedures.

It is well settled that voir dire plays “a critical function in assuring the criminal defendant that his Sixth Amendment right to an impartial jury will be honored.” Rosales-Lopez v. United States, 451 U.S. 182, 188 (1981). Indeed, “part of the guaranty of the defendant’s right to an impartial jury is an adequate voir dire to identify unqualified jurors.” Morgan v. Illinois, 504 U.S. 719, 729 (1992). Without a searching voir dire, the trial judge cannot fulfill the “responsibility to remove prospective jurors who will not be able impartially to follow the court’s instructions and evaluate the evidence.” Rosales-Lopez, 451 U.S. at 188. See also Dyer v. Calderon, 151 F3d 970, 973 (9th Cir. 1998) (en banc) (“one important mechanism for ensuring impartiality is voir dire, which enables the parties to probe potential jurors for prejudice”). Questions of the venire must probe the jurors’ beliefs and attitudes about the law because “the bias or prejudice of even a single juror would violate [the defendant’s] right to a fair trial.” Dyer, 151 F3d at 973. “The presence of a biased juror cannot be harmless [and] such error requires a new trial without a showing of actual prejudice.” Id. at n.2.

As stated by the Oregon courts, “The purpose of voir dire is to ascertain the existence of grounds for a challenge for cause and to enable litigants to obtain enough information to make an intelligent decision whether to exercise a peremptory challenge.” State v. Williams, 123 Or App 546, 550-551 (1993). In sex cases, in particular, “the trial court must be zealous to protect the rights of the accused.” Dennis v. United States, 339 U.S. 162, 168 (1950).

Although the scope of voir dire examination by the attorneys is in the trial court’s discretionary power to efficiently and expeditiously conduct the trial, the court cannot “prohibit counsel from seeking information about prospective jurors which is obviously relevant.” State v. Barnett, 251 Or 234, 237-38 (1968); See also Williams, 123 Or App at 551 (1993) (in drug case court abused its discretion in limiting defense counsel’s voir dire to 44 minutes). This is true in part because “jurors are reluctant to admit actual bias, and the reality of their biased attitudes must be revealed by circumstantial evidence.” United States v. Gonzalez, 214 F3d 1109, 1111 (9th Cir. 2000).

Questionnaires are particularly appropriate when volatile and sensitive issues will arise at trial. See, e.g., State v. Compton, 333 Or 274, 284 (2002) (questionnaire used in case of murder and sex abuse). Further, where circumstances raise questions as to a juror’s impartiality, “little stock need be placed in jurors’ claims” that they will not be prejudiced.” Dyas v. Poole, 309 F3d 586, 589 (9th Cir. 2002) (quoting Holbrook v. Flynn, 475 U.S. 560, 570 (1986)). See also United States v. Egbuniwe, 969 F.2d 757, 762 (9th Cir. 1992) (“juror’s assertion that he or she can reach a fair and impartial verdict is not dispositive”).

Argue to the trial court that in sex crime cases, a jury questionnaire followed by in camera questioning on certain topics is a fair, efficient, and effective way to help identify the extent of the potential jurors’ prejudices and biases. This method avoids contaminating other members of the panel, and will aid the exercise of challenges, whether peremptory or for cause.

Practice Tip: In camera Questioning of Jurors

After the jury questionnaires are answered, counsel should ask the court for the opportunity to question, individually and in camera, any jurors who provide an answer that suggests prior knowledge of the case, an emotional reaction they cannot put aside, or negative experiences with conduct similar to the charged conduct. This will encourage truthful and complete disclosure by the juror and also avoids contamination of the other prospective jurors.

X. Motion to Exclude Lay Opinion

Experts are not the only trial witnesses who attempt to offer opinions as evidence. Defense counsel must be diligent in objecting to, and moving to exclude impermissible opinion evidence from non-expert witnesses. Various sections of the Oregon Evidence Code and select appellate case law provide tools to exclude damaging lay opinion evidence. Motions to exclude lay opinion testimony can be made in limine and in writing, and the following concepts are applicable to a broad variety of testimonial evidence.

Under OEC 701, a lay witness’s opinion must “be rationally based on the perception of the witness,” and “helpful to a clear understanding of testimony of the witness or the determination of a fact in issue.” ORS 40.405 (emphasis added).

A. Opinion Not Rationally Based on Perception of Witness

OEC 701 allows for lay opinion testimony only when it is grounded in personal knowledge and is rationally based upon the perceptions of the witness, and disallows opinions based upon conjecture or speculation. Defense attorneys should move to exclude any comments from lay witnesses that approach a comment on the credibility of a witness or address the intention of a party. State v. McQuisten, 97 Or App 517 (1989); Ensley v. Fitzwater, 59 Or App 411 (1982).

Admissible lay opinion testimony is based on something visual or otherwise tangibly detectable by the average person. See, e.g., State v. Wright, 315 Or 124, 132 (1992) (testimony an individual was under the influence of alcohol); State v. Lerch, 296 Or 377 (1984) (smell of decaying flesh); Crosby v. Portland Ry. Co., 53 Or 496 (1909) (physical condition of a person). This can extend to intent, motives, and emotions, but only where those emotions are manifested and observable by the witness. State v. Broadhurst, 184 Or 178, 249 (1948) (“[i]t is generally held that a witness may testify as to emotions manifested by another and observed by him”). This is why officers and others may testify about another’s “demeanor.” However, all such opinions require observation of specific expressions or behaviors of an individual that allow a person of common sense to deduce a conclusion about the condition of another. An officer cannot testify to a defendant’s demeanor if the foundation for that testimony is the officer’s intuition, hunch, or something else that cannot be tangibly observed.

In addition, opinion testimony regarding “demeanor” may violate the rule against a comment on the credibility of a witness. Any testimony suggesting an opinion about a witness’s or defendant’s credibility must be excluded. See, e.g., State v. White, 119 Or App 424, 428 (1993) (officer’s testimony that victim’s demeanor is “forthright” is impermissible comment on credibility). Testimony that a witness is “earnest” or “straightforward” is similarly objectionable, as would be testimony that a witness was “evasive” or “hard to pin down.” Defense practitioners can move in limine for cautionary instructions to witnesses to avoid any such dangerous area.

B. Same Considerations to Exclude Expert Witness Testimony

The above concepts are not limited to lay opinion testimony but apply equally to expert opinion testimony. While a full analysis on admissibility of expert testimony under the Brown/O’Key factors is outside the scope of this chapter, defense practitioners should consider attacking expert testimony using the same concepts as lay opinion testimony. While expert testimony is covered by a different rule than lay opinion testimony, to be admissible it still must assist the trier of fact “to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue,” and its probative value must not be substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice. OEC 403, 702. So, for example, an expert cannot testify to a diagnosis of sexual abuse in the absence of physical findings, because such testimony’s probative value is low and the danger of unfair prejudice quite high. State v. Southard, 347 Or 127 (2009) (citing with approval jurisdictions which disallow such testimony because it does not assist the trier of fact). The expert must also be qualified “by knowledge, skill, experience, training, or education.” OEC 702. Expert opinion testimony is also limited by relevance and foundational requirements, and by the prohibition on comments regarding witness credibility. See, e.g., State v. Marrington, 335 Or 555 (2003) (reversing conviction where insufficient foundation for expert testimony that delay in reporting of sex abuse by a child is a prominent feature in child disclosures); State v. Lupoli, 348 Or 346 (2010) (in absence of physical evidence, nurse’s testimony about her diagnosis of sexual abuse based on child’s statements was impermissible comment on credibility).

XI. Motion to Admit Extrinsic Evidence of Prior False Accusations

In many cases there is evidence that a complaining witness has made prior false accusations of sexual misconduct. Such evidence is not covered by OEC 412 as prior false accusations do not constitute prior sexual conduct. Every effort should be made to fully develop those accusations—specific instances of conduct of a complaining witness—before the jury.

OEC 608 ostensibly prohibits extrinsic evidence and cross-examination on specific instances of conduct, even if probative of truthfulness. However, as detailed below, in sex crime cases defense attorneys can cross-examine a complaining witness regarding specific instances of prior false accusations, notwithstanding the prohibitions of OEC 608. Further, defense attorneys should also move the court to allow presentation of extrinsic evidence of prior false accusations where such evidence exists and is otherwise admissible, using the following arguments. Admitting extrinsic evidence of prior false accusations would obviously be critical if, on cross-examination, the complaining witness denied any prior false accusations, as without extrinsic evidence, the defense attorney must simply accept the answer of the witness.

Trial courts have historically disallowed such extrinsic evidence, nearly always because of a misunderstanding of the reasoning underlying the case of State v. Driver, 192 Or App 395 (2004). However, no controlling cases or evidentiary rules forbid the introduction of extrinsic evidence of prior false accusations, and in fact persuasive authority from other jurisdictions support it.

A. Evidence of Prior False Accusations by the Complaining Witness Is Admissible Under OEC 608 and the Confrontation Clauses of the Oregon and United States Constitutions

=

Rule 608 normally prohibits cross-examining a witness on specific instances of conduct when attacking a witness’s credibility. However, the Oregon Court of Appeals has held that in sex crime trials a defendant’s right to confront witnesses under Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution carves out an exception to Rule 608’s prohibition on delving into specific acts on cross-examination. State v. LeClair, 83 Or App 121 (1986). More specifically, where there is evidence of prior false accusations by the complaining witness, the Confrontation Clause “tips the scales” in favor of permitting cross-examination on the prior false accusations. Id. at 129.

The right to cross-examination on the subject of prior false accusations is of particular significance in cases where the critical issue is the credibility of the complaining witness:

Evidence of prior false accusations by a complainant is certainly probative on the issue of credibility. Courts have uniformly stated that, when a complainant has recanted prior accusations or they are otherwise demonstrably false, the trial court must allow the defendant to cross-examine the complaining witness regarding them.

Id. (citing cases).

LeClair identified three situations in which, “regardless of the prohibitions of OEC 608,” the defendant’s confrontation rights “require[] that the court permit a defendant to cross-examine the complaining witness in front of the jury concerning other accusations she has made.” Id. at 130. These situations are if: “1) she has recanted them; 2) the defendant demonstrates to the court that those accusations were false; or 3) there is some evidence that the victim has made prior accusations that were false.” Id. (emphasis added).

Defense counsel should further argue that to allow the full exercise of the right to confrontation under the Oregon and United States constitutions, as well as the right to due process under the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, presentation of extrinsic evidence on prior falsities should be allowed as well. Though State v. Driver, 192 Or App 395 (2004), held that the exception to OEC 608 announced in LeClair is limited to cross-examination of the complaining witness, other jurisdictions have suggested and permitted extrinsic evidence on the issue. See, e.g., Davis v. Alaska, 415 US 308, 316–17 (1974) (distinguishing between general and particular attacks on credibility, and noting that examination of a witness “directed toward revealing possible biases, prejudices, or ulterior motives of the witness as they may relate directly to issues . . . in the case” is a particular attack on credibility and thus entitled to a special protection under the Confrontation Clause); State v. Guenther, 854 A2d 308 (NJ 2004) (holding, in sex abuse case, that where complaining witness denies prior false accusations, “the interests of justice require that we relax the strictures against specific conduct evidence,” and further that if certain standards are met, defendant may introduce extrinsic evidence on the matter); State v. Barber, 766 P2d 1288, 1290 (Kan App 1989) (finding that the defendant’s confrontation rights trumped the evidentiary rule barring specific conduct evidence and holding that “in a sex crime case, the victim/complaining witness may be cross-examined about prior false accusations, and if she denies making those accusations, defendant may put on evidence of those accusations”).

In addition to New Jersey and Kansas, courts in Alaska, Arizona, Arkansas, Georgia, Idaho, Indiana, Louisiana, New Hampshire, Nevada, and Virginia have all held that, in sex crime cases extrinsic evidence of prior false accusations must be permitted to avoid violating the confrontation clauses of the United States and various state constitutions. See, e.g., Morgan v. State, 54 P3d 332, 336 (Alaska Ct App 2002); State v. Hutchinson, 688 P2d 209, 212–13 (Ariz Ct App 1984); West v. State, 719 SW2d 684, 686–87 (Ark 1986); Smith v. State, 377 SE2d 158, 160 (Ga 1989); State v. Schwartzmiller, 685 P2d 830, 833 (Idaho 1984); Little v. State, 413 NE2d 639, 643–44 (Ind Ct App 1980); State v. Smith, 743 So2d 199, 202 (La 1999); State v. Gordon, 770 A2d 702, 704–05 (NH 2001); Miller v. State, 779 P2d 87, 89–90 (Nev 1989); Clinebell v. Commonwealth, 368 SE2d 263, 266 (Va 1988).

Moreover, OEC 613 allows for extrinsic evidence of a prior inconsistent statement by a witness, as long as the witness “is afforded an opportunity to explain or deny the same.” OEC 613(2). In short, if the complaining witness denies the prior false accusations on cross-examination, OEC 613 should permit extrinsic evidence on the matter.

B. Extrinsic Evidence of Prior False Accusations by the Complaining Witness Is Admissible Under OEC 404(3)

In response to the above, the state will almost surely argue that LeClair and Driver prohibit introduction of extrinsic evidence of prior false accusations. However, those cases were not so broad. Rather, LeClair’s holding, affirmed in Driver, only addressed the constitutional, confrontation-based exception to OEC 608. Driver, 192 Or App at 398; LeClair, 83 Or App at 127. Recall LeClair’s holding that in sex cases, a defendant’s constitutional right to confrontation overrides OEC 608, allowing a defendant to cross-examine a complaining witness about prior false accusations. The LeClair holding, however, “does not address extrinsic evidence.” Driver, 192 Or App at 401. Driver did address extrinsic evidence, but narrowly held that extrinsic evidence was not admissible under the specific exception to OEC 608 announced in LeClair. Id. at 401-02 and n.6. Driver did not say extrinsic evidence of prior false accusations was never admissible—only that it was not admissible under the LeClair OEC 608 exception. More specifically, Driver does not address the argument that extrinsic evidence of prior false accusations is admissible under OEC 404(3) and 405 as prior acts of the complaining witness.

Character evidence is permitted under OEC 404. More specifically, extrinsic evidence of specific acts relating to a witness’s character is permitted under OEC 404(3), the subsection covering “Other crimes, wrongs or acts.” Though normally the state takes advantage of this rule to introduce prior bad acts of a defendant, a defendant likewise enjoys the benefits of this rule. See, e.g., State v. Cox, 337 Or 477 (2004) (en banc) (evidence of criminal acts of victim toward persons other than the defendant allowed under OEC 403(3)). See also Laird C. Kirkpatrick, Oregon Evidence § 404.06[10] (6th ed 2013 and 2015 supp). In fact, when the defendant offers evidence under this rule, “the standard for admission is relaxed.” Id. (quoting United States v. Cohen, 888 F2d 770, 776 (11th Cir. 1989). Further, evidence admitted under OEC 404(3) may be presented in the form of specific instances of conduct. OEC 405.

Evidence offered under OEC 404(3) is not limited to evidence related only to “proof of motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or accident,” the situations explicitly listed in the rule. Rather, OEC 404(3) is an inclusionary rule, and the list of purposes set forth in 404(3) “is not meant to be exclusive.” State v. Johns, 301 Or 535, 542 (1986) (en banc) (quoting the legislative commentary to Rule 404). Oregon has “adopted the inclusionary approach,” and as such Oregon judges may “resort to any theory of logical relevance when ruling on ‘prior crime’ evidence that does not run afoul of the ‘propensity to commit crimes or other acts’ prohibition.” Id. at 548.

In People v. Hurlburt, 333 P2d 82 (Cal App 1958), the California Court of Appeals examined the issue. That court noted that in sex abuse cases, where the crucial issue is the credibility of the complaining witness and the defendant, evidence of prior falsities is not offered merely for the purpose of impeaching or discrediting the general character or reputation of the witness. Rather, it

is offered to disprove the very charge before the court. It is relevant as to the state of mind of the prosecutrix. Because of its high degree of relevancy and its high probative value, such evidence should be admitted.

Id. at 87.

Most other jurisdictions that have examined the issue have followed Hurlburt. For example, the Supreme Court of Montana noted that, though excluded by Rule 608(b) for credibility purposes, such evidence

may be admissible if probative of the witness’ state of mind, motive, or biases with respect to making the more current accusations. The principle expressed in Hurlburt—that evidence of prior false charges may often be probative of the complaining witness’ specific reputation for untruthfulness—is approved by most jurisdictions.

State v. Anderson, 686 P2d 193, 199 (Mont. 1984) (citing cases). See also Peeples v. State, 681 So2d 236, 237–39 (Ala 1995) (finding evidence relevant to the issue of whether the complaining witness was merely continuing her habit of making threats and false accusations to manipulate persons around her, and holding “[t]his does not amount to an attempt to impeach the victim’s character based on her prior bad acts and is, therefore, admissible”).

OEC 404(3) prohibits evidence of prior bad acts “only when the evidence is offered solely to prove (1) the character of a person, and (2) that the person acted in conformity therewith. Both elements are required.” Johns, 301 Or at 548. But see State v. Williams, 357 Or 1 (2015), en banc (prior bad acts of defendant admissible if relevant, even if offered for propensity purposes). [Note: See Chapter 9, “They Introduce What?: Other Act Evidence.”] While Williams held propensity evidence admissible against a defendant, there was no reciprocal rule announced with respect to complaining witnesses. Thus, the defendant must present a non-character purpose for introducing extrinsic evidence of prior false accusations under OEC 404(3). Again, rely on Johns: evidence under 404(3) is not limited only to evidence related to “proof of motive, opportunity, intent, preparation, plan, knowledge, identity, or absence of mistake or accident,” the situations explicitly listed in the rule. Rather, Rule 404(3) is inclusionary, and the list of purposes set forth in 404(3) “is not meant to be exclusive.” State v. Johns, 301 Or 535, 542 (1986) (en banc) (quoting the legislative commentary to Rule 404). Be creative in your theories.

Of course, the distinction between whether evidence is offered to show conformity with a character trait or for some other permissible purpose under 404(3) is often blurred, and the decision must be made on a case-by-case basis. The fine line between conformance and some other permissible purpose is apparent in many cases. Johns is again instructive. There, the defendant was on trial for the murder of his spouse. The court held that evidence that the defendant had attempted to kill a different spouse six years earlier was admissible under Rule 404(3). According to the court, the evidence was offered not as proof that the defendant had acted in conformity with his conduct on the prior occasion, but rather that the evidence proved intent and absence of mistake or accident. Johns, 301 Or at 559. See also State v. Rogers, 313 Or 356 (1992) (evidence of other acts of inflicting pain on other prostitutes admissible to prove defendant had the same objective to intentionally cause intense pain to his murder victims); State v. Davis, 156 Or App 117 (1998) (prior recent acts of violence by defendant toward homicide victim relevant on issue of motive). A discretionary call by the trial judge, the decision should be informed by ideas of fairness and a defendant’s due process right to present a defense. Indeed, “courts have been quite liberal, and properly so, in allowing the admission of evidence of prior acts of the defendant toward the prosecutrix. Equally relevant, and for the same reasons, is evidence that the prosecutrix has in the past lied about the type of acts charged to the defendant.” Hurlburt, 333 P2d at 87. This passage from Hurlburt is particularly germane in light of the Williams decision.

Argue that extrinsic evidence must be admitted based on the above principles, and point out that Driver and LeClair only address a limited exception to Rule 608, not Rule 404. “[W]hen the mechanistic application of a state’s rules of evidence or procedure would undermine the truth-finding function by excluding relevant evidence necessary to a defendant’s ability to defend against the charged offenses, the Confrontation and Compulsory Process Clauses must prevail.” State v. Garron, 827 A2d 243, 256 (NJ 2003) (citing Chambers v. Mississippi, 410 US 284, 302 (1973)).

XII. Motion to Exclude Hearsay of Complainant

While as a general rule hearsay is not admissible, OEC 803(18a)(b) provides for a limited, yet devastating exception which allows hearsay statements “concerning” an act of sexual abuse made by a child under 18, if certain requirements are met. One of those requirements is that the state must “make known to the [defendant] the [state’s] intention to offer the statement and the particulars of the statement no later than 15 days before trial.” OEC 803(18a)(b). This clause may provide the defendant with a way to keep the statements out of evidence.

A. Timing and Particulars Requirements from OEC 803(18a)(b)

Obviously, if the notice does not come at least 15 days before trial, the evidence must be excluded under OEC 803(18a)(b). State v. Bradley, 253 Or App 277, 283 (2012) (“The sole sanction for violations [of OEC 803(18a)(b)], as provided by the rule, is exclusion of the offered statements”). It is also insufficient for the state to simply provide notice to the defense of its intent to offer statements without the substance, or “particulars,” of the statement. It is similarly insufficient to merely identify where the statements can be found in discovery. Rather, the “particulars” requirement of OEC 803(18a)(b) must be scrupulously honored, providing the substance of the statements and not merely a general overview. In State v. Chase, for example, the state noticfied the defendant that it would introduce 18a(b) statements, and that the statements were found in “foregoing and subsequent reports” that “contain particulars of statements made by [the victim].” 240 Or App 541, 544 (2011). Chase noted the “particulars” requirement refers to “an individual fact, point, circumstance, or detail . . . [a] specific item of information.” Id. at 546. The state’s notice must, “at a minimum . . . identify the substance of the statement sought to be introduced.” Id. at 546-47. See also State v. Edblom, 257 Or App 22 (2013) (reversing convictions where state’s notice failed to provide substance of statements it intended to introduce); State v. Bradley, 253 Or App 277 (2012) (reversing convictions where state failed to properly identify the particulars of a complaining witness’s statement in its pretrial notice, because the notice did not identify the “substance” of the hearsay statements); State v. Wood, 253 Or App 97 (2012) (reversing convictions and citing Chase where state’s notice provided to whom the statements were made, but failed to provide the substance of the statements).

Further, that the “particulars” of the statements have already been provided to the defendant in discovery does not excuse the state’s failure to include the “particulars” of the statement in its notice. E.g., Edblom, 257 Or App at 30 (“[D]iscovery does not constitute notice for the purposes of OEC 803(18a)(b).”); State v. McKinzie, 186 Or App 384, 393 (2003) (“[T]he fact that defendant received discovery of the out-of-court statements of the victim does not satisfy the rule’s requirements”). Case law is clear—the only remedy for the state’s violation of 803(18a)(b)’s notice requirement is exclusion of the statements. Bradley, 253 Or App at 283.

B. Exclusion Based on OEC 403

[Note: Also see Chapter 9, “They Want to Introduce What?: Other Act Evidence.”]

OEC 403 provides that evidence may be excluded “if its probative value is outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury, or by considerations of undue delay or needless presentation of cumulative evidence.”

If the complaining witness is testifying, argue that the probative value of any prior statements concerning the allegations is negligible:

When the witness has merely testified on direct examination, without any impeachment, proof of consistent statements is unnecessary and valueless. The witness is not helped by it; for, even if it is an improbable or untrustworthy story, it is not made more probable or more trustworthy by any number of repetitions of it.

4 Wigmore, Evidence § 7124 at 255 (Chadbourn rev. 1972).

Argue further that if the state were to offer such hearsay, it would simply consist of bolstering the testimony of the complainant. This is impermissible under State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621, 629–30 (1988), and its progeny, which prevents comment by any witness upon the credibility of another and, by implication, use of simple repetition of one witness’s statements to buttress the credibility of the complainants. The danger of unfair prejudice is particularly concerning in cases which will be a “typical ‘credibility contest’ . . . [where] the young wom[a]n only belatedly complained of sexual abuse by defendant[,] these complaints were uncorroborated by any witnesses or physical evidence[, and] defendant vehemently denie[s] any involvement.” Id. at 628. See also United States v. McCulley, 178 F3d 872, 876 (7th Cir. 1999) (“It is, of course, improper to admit a previous statement for the mere purpose of bolstering a statement made at trial”); Montana v. Harris, 808 P2d 453, 458 (Mont. 1991) (holding hearsay statements of complaining witness to another regarding sex abuse inadmissible because, since hearsay declarant was available to testify, the hearsay statements “were not the most probative evidence on the matter [and such] testimony on the issue was merely cumulative, serving only to bolster [complaining witness’s] testimony”). As one court has noted, the introduction of prior statements consistent with the complaining witness’s testimony only serves as “corroboration by repetition,” and should not be admissible because “this tactic preys on the human failing of placing belief in that which is most often repeated.” People v. Sanders, 375 NE2d 921, 924 (Ill. App. 1978). Argue that admission of these repeated, emotionally charged statements will add nothing substantive to the state’s case, but would, however, render the trial unfair due to the improper bolstering of a critical witness’s statements.

C. Exclusion Under OEC 801(4)

Even if kept out of its case in chief, the state may attempt to introduce such hearsay statements under OEC 801(4), which allows that a prior statement by a witness is not hearsay if the statement is “[c]onsistent with the testimony of the witness and is offered to rebut an inconsistent statement or an express or implied charge against the witness of recent fabrication or improper influence or motive.”

Before the state can offer evidence under the rule, however, the state must establish three things: 1) that the statement in question is consistent with the testimony of the complaining witness at trial, 2) that the defense expressly or impliedly charged that the complaining witness fabricated her story or had an improper motive or influence, and 3) that the fabrication was “recent.” Defense attorneys can argue in limine that the court must hold a hearing, outside the presence of the jury, on whether each statement meets the requirements of OEC 801(4) before it can be introduced. Case law further fleshes out these requirements.

Whether there has been a charge of “recent fabrication” is a question for the court to determine. Powers v. Cheeley, 307 Or 585, 593 n.5 (1989). Merely impeaching the testimony of a witness with contradictory evidence does not create an implied charge of recent fabrication or improper motive or influence. Id. (“The historical meaning of ‘implied charge’ and its present meaning do not include cases of simple contradiction concerning the facts of the case among or between witnesses”). Indeed, as nearly every trial inevitably involves contradictory evidence, to hold otherwise would “repeal the rule of OEC 801(4)(a) [and] would reward the garrulous but not the reticent.” Id. at 594. In addition, the state cannot attempt to create an implied charge of fabrication through cross-examination of a defense witness—by asking the witness to say that anything contrary to that witness’s version of the events would be a fabrication. Id. at 593 and n.5.

Moreover, prior statements must have been made before the time when the alleged motive to fabricate arose. Id. at 591 (“[T]he prior consistent statement which may be admitted is one made before the alleged motive to fabricate arose”); State v. Middleton, 294 Or 427, 431 (1982) (where defendant contended complaining witness invented rape story so she could move out to live with her boyfriend, “the desire to move away from her home was a motive that could have been present before her initial report, so it would not support the admission of prior consistent statements”).

Thus, if the state seeks to introduce the prior statements of the complaining witness under OEC 801(4)(a)(B), the defendant should request a hearing, outside the presence of the jury, to determine 1) whether there has been an explicit or implied charge of recent fabrication under the law, and if so 2) whether the statement or statements in question are consistent with the complaining witness’s trial testimony, and 3) whether the statements were made before the motive to fabricate arose.

XIII. Motion to Redact Recordings

The state often moves to introduce the recorded statement of the defendant in evidence. While a defendant’s statements are generally admissible, invariably those recordings contain inadmissible, and sometimes quite damning, evidence as well. Recorded police interrogations, in particular, present this risk. For example, an interrogator may, as part of her interrogation, suggest or outright state that she does not believe what the defendant is saying. This improper comment should not come before the jury (see below). Another example is when officers read a search warrant to a defendant prior to an interrogation—the search warrant will contain language that a judicial officer has found probable cause that the crime was committed by the defendant. It would violate due process for a jury to hear this. The defense attorney should review any recorded statements in detail and move to redact any irrelevant or otherwise inadmissible portions. A motion in limine to redact the statement alerts the court to these issues prior to trial and thus avoids taking time out for the redaction process during the trial.

Practice Tip: Argue Due Process, Not Vouching

Importantly, defense counsel should not argue for redaction based on the longstanding prohibition against vouching testimony, i.e., that one witness cannot comment on the credibility of another. See, e.g., State v. Middleton, 294 Or 427, 438 (1983) (“We reject testimony from a witness about the credibility of another witness”). Instead, argue that the interview must be redacted and the statements excluded as both irrelevant and because their probative value is far outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice under OEC 403. State v. McQuisten, 97 Or App 517, 520 (1989) is helpful. There, the Court of Appeals reversed a conviction where the trial court allowed the prosecution to play portions of a defendant interview in which officers expressed their belief in the complaining witness’s allegations:

The principal issue at trial . . . turns on whether the jury believed defendant or the complaining witness. From the context of the officer’s comment to defendant, the jury could reasonably have drawn the inference that the officer believed the story of the complaining witness, bolstering her credibility in its estimation. . . . [T]he prejudicial effect of the officer’s opinion as to the credibility of the complaining witness outweighs the any probative value of defendant’s response [when confronted with the officer’s opinion].

Id. at 520 (reversing under OEC 403). See also Earls v. McCaughtry, 379 F3d 489, 493 (7th Cir 2004) (ineffective assistance for failing to redact vouching comments of interviewer, because duty to assess witness credibility is “left solely to the jury,” and witness comments on credibility are “harmful at trial because it impermissibly interferes with the jury’s role”).

XIV. Motion to Introduce Evidence of Defendant’s Sexual Propriety with Minors

In some cases, it may benefit a defendant to introduce character evidence related to the defendant’s sexual propriety. Further, such character evidence can, if desired, be limited to evidence of defendant’s sexual propriety specifically around minors. The decision whether to introduce such evidence should not be made lightly—character witnesses have the potential to backfire under cross-examination. Putting on character evidence of this sort could also draw the jury’s attention to issues or create inferences that would not otherwise have been considered. Once the decision is made, the following arguments support the introduction of this character evidence.

A defendant’s character for sexual propriety around minors is pertinent character evidence and admissible under OEC 404(2)(a). See State v. Enakiev, 175 Or App 589 (2001). Enakiev reversed a trial court’s exclusion of evidence of a defendant’s character for sexual propriety, applying a four-part test for admissibility in cases where character is not an essential element of the charge or defense:

[C]haracter evidence proffered by the defense under OEC 404(2)(a) is admissible only if: (1) the evidence concerns a “trait of character”; (2) that trait is “pertinent” to the crime charged; (3) the evidence is offered in the proper form under OEC 405, and (4) the evidence was not of “specific instances of conduct.”

Id. at 594.

Enakiev held squarely that character for sexual propriety is the type of “pertinent” character trait contemplated by OEC 404(2)(a). Id. at 595 (“sexual propriety is materially indistinguishable from other examples of character traits . . . and is properly deemed a character trait”). Enakiev relied on State v. Marshall, defining “character” as “a person’s disposition or propensity toward certain behavior.” [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=1921580370191220656&q=Marshall,+312+Or+367,(1991)&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 Marshall], 312 Or 367, 371-72 (1991). Sexual propriety around minors clearly speaks to a “disposition or propensity toward certain behavior.”

Marshall held that a character trait could be a particular subset of a more broadly defined category. The Marshall defendant wished to admit character evidence not only on the victim’s general reputation for truthfulness, but also whether she had a reputation for telling “any particular kind of lie.” Id. at 369, 372. The court approved this narrowed character trait as “within the scope of OEC 404(2)(b).” Id. at 372.

Further, evidence of a character trait is admissible if the trait is “pertinent,” i.e., “relevant to the offense charged.” Enakiev, 175 Or App 595-96. Thus character evidence is “pertinent” if it has “‘any tendency’ to make the existence of a fact of consequence ‘more or less probable than it would be without the evidence.’” Id. at 596 (citing OEC 401, defining relevance).

It follows that where the indictment includes charges containing elements that allege the victim was “a child” or a person “under the age of fourteen years,” for example, the defendant’s character for sexuality propriety around minors is “pertinent”—it has the tendency to make the existence of a fact of consequence (age of the complaining witness) more or less probable than it would be without the evidence. Enakiev, 175 Or App at 596; OEC 401. See also Arizona v. Rhodes, 200 P3d 973, 976 (Ariz App 2008) (“Defendant’s sexual normalcy, or appropriateness in interacting with children, is a character trait, and one that pertains to charges of sexual conduct with a child”) (citing Enakiev).

Under Marshall and Enakiev, a defendant’s sexual propriety around minors is a permissible “trait of character” under OEC 404(2). See also State v. Rothwell, 294 P3d 1137, 1143 (Idaho App 2013) (citing Enakiev and cases from several other jurisdictions allowing character evidence on sexual propriety around minors).
