Defending Sex Cases

Defending Sex Cases: Strategies from Allegation to Resolution, 2018 edition

Chapter 16. Jury Instructions and Stipulations

Jason E. Thompson and Zachary J. Stern

I. Introduction

Listen, we get it. Sex cases are hard. Really hard. After fighting prosecutors for discovery, figuring out what you are actually defending against, wrapping your head around OEC 404(4), prepping voir dire, directs, crosses, opening and closing, that Uniform Criminal Jury Instruction Handbook starts to look pretty tempting. But, don’t give in. Jury instructions are important for both practical and tactical reasons.

Practically, they are important because it is your one opportunity to get the judge to be your mouthpiece to tell the jury what the law is. Crafting a persuasive closing argument is one thing. However, your argument is that much better when after you sit down the court recites the jury instructions you drafted that—by no coincidence—align perfectly with your theory of the case.

Tactically, jury instructions are important because should your client get convicted and the court refuse to give your requested instruction, on appeal the court (with respect to the jury instruction issue) will review the evidence in the light most favorable to the party requesting the instruction, i.e., your client!

II. Rules for Jury Instructions

A. ORS 136.330

ORS 136.330 lists the applicable rules of civil procedure applicable to criminal cases for jury instructions. The following are certain rules of note:

1. ORCP 59(B). Jury instructions can be reduced to writing or recorded and given to the jury for deliberations. a. Must be requested at the commencement of the trial.

b. If you are requesting taped instructions be provided to the jury for deliberations, it is a good idea to bring your own tape recorder or check with the clerk before instruction to find the best way to make the recording. See State v. Looper, 77 Or App 660, 713 P2d 1099 (1986); Hansen v. Bennett, 162 Or App 380, 986 P2d 633 (1999) (failure of trial court to comply with ORCP 59(B) requires a new trial, but trial court’s failure to comply must be on the record in order for reviewing court to determine compliance).

c. UTCR 6.070 provides that:

i. A party requesting written or recorded jury instructions as provided by ORCP 59 (B) shall have the responsibility for recording or reducing the court’s instructions to writing in a timely manner; and

ii. No identifying information relating to the parties or any other extraneous material, including authorities, must appear on the typed or copied instructions.

2. ORCP 59(C). Deliberation.

a. The jury may take four items with them into deliberations:

i. An impartial written statement summarizing the issues to be decided by the jury.

ii. Their notes.

iii. Exhibits received (jury cannot take any exhibit not received into evidence into the jury room).

iv. Copies of documents that may be substituted for any public records or private documents that ought not, in the opinion of the court, be taken from the person in possession.

b. The jury must remain together and separate from all others, absent a valid order of the court; if allowed to separate, must be instructed by the court on the reason for separation.

3. ORCP 59(D). Questions by jurors.

a. Judge may require the jury to be brought back into court and reinstructed.

b. Counsel must be present or notified prior to reinstruction.

c. If jurors have questions, the question is presented to the court through the bailiff. The question can be answered. Sometimes, the nature of the question merely requires that the jury be reinstructed.

d. For preservation purposes, make sure to object on the record and state any and all reasons as to why the instruction/response from the court was inaccurate, insufficient, or unnecessary.

4. ORCP 59(G). Polling the jury; the hunt for inconsistencies and misconduct.

There is a strong preference for finality of jury verdicts. DC Thompson and Co. v. Hauge, 72 Or App 116 (1985) (jurors’ post-verdict affidavits stating that they did not understand jury verdict forms are not sufficient misconduct to cause reversal); [file:///Users/jennifer/Documents/Publications/Sex%20Crimes%20-%20Sherlag%20edit/chapters/**%20ready%20for%20hyperlinks%2c%20indesign/thompson%20and%20stern/o State v. Gardner], 230 Or 569, 371 P2d 558 (1962); Leland Properties v. Burton Eng’g and Survey, 152 Or App 557, 954 P2d 851 (1998) (lengthy discussion on long history of refusing to consider jurors’ post-verdict affidavits to impeach a verdict).

a. Poll may be requested by either side.

b. Always poll each individual juror. See State v. DeVault, 78 Or App 307, 715 P2d 1353 (1986). Even if a juror votes for conviction in the jury room, that juror may change his or her vote when polled and defeat the verdict. Defendant is entitled to have deliberations continue until enough jurors are willing to assure individual responsibility for verdict to permit verdict to stand. State v. DeVault, 78 Or App at 309.

c. If jurors answering in the affirmative are less than the number required to reach a verdict, the jury shall be sent out for further deliberation. State v. Imlah, 204 Or 43, 281 P2d 973 (1955). However, once the jurors’ verdict has been received by the court, absent some egregious juror misconduct, the verdict will stand, even if the jurors come back to the court later claiming error. DC Thompson and Co. v. Hauge, 72 Or App 116 (1985).

d. Although there is a strong judicial preference for finality, ORCP 64, applicable in criminal proceedings pursuant to ORS 136.535, provides grounds in which a defendant can move the court for a new trial.

Practice Tip

Never poll a jury following a not guilty verdict. In the event that the jury is considering lesser-included offenses, discuss with your client—before the jury returns its verdict—whether you will ask to poll the jury in the event that it returns a not guilty verdict to the charged offense but guilty verdict to a lesser-included offense.

5. ORCP 59(H). Exceptions.

a. Although it is certainly the preferred practice to “except” to the court giving, or failing to give, a particular jury instruction, an attorney’s failure to have an “exception” noted by the trial court is not a prerequisite to appellate review. State v. Vanornum, 354 Or 614, 629 (2013) (“ORCP 59 H does not govern preservation of instructional error”).

b. Exceptions must be made and recorded immediately after the court instructs
the jury.

Practice Tip

In order to ensure preservation of error, always object to the instructions before they are given and then take an exception to the instruction after it is read and provided to the jury if there is a reason to do so. State sources of law to support your position. Except to an instruction you requested and which the judge refused to provide, and state (1) why it should have been provided and (2) that it was not covered in any other instruction. Requesting instructions based on direct quotes from published Oregon appellate opinions is typically the best source of law for instructions, even if dicta. Also, except to instructions provided that do not correctly state the law that are prejudicial to your case. Explain why the instruction is an inaccurate statement of law. Failure to object and take exception to the trial court’s giving an instruction or failing to give your instruction will not preclude appellate review under Vanornum, but the appellate court will only review for “plain error.”

B. Instructions Requested in Writing

Check supplementary local court rules (SLRs) and UTCR 6.070. Most SLRs and the UTCR can be found online.

Instructions must be provided by the party requesting it, but the proposed instruction cannot contain information that identifies the party or points and authorities. It is the responsibility of the requesting party to make the record clear for appeal; the party cannot simply rely on the “usual” practice of the trial court. Leland Properties v. Burton Eng’g and Survey, 152 Or App 557, 954 P2d 851 (1998).

C. Uniform Criminal Jury Instructions

UCrJI 1600-1662 sets forth all of the instructions regarding specific sex offenses. The UCrJIs are a good starting point for crafting your jury instructions. But, as noted in the introduction, the UCrJIs are not your last line of defense.

Practice Tip

Always review these jury instructions and make sure that no stated defenses—such as UCrJI 1636, 1647, 1649-1651, 1654-1656, and 1660—apply to your case. Obviously, if any do, request those instructions.

Why the UCrJI aren’t enough—Although the UCrJI’s are convenient, Oregon appellate courts have, on occasion, found them to be inaccurate statements of the law. See, e.g., State v. Sandoval, 342 Or 506, 508 (2007) (UCrJI instruction on defense of self is misstatement of law); see also State v. Lopez-Minjarez, 350 Or 576, 588 (2011) (UCrJI accomplice liability instruction is misstatement of law).

III. Limiting Instructions

Limiting instructions are extremely important. In complex sex cases—probably more so than any other area of criminal law—evidence is often admissible for one purpose but no other. See State v. Moore/Coen, 349 Or 371, 391 (2010) (citing State v. Thompson, 328 Or 248, 271 (1999)) (“trial courts have the authority to give limiting instructions to juries that require them to consider evidence only for a particular purpose or in regard to a particular element”). It is incumbent upon the trial attorney to request that the court instruct the jury regarding the parameters with which jurors may consider such evidence. Here are a few ground rules:

1. The court shall, upon request, give a limiting instruction. OEC 105.

Practice Tip

If you ask for a limiting instruction, the court is required to instruct the jury on the permissible bases jurors may consider the challenged evidence. If you do not ask, and the court does not give a limiting instruction, the jury can use that evidence for any purpose. Request the instruction immediately after the evidence comes in, unless you think it would draw undue attention to the prejudicial testimony.

Practice Tip

There are some circumstances where it makes more strategic sense not to draw attention to the disputed evidence at the time of introduction. Under those circumstances, you can request that the limiting instruction be given at the close of evidence, just prior to deliberations.

2. Request the Instruction Again

Even if a limiting instruction is given immediately after the evidence comes in, be sure
to request it again at the end of the case together with the other instructions.

3. Case Law

“Generally, once evidence has been admitted without restriction, it can be used for any purpose.” State v. Clegg, 332 Or 432, 442 (2001) (emphasis in original). And, OEC 105 places the burden on trial counsel to request a limiting instruction if evidence is admissible for one purpose but not another.

Oregon trial courts have the authority to give limiting instructions to juries that require them to consider evidence only for a particular purpose or in regard to a particular element. State v. Moore/Coen, 349 Or 371, 391 (2010).

When litigating a sex case involving multiple offenses or multiple victims, an attorney should always consider requesting a “Kitzman-like” instruction that requires the jury to consider the evidence that pertains to each charged offense separately. State v. Kitzman,
129 Or App 520, 530, (1994), aff’d in part, rev’d in part, 323 Or 589 (1996).

In Kitzman, the trial court properly gave defendant’s requested limiting instruction:

[Defendant] has been charged with several unrelated counts in a single indictment. A separate crime is charged in each count of the Indictment. Each charge and the evidence pertaining to it must be considered separately by the jury…. In other words, it is your duty to consider the evidence solely for the charge it pertains to and no other charge.

Id. at 528-29. That limiting instruction was sufficient to ensure the jury considered only the evidence that corresponded to each charged offense as opposed to allowing the jury to “cross-reference” or “cross utilize” evidence pertaining to one charge while deliberating on another.

For example, in a case with two complaining witnesses, if the evidence with respect to one victim convinces a juror your client was guilty, a Kitzman-like instruction would prohibit that juror from determining your client was guilty with respect to the second victim because that juror would not be allowed to use the evidence from the first vic-
tim when considering whether your client was guilty of the conduct relating to the second victim.

Also, after State v. Chandler, 360 Or 323 (2016), an out-of-court statement that comments on the credibility of another witness is no longer subject to the categorical prohibition against vouching evidence so long as the statement is offered for a “relevant, non-opinion purpose.” For that reason, it is especially important in sex cases to consider requesting a limiting instruction like the following:

“As part of the questioning, ladies and gentlemen, you are about ready to hear an interview between Detective Mindreader and the Defendant. As part of the questioning, the detective may have made statements of disbelief in the defendant’s
story and expressed opinions concerning the defendant’s guilt.

“You should view the questions and answers in the same way you view the questions and answers in the courtroom. In other words, the questions are only pertinent as they explain what the answers are.

“You are not to assume as true anything Detective Mindreader says in his question; the questions are only pertinent as they give meaning to the answers.”

Practice Tip

Remember to think about these issues in advance of trial. There may be strategic reasons for requesting the instruction before the comment is made, immediately after it is made, or waiting until the end of trial and lumping in that special instruction with the rest of the instructions. Think through your theory of the case, the story you are presenting to the jury and at what juncture a Chandler-based limiting instruction fits in with your theory.

Notably, if the judge is giving a “limiting instruction,” this means evidence to which you have objected is likely coming in (and a concomitant motion for mistrial has been denied). In that case, when the limiting instruction is given, a good tactic is to tell the jury a few times that the judge is precluding them from considering the evidence except for the single purpose the judge found the evidence to be admissible and for no other purpose.

A. OEC 105

“When evidence which is admissible as to one party for one purpose but not admissible as to another party or for another purpose is admitted, the court, upon request, shall restrict the evidence to its proper scope and instruct the jury accordingly.”

B. Importance

Limiting instructions should be utilized to focus the jury on what it may do with particular evidence. Remember, the jury will also be instructed that it must follow the court’s instructions.

1. At Trial (for the Jury)

Generally, once evidence has been admitted without restriction, it can be used by the jury for any purpose. State v. Clegg, 332 Or 432, 442 (2001). Without a limiting instruction, the jury can make improper inferences about a defendant’s propensity to commit sex offenses against one victim based on evidence that was admissible only with respect to other charges involving different victims. Green v. Franke, 357 Or 301, 311 (2015).

Remember, your goal in requesting a limiting instruction is to prevent the jury from using evidence for an impermissible purpose.

2. On Appeal (the Record)

If evidence is received without objection at trial, it inherently becomes part of the record on appeal. On appeal, the appellate court will only consider evidence in the record. Thus, if evidence is admissible for one purpose but not another, a limiting instruction in the trial court will ensure that the appellate court considers the challenged evidence solely for the purposes it was admitted at trial. Failure to request a limiting instruction invites the appellate courts to use the challenged evidence for any purpose. State v. Clegg, 332 Or 432, 443 (2001) (quoting McCormick on Evidence, § 54, 242, 5th ed 1990); Dalk v. Lachmund, 157 Or 152, (1937).

C. How the Court’s Failure to Give a Requested Instruction Helps on Appeal

When a defendant requests an instruction the trial court refuses to give, an appellate court will view the facts in the light most favorable to the defendant. State v. Beck, 269 Or App 304, 309 (2015).

Practice Tip

This is one of the few circumstances in which a defendant can lose at trial and on appeal have the facts construed in the light most favorable to the defendant. That’s why jury instructions are really important. If you propose a jury instruction that is an accurate statement of law and there is evidence in the record to support it, your client’s chances of prevailing on appeal increase dramatically if the trial court refuses to give it.

D. When Special Requested Instructions Must Be Given

A party is entitled to have the jury instructed on the law which supports that party’s theory of the case where (1) there is evidence in the record to support that theory and (2) the instruction correctly states the law. State v. Loew, 130 Or App 370, 373 (1994).

A jury instruction is supported by the evidence if there is any competent evidence in the record to support giving the instruction. State v. Beck, 269 Or App 304, 309 (2015).

E. Special Instructions with Multiple Victims

In a case involving multiple victims, you may request the court to instruct that the jury limit its consideration of certain evidence to particular counts. This instruction ensures that evidence is restricted to its proper scope, preventing the jury from cross-referencing evidence to prove any fact in dispute.

Kitzman Multiple Victim Instruction — The special instruction given in State v. Kitzman, 129 Or App 520 (1994), ensured the jury could convict only upon a determination that the particular act had occurred on the particular occasion.

F. Mental State as a Material Element

When a mental state is a material element of a crime, a court should instruct a jury that it must find beyond a reasonable doubt that defendant acted with the required mental state. State v. Nelson, 241 Or App 681, 686 (2011).

Generally, there are three categories of material elements in Oregon’s criminal statutes: conduct, circumstance, and result. State v. Crosby, 342 Or 419, 429 (2007). A material element, generally, is one that “concerns the substance or quality of the crime—the harm or evil sought to be prevented[.]” State v. Rainoldi, 236 Or App 129, 147 (2010).

For example, a person commits first-degree rape if that person, inter alia, (1) has sexual intercourse with another person, and (2) the victim is subjected to forcible compulsion. ORS 163.375(1)(a). Although the statute fails to identify a particular mental state that would render somebody criminally liable, the Oregon Court of Appeals has held that “subjected to forcible compulsion” was a material element that “necessarily requires a culpable mental state” because it directly “concerns the substance or quality of the crime—the harm or evil sought to be prevented.” In Nelson, the Court of Appeals held that it was sufficient to prove that the defendant acted “knowingly” with respect to whether the victim was subjected to forcible compulsion.

G. Ineffective Assistance

Jury instructions are important, and failing to request instructions that advance a defendant’s theory of his or her case may constitute ineffective assistance of counsel. See, e.g., Mellerio v. Nooth, 279 Or App 419 (2016) (holding counsel ineffective for failing to request concurrence instruction when alternative factual occurrences were presented at trial and any one of which could have supported the single charge); Everett v. Premo, 279 Or App 470 (2016).

IV. Williams and Propensity

A. Child Sex Abuse Crimes

In a prosecution of child sexual abuse, propensity is admissible under OEC 404(4), so long as the evidence is (1) relevant under OEC 401 and (2) more probative than prejudicial. A court must apply due process balancing to determine whether the risk of unfair prejudice posed by the other acts evidence outweighs its probative value under OEC 403. State v. Williams, 357 Or 1, 24 (2015).

If a trial court admits such “other acts” evidence, you should request a limiting instruction to lessen the risk of misuse of that evidence by the jury. A sample limiting instruction is provided at the end of this chapter.

Practice Tip

Once the limiting instruction is given, be on high alert during closing arguments. If the prosecutor uses that other act evidence for other than the purpose it was admitted, be ready to object and move to strike so the jury is reminded, by the court, that other acts evidence is to be considered only for its admissible purpose. You should also move for a mistrial.

B. Sex Crimes Other than Child Sex Abuse

When a defendant has been charged with a crime other than child sexual abuse, the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment precludes the admission of other acts evidence to prove defendant’s character and propensity to act accordingly. See State v. Williams, 357 Or 1, 17 (2015). Again, in order for other acts evidence to be admissible in such cases, the evidence must be (1) relevant under OEC 401 and (2) more probative than prejudicial under due process balancing. Id. See Chapter 9, “They Want to Introduce What?: Other Act Evidence.” Also see State v. Tena, 281 Or App 57, 71-73, 384 P3d 521 (2016) (addressing when to give instruction pursuant to State v. Leistiko, 352 Or 172 (2012), where other act evidence has been admitted). The Oregon Supreme Court accepted review in Tena and reversed the Court of Appeals; they held that the evidence was not admissible for a nonpropensity purpose and the trial court should have conducted an analysis under OEC 403. The Tena court stated that when other act evidence is not relevant for a nonpropensity purpose, “more significant due process concerns are implicated, and, generally, the danger of unfair prejudice will substantially outweigh the probative value of the evidence.” Id. at 405, 393 P3d 1132. State v. Tena, 362 Or 514, 525, 412 P3d 175, 182 (2018).

V. Jury Concurrence

When a single crime has been charged but the evidence is sufficient for the jury to find there were multiple separate occurrences of the charged crime involving the same victim and the same perpetrator during the period of time alleged in the indictment, the state either must elect which occurrence constituted the charged crime or, alternatively, the defendant is entitled to an instruction that ten or more jurors must concur on which occurrence constituted that crime.
State v. Ashkins, 357 Or 642, 643 (2015).

Practice Tip

There are some occasions when it makes more strategic sense to move for an election instead of moving for a factual concurrence instruction at the close of the evidence and vice versa. Be sure to speak with your client before trial to determine which tactic stands to benefit your theory of the case and act accordingly. State v. Antoine, 269 Or App 66 (2015).

VI. Sample Special Jury Instructions

A. Special Jury Instruction No. 1—unanimous verdict—felony case

When you return to the jury room, select one of your members to act as presiding juror. The presiding juror has no greater voting weight but is to preside over your deliberations and be the spokesperson for the jury. You should then deliberate and find your verdict.

If it becomes necessary during your deliberations to communicate with me, do so in writing. I will consult with the parties before responding. Remember that you are not to tell anyone, including me, how the jury stands numerically until you have reached a lawful verdict or have been discharged.

This being a criminal case, all 12 jurors must agree on your verdict. When you have arrived at a verdict, the presiding juror will sign the appropriate verdict form.

After you have reached your verdict, signal the bailiff. The court will be reassembled to receive your verdict.

Blakely v. Washington, 542 US 296 (2004)
Crawford v. Washington, 541 US 36 (2004)
McDonald v. City of Chicago, 561 US 742 (2010)
Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution

B. Special Jury Instruction No. 2—“subjects”

“Subjects” means to cause to undergo a particular action or effect. “Subjects” describes some level of causation through influence or participation in acts, rather than actual control or dominion. “Subjects” does not mean the same thing as forcible compulsion.

State v. Bernhardt, 277 Or App 868, 874-75 (2016)

C. Special Jury Instruction No. 3—forcible compulsion

Forcible compulsion must be force that is not force inherent in the sexual conduct at issue for the force to constitute forcible compulsion.

State v. Magel, 246 Or App 725 (2011)
State v. Marshall, 350 Or 208 (2011)

D. Special Jury Instruction No. 4—use of evidence (State v. Leistiko)

You may not consider evidence concerning one complaining witness while deliberating on evidence of guilt in regards to another complaining witness unless and until you conclude, without considering the other evidence, that the physical acts discussed in each count you are deliberating on actually transpired. In other words, you must first conclude that the act alleged in each count occurred before considering any other evidence regarding that count, including the defendant’s mental state.

State v. Leistiko, 352 Or 172 (2012)
State v. Pitt, 352 Or 566 (2012)
State v. Jones, 258 Or App 1 (2013)
State v. Kitzman, 129 Or App 520 (1994)
State v. Buyes, 280 Or App 564 (2016)

E. Special Jury Instruction No. 5—factual concurrence instruction

• Concurrence Instruction (for Unanimous Jury)

In order to reach a lawful guilty verdict as to any count, the jury must agree unanimously on what factual occurrence constituted the crime. Thus, in this case, in order to reach a guilty verdict on any count, the jury must unanimously agree on which factual occurrence constituted the offense.

• Concurrence Instruction (for Nonunanimous Jury)

In order to reach a lawful guilty verdict as to any count, ten jurors must agree on what factual occurrence constituted the crime. Thus, in this case, in order to reach a guilty verdict on any count, ten jurors must agree on which factual occurrence constituted the offense.

State v. Boots, 308 Or 371 (1989) (jury concurrence requirement requires that the requisite number of jurors agree on the factual occurrence that constitutes a crime)
Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution.

United States v. Gipson, 553 F2d 453, 457-58 (5th Cir 1977) (6th Amendment to United States Constitution requires that jurors agree to factual predicate that constitutes a crime.

Wilson v. Premo, 280 Or App 372 (2016)

F. Special Jury Instruction No. 6—separate consideration of the allegations of each count

Although defendant is being tried for counts against three alleged victims, you must consider the allegations made in each count separately. In doing so, you must decide what the evidence proves as to each count, without considering any evidence that may have been received solely as to a separate count.

For example, you may not use evidence from one count to infer or bolster the credibility on another count, infer defendant has bad character, or prove the defendant is more likely to have committed the crimes charged because of evidence that relates to a separate count. The defendant is entitled to have the case decided on the evidence and on the law applicable to each count, as if the allegations made in each count were offered in a separate trial.

G. Special Jury Instruction No. 7— supplemental instruction regarding detectives’ and defendant’s statements (State v. Chandler)

Example One

As part of the questioning, ladies and gentlemen, you are about to hear [just heard] or [have heard] an interview between [witness] and defendant. As part of the questioning, the [witness] may have made statements of disbelief in the defendant’s story and expressed opinions concerning the defendant’s guilt [or complainant’s credibility]. You should view the questions and answers in the same way that you view the questions and answers in the courtroom. In other words, the questions are only pertinent as they explain what the answers mean. You are not to assume as true anything [witness] says in his question as the questions are only pertinent as they give meaning to the answers.

Example Two

  • Short Version: Given before interrogation is played for jury

Ladies and gentlemen, you are about to hear an interrogation of [defendant] by Detective [name].

As part of the questioning, the detective may have made statements of disbelief in [defendant’s] answers and may have expressed opinions concerning [defendant’s] guilt or other witness’s credibility. Such statements and opinions are standard techniques employed by law enforcement officers when investigating alleged criminal activity, and as such, such statements do not constitute evidence in this matter. Only [defendant’s] responses to the questions may be regarded as evidence to be given consideration for what you deem them to be worth. You are not to assume as true anything Detective [name] says during his interrogation of [defendant] in his questioning of [defendant] because Detective[name]’s statements and questions are only pertinent as they give meaning to [defendant’s] answers.

  • Long Version: Given in final instructions

During this trial, you heard an interrogation of [defendant] by Detective [name].

As part of the questioning, the detective made statements of disbelief in [defendant’s] answers and expressed opinions concerning [defendant’s] guilt or other witness’s credibility. Such statements and opinions are standard techniques employed by law enforcement officers when investigating alleged criminal activity, and as such, such statements do not constitute evidence in this matter. Only [defendant’s] responses to the questions may be regarded as evidence by you to be given consideration for what you deem them to be worth. You are not to assume as true anything Detective [name] said during his interrogation of [defendant] in his questioning of [defendant] because, as I instructed you prior to playing the recorded interrogation, Detective [name]’s statements and questions were only pertinent as they gave meaning to [defendant’s] answers.

When deliberating, you must remember:

1. Questions, statements and opinions of law enforcement officers during interviews or interrogations are not evidence.

2. Questions, statements and opinions of law enforcement officers during interviews or interrogations shall not be understood as the law enforcement officer’s personal opinion.

3. Only the witness’s responses to questions posed by law enforcement officers are material to your consideration.

State v. Chandler, 360 Or 323 (2016)

Dubria v. Smith, 224 F3d 995, 1001-1002 (9th Cir., 2007)

H. Special Jury Instruction No. 8—witness referred to as a victim

Throughout this case you may have heard [name of complaining witness] referred to as a victim. Given the issues presented in this case, however, it is for you alone to decide whether or not [she/he] has been victimized by defendant. You must determine from the evidence presented here in court whether [name] was the object of the offenses alleged to have been committed by [defendant’s name]. Whether [she/he] was the object of each of the crimes alleged by the state are matters for you to evaluate and decide.

State v. Nomura, 79 Haw 413, 903 P 2d 718, 721 (1995)

I. Special Jury Instruction No. 9—relating to suggestive wording and leading questions

The use of suggestive wording and leading questions tends to result in answers that more closely fit the expectation embedded in the question. Witness memory can become contaminated by external information or assumptions embedded in questions or otherwise communicated to the witness.

State v. Lawson/James, 352 Or 724, 743 (2012)

J. Special Jury Instruction No. 10—witness’s original memory—evidentiary value

Like many forms of evidence, once contaminated, a witness’s original memory is very difficult to retrieve; it is, however, only the original memory that has any forensic or evidentiary value.

State v. Lawson/James, 352 Or 724, 748 (2012)

K. Special Jury Instruction No. 11—state of mind exception

You are about to hear evidence that is only admissible to prove the effect that the statement had on the listener, not for the truth of the matter asserted. You are not to consider the truth or falsity of the statement, only its effect, if any, on the listener, namely this witness.

OEC 801(3)

State v. Hren, 237 Or App 605, 607 (2010) (statement not hearsay where offered not for truth, but for effect on listener).

L. Special Jury Instruction No. 12—limiting instruction—conditional relevancy

You have heard evidence that outside of this trial a witness may or may not have made statements that differ from [his/her] trial testimony. You are specifically instructed that you are not to consider the statements the witness may or may not have said out of court as substantive evidence of the charged crimes. In other words, the statement that may or may not have been made out of court cannot be considered in determining whether or not the defendant is guilty of the charged offense.

You may, however, consider those out-of-court statements the witness may or may not have said when determining whether that witness’ in-court testimony is, or is not, credible.

That evidence is not to be used, and is not admissible, for any other purpose. OEC 105.

State v. Reyes, 209 Or 595 (1957) (where evidence of other crimes is properly admitted against defendant for particular purpose, an instruction limiting its consideration to such purpose should be given when requested to minimize use of the evidence by jury for an inadmissible purpose).

Green v. Franke, 357 Or 301 (2015) (when a trial court declines to sever joined offenses, and evidence relating to one offense is not admissible to prove another joined offense, a trial court ordinarily will instruct the jury to consider the evidence on each offense separately to prevent the jury from using the evidence offered to prove one offense to decide another joined offense).
