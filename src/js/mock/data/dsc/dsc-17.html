Defending Sex Cases

Defending Sex Cases: Strategies from Allegation to Resolution, 2018 edition

Chapter 17. Mitigation, Negotiation & Sentencing

By Thad Betz

I. Introduction

Negotiations with the prosecutor are a dance that begins the day one begins representation. One might say that we as defense attorneys have to work for peace (a plea deal) while preparing for war (a trial). Many prosecutors have been trained by defense attorneys to expect mitigation to come to them in some specific forms if there is any discussion of a pretrial resolution:

• Psycho-sexual evaluations

• Proof of treatment for any issues identified by the Psycho-sexual report

• Other psychological evaluations for disorders such as mental incapacity (extremely low IQ

• Bi-polar disorder

• Severe depression

• Obsessive compulsive disorder etc)

• Passed (one hopes) polygraphs (especially as to other alleged victims or

• In the face of an allegation of a sex offense that does not involve contact

• A “no contact with minors” polygraph)

• Substance abuse evaluations and attendant documentation of successful compliance with an appropriate treatment modality where use of controlled substances and/or alcohol is alleged to have been part of the crime(s) alleged

• Attendance at AA/NA and/or SA meetings (with cards and/or sheets showing attendance)

• Letters of support from the community members at large

• Funds (if available) for payment of restitution and/or compensatory fines

• Challenges (if any) to the credibility of the person/child making the allegations (or to the credibility of those championing the person/child as being truthful and legal and factual challenges to the charges themselves and how the legal/factual challenges could adversely impact the outcome of the state’s theory of the case and/or desired outcome.

Obviously, many of these can only occur if the accused is out of Custody (see chapter 2, “Bail and Release”). but many can be utilized even when the individual remains incarcerated pending a final resolution.

Not all of these “tools” are practical in every case but the use of some of them in every case is likely to aid the Accused in attempting to lower the impact on the Accused that may have been initially contemplated by the DDA prosecuting the case. This chapter is to assist the practitioner to identify which tools to use and, in some cases, to not use in preparing your case for negotiations and/or sentencing should a conviction ultimately occur whether it be by a plea or conviction after trial.

II. Mitigation

A. Psychosexual Evaluations

Mitigating a sex offense accusation ought to literally define the phrase “tightrope walking.” Provide too little and your mitigation is vague and unhelpful. Provide too much and you tip your client off the rope and open your client up to unwarranted and unneeded additional scrutiny. Worse yet, one provides evidence of uncharged conduct without proper protection (OEC 407 and ORS 135.435) or provides aggravating sentencing information.

A psychosexual evaluation can prove to be the most important tool of mitigation.1 A psychosexual evaluation typically employs the same forensic methods as a general psychological evaluation with additional tests, inquires, and actuarial tools related to deviant sexual behavior. The purposes of a psychosexual evaluation are, generally speaking:

  • Risk assessment.
  • Whether treatment can be effective in addressing the risk at issue.
  • Helping develop a clinical treatment plan.
  • Pinpointing a sexual interest to alleviate concerns about particular types of contact while on supervision.

A competent psychologist will want to review your client’s relevant medical and psychological records. School records should be gathered for young clients as well. Of course, any prior sex offense reports should be gathered. (Counsel must naturally weigh what is worth disclosing to the evaluator and what is not.)

Next, an evaluator will want to review discovery in the case and any polygraph data (see discussion below) and administer psychological testing as well as specific sexual instruments. It is common practice for the evaluator to obtain interviews from collateral sources. A collateral source is commonly a spouse/partner, sibling, parents, or someone who knows the client very well. Typically, the evaluation is written from the perspective that the allegations bear truth.

B. The Interview

Clients should not be placed in a psychosexual interview without a briefing by counsel concerning the procedures used, topics covered, and an overview of testing instruments.
Far too many post-psychosexual interview conversations between an attorney and client go like this:

Lawyer: The evaluation didn’t go well.

Client: What? I did exactly what my cellie/friend/relative told me to do?

Lawyer: What did they tell you?

Client: To just lie on all the tests so that they’re invalid, and tell the doctor that there’s nothing wrong with me, I’m innocent, so why would I need treatment.

Lawyer: Perfect.

Client: Can you fix this?

So what should a client know? A client should know that psychological instruments have built-in (purported) controls and should understand the purpose of particular tests. For example, there is no benefit to lie on a question such as “I have never thought about hitting my children” or “I never raise my voice when angry.” Misrepresentations on questions like this result in conclusions that the examinee has antisocial personality traits. Likewise, during the interview portion of the examination, the accused will be asked about treatment. If they express an unwillingness to engage treatment or view it as unnecessary, an evaluator may conclude that they are not amenable to treatment.

A psychosexual evaluation may be regarded as incomplete by the state unless it contains a discussion by the accused of the accusation. For a variety of reasons, this is often not the most legally appropriate strategy. If it is your wish that your client not communicate about the index offense with the evaluator, a short note or phone call to the evaluator is well advised in order to avoid any potential discussion of the incident. In the vast majority of cases, an evaluator is able to render a risk determination without a specific discussion of the incident. In some instances it is helpful to ask the evaluator to not discuss the allegations with the accused but make the determination based on an assumption that the allegations, if true, would not change the testing scores or the ultimate conclusion.

C. Psychological Instruments

Every evaluator uses their own combination of psychological testing. Oftentimes, evaluators use general personality instruments, control/deception tests, psychopathy measures, and sexual interest scales.

Typical tests include:

  • Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory-3 (MCMI-3). This personality and psychopathology test seeks to provide information on traits along Axis II and Axis I disorder. There is also an adolescent version.
  • Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI). The PAI is a self-administered objective test of personality and psychopathology. The PAI contains scales designed to determine whether or not the examinee is attempting to portray themselves in a favorable or unfavorable manner. These scales are typically what is used for the psychologist to determine whether or not the examinee is “malingering” or feigning more severe psychological symptoms—a most unhelpful determination. There is also an adolescent version.
  • Hare Psychopathy Checklist (PCL-SV). This test reviews background documents on the subject combined with a clinical interview and then rates the individual on a scale to determine overall level of psychopathy.
  • Paulus Deception Scales. This is a 40-item questionnaire intended to measure socially desirable response, rather than truthful response. The questions are intended to detect whether the examinee is engaged in either “self-deception” or “impression management.” In other words, is the examinee attempting to portray themselves in a overly virtuous and uncommon light (i.e., “I never swear”) or does the examinee lack honest self-appraisal (e.g., “I am completely rational”). In essence, the PDS is a tool designed to determine whether or not an examinee is a reliable self-reporter.
  • General Ability of Measure for Adults (GAMA). The GAMA measures general mental ability in an IQ score.

• Bumby Cognitive Scales. In this test, examinees respond to a list of thinking errors focused on whether the subject endorses sexual assault in any form.

  • Abel Assessment of Sexual Interest-3 (AASI-3). This instrument uses viewing time of particular stimuli (female, male, various ages, etc.) to make conclusions about sexual interest.
  • Penile Plethysmograph: It’s as bad as it sounds.
  • Internet Sex Screening Test (ISST). This instrument asks the examinee to endorse or disavow a number of behaviors related to internet use of sexually explicit material.
  • Child Abuse Material Instrument (CAMI). This tool has more application in supervision of sex offenders convicted of child pornography but can also be used in a forensic evaluation of one facing such offenses (or for sentencing mitigation).

• Static-99R. This risk assessment tool became the first “actuarial” method of measuring future risk. Contrary to previous risk assessments, which were entirely subjective on the part of the examiner, the Static-99R reflects back at thousands of sex offenders and locates risk factors associated with sexual recidivism. Recent research has found the Static-99R to be highly overpredictive. Also this is the tool being used for risk assessment by the Oregon Department of Correction, the Oregon Board of Parole and Post Prison Supervision and the state police to determine risk of a convicted offender. (Note: See OCDLA’s 2016 Winter Conference presentation by BPPPS Chair Michael Wu: “Sex Offender Classification and Implementation of House Bill 2549, Tiering the Sex Offender Registry,” and OCDLA’s 2017 Sex Crimes Seminar presentation by Emily Simon: “Tiers and Fears: Navigating This Strange New World of Relief from Sex Offender Registration.”)

  • Sexual Violence Risk-20 (SVR-20) is a more subjective measure and intended to account for changes in the examinee’s life and circumstance.

  • Juvenile Sex Offender Assessment Protocol-II (J-SOAP-II) remains, in most views, an experimental risk assessment tool for juveniles and does not have enough data to be considered actuarial.

  • Estimate of Risk of Adolescent Sex Offender Recidivism (ERASOR) holds itself out to be the empirically valid risk assessment tool that measures 25 factors.

[Note: OCDLA’s Mental Health & Criminal Defense manual contains a full chapter about clinical testing, with an extensive review of these and other instruments.]

Occasionally prosecutors will request particular tests in order to negotiate a case, likely because they have attended a CLE or training that emphasized one particular test over another. Practitioners should know that there is literature criticizing any one of the above tests along racial lines, overpredictive results, and lack of empirical data. Counsel should request their evaluator to include relevant criticism of any adverse result. For example, a younger offender who has never been married or lived with an intimate partner and victimized a family friend (and thus, a stranger) will score as a high risk to reoffend despite having no criminal history. However, as noted above, recent literature provides ample data that the Static-99R is radically overpredictive.

Practice Tips

First, many prosecutors have “preferred” evaluators, and some counties will only accept the evaluation of specific evaluators from a specific list (Yamhill and Marion counties, for example). If you practice in one of those counties and not as a “local” practitioner, always find someone who will give you the straight skinny on the providers who made “the list” and which ones are more likely prone to finding people to be sexual predators needing a lifetime of treatment (yes, these providers do exist).

Second, be prepared to tell the prosecutor you are not going to use someone from their list. Pick the provider you trust and with whom you are most confident will give you an honest evaluation. Get a copy of that provider’s CV, plus a list of other cases they have been involved with, and the DA offices that have accepted that provider’s expert opinion. Be prepared to challenge the folks on the prosecutor’s list as being biased. A successful argument to a judge could state that if a provider is on a prosecutor’s list, then that provider is at least implicitly biased because the provider gets business based upon being on the list; and, if the provider comes back with two many favorable recommendations for the accused, they will not continue to be on the list.

Third, be prepared to have multiple psychosexual evaluations. In some instances, have two separate evaluations completed, without having the two providers collaborate and send both to the DDA. If the DDA still does not want to accept, tell the DDA that your client will submit to a provider on their list but will not fund it. If the DDA agrees to fund it, then send the two evaluations you have to that provider and insist on being present for any interview.

Fourth, do not be afraid to have your client submit to an evaluation where they have not admitted to the criminal conduct. But you do not want them to discuss the criminal conduct in the event the case may still go to trial. One can easily tell the provider, “Here are the police reports—conduct the evaluation as if the allegations are true.” Many times, even if the provider accepts the facts as true, your client will still be seen as a low risk to “recidivate.”

D. Polygraphs

The use of polygraphs can be a helpful tool in mitigation, despite obvious problems of admissibility. Types of polygraphs that can be useful are an incident examination (for example, “Did you have sexual intercourse with x”), limited sexual history polygraph (“Not including [the charged incident], have you had sexual contact with any other minor”), full disclosure sexual history (anything and everything type of contact), or a confirmatory test (narrative explanation of an incident and then a question about whether or not the statement was true).

Passed polygraphs can greatly help in negotiation or mitigation of a sentence in order to show limited criminal conduct, isolated conduct, negate the question of uncharged conduct, and quell doubts about sexual deviancy. Psychosexual evaluators also rely on polygraph results in determining risk assessment.

Practice Tip

Polygraph tests on a collateral issue or which undermine the credibility of a state’s witness or theory can be helpful. For example, if a minor complainant says she was given alcohol and/or controlled substances by the accused, and the accused denies the use, a passed polygraph can undermine the credibility of that complainant on other issues (like whether or not she gave the accused her correct age).

E. Predisposition Treatment

Engagement with early treatment can prove a useful mitigation tactic. Where a case is certain to plea, or has little in the way of a defense (such as an age-based prosecution with a victim under 16), engagement with a therapist provides helpful resources on several fronts. Most importantly, a psychosexual evaluator would typically view engagement with treatment as positive, as it is evidence that there are no barriers to treatment. Typically, counsel should bar the treatment provider from discussing the case at issue until sentencing is completed.

Practice Tip

In some counties, failure to engage in post-incident/pre-plea treatment is a factor in negotiations, and the failure to be in treatment might remove a probation-style offer. Getting the accused into some type of treatment modality is always helpful. In addition, if there are allegations of controlled substances/alcohol use related to the alleged incident, then having the accused enroll in those treatment programs is a good idea. Finally, many individuals accused of sex crimes, especially crimes involving child pornography, suffer from some mental health disorder such as depression, bi-polar syndrome, OCD, or ADHD that may have contributed to the conduct. Evaluations for all of these possible mitigation issues can be woven into a psychosexual evaluation as well as a sex offender treatment modality.

F. Letters of Support

1. Get the names and contact information of possible supporters from your client.

2. Contact supporters directly or have your investigator contact them.

3. Have a plan for what you want from each person and get a feeling for whether they can express the need you have in a way that is persuasive to the DDA.

4. Let them write it in their own words and send to you in a draft.

5. Review the drafts and make sure there is nothing damaging or damning, and remember
the old adage, “The road to hell is paved with good intentions.”

6. If someone is particularly persuasive in person, do a short video of that person giving their reasons for supporting your client. Drop the video onto a CD or thumb drive and provide it to the DDA. This can be very compelling to both a DDA and a judge who can see and hear a person rather than just speed reading a letter (this is also good for sentencing to provide these videos to the judge).

7. Finalize the letters, save them as PDFs so they are not subject to amendment, send them to the writer to make sure that they approve and then send with your packet to the DDA as part of your negotiations (see below).

G. Non-Psychosexual Psychological Evaluations2

Many clients suffer from a variety of mental disabilities that may or may not be relevant to your negotiations (for example, see OCDLA manual on veterans, Still at War: A Guide for Defenders, Prosecutors and Judges Dealing with Oregon’s Veteran Defendant Crisis). Each potential disability can impact the behavior of the accused. For example, a person who suffers from obsessive compulsive disorder can explain how they meticulously kept and categorized types of child pornography. In addition, some disabilities can provide a defense regarding mental states of intentional or knowingly (similar to voluntary intoxication can negate intent in a sexual encounter).

H. Substance Abuse Evaluations

Generally, it is best to keep these to a letter from the provider which says the following: Client came to my office on this date for an evaluation. Based upon the applicable criteria, it is recommended that the client engage in the following treatment modality [describe the modality]. An exception to this course is if you are going to rely on a voluntary intoxication claim to negate the mental state of the crime. If that is the case, you want a more complex and detailed report from an expert who can also say that based upon the BAC at the time of the crime (if you have it), the reported behaviors of the person as observed by witnesses and/or self report regarding consumption by the accused.

III. Negotiations

A. The Dance

An interesting law review article details what motivates a prosecutor to engage in negotiations and drives their feelings about obtaining resolutions. See “Prosecutorial Passion, Cognitive Bias, and Plea Bargaining,” written by Alafair Burke, a former Multnomah County DDA and now a successful crime novelist.3 The article provides some insight into how to approach prosecutors and what, if anything, motivates them to seek resolutions. Also, remember that many things that motivate a prosecutor these days are driven by “committees,” where the prosecutor takes your case and your client’s story to a group of people with whom you may not be dealing directly and who, in the end, do not have to try the case if it does not settle. So, when negotiating with a prosecutor, realize that some “hide” behind their committees such that they can have cover by offering you something not as attractive as first contemplated. Others may move the committee to something you and the DDA agree on if you have provided the DDA with the support necessary to convince the committee that a particular resolution is appropriate.

Therefore, before you start negotiations, have a firm handle on a realistic resolution that is acceptable to your client. Also recognize that many times, especially in court appointed cases, the timing as to when to engage in negotiations is critical. In many instances, a rush to get an offer will be seen by a client as a “sell out.” Every client must trust the attorney who represents them such that if, and, or when the time comes to enter into negotiations and/or accept the “final offer,” the client has faith enough and trusts you to acknowledge an offer as being final and to followyour recommendation whether or not to accept that offer.

B. Discovery

Always file a “request for discovery” under ORS 135.815 and make it clear to the DDA that it would be unethical, and malpractice, to engage in plea negotiations prior to being provided all of the discovery in the case. If there is physical evidence from the execution of a search warrant, then request a form be sent to the property evidence room giving you or your agent permission to view, copy or photograph the evidence. Request that the DDA make an affirmative statement that all evidence has been provided under ORS 135(1)(g) and 2(a)4. Under the 2018 law (HB 4149, infra), this provision cannot be waived as a condition of plea negotiations, so make sure you get an affirmation from the DDA that it has been provided.

Practice Tip

The DDAs seem to be taking umbrage at this provision as “officers of the court,” but it is simple to explain that no matter what is said by any defense attorney, because it is statutorily required to be produced prior to any plea or sentencing, failure to obtain a letter in writing from the DDA that they have completed their obligations and provided all required under 135.815(1)(g) and (2)(a), if information is discovered later, post-conviction relief proceeding against the defense attorney would be fairly quick. Get assurances that all of the statutory obligations had been complied with by the DDA.

C. Starting Negotiations

Each case involves it’s own rhythm for negotiations, and each DDA is different. First, start with the statutory scheme, ORS 135.405-135.415. Read and understand the various provisions of this statutory scheme, especially with the passage of 2017 c.650 § 85 and HB 4149 (2018)6. Make sure that whenever you send any mitigation to the DDA that you include a cover letter and/or specific provision at the top of the email and/or correspondence that unequivocally states: “For purposes of plea negotiations only pursuant to ORS 135.415.” Also know your obligations as set forth by the statute, sometimes it helps to let your client know them as well. ORS 135.425 states the following:

135.425 Responsibilities of defense counsel. 

(1) Defense counsel shall conclude a plea agreement only with the consent of the defendant, and shall insure that the decision whether to enter a plea of guilty or no contest is ultimately made by the defendant.

(2) To aid the defendant in reaching a decision, defense counsel, after appropriate investigation, shall advise the defendant of the alternatives available and of factors considered important by the defense counsel or the defendant in reaching a decision. 

Practice Tip

If you plan on providing the DDA with mitigation, request from them in advance an assurance that they will only use it for purposes of negotiations, that the documents are privileged and private, may not be used for any purpose other than plea negotiations and must be returned to the defense if no resolution is reached or after sentencing, whichever comes later.

DDAs want to see this information and more likely than not will agree to such a request. If not, point out that your request is no different than a request by a child abuse center to not further disseminate the materials and return them to the CAC after the trial.

Finally, you can seek a protective order from the court under ORS 135.873(2) or (3) for any information that you are providing to the DDA and the Ccourt will determine the rules regarding disclosure and production.

D. Draft Motions

Sometimes if there are specific legal issues in play, it can be helpful to draft your motions (compel, suppress, demurrer, etc.) and provide those drafts to the DDA. Certainly this is a tactical decision as it may give the DDA more opportunity to research the issues or, in the case of suppression motions, confer with law enforcement officers who might remember new details after seeing your motion. However, sometimes it is helpful, especially in cases where the filing of a potentially successful demurrer could cause the DDA to have to return to a grand jury for a new indictment.

E. Meet in Person

Many DA offices, especially in serious cases, are now not making initial offers but, rather, waiting for the defense attorney to provide mitigation and an initial offer. This is akin to bargaining against yourselves. Therefore, it can be advantageous to provide your mitigation packet to the DDA with a request for a personal meeting and not requesting a specific resolution. Many times these are accepted, and this can be very informative to see where the prosecutor believes the floor to be, how the committee might view the case, and what offer will be seen as so unreasonable that negotiations would abruptly end. The “ceiling” and “floor” are important to understand, so it is best to be prepared not to ask for probation in a case that screams for some prison sentence unless you have some strong mitigation and legal or factual issues that support the lower resolution.

F. Judicial Settlement Conferences

ORS 135.432 sets forth the specific provisions for judicial participation in plea negotiations. Many counties have judges who are designated for judicial settlement conferences or a JSC. Each of those judges, in turn, have what they would like to have in front of them in order to conduct an effective JSC. Some judges are more forceful with the parties than others but all are, in part, acting under the provision that no judge can bind the parties and that all discussions, pursuant to statute, are “advisory.”

Practice Tip

It seems to be advantageous to set up and engage in a JSC before any initial discussions and before any offer has been made by the DDA. In addition, check with the JSC judge in advance to know what they expect to receive from you and how far in advance of the JSC to expect it in order to be prepared to conduct the JSC effectively.

IV. Sentencing

Sentencing in a sexual offense case presents a number of challenges ranging from probationary obligations, mandatory prison sentences, maximum post-prison terms, parole issues and collateral consequences such as sexual offender registration obligations.

A. Mandatory Sentences

There are a number of sex offenses that carry mandatory prison terms. The traditional Measure 11 offenses are found in ORS 137.700 and include:

  • Rape in the First Degree under subsection 1(a), 1(c) and 1(d) (100 months)
  • Rape in the Second Degree (75 Months)
  • Sodomy in the First Degree under 1(a), 1(c), 1(d) (100 months)
  • Sodomy in the Second Degree (75 months)
  • Unlawful Sexual Penetration in the First Degree under 1(a) or 1(c) (100 months)
  • Unlawful Sexual Penetration in the Second Degree (75 months)
  • Sexual Abuse in the First Degree (75 months)
  • Using a Child in a Display of Sexually Explicit Conduct (70 months)
  • Compelling Prostitution (70 months).

These same penalties apply to juvenile offenders as young as 15 under ORS 137.707.

Certain offenses, or subsections of offenses, demonstrate mandatory minimums on steroids. Otherwise known as Jessica’s Law, these penalties attach to crimes against children under the age of 12.

The following all carry a mandatory minimum prison term of 300 months:

  • Rape in the First Degree
  • Sodomy in the First Degree
  • Unlawful Sexual Penetration in the First Degree
  • Kidnapping in the First Degree (when committed for the purpose of one of the Jessica’s Law offenses)

These requirements are found in ORS 137.700, et seq.

Likewise, ORS 137.690 requires a 25-year mandatory minimum term for any “major felony sex crime” if the offender has one or more prior convictions for a “major felony sex crime.” Notably, these “priors” can emerge from the same sentencing proceeding so long as they are from separate criminal episodes.

B. How Mandatory Is Mandatory?

1. Escape Clause Provisions

ORS 137.712 governs exceptions to the mandatory minimum provisions in some Measure 11 offenses. There are no statutory exceptions for 137.690 nor for Jessica’s Law offenses. The sex offenses that fall within the exceptions to mandatory minimums are:

  • Rape in the Second Degree
  • Sexual Abuse in the First Degree
  • Sodomy in the Second Degree
  • Unlawful Sexual Penetration in the Second Degree

Each exception to a mandatory minimum has a number of qualifications, which typically include the offender not having priors within ORS 137.700, be an age-based offense, and require the court to make particular findings. Once an accused is out of the confines of ORS 137.700 they are subject to the felony sentencing guidelines, which can include either a downward durational departure or a downward disposition to probation.

2. Proportionality Clause

The Oregon Supreme Court dropped a bombshell on Measure 11 when State v. Rodriguez/Buck, 347 Or 47 (2009), was released. Ms. Rodriguez was convicted of sexual abuse in the first degree when the back of the victim’s head came into contact with the accused’s clothed breasts. Mr. Buck’s hand remained on the victim’s clothed buttock and the accused also wiped dirt off the back of the victim’s shorts. Recognizing that conduct such as sexual abuse in the first degree7 can encompass a broad range of behavior, the court found that this conduct was “insufficiently grave” and “less severe than the cases in the vast majority of…other reported first degree sexual abuse cases since Measure 11 was passed.”

Rodriguez/Buck predicated consideration of disproportionality of the sentences on three nonexclusive factors:

a. Comparison of the severity of the penalty and the gravity of the crime (amount of time offender must spend in jail or prison).

b. Comparison of penalties imposed for other related crimes.

c. Criminal history of the accused.

They went on to consider the “specific circumstances of the facts of accused’s conduct that come within the statutory definition of the offense as well as other case specific factors, such as characteristics of the accused and the victim, the harm to the victim, and the relationship between the accused and the victim.”

Following is a sampling of cases found not to be disproportionate by the court:

  • Padilla, 277 Or App 440 (2016) (the accused rubbed his penis on the victim and was found to be in a position of trust with the victim, accused had no priors).
  • State v. Camcho-Garcia, 268 Or App 75 (2014) (assessing gravity of offense is “specific defendant’s particular conduct toward the victim that constituted the crime, as well as the general definition of the crime in the statute. To determine whether the penalty is proportioned, it is appropriate to consider the gravity of the instant conduct in comparison with other criminal conduct in light of the relative harm to victims and society and relative culpability”).
  • State v. Alwinger, 236 Or App 240 (2010) (25-year mandatory minimum not disproportionate where defendant’s conduct involved single act of digital penetration of three-year-old child where defendant had sexually related prior convictions).
  • State v. Simonson, 243 Or App 535, 259 P3d 962 (2011) (violation of Article I, section 16, where a penalty is disproportionately severe when compared to a sentence that may be imposed for other related crimes).8
  • State v. Burge, 252 Or App 574, 288 P3d 565 (2012) (court rejected state argument that Simonson was wrongly decided and reversed the sentence imposed by the trial court).
  • Sexton v. Persson, 268 Or App 63, 341 P.3d 881 (2014).

In Sexton, the court clarified a footnote in Simonson regarding the proportionality analysis:

In a footnote in Simonson, we stated that vertical proportionality is “measured by the sentences available for the conduct at issue, not on what any individual defendant actually receives” and appeared to suggest that an analysis of vertical proportionality requires a comparison with the minimum sentence available for the more serious crime. 243 Or App at 542 n. 8, 259 P3d 962. However, as we have explained on a number of occasions, to determine whether a sentence is disproportionate for purposes of an Article I, section 16, we compare the actual sentence a defendant received with the maximum sentence allowed for the greater crime. See Barajas, 254 Or App at 111, 292 P3d 636; Dobash, 210 Or App at 147, 149 P3d 1235; [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=7772064987914525574&q=Koch,+169+Or.+App.,+7+P.3d+769&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 Koch], 169 Or App at 227, 7 P3d 769; see also [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=12498319768823710840&q=Shumway,+291+Or.,+630+P.2d+796+&hl=en&as_sdt=4,38 Shumway], 291 Or at 163–64, 630 P2d 796 (comparing the required sentence for intentional homicide with the required sentence for aggravated intentional homicide). To the extent we suggested otherwise in the Simonson footnote, that suggestion was contrary to precedent and we disavow it.

Sexton v. Persson, 268 Or App 63, 70, 341 P3d 881, 885 (2014) (emphasis supplied).

Practice Tip

In both Simonson and Burge, the Court of Appeals found that the crime of Rape III (sexual intercourse or sexually deviant intercourse with a person under the age of 16) was a more serious crime than the crime of Sex Abuse II (sexual intercourse or deviant sexual intercourse without consent). Counsel for the accused should always review the crimes charged and compare to determine if there is a proportionality argument under Simonson and Burge.

C. Presumptive Life Terms—“Three Strikes Law”

ORS 137.719 is a recidivist statute. This statute proscribes a presumptive sentence of life without the possibility of parole for an offender convicted of a felony sex crime who has been sentenced for felony sex crimes in two prior sentencing proceedings.

Counsel should note the following about this harsh statute:

  • The sentence is presumptive. In other words, any other sentence “authorized by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission” is possible. See State v. Rhoades, 210 Or App 280 (2006).
  • The requisite prior must arise from separate sentencing proceedings. In other words, a defendant cannot be elevated to a true life sentence in the same proceeding even if all of the sentences arise from separate criminal episodes joined together for trial.
  • What is a sentence and what is required to prove a prior felony sentencing occurred? See Gordon v. Hall, 232 Or App 174 (2009) (finding pre-guidelines California probation to not constitute a sentence); State v. Mollette, 255 Or App 29 (2013) (rejecting unpreserved argument on the level of proof required at a sentencing hearing).
  • The prior offenses, if from another jurisdiction, must be nearly identical. “In sum, the text of ORS 137.719(3) supports (as one of two alternative primary meanings) the conclusion that a ‘comparable offense’ under ORS 13.719(3)(b)(B) must closely match the elements of an Oregon felony sex crime, and the context of that statute indicates that that more restrictive primary meaning is the one that the legislature likely intended. Accordingly, we conclude that the term ‘comparable offenses’ in that statute refers to offenses with elements that are the same as or nearly the same as the elements of an Oregon felony sex crime, not to offenses that merely share a core similarity with such an offense.” State v. Carlton, 361 Or 29, 43 (2016).
  • A true life term may violate the proportionality clause of the Oregon Constitution. See State v. Davidson, 271 Or App 719 (2015) (review allowed) (finding such a sentence did violate proportionality where the offenses were the defendant committed five acts of public masturbation without indication of violence); State v. Sokell, 273 Or App 654 (2015); State v. Wheeler, 343 OR 652 (2007); State v. D. Two Strikes Law

In 2017 the governor signed into law Senate Bill 10509. This act uses the same structure as ORS 137.719 and presumes a true life penalty for any repeat offender of rape in the first degree, sodomy in the first degree, unlawful sexual penetration in the first degree (or equivalent offenses in another state or federal court). Under the provisions of SB 1050, the priors must be from a different sentencing proceeding, not the same sentencing proceeding. See the appendix to this chapter for the text of the bill and OCDLA’s bill summary prepared by lobbyist Mary Sofia for the 2017 Legislative Analysis.

Notably, practitioners would have the same proportionality challenges to the imposition of such a sentence and the court can impose a departure sentence authorized by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission based upon findings of substantial and compelling reasons.

E. Post-Prison Supervision

An offender can be subject to lifetime post-prison supervision if they fall within the confines of ORS 137.765. This statute requires the state to show someone to have “psychopathic personality features, sexually deviant arousal patterns, or interest and a history of sexual assault and present a substantial probability of committing a [serious sexual offense].” If these facts, and more, can be proven to a jury,10 then the offender is subjected to lifetime supervision. Notably, after 10 years a person sentenced as a sexually violent dangerous offender may petition the court for a reduction in sentence under ORS 137.771.

Regardless, an offender convicted of most sexual offenses is subjected to a maximum determinate term of post-prison supervision when added together with the term of incarceration. Practitioners should also review ORS 144.635, 144.642, and Weems v. Board of Parole and Post Prison Supervision, 347 Or 586 (2010), for other supervisory issues.

F. Limitations on Sentences

Counsel should always review the sentencing limitations that appear in the Felony Sentencing Guidelines such as the “shift to I rule” and “200/400% rule.” State v. Worth, 274 Or App 1 (2015) (consecutive sentence structure violated the 400% rule where the court prescribed a determinate dangerous offender sentence).

1. Merger

Merger is a complicated topic with an extensive body of case law. The fundamental principle of merger is that despite a finding of guilt on multiple offenses, the separate offenses combine together become a single conviction. ORS 161.067 is the statutory basis for the argument as to when convictions merge:

(1) When the same conduct or criminal episode violates two or more statutory provisions and each provision requires proof of an element that the others do not, there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are separate statutory violations.

(2) When the same conduct or criminal episode, though violating only one statutory provision involves two or more victims, there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are victims. However, two or more persons owning joint interests in real or personal property shall be considered a single victim for purposes of determining the number of separately punishable offenses if the property is the subject of one of the following crimes:

(a) Theft as defined in ORS 164.015.

(b) Unauthorized use of a vehicle as defined in ORS 164.135.

(c) Criminal possession of rented or leased personal property as defined in ORS 164.140.

(d) Criminal possession of a rented or leased motor vehicle as defined in ORS 164.138.

(e) Burglary as defined in ORS 164.215 or 164.225.

(f) Criminal trespass as defined in ORS 164.243, 164.245, 164.255, 164.265 or 164.278.

(g) Arson and related offenses as defined in ORS 164.315, 164.325 or 164.335.

(h) Forgery and related offenses as defined in ORS 165.002 to 165.070.

(3) When the same conduct or criminal episode violates only one statutory provision and involves only one victim, but nevertheless involves repeated violations of the same statutory provision against the same victim, there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are violations, except that each violation, to be separately punishable under this subsection, must be separated from other such violations by a sufficient pause in the defendant’s criminal conduct to afford the defendant an opportunity to renounce the criminal intent. Each method of engaging in deviate sexual intercourse as defined in ORS 163.305, and each method of engaging in unlawful sexual penetration as defined in ORS 163.408 and 163.411 shall constitute separate violations of their respective statutory provisions for purposes of determining the number of statutory violations.

There is an excellent collection of materials on merger on OCDLA’s Library of Defense, which is regularly updated: https://libraryofdefense.ocdla.org/Merger. Merger occurs most commonly in sex cases when the offenses contain a lesser included or a functional equivalent, or where the same offense occurs in quick succession so closely in time that there is no sufficient pause between the separate counts.

First, an indictment may charge offenses that are essentially lesser-included offenses, functional equivalents, or allege different theories of the same statutory provision. All situations are ripe for merger. This route to merger is found in subsection (1) of ORS 161.067, which only allows for separate convictions if three conditions are met: the conduct was a single criminal episode, (2) an accused’s acts violated two or more statutory provisions, and (3) each statutory provisions requires proof of an element that the other does not. State v. Pass, 264 Or App 583 (2014). Notably, the phrase “criminal episode” is not altogether obvious and something to be explored.

Examples of recent merger cases under subsection (1) include:

  • State v. Breshears, 281 Or App 552 (2016): the court held that Sex Abuse 2 and Rape 3 merge because proving that a victim is under 16 necessarily proves that victim is incapable of consent, as Sex Abuse 2 requires.
  • State v. Pass, 264 Or App 583 (2014): the court ruled that Sex Abuse 2 and Sodomy 3 merge where each alleges “deviate sexual intercourse” and the former alleging the victim to be under 18 and the latter alleging the victim to be under 16.
  • State v. Black, 270 Or App 583 (2014): merger should occur where different counts of Sex Abuse 1 allege different subtheories of the offense, which are not separate statutory provisions (forcible compulsion and physical helplessness); see also State v. Parkins, 346 Or 333 (2009).

Second, repeated acts of abuse under the same statutory provision against a single victim in the same criminal episode (there’s that phrase again) may merge unless the State can prove there was a sufficient pause for an accused. State v. McConville, 243 Or App 275 (2011).

Practice Tip

Although cases have repeatedly referenced the court as the factfinder, there does not appear to be binding case law regarding whether such a finding triggers Blakely/Apprendi procedural protections. However, see State v. Reed, 256 Or App 6 (2013), holding that whether or not a pause exists is a question of law and not fact).

Fundamentally, the state is tasked at sentencing with determining whether or not there was a sufficient—or sometimes referred to as “significant” (State v. Watkins, 236 Or App 339 (2010))—pause between criminal acts. If an accused is found to have “paused” and then continued with other acts, then those acts or counts, despite being part of the same criminal episode, would not merge. Leading and recent cases are:

  • State v. Williams, 284 Or App 194 (2017): the Court requiring merger between counts of sex abuse because [___] “does not contain evidence that would allow a nonspeculative interference ‘that something of significance’ occurred between defendant’s sequential acts of touching.”
  • State v. Quandry Nelson II, 282 Or App 427 (2016): Court of Appeals reversed a trial court’s determination that there was not a sufficient pause between Sex Abuse 1 and Sex Abuse 3; not finding evidence of a sufficient pause between two counts of Sex Abuse 1 and one count of Sex Abuse 3 that occurred in the same course of conduct in a confined space and no “significant” event separated the various counts. Specifically rejects state’s argument that successive acts cannot constitute “same conduct or criminal episode.”
  • State v. Avila, 283 Or App 262 (2016): merger not precluded merely by the accused’s contact with different body parts of victim.
  • State v. West-Howell, 282 Or App 393 (2016): In this case, the accused and victim were married and a physical altercation began in the home and moved into the bedroom. Inside of the bedroom an act of Sodomy 1 occurred (forcible compulsion theory). The accused then choked the victim to unconsciousness and tried to rape her. Failing that, he again committed an act of forcible compulsion sodomy. The court’s essential legal summary on merger between the various counts of sodomy is that one crime must end before another begins and each crime must be separated from the others by a sufficient pause in the accused’s criminal conduct to afford him an opportunity to renounce his criminal intent. Here, strangling the victim and attempting to rape her was the sufficient pause. See also State v. King, 261 Or App 650 (2014), for what intervening acts can constitute sufficient or significant pauses.
  • State v. Reeves, 250 Or App 294 (2012): in order for the downloading of child porn not to merge, the state must show the files are not downloaded concurrently. See also State v. Cale, 263 Or App 635 (2014) (rejecting the state’s argument that “Each time you take a picture of a child, it’s a separate crime”).
  • State v. Sanders, 185 Or App 125 (2002): “Mere passage of time by itself does not establish that one assault ended before the other began.”
  • State v. Dugan, 282 Or App 768 (2016): Court rejected the state’s argument that the mere sequential nature of an accused’s touching and opportunity to retreat were sufficient pauses that would bar merger. Here, the accused touched the victim’s breasts and vagina over the course of about ten minutes.

2. Criminal Episodes

OCDLA’s Felony Sentencing in Oregon: Guidelines, Statutes, Cases contains an excellent review of the nuances of criminal episodes. By way of review, and important to the practitioner in this context, a court may only consider convictions for purposes of calculating the offender’s criminal history that emerge in the same sentencing proceeding if the convictions do not arise out of a single criminal episode. State v. Cuevas, 358 Or 147 (2015), at 149-50. In other words, a “court may use a defendant’s convictions arising from earlier criminal episodes to calculate the defendant’s criminal history score with respect to a crime arising from a later criminal episode.” State v. Witherspoon, 250 Or App 316, 321, 280 P3d 1004 (2012).

Courts are now instructed to examine the evidentiary record and determine if there is sufficient evidence to demonstrate that crimes were committed on separate days, even if they were ultimately discovered at the same time. In State v. Dulfu, 282 Or App 209 (2016) (review accepted), the defendant’s contention was rejected—that because all images of child pornography were discovered on a single day it was one single criminal episode but, rather, since the images were acquired on different days, they were separate criminal episodes. This is an important case to watch.

G. Constitutional Considerations on Supervisory Conditions

Sex offense supervision is rife with enhanced restrictions on associations and liberty. Generically, most sex offense supervisions require treatment, polygraph testing, refraining from frequenting places visited by children, no contact with children, and obtaining permission to use the internet, among a myriad of other conditions. There is an emerging body of case law regarding whether or not some of these restrictions violate constitutional rights to intimate association11 and the probationer’s right to remain silent.

The right to intimate association has long been recognized in the United States Constitution and is considered “central” to any notion of liberty. Roberts v. Jaycees, 468 US 609 (1984). Additionally, liberty restrictions which are part of a probation must not be overbroad and are where less burdensome conditions satisfy the same end. State v. Bassett, 269 Or App 701 (2015). Counsel should object to association restrictions (reporting new “intimate relationships,”) as not reasonably related to the purposes of probation and not narrowly tailored sufficient to avoid infringement on the probationer’s constitutional rights. See United States v. Brock, 2017 WL 3027078 (2017) (vacating notification requirement for upon establishment of “significant romantic relationship”).

Polygraphs are routinely ordered by sentencing courts, required by probation officers, and are also incorporated into sex offense treatment programs. Quite foreseeably, the probationer is faced with a dilemma of refusing to take a polygraph or disclose information that could be harmful to their probation—or even lead to new charges. In United States v. Antelope, 395 F.3d 1128 (Ninth Cir. 1995), the probationer refused to take polygraphs without the government first granting immunity over any response he may provide. As a result, his probation was revoked and he was sentenced to prison. The Ninth Circuit reversed the district court and required that any past sexual history polygraph questions do not have to be answered by Antelope (if may they trigger liability) until the government provides immunity for those acts. Counsel should assure that any conditions of probation involving mandatory polygraphs allow for “use and derivative use immunity.”

There are also several Oregon cases that address this issue—two of the main ones are State v. Tenbusch, 131 Or App 634, 886 P2d 1077 (1994), and State v. Gaither, 196 Or App 131, 100 P3d 768 (2004) (statement to probation officer regarding sexual history was involuntary but defendant failed to show nexus between that disclosure and later “voluntary” confession to police and therefore confession to police admissible). Under Oregon law, the accused has to show (1) the statement to probation officer was involuntary and (2) facial nexus between that statement and later statement to police. Also, the accused can refuse to answer the questions presented and, if that happens, then address whether that refusal constitutes a violation of the terms and conditions of probation. See also United States v. Saechao, 418 F.3d 1073 (2005) (holding that probationer was compelled to give up information and distinguishing
Tenbusch and Gaither in footnote 6).12

Practice Tip

Read Tenbusch, Gaither and Saechao; they are interwoven and give insight into how to advise your client once they are faced with these Hobson’s choices while on probation or post-prison supervision. Saechao is a Reinhardt opinion with 22 Negative Treatments, so there are lots of subsequent Ninth Circuit panels accepting the Saechao analysis and holding and then distinguish it.

As the internet encroaches more on daily life, use restrictions for probationers and convicted sex offenders prove increasingly burdensome and often completely unnecessary. The Supreme Court struck down a North Carolina statute barring any sex offender from social network websites as violative of free speech. Packingham v. North Carolina, 137 S. Ct. 1730 (2017). In Brock, infra, this restriction was found acceptable in a probation context.

V. Conclusion

Building successful arguments for plea negotiations and sentencing starts early. Investigate the available tools, think beyond the normal mitigation and discovery, and determine what will be helpful versus hurtful to your client. Unless you are obtaining an acquittal, your role as a plea and sentencing negotiator is the most significant way you will assist your client.
