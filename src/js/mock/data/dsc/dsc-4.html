I. Introduction

Representing a person charged with a sex crime presents a host of unique challenges. While many of the more ostensible challenges are addressed in the other chapters of this manual, it is worth noting that even the basics of criminal procedure receive special treatment in the context of sexual abuse or sexual assault.

It is essential to take a proactive approach in evidence gathering. Unlike many other criminal cases the defense attorney cannot rely primarily on the state’s statutory and constitutional discovery obligations to ensure that they are operating with even the most basic information required to present an adequate defense.

Myriad statutes and constitutional provisions limit disclosure of essential evidence. The prosecutor will likely utilize those to reduce the information disclosed to the defendant without demand. Expect that the prosecutor will take an aggressive position in determining what information they must produce in the discovery process.

Every case is different and there is no universal approach. Nevertheless, there are recurring themes, agencies, obstacles and dilemmas in sex crime defense. This chapter endeavors to serve as a resource for some of the most common roadblocks in gathering evidence when defending someone charged with a sex crime.

The chapter is broken into three sections. The first section briefly explores the statutory and constitutional discovery obligations of both parties. The section provides the defense attorney with an understanding of the evidence he should and should not expect to have once the prosecutor has fulfilled her responsibilities. The second section delves into subpoenas and the in camera review process. This “nuts and bolts” section provides the framework for obtaining necessary evidence that is not produced in discovery. The final section deals with common areas of focus in the evidence gathering process. We strive to alert the practitioner to potential pitfalls in evidence gathering
and provide work-arounds where possible.

II. Discovery Statutes

A. Statutory Discovery Scheme

1. Statutory Discovery: In General (ORS 135.805 – ORS 135.873)

ORS 135.805 – ORS 135.873 govern pretrial discovery in Oregon. These statutes set forth the materials the prosecutor and defense attorney must share with each other. The statutory discovery obligations are based, in part, on the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution and Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution. In 2013, ORS 135.815(1) (g) was added to the prosecutor’s discovery obligation as an effort to codify the requirements of Brady v. Maryland, 373 US 82 (1964).

Practice Tip

When arguing that the prosecutor has failed to meet his statutory discovery obligations, object under ORS 135.815, Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution, and the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution.

2. Statutory Discovery: What to Expect

We cannot expect to simply open the files of the district attorney for a general discovery of their contents. State v. Tranchell, 243 Or 215 (1966). Accordingly, we cannot assume that we have a copy of all information the prosecutor has in her possession. In addition to attorney work product and the like, images of child pornography and video recordings from child advocacy centers are excluded from discovery. The reasons behind these omissions and others are discussed further in
this section.

The chart provided below briefly summarizes the most common discovery obligations of the prosecutor and defense attorney. It is not an exhaustive list. For a more thorough discussion of statutory discovery law and practice, see Chapter 2 in the OCDLA Trial Notebook.

Practice Tip

Defense attorneys should be aware that the statutory obligation to disclose impeachment materials against the state’s witnesses can begin prior to the witness’s testimony. In other words, the defense attorney needs to meaningfully consider the testimony they expect each witness to provide at trial. Simply presuming that a state’s witness will testify “truthfully” and therefore there will be no need to impeach is not satisfactory. If you believe you are in possession of potential impeachment materials, you likely need to discover them to the state under ORS 135.835. Case law requires attorneys to “reasonably predict” the need for impeachment materials and requires timely production to the prosecutor. See State v. Burdge, 295 Or App 1 (1983) (impeachment witnesses), and State v. Young, 94 Or App 683 (1989) (exhibits). It is important to inform potential witnesses and investigators not to leave voicemails or send emails that could constitute witness statements required to be turned over.

B. Possession or Control

ORS 135.815(1) requires the prosecutor to produce materials and information within his possession or control. Recent cases have brought into question what is “directly” obtainable by the state and, thus, within its possession or control.

As we held in State v. Wixom, 275 Or App 824, 831-32 (2015), “[t]he legal standard for prosecutorial control of records is not disputed. As we have recently explained, ‘if an entity is required to disclose records to the district attorney or to police, the prosecution has control over the records and, accordingly, must disclose to a defendant any of those records that fall within ORS 135.815.’ State v. Daniels, 261 Or App 519, 528 rev. den, 355 Or 668 (2014) (emphasis in original). And, ‘information that the prosecutor may obtain directly is within the prosecutor’s “control” *** even if it was not in the prosecutor’s physical possession.’ State v. Warren, 304 Or 428, 433 (1987); accord State ex rel Wilson v. Thomas, 74 Or App 137, 141, rev. den 300 Or 64 (1985) (‘[T]he police are an arm of the prosecution for the purposes of the discovery statute’).” (Omission
in original.)

State v. Bray, 281 Or App 584, 596 (2016), review allowed, 361 Or 543 (2017).

For a more complete analysis of “possession and control” as it relates to DHS records please see discussion of State v. Wixom, 275 Or App 824 (2015), in IV. E. below.

C. Prosecutor’s Federal Due Process Obligations

Ultimately, “the individual prosecutor has a duty to learn of any favorable evidence known to the others acting on the government’s behalf in the case, including the police.” See Kyles v. Whitley, 514 US 419 at 437. Under the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, the state must disclose all information favorable to the defense in respect to guilt or punishment that is within its “actual or constructive possession.” Bray, 281 Or App at 601. Constructive possession extends to information “known to the others acting on the government’s behalf in the case, including the police,” and the prosecutor has a “duty to learn of any favorable evidence” possessed by all agents of the prosecution. Kyles at 437. Even an innocent failure to learn of favorable evidence known to police violates this standard. United States v. Price, 566 F3d 900 (9th Cir. 2009); United States v. Kohring, 637 F3d 895 (9th Circuit 2011) (Brady obligations extended to impeachment materials of state’s witnesses under Giglio).

This constitutional obligation to “learn of” and disclose favorable evidence pretrial may not be limited by the prosecutor’s “speculative prediction about the likely materiality of favorable evidence . . . because it is just too difficult to analyze before trial whether particular evidence ultimately will prove to be ‘material’ after trial.” United States v. Olsen, 704 F.3d 1172, 1183 n.3 (9th Cir. 2013) (good discussion and case citations of why pretrial disclosure does not require a post-trial Brady materiality analysis). Compare, United States v. Lloyd, 992 F.2d 348, 351 (D.C. Cir. 1993) (evidence should be disclosed as “material” if it will play an important role in uncovering admissible evidence, aiding witness preparation, corroborating testimony, or assisting impeachment or rebuttal). Making a demand expressly under the Fourteenth Amendment is also necessary to obtain post-conviction review in the federal system once state remedies are exhausted.

D. Motion to Compel Discovery

Use ORS 135.865 to file motions to compel discovery even when there is no reason to
believe the district attorney is not complying with his statutory discovery obligations. It is important to subpoena investigating officers to bring their notebooks, handwritten notes, e-mails, text messages and phone records. There is no reason to believe that when a DDA goes to a law enforcement officer, or any witness, that the witness provided that DDA with all relevant material. Even if you do not succeed in obtaining a court order for the material in question, you will learn the basis for the prosecutor’s failure to produce the material. This is helpful in planning future efforts to obtain the materials, and helps clarify your record for appellate review. In addition to statutory grounds to compel discovery, assert Article I, section 11, and the Fourteenth Amendment as well.

Before filing a motion to compel, it is necessary to document your demand for specific information reasonably believed to exist within the “possession or control” of the prosecutor. This can be done either by filing a demand for discovery pleading, or more commonly, by writing a letter to the prosecutor that can later be attached as an exhibit to your motion. In either method, it is important to cite specific authority for each record you seek. This demand becomes the outline for your motion.

Practice Tip

Start a list of “missing” discovery when you review what has been provided. This requires careful inspection of police reports, which may list involved individuals whose statements are not in the report, refer to another officer’s report which has not been provided, or reference involved agencies whose records have not been disclosed. Defense investigation may produce reason to believe that other discoverable information exists. Remember, Brady includes information that could impeach the state’s witnesses, and is not limited to written material or to the statements of witnesses the state intends to call. State v. Fleischman, 10 Or App 22, 32 (1972), State v. Fortune, 112 Or App 247, rev den 313 Or 627 (1992). For example, detectives’ notes and changes to a witness’s statement not reduced to writing, are worth seeking as likely impeachment information.

Courts tend to accept blanket assurances by the prosecutor that the state has fully complied with its discovery obligations, and reject broad categorical defense requests—“all impeachment material,” “all officer notes”—so any broad category should be further defined as “including but not limited to” very specific records or information from specific sources. Be prepared to argue, if not prove, 1) why you believe the information requested exists, 2) why you believe it is within the state’s “possession or control” and 3) why the information is important to the defense. Anticipate the state’s most common responses: “fishing expedition” and “we don’t have it.” Also subpoena the investigating officers with comprehensive subpoenas duces tecum. Argue that your SDTs are not subject to Cartwright as you are only demanding production of what is required under 135.815(g) and Brady and its progeny (see section III).

Also file public records requests with the DA’s office, police department, sheriff’s department or any government agency that you think might have emails, documents or text messages related to your case.

E. Material Commonly Excluded from Statutory Discovery Obligations

1. Attorney Work Product

ORS 135.855(1) (a) explicitly excludes the production of work product, legal research, records, correspondence, reports or memorandum to the extent that they contain the opinions, theories or conclusions of the attorneys, peace officers or
their agents in connection with the investigation, prosecution or defense of a criminal action.

Practice Tip

Attorney work product must be asserted and found to exist by the trial court, so do not simply accept that there is some work product privilege where it is being used to hide Brady/Kyles material. Also, under State v. Riddle, the court concluded there is no absolute privilege arising out of OEC 503, the work-product doctrine that prevents a litigant’s expert, employed to investigate a factual problem, from testifying for the other side as to the expert’s thoughts and conclusions that are segregated from confidential communications. So if denied in discovery under work product, it could still be admissible at trial if the record is made.

2. Grand Jury Notes/Recordings

ORS 135.855(1)(c) explicitly excludes the production of transcripts, recordings or memoranda of testimony of witnesses before the grand jury, except transcripts or recordings of statements made by the defendant. This has changed with the 2017
passage of Senate Bill 505, but as of March 1, 2018, grand jury testimony is recorded only in select counties—those “comprising a judicial district with a population between 150,000 and 300,000 or over 700,000.”

Practice Tip

If you believe a witness testified differently before the grand jury than in other stages of the investigation, file a motion to compel grand jury notes/recording pursuant to ORS 132.220(1), ORS 135.815(1)(g) and ORS 135.865. See Section IV. Consider drafting or requesting a protective order pursuant to ORS 135.873 to quell the prosecutor’s arguments about necessity of grand jury secrecy.

3. Child Advocacy Center Videos

ORS 418.794 prevents the disclosure of a video obtained at a child advocacy center without a lawful subpoena and protective order. In many jurisdictions the prosecutor will not copy the video of a child witness’s interview pursuant to ORS 418.794. While you should be able to view the video, you will need to subpoena your own copy for meaningful investigation. See section IV addressing how to obtain records from child advocacy centers. Also attached is a motion and protective order for CARES Northwest.

4. Child Pornography

The Adam Walsh Child Protection and Safety Act of 2006 has negatively impacted a defendant’s rights regarding discovery in child pornography cases. Codified at 18 U.S.C. §3509(m), the Adam Walsh Act provides:

…the court shall deny, in any criminal proceeding, any request by the defendant to copy, photograph, duplicate or otherwise reproduce any property or material that constitutes child pornography, so long as the Government makes the property or material reasonably available to the defendant.

The statute explains:

[these materials are] reasonably available to the defendant if the Government provides ample opportunity for inspection, viewing, and examination at a Government facility of the property or material by the defendant, his or her attorney, and any individual the defendant may seek to qualify to furnish expert testimony at trial.

The federal constitutionality of this provision has been litigated, and mostly upheld under the Fourteenth Amendment, in federal district courts. See U.S. v. Butts, 2006 WL 3613364, U.S. v. Knellinger, 471 F. Supp. 2d 640 (ED Va. 2007), and U.S. v. O’Rourke, 470 F Supp 2d 1049 (D Ariz 2007).

Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution provides no clear remedy to this limitation, and the defense attorney must prepare to view and examine child pornography in the possession of the prosecutor.

III. Subpoenaing Records

Often the evidence provided through the discovery process is insufficient to afford an adequate defense. To that end, the defense attorney must expect to obtain much of the necessary evidence on her own. A working knowledge of the subpoena statutes and case law as well as an understanding of the in camera review process is essential. This section highlights the important matters of concern in these two areas.

A. Subpoena to Trial or Evidentiary Hearing (Ordinary Subpoena) – ORS 136.580(1)

ORS 136.580(1) is the statutory authority authorizing the subpoena of records to a trial or evidentiary hearing. Defense counsel is authorized to subpoena records to trial without prior authorization from the court. Although the statute gives the defense attorney the right to subpoena the records to trial, whether the court will provide the records to the party is a secondary question. These two issues are addressed below.

If books, papers, or documents are required, a direction to the following effect shall be added to the form provided in ORS 136.575: “And you are required, also, to bring with you the following: (describing intelligibly the books, papers or documents required).

ORS 136.580(1). Case law in this area has established that the “books, papers and documents” provision of the statute is not limited to printed material. It can also include other tangible objects and electronic information, as follows:

• Computer hard drive, an image of the hard drive, and the contents within. State v. Bray, 281 Or App 584 (2016).

• Audiotapes. State v. Cartwright, 336 Or 408, 414 n.4 (2004).

• Compelling a witness to provide a handwriting exemplar. State v. Jackson, 223 Or App 429, 433-434 (2008).

A criminal defendant has a “broad right” to compel the production of evidence, and a witness has an “expansive duty” to accommodate that right. The limitations on that expansive duty are 1) relevance and 2) privilege. Bray, at 612 citing Cartwright, 336 Or at 417. A witness’s constitutional right to privacy does not limit the defendant’s right to compel production. This is true for victims as well. Bray, at 617.

Once the witness provides the records to the court, there is still no guarantee the court will provide these records to the parties. If the court, prosecutor or witness believes the records should not be disclosed because they are privileged or irrelevant the court may first perform an in camera inspection of the records. It is the responsibility of the proponent of the evidence to establish the need for an in camera inspection once a relevance or privilege challenge has been leveled.

In Camera Review

State v. Lammi, 278 Or App 690 (2016), establishes the minimum standard for a court to even consider an in camera inspection of documents. Under this ruling the defense attorney must demonstrate that the subpoenaed records “might yield” relevant evidence subject to disclosure. Citing State v. Reed, 173 Or App 185 (2001).

It is important to note that on reconsideration in State v. Lammi II, 281 Or App 96 (2016), the Court of Appeals restates that the “might yield” standard simply permits the court to consider an in camera review of the subpoenaed documents.

Once the proponent of the evidence has established that the subpoenaed records might yield relevant evidence subject to disclosure, the court is to consider the factors set out in Unites States v. Zolin, 491 US 554 (1989), in determining whether they will conduct the inspection.

The Zolin factors for discretionary review are:

1. Facts and circumstances of the particular case.

2. Volume of materials at issue.

3. Relative importance of information sought.

4. Whether such information might be available from non-privileged sources.

Many of the requested records in sex crime cases will have some privilege attached. Defense counsel should be prepared to address the issue of privilege and why privilege has been or will be waived or why the defendant’s due process right trumps the privilege asserted.

In determining whether to conduct an in camera review of confidential records to search for possible Brady material, a trial court must engage in a two-step process. The first step requires the party seeking such a review to make a threshold showing it is reasonable to believe that the records for which review is sought contain evidence of sufficient import to the defendant’s guilt as to require disclosure of the evidence to defendant. State v. Covington, 291 Or App 514, 517 (2018). After that initial showing is made the trial court then has discretion whether to undertake an in camera review of the material. Id.

In State v. Guffey, 291 Or App 729 (2018), the trial court denied a defense motion for in camera inspection of DHS and school records. Ultimately, the Court of Appeals remanded the case for the court to engage in step two of the analysis and determine whether the court should exercise its discretion to conduct an in camera inspection of some of the school records but none of the DHS records. The second step requires the trial judge on remand to determine whether she should engage in the in camera inspection after considering the following: “the facts and circumstances of the particular case, the volume of material at issue, the relative importance of information sought and whether such information would be available from non-privileged sources.” The Guffey opinion provides a very good analysis of the type of record a defense attorney must build to successfully obtain an in camera inspection of privileged records.

ORS 419B.040 provides some relief when arguing that privilege does not apply in child sex abuse cases – Certain privileges not grounds for excluding evidence in court proceedings on child abuse: “In the case of abuse of a child, the privileges created in ORS 40.230 to 40.255, including the psychotherapist-patient privilege, the physician-patient privilege, the privileges extended to nurses, to staff members of schools and to regulated social workers and the spousal privilege, shall not be a ground for excluding evidence regarding a child’s abuse, or the cause thereof, in any judicial proceeding made pursuant to ORS 419B.010 – ORS 419B.050.”

Practice Tip

Preserving the record. Include a demand in the motion: should the court deny in camera review of the subpoenaed records request, the records must be sealed and preserved for appellate review. If the subpoenaed party does not comply with the subpoena and does not bring the records or items to court, and the judge ultimately concludes that the records were not subject to disclosure, ask the court for an order directing the party to provide a copy of the records or items to the court for appellate review. See State v. Bray, 352 Or 809 (2012) (court directs victim to provide an image of computer hard drive to court under protective order for purposes of appellate review). See also State v. Harvey, 203 Or App 343, 347 (2005) (defense counsel’s failure to ensure that the records were made part of the file resulted in the court being unable to determine whether the trial court’s failure to inspect the records was prejudicial).

B. Pretrial Production Subpoena (Cartwright) – ORS 136.580(2)

Given the complex nature of medical records, therapy records, school records and the like it is often helpful to obtain the records well before trial. Subpoenaing records before trial require the defense attorney to articulate a heightened need for the records as described in State v. Cartwright, 173 Or App 59 (2001). This section deals with pretrial production of records that are not in the possession or control of the prosecutor.

Upon motion of the state or the defendant, the court may direct that the books, papers or documents described in the subpoena be produced before the court prior to the trial or prior to the time when the books, papers or documents are to be offered in evidence and may, upon production, permit the books, papers or documents to be inspected and copied by the state or the defendant and the state’s or the defendant’s attorneys.

ORS 136.580(2). Obtaining subpoenaed records prior to trial requires a motion and order from the court allowing their pretrial subpoena and production. We have provided a sample Cartwright motion and order for the CARES file at the end of this chapter, see Appendix A.

The requirement for a court to authorize a subpoena for pretrial production of records is different, and substantially higher, than that necessary for in camera review of records under an ORS 136.580(1) trial subpoena. The requirements are set out in State v. Cartwright and they are adopted from the federal standard.

For a party to successfully subpoena records prior to trial, they must establish for the court that the records are as follows:

1. Evidentiary and relevant: The defense must establish that the records or items in question are evidentiary and relevant to trial.

2. Not otherwise procurable: The defense must establish that the evidence is not otherwise procurable reasonably in advance of trial by exercise of due diligence.

Practice Tip

This requirement often gets overlooked and can cause significant delay in obtaining records or having the subpoena authorized. Often we are attempting to obtain the victims records (medical, counseling, etc.). It may be a good practice to ask the victim for consent to obtain the records or ask the prosecutor to ask the victim if they will consent prior filing the motion for pre-trial production.

3. Records are required to properly prepare for trial: The defense must establish that the records are required in order to properly prepare for trial and failure to have the records early may delay the trial unnecessarily.

4. Pretrial inspection is not a general fishing expedition: This goes hand in hand with the first requirement. The key point the court is making when analyzing pretrial subpoenas is to clarify that a subpoena duces tecum is not a discovery or investigation tool. Pretrial subpoena power is a mechanism to compel production of known information for presentation at trial.

By way of example, in 2015 the Court of Appeals concluded that the defendant was not entitled to DHS records when the defense attorney’s showing was inadequate, and therefore a fishing expedition, because “he did not point out exculpatory facts that the DHS records would show or lay a foundation for why there was a reason to believe that the DHS records would contain any such facts, such as by articulating facts known by him or others that led to his belief or suspicion.” State v. Wixom, 275 Or App 824, 828-829 (2015).

Defense counsel must meet all four (4) Cartwright factors in order to obtain pretrial production of records.

Practice Tip

Argue pragmatically that pretrial review saves trial resources and time, and the necessity of a continuance if materials are produced at time of trial.

Practice Tip

If Cartwright motion to issue pretrial subpoena for records is denied, issue a HIPAA compliant subpoena duces tecum for trial. Remember time frames for HIPAA compliant subpoena require that you act early and often.

C. Obtaining Records or Evidence from Victims

Crime victims’ rights legislation has resulted in substantial rights for victims of crimes. Those rights can be challenging for defense attorneys, and victims’ lawyers often join with prosecutors to exploit those rights to the defendant’s detriment.

Article I, section 42(1)(c), of the Oregon Constitution provides the following right to crime victims:

…the right to refuse an interview, deposition or other discovery request by the criminal defendant or other person acting on behalf of the criminal defendant provided however, that nothing in this paragraph shall restrict any other constitutional right of the defendant to discovery against the state.

Do not overlook this constitutional provision when planning a defense. Know that alleged victims can refuse any discovery request by the defendant. However, recent case law is beginning to clarify the limitations of this constitutional provision.

In State v. Bray, 281 Or App 584 (2016), defense counsel subpoenaed the alleged victim’s computer to perform a forensic examination. The court refused to allow the subpoena of the hard drive but ordered that a copy of the hard drive be turned over to the court for appellate review. The victim filed an interlocutory appeal of the order arguing that even an order requiring her to provide an image of the hard drive to the court violated her Article I, Section 42(c) right to refuse discovery.

This was the issue that was addressed in State v. Bray, 352 Or 809 (2012) and the Oregon Supreme Court ruled that it did not violate Article I, section 42(1) (c), to require the victim to turn over an image of her hard drive to the court. The court did not address the issue of whether a defense subpoena to the victim requesting the same image at trial as opposed to preserving the evidence for appeal would violate Article I, section 42(1) (c).

In the 2016 Bray decision the state did not argue violation of the victim’s right to refuse discovery. Instead, the state argued that defendant’s subpoena of the hard drive violated the victim’s privacy right to be free from unreasonable searches and seizures under Article I, section 9, and the Fourth Amendment. The Court of Appeals agreed that the victim had a privacy interest in the hard drive but concluded that that interest did not trump defendant’s ability to subpoena those records. The Court of Appeals reversed the conviction and required the trial court to apply the factors adopted in State v. Lammi (see discussion above) to determine whether an in camera review is appropriate. Note that the Oregon Supreme Court has allowed review on Bray’s petition.

Ultimately, the question of whether a defense subpoena of books, papers, documents, etc. from an alleged victim violates Article I, section 42(1) (c), is an open question. However, the language in the 2012 Bray decision provides a strong indication that a court should enforce a defense subpoena for such records.

IV. Common Areas of Concern

Certain agencies and records are routinely present in a sex crime investigation. This section identifies the most common evidence in sex crime defense and provides practice tips to procure the evidence.

A. Medical Records: ORS 136.447 and ORCP 55 H

1. ORS 136.447

“Medical records may be obtained by subpoena as provided in ORCP 55 H and shall be sent only to the court or clerk of the court before which the matter is pending. In relation to grand jury proceedings, notice need not be given as required in ORCP 55 H and the medical records shall be sent only to the grand jury.”

As a threshold matter, the prosecutor can subpoena medical records to the grand jury. In this circumstance, the defense attorney should expect the prosecutor to turn those records over through the discovery statutes.

In the event that necessary medical records are not subpoenaed to the grand jury, the defense attorney will need to file an ORCP 55 H compliant subpoena. Given the complex nature of medical records, defense attorneys should plan on filing a Cartwright subpoena. An added benefit to a Cartwright subpoena for medical records is that once a court has authorized a subpoena, the ORCP 55 H requirement for a court order has been satisfied. If the court denies a Cartwright subpoena, a trial subpoena pursuant to ORS 136.580(1) is appropriate. Be prepared to meet the Lammi requirements to have the court perform an in camera inspection to provide relevant materials to the parties at your hearing or trial.

Practice Tips

Be helpful to the court.

Offer to scan/copy/redact records for the court—it sometimes works. Follow up to make sure the subpoenaed records make it to clerk’s office and judge’s chambers in advance of the hearing to minimize false starts, and you might gain information as to how voluminous the records are. If you learn there is a small amount, be prepared to argue the records would not be arduous to review; or if they comprise a large amount, you may contend that the volume of records supports exculpatory information will be found. Makes sure each agency has produced the subpoenaed records.

If the records custodian sends records directly to your office instead of the court, do not review. Instead, transmit the records to the clerk of court under seal. The law does not provide authority for you to receive the records directly in response to the subpoena. However, if there is a cooperating parent or other individual with authority to obtain the records directly and who chooses to provide them to you, that is an alternate route.

2. ORCP 55 H

ORCP 55 H governs the subpoenaing of “individually identifiable health information.” This information is defined in ORCP 55 H(1)(a), however it generally applies to all personal medical records.

When serving a custodian of records with an ORCP 55 H compliant subpoena, the attorney must also provide either a qualified protective order or affidavit. In most cases, obtaining a protective order is the easier practice.

a. Qualified Protective Order

ORCP 55 H(1)(b) states that a qualified protective order means an order of the court that prohibits the parties from using or disclosing individually identifiable health information for any purpose other than the litigation for which they were subpoenaed. Additionally, the order must require the return or destruction of the materials at the end of litigation.

Practice Tip

When filing a motion for a Cartwright subpoena for medical records provide the court with a qualified protective order for review. At the hearing on the Cartwright factors, the court can approve the protective order thereby satisfying the obligations under ORCP 55 H.

b. Affidavits

If it is unworkable to obtain a qualified protective order prior to issuing a subpoena for medical records, the attorney must provide an affidavit along with the subpoena. The affidavit must include the following information:

1. That the attorney has made a good faith attempt to provide written notice to the patient or patient’s attorney and that the patient has had 14 days to object. ORCP 55 H(2)(a)(i).

2. The notice to the patient included a copy of the proposed subpoena. Additionally, the notice provided sufficient information about the litigation and information requested to permit the patient to object. ORCP 55 H (2)(a)(ii).

3. That the patient did not object to the subpoena or that any objections have been resolved. The attorney issuing the subpoena must certify they will allow the patient to promptly allow the patient to inspect and copy the records upon request. ORCP 55 H(2)(a)iii.

If the records are delivered to the court pursuant to an ORCP 55 H affidavit, be prepared to make a Lammi showing for an in camera inspection and disclosure of records to the parties.

B. Evidence from Child Assessment Centers

ORS 418.782, et. seq. discusses the role of child assessment centers in investigations that involve allegations of child abuse. In any child sex case expect that the alleged victim, if they are still under 18 years old at the time of the investigation, was seen by a child assessment center (CAC). [For a list of CAC’s, see Chapter 6, “Expeprts,” by Richard Cohen.]

There are three important steps of an evaluation at a CAC: 1) pre-examination interview of guardians, 2) the head-to-toe “well child” medical examination, and 3) the forensic interview. The CAC evaluation is perhaps the most important area of evidence gathering in child sex abuse cases.

The file at the CAC should include an intake questionnaire, any medical, DHS and police reports provided to the CAC prior to the interview, photographs from the colposcopic examination, the CAC medical examination report, the CAC forensic interview report and a video recording of the forensic interview.

To obtain the complete CAC file, the defense attorney should file a motion for an ORCP 55 H compliant Cartwright subpoena. At the time of the hearing to address the Cartwright factors the judge will likely expect a copy of the qualified protective order to issue with the subpoena.

Practice Tip

The prosecutor almost certainly has a copy of the CAC interview in their file, however it may not be produced in reciprocal discovery. ORS 418.794 prevents the video from leaving the CAC without a lawful subpoena and protective order. Although it is not directly on point, many prosecutors rely on this statute as the reason they will not provide the video in discovery. A pretrial subpoena for the CAC video is a necessary step to investigating child sex abuse cases.

If you are requesting colposcopic photographs be prepared to explain the need for the photographs as judges frequently apply a higher level of scrutiny in ordering their production. Generally these photographs are only necessary in cases where there are allegations of vaginal or anal penetration.

Consider requesting other CAC file information and documents maintained, such as any drawings of the child or other information contained in the interviewer’s file. By reviewing the CAC video recording, you may learn of other diagrams or drawings, anatomical or otherwise, used in the interview which may be helpful to understand details from the interview.

Investigation may reveal there have been earlier CAC investigations and interviews. These may be crucial to understanding the complainant’s source of knowledge, patterns of disclosure or manipulation, lying, or other historical information. If they exist, it is crucial to obtain these materials.

See the appendices at the end of this chapter for a motion, memorandum, protective order and subpoena duces tecum for pretrial production of a CARES file.

C. Juvenile Court Records – ORS 419A.255

Often in child sex cases DHS will file dependency petitions in juvenile court against the parents of the child for the sexual assault and/or the other parent’s failure to protect the child. If there is a contested hearing on the dependency petition it is likely that the child will be called as a witness and subject to cross-examination on the specific allegations of your case. Obtaining a copy of the child’s testimony is vital to preparing a defense in your case.

While juvenile court proceedings and hearings can be a source of information and evidence, ORS 419A.255 protects juvenile court records from public inspection with the exception of certain individuals associated with the juvenile court proceedings. See ORS 419A.255(1)(b) and (2)(b). Criminal defense attorneys are not explicitly authorized persons under the statute. A motion and affidavit for juvenile court records can be found in the appendices—Appendix C and Appendix D.

Disclosure to Other Persons: ORS 419A.255(3) restricts disclosure of the juvenile court records to persons other than those listed (1)(b) and (2)(b) without the consent of the court. ORS 419A.255(1)(b)(Q) authorizes parties that are not explicitly authorized to obtain juvenile court records pursuant to ORS 419A.258.

ORS 419A.258 requires the party seeking juvenile court records to file an affidavit or declaration addressing (1) the reasons the party is seeking the records, (2) the relevance, if any, of the disclosure to the juvenile court proceeding, and (3) the balancing of interests set forth in ORS 419A.258(6). ORS 419A.258(6) instructs the court to consider the following interests:

(1) The privacy interests and particular vulnerabilities of the child, ward, youth or youth offender, or of family members, that may be affected by the copying of all or part of the record of the case or the supplemental confidential file.

(2) The interests of the other parties to, or victims in, the juvenile court proceedings;

(3) The interests of the person or entity filing the motion; and

(4) The interests of the public.

While addressing these interests consider Davis v. Alaska, 415 US 308 (1974) (privilege
of juvenile court records must give way to the right of effective cross-examination
for bias.)

Practice Tip

Filing a proposed protective order with language consistent with the limitations of disclosure contained within ORS 419A.255 will make your request more reasonable in the eyes of
the court.

D. Grand Jury Notes/Recordings

ORS 135.855(1)(c) explicitly excludes the production of transcripts, recordings or memoranda of testimony of witnesses before the grand jury, except transcripts or recordings of statements made by the defendant. However, such notes and recordings are held in the possession of the district attorney’s office.

When investigation of a case suggests that a witness provided testimony at the grand jury that is inconsistent with her other statements, the defense attorney has a legitimate claim to the grand jury notes and recordings. However, the prosecutor will likely rely on ORS 135.855(1) in refusing to turn over the notes/recordings absent a court order.

Utilizing the codification of Brady in ORS 135.815(1)(g), the defense attorney should have success in obtaining the records. Meaningfully inconsistent statements made by a witness are impeachment materials for the purpose of Brady. If a prosecutor called a witness to grand jury, it is likely they intend to call the witness to trial. Therefore, it is appropriate to file a motion to compel production of grand jury notes/recording under ORS 135.865 for a failure to disclose evidence as required by ORS 135.815(1) (g)(C). If the prosecutor cites ORS 135.855(1) as a basis for not disclosing the records, argue that production is required under Article I, section 11, and the Fourteenth Amendment. If concerns persist, offer a protective order to limit disclosure of the sensitive information in the notes. State v. Covington, 219 Or App 514 (2018), held that the trial court abused its discretion in denying a defense request for an in camera inspection of grand jury notes when the complainant ostensibly changed her testimony between grand jury and trial.

E. DHS Records – ORS 419B.035

DHS is often involved with allegations of interfamilial or household child sexual abuse. OAR 413-015-0400 directs DHS employees to conduct a face-to-face interview with a child who is alleged to be the victim of abuse. Additionally, the DHS policy manual requires workers to inquire about substance abuse, domestic violence and other abuse at virtually every meeting with a child, regardless of the nature of the meeting. Accordingly, DHS records can be a rich area for defense investigation.

However, ORS 419B.035 prevents the disclosure of DHS records to defense attorneys in criminal proceedings absent a court order. Accordingly, the Cartwright pretrial subpoena is the preferred method of obtaining DHS records. If the Cartwright motion fails, the defense attorney should issue an ORS 136.580(1) trial subpoena and argue for an in camera inspection pursuant to Lammi.

Practice Tip

DHS is a statewide agency with many local offices. Given the size of the agency it can be difficult to obtain all the records they possess related to your case. Additionally, DHS seeks to disclose as few records as possible when complying with a subpoena. To that end, the defense attorney should request the following documents in a DHS subpoena: all assessments, case notes, case plans, child specific case plans and permanency planning records.

The Department of Justice represents DHS and will frequently file motions to quash defense subpoenas. Generally, DOJ is simply requesting that the court order a specific form of protective order. Once the language of the protective order is satisfactory they will advise DHS to comply with the subpoena. However, defense attorneys should be prepared for resistance from the DOJ when subpoenaing records, especially on subpoenas that are not narrowly tailored.

We have provided the DOJ’s preferred protective order for DHS records in the materials for this chapter.

Are DHS Records in the Possession and Control of the State
for the Purposes of Reciprocal Discovery?

Yes and no, unfortunately.

State v. Warren, 304 Or 428 (1987). DHS records pertaining to the investigation of allegations of the instant abuse are within the possession and control of the prosecution.

State ex rel. Dugan v. Tiktin, 313 Or 607 (1992). Oregon Supreme Court rejecting the argument under ORS 135.815(1) that the prosecutor has access to both the investigation records concerning alleged sexual abuse of the victim and the “background” records that DHS has concerning the victim.

State v. Wixom, 275 Or App 824 (2015). Affirms the holding in Warren that DHS records pertaining to the investigation of allegations of abuse are within the possession and control of the prosecutor. However, records that concern DHS’s involvement with the victim and placement in foster care prior to the alleged abuse are not in the possession or control of the state.

In Wixom, the most recent case on this matter, the state turned over DHS records relating to the investigation of allegations of abuse by the defendant. As such, there was no need for the defense attorney to litigate this issue. However, despite the case law cited above, many district attorneys will argue that they are prevented from discovering any DHS records to the defense. They are incorrect.

ORS 419B.035(1) (a) requires DHS to turn over their records relating to child abuse to “law enforcement.” ORS 419B.005(4), which defines “law enforcement” for the purposes of ORS 419B.035(1) (a) explicitly excludes prosecutors. But the analysis does not end there, since “law enforcement” includes county sheriffs and city or municipal police officers. Again, State v. Bray, 281 Or App 596 (2016), is instructive:

As we held in State v. Wixom, 275 Or App 824, 831-32 (2015), “[t]he legal standard for prosecutorial control of records is not disputed. As we have recently explained, ‘if an entity is required to disclose records to the district attorney or to police, the prosecution has control over the records and, accordingly, must disclose to a defendant any of those records that fall within ORS 135.815.’ State v. Daniels, 261 Or App 519, 528 rev. den, 355 Or 668 (2014) (italics in original). And, ‘information that the prosecutor may obtain directly is within the prosecutor’s “control” *** even if it was not in the prosecutor’s physical possession.’ State v. Warren, 304 Or 428, 433 (1987); accord State ex rel Wilson v. Thomas, 74 Or App 137, 141, rev. den 300 Or 64 (1985) (‘[T]he police are an arm of the prosecution for the purposes of the discovery statute.’).”
(Omission in original.).

See also State v. Wood, 112 Or App 61 (1992), where “the prosecutor was required to disclose the portion of the CSD (now DHS) file containing written reports by [a CPS/DHS worker they intended to call as a witness.]” Note: the authority requiring DHS to turn over their records (OAR 412-01-145) has been repealed, Bray combined with ORS 419B.005(4) overcame this objection.

Practice Tip

When a broad request is denied, consider as a fallback a less cumbersome request from the court—that the DHS face sheet and DHS caller logs be produced. They will have less information but may be rich with details to support a subsequent broader inspection and may allow counsel to learn about the context of the first disclosure—which is crucial to understanding whether the complainant’s story has changed or been influenced in any significant fashion.

F. Social Media

A defendant may issue a subpoena for social media records (Facebook, Twitter, Instagram, Google, etc.) using the procedures discussed above for an ORS 136.580(1) trial subpoena or a Cartwright subpoena. However, as the records are likely located outside of the state of Oregon, defense attorneys will also have to cite and meet the standard under ORS 136.583.

Practitioners should anticipate that the social media provider will move to quash the subpoena. The motion to quash will likely turn, in part, on an objection under the social media user’s constitutional privacy rights and protections from unreasonable searches and seizures. See State v. Bray, 281 Or App 584 (2016), rejecting those arguments and requiring the weighing of the factors under Lammi.

More importantly, a social media provider is also likely to object on the Stored Communications Act (SCA), 18 U.S.C. § 2701 et seq. While there is no SCOTUS or Oregon decision on point, various states courts have begun interpreting the SCA. Facebook, for instance, is located in California where appellate courts have decided that pretrial defense subpoenas for social media records violate the SCA. See Facebook, Inc. v. Superior Court, 240 Cal App 4th 203 (2015) (review by the California Supreme Court has been granted).

In fact, the social media provider may simply refuse to perform on a defense subpoena based on the above decision. Should the provider not comply, counsel must file a contempt of court proceeding against the witness in Oregon. If the court grants the order, the next step is to register the order in the state where the social media provider is located. Litigation on the contempt order is likely to commence outside of Oregon.

To that end, while Facebook, Inc. v. Superior Court was an appeal from a California trial court and not a contempt action, it is important to know that California appellate courts are not inclined to allow the pretrial subpoena of social media records subject to the SCA. How they would rule on a trial subpoena specifically is still an open question.

G. Psychotherapy and Counseling Records

If a defense attorney wishes to obtain psychotherapy and/or counseling records, a subpoena or Cartwright subpoena is the most efficient mechanism to obtain the records. In such a filing, however, a defense practitioner should be aware of the following cases because of their implications in the first Cartwright factor (that the records sought must be evidentiary and relevant).

State v. Evans, 260 Or App 270 (2013), a sex abuse case in which the defense attorney attempted to offer evidence in the form of 1) a report from the child’s therapist and 2) a report from the child’s school counselor. The reports were dated approximately one year prior to the abuse for which the defendant was accused. The reports contained information that the victim preferred to live with her mother rather than the defendant, she exhibited defiant behaviors, that the child might have exaggerated anything that she thought might get some attention, and that the victim took hallucinations.

The defense argued that the evidence should be admitted under OEC 607, 608(1), and 609-1(1) in order to attack the credibility of the victim. The defense acknowledged that the records were subject to the psychotherapist-patient privilege, OEC 504(2), but argued that the child abuse exception under ORS 419B.040(1) allowed for admission of the therapist’s testimony. The trial court denied the admission of the evidence and the Court of Appeals affirmed. In reaching its decision the Court of Appeals addressed the following cases:

State v. Hansen, 304 Or 169 (1987) which held that “a victim’s statements to a psychotherapist that she had not been abused by the defendant were not protected by the psychotherapist-patient privilege and fell within the scope of ORS 419B.040(1), noting that the legislative history supported the conclusion that the exception reached evidence that the defendant is not guilty, not just evidence of guilt.” Hansen, 304 Or at 179-180.

State v. Reed, 173 Or App 185 (2001) which held that the absence of statements by the victim mentioning abuse by the defendant was not admissible under ORS 419B.040(1). Reed, at 200. The holding noted that extending the scope of the rule in Hansen to the absence of statements mentioning abuse would “swallow the privilege ‘whole’ in juvenile sex abuse cases. … “We concluded that Hansen was limited to explicit denials of abuse that directly exonerated a defendant, and that statements not mentioning abuse were protected by the privilege. Reed, at 200-201.

Practice Tip

To overcome this objection to a Cartwright subpoena for psychotherapy and counseling records, argue that due process under the Fourteenth Amendment requires the admission of this evidence. Should that argument fail, issue a trial subpoena for in camera review pursuant to Lammi. If the judge refuses the inspection, be sure to have the records sealed as part of the court file for appellate review.
