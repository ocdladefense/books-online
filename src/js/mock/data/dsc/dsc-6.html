Defending Sex Cases

Defending Sex Cases: Strategies from Allegation to Resolution, 2018 edition

Chapter 6 Experts and the Multidisciplinary Team in Child Sex Abuse Cases

By Richard D. Cohen

I. Introduction

Experts arrive in a curious form in child sex abuse prosecutions in criminal and dependency cases. They arrive to testify in the courtroom, and their testimony is delivered as if it has scientific, medical, objective foundation, but actually it is often founded on (or grounded in) an absence of science or expertise that defies OEC 702. They arrive without the scientific validity required by Frye and Daubert standards,1 or in Oregon, the Brown and O’Key standards.2 Experts too often enter the courtroom to bolster the state’s case by continuing the less than honorable tradition of junk science that has pervaded the world of child sex abuse investigation and prosecution since the field exploded in the United States in the late 1970s.

Core Concepts

There are two core concepts for the defense practitioner to understand:

1. The legislature and the judiciary function in a manner that assists the successful prosecution of child sex abuse cases.

Beginning in 1989 and continuing through 1995, the Oregon legislature created the Child Abuse Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) system (ORS 418.746 – 418.800), which formalized the partnership of the attorney general’s office, district attorneys, DHS and child abuse assessment centers.

2. There was an effort to create the appearance that these entities function independently and objectively.

However, per statute at the county level, the district attorney is in charge of the coordination and function of child abuse investigation; at the state level, the attorney general’s office functions with oversight and funding contributions in connection with purportedly objective child abuse assessment centers. Child abuse assessment centers often function to support, rather than investigate, the prosecution of reported child sex abuse. They then provide the prosecution with expert testimony that purports to come from neutral scientific precepts.

The defense lawyer will most commonly encounter a state’s expert in the form of a pediatrician or pediatric nurse practitioner who is affiliated with a MDT regional assessment center or child abuse assessment center, as well as a non–medically trained interviewer connected to an assessment center. The credentials of MDT interviewers range from a bachelor’s degree to a Ph.D. Most interviewers at most large facilities in Oregon have master’s degrees and many are licensed clinical social workers, licensed professional counselors, and licensed marriage and family counselors.

Be prepared for the fact that judges frequently allow detectives and DHS workers to be qualified as experts having specialized knowledge and expertise when they do not. The problem of trial judges supporting state efforts to exceed the scope of what experts should be allowed to address under OEC 702 is long-standing in Oregon. State v. Middleton, 294 Or 427, 657 P2d 1215 (1983), and State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621, 756 P2d 620 (1988), have since the 1980s prohibited the introduction of both vouching and syndrome evidence, yet the circuit courts and the Court of Appeals denuded these cases; for a quarter century, vouching and syndrome evidence, regardless of the intensity of objection, have flooded into child sex abuse trials and influenced the triers of fact.

The Oregon Supreme Court had a chance to address the core issue of bad practice diagnosis in State v. Southard, 347 Or 127, 218 P3d 104 (2009), and got the right conclusion via the wrong direction—by asserting that in the absence of observable physical symptoms, a diagnosis of sex abuse does not come into evidence in a child sex abuse case because of its prejudicial impact. Southard presented the Oregon Supreme Court with an opportunity to correct decades of bad law. The court should have ruled that in the absence of observable physical symptoms, a diagnosis of sex abuse is not based on medically objective data, lacks scientific validity, and fails to meet the Brown and O’Key standards.

In child sex abuse prosecutions, presumptions of innocence and core defendant rights are overshadowed by a statutory regime and judicial culture driven by a desire to protect children. That leads to disconnects between how evidence and law are normally applied when the desire to protect children distorts the application of law and rules of evidence.

This is particularly apparent in the inequitable application of expert testimony rules in child sex abuse prosecutions. Even post-Lupoli the state is allowed to address how sexually abused children report, what influences children to report, what can explain delayed reporting, why children are commonly confused about specific facts and details, and the fact that children in general rarely self-generate a bad-motive complaint of sex abuse against an individual. None of these areas of testimony embrace solid scientifically validated principles. They are, in fact, all examples of “syndrome” evidence.

A good working definition of syndrome evidence is evidence of the characteristics or general way in which human beings in a given situation will behave or conduct themselves. In child sex abuse cases, interviewers, physicians, and nurse practitioners regularly testify about child’s behavior and whether those behaviors are common among sexually abused children. Such testimony should violate OEC 702 scientific validity, syndrome, and vouching standards. Yet, even post-Lupoli and its purported ban on vouching, such testimony regularly comes into evidence.

In addition, the defense lawyer faces the challenge of how to combat experts, both real and pseudo, with defense experts when defending against charges of child sex abuse. Many trial courts are quick to accept the expertise of the government’s purported expert witnesses, but quick to question the helpfulness and objective knowledge of defense experts. The problem is compounded because sexual abuse of children occurs too often in our society. In the legitimate concern to protect children, bad methods were created and are perpetuated in our courts.

Lastly, a majority of Oregon child abuse assessment programs examine child abuse concerns based on a medical model. The medical model was based on an incorrect assumption that physical symptomatology would frequently be helpful in defining whether or not a child had been sexually abused. The medical models were established in the 1980s and much of the scientific documentation of what is relevant regarding physical symptomatology of child sex abuse emanates from research between 1989 and 1995, post-dating the medical model.

II. Strategic Overview: The Road From and To Hell Is Paved With Good Intentions

It is important to understand how sex abuse investigation and prosecution evolved in the United States and Oregon and how it manifests itself contemporaneously. We who defend are faced with very significant contemporary challenges stemming from emotion substituting for reason, which impacts our clients’ liberty and rights and our ability to successfully advocate for our clients. More specifically, once the possibility that sexual abuse may have occurred is established, emotional concern supersedes a reasonable analysis of facts and evidence as a basis to determine outcome.

There have been two periods during which prosecution of child sex abuse cases was driven by histrionics and drama—from the mid-1970s to the mid-1990s, and at present. The drama of the first period was partially redirected by internationally recognized scientific documentation. The pediatric and behavioral sciences debunked the misrepresentation of medical, pediatric, and social work experts. Many lives were permanently ruined during that time by often disingenuous, dishonest, or grossly inaccurate expert testimony. Too many children were removed from their families because of erroneous convictions based on outright falsities in expert testimony.

The following are two areas in which such falsities occurred during the mid-1970s to the
mid-1990s:

1. Pediatricians affiliated with government facilities regularly testified that certain factors in the anogenital presentation of Tanner stage I (preadolescent females) meant that the child had, to a medical certainty, been sexually abused. In fact, normative research from 1989 to 1995 by forensic pediatricians with the highest international standing proved that several of those factors were of no significance as to whether a child had been sexually abused or not.

2. Interviewers and medical personnel serving as state experts testified that children do not make false allegations. This misrepresentation has been debunked by every significant behavioral science study that has addressed the topic. The most famous study was published in 1987 and is generally referred to as the Jones and McGraw study.3 The first medical director of CARES Northwest, the largest of Oregon’s MDT child abuse assessment centers, misrepresented under oath the existence of the study’s conclusions. The medical director testified that the study supports the concept that “young children never make false allegations about being sexually abused.” This unfortunate twisting of factual accuracy is addressed in Sistrunk v. Armenakis, 271 F3d 1174 (9th Cir. 2001).4

In education and testimony, MDT experts today continue to propound scientifically invalid concepts and assertions in child sex abuse prosecutions. So stilted is our legal culture that our judges continue to embrace the admissibility of MDT expert testimony without holding them to scientific principles and with seeming ignorance or indifference towards the historic acute misuse of such testimony.

In recent years (or the second period of excessive histrionics and drama in child sex abuse investigation), the quality of child abuse assessment and interviewing has deteriorated in Oregon for several clearly observable reasons.

A. Subjective Bias

A new generation of MDT pediatricians, pediatric nurse practitioners and social workers are frequently ignorant of historic studies and developments and engage in outmoded practices that substitute subjective bias for objective analysis in forming opinions about child sex abuse. This is done in a form of advocacy, such as labeling themselves as child abuse protection advocates or child abuse advocates. Advocacy seems to override objectivity and methods connected to scientific validity.

While Southard cut off the head and does not allow testimony about the conclusion or diagnosis of child sex abuse absent physical evidence, two major dangers remain: 1) there is an increase in recent years of false claims of physical symptomatology relevant to child sex abuse and 2) although the MDT expert is, post-Lupoli and Southard, not allowed to testify about their conclusion, they are still frequently allowed to testify about their observations and syndrome assessments, which thus implies guilt on the part of a specific defendant. The stream of interconnected general commentaries introduced to legitimize the presence of sex abuse in an individual case is harder to defend against.

B. Victimology

Most existing MDT child abuse assessment center interviewers are adherents of victimology. This is a relatively new field and seems to be primarily inhabited by child abuse advocates. The field looks at the relationship between victims and their offenders and the impact of offender behavior on victims. The field is weak on scientifically valid studies and seems heavily influenced by innate support for victims regardless of whether, objectively, a person making an allegation is in fact, by legal definition, a victim. This field tends to turn away from research and science, preferring to underscore the function of the MDT as embracing the comfortable elicitation and documentation of victimization complaints.

This is the clear direction that the investigation of child abuse and sex abuse has taken in recent years and is one of the reasons for the increased dysfunction and a reduced quality of objective investigation by DHS. The MDT utilizes a simplified, subjective “embrace the victim” approach to investigation. The 2012 edition of the Oregon Interviewing Guidelines eliminated much of the research present in the 2004 and 1998 editions. The guidelines remain poised to bolster the accuracy of information received. But the guidelines have abandoned the vast majority of information they previously contained that assisted an investigator in understanding contextual complications and in objectively analyzing
whether a report is more or less likely to be accurate.

Practice Tip

Be prepared for MDT experts who cite the work of Bruce D. Perry, M.D., Ph.D., a psychiatrist and senior fellow of the Child Trauma Academy in Houston, Texas, and an adjunct professor of psychiatry and behavioral sciences at the Feinberg School of Medicine in Chicago, Illinois, as well as the work of Bessel A. van der Kolk, M.D., Medical Director of The Trauma Center in Boston, Professor of Psychiatry at Boston University Medical School and Co-Director of the National Center for Child Traumatic Stress Complex Trauma Network. The work of Dr. Perry and Dr. van der Kolk is valuable and inspirational but largely conceptual and not predicated on objective, scientifically validated studies, nor does it purport to be. Their work is in some ways reminiscent of Dr. Richard Gardner and Dr. Roland Summit who in the early 1980s introduced child abuse investigators to critically important concepts. Those concepts were misapplied to a syndrome analysis, however, and now simplifying adherents of Drs. Perry and van der Kolk distort their conceptual work and misapply it to specific situations. Much of the work of these doctors focuses on the impact of victimization on children and thus is less helpful to define whether children have been victimized.

All of this begs the question: why are experts affiliated with the MDT, whose expertise is supposed to be involved with objective investigation, caught up in and being governed by a methodology that assumes facts which are not established nor subject to scientific validation?

C. False Allegations and Parental Alienation (Gatekeeping)

A common but not exclusive source of false allegations of sex abuse is a child influenced by a parent where that parent is involved in extreme conflict with the other parent who is the subject of the accusation. Some MDT experts will claim they are familiar with the concept of “parental alienation,” i.e., the process by which one parent pervasively seeks to undermine the child’s relationship with the other parent.

The concept of parental alienation, now called gatekeeping, was popularized by Judith Wallerstein and Joan Kelly, perhaps the two greatest giants in the field of psychology dealing with child custody issues in the history of that subcategory of the behavioral sciences. In early publications, Wallerstein and Kelly describe it as a “pathological alignment between an angry parent and the child that arose from the dynamics of separation and divorce.”5

Oregon MDTs currently lack expertise to understand how a high-conflict relationship between parents or caregivers can influence the reporting of sex abuse by children. Many MDT experts affiliated with the prosecution do not understand that there is a difference between Dr. Perry’s use of the term “alienation” and how that term has been understood for 40 years relevant to an aligned parent or trusted figure undermining a child’s relationship with another parent and influencing that child’s perceptions and reports about that other person. This underscores the degree of contemporary bias and ineptitude present in most child abuse assessment centers in Oregon and underscores how their interviewers are unable to objectively investigate child sex abuse allegations.

Practice Tip

Highlight the ignorance the interviewer has about high-conflict relationships between parents and caregivers and how that influences children’s reporting. Demonstrate how the interviewer’s own conduct in the case impacts the quality of evidence from a range of being not helpful to being biased.

D. Dependency Work

Defense lawyers who practice dependency law and defend parents in juvenile court proceedings know that the standard for deprivation of a parent’s liberty interest in their children at a shelter hearing and prior to adjudication is incredibly low and bears no practical relationship to probable cause. Prior to adjudication, the juvenile court system analysis tends to be predicated on concern over evidence. There is a systemic misunderstanding of the core balance between the importance of respecting parents’ rights, family liberty interests, and children’s rights to be with their parents, and the judicial court concern, which should be subject to substantiation through evidence before disruption of those rights.

Defending the falsely accused is intensified by the challenge of the ever increasing investigative dysfunction of the Department of Human Services (DHS) Child Welfare Services (CWS). Most long-term observers of DHS agree that CWS has not functioned at a level of lower competence since the inception of the Children’s Services Division (CSD) in 1971.

E. Criminal Work

Traditionally and contemporaneously, few trial judges can withstand the concern aspect and render a decision in these cases of not guilty. Child sex abuse cases should almost always be tried to a jury, where there is at least the possibility that discussing the need in voir dire for objective, evidence-based judgment can overcome bias. Prepare your client for the looks of communal dislike and disgust towards him and you that, at best, appear on the faces of many on the jury panel at the start of voir dire.

The defense lawyer is particularly challenged in child sex abuse cases by the fact that we
live in a society not prone to making decisions based on facts as opposed to emotion.
Traditionally, a successful defense tactic points out how a deficiency in the facts leaves reasonable doubt. Traditionally, a successful defense tactic points out how a biased investigation leaves unanswered questions and leads to reasonable doubt. While these approaches should not be abandoned, using these approaches alone runs a risk of being unsuccessful in contemporary society. We in the defense bar are being forced to play the prosecution’s game—stirring up emotion to empathize with someone being a victim. We actually may be arriving at the point where we must argue that the facts show our client is the victim.

Prosecutors understand “emotion over reason” because in recent years their primary methodology has been to turn away from discussion of evidence and facts and instead to weave a story for the jury that skips over detail, consistency, and intellectual integrity to focus instead on bullet point pleas to the jury’s emotion for and connection with the victim. “Beyond a reasonable doubt” is not much protection when reason is not the vehicle for decision-making. When the prosecution’s case is predicated on avoiding reason, instructions as to the presumption of innocence, reasonable doubt, and application of reasonable doubt do not resonate with a jury that is prepared from the get-go to decide guilt from a place of emotional concern for the child.

The child abuse investigation/prosecution system is often predicated on the validation of victimization rather than on investigation of whether victimization has occurred. Child abuse investigation methods often lack honesty, integrity, scientific validity and objectivity. If we enthusiastically combat, our methods are identified as prejudicial and inadmissible. We in the defense bar need to educate general society that the distortion of constitutional principles and presumptions of guilt do not serve our society.

F. Child Sex Abuse Investigation

To understand the interrelationship between defense experts and the primary source of prosecution experts (employees of the MDT in Oregon), it is helpful to have a brief contextual understanding of the history of child abuse investigation in the United States.

Child abuse investigation is a relatively recent phenomenon. There are debatably two originating pioneers of child abuse investigation in the United States—John Caffey, a radiologist who in 1946 began writing about his observations of the difference between images of long bone injury in children and parental explanations, and pediatrician Henry Kempe, who in the 1960’s wrote The Battered Child which popularized child abuse to national attention.

Child abuse investigation and the prosecutorial approaches we encounter today began in the 1970s. In Oregon at that time the most sophisticated child abuse investigation center, in terms of pediatric overview, was the Health Science Center in Portland. On a national scale the Kempe Center, named after Henry Kempe, was founded in Denver in 1972 to focus on assessment of child abuse. Dr. Kempe largely popularized to the professional consciousness the concept of the need to protect children from sexual abuse in 1977–1978 with a lecture and subsequent article entitled, “Sexual Abuse, Another Hidden Pediatric Problem.”6

To understand and strategize how to defend your client against child sex abuse allegations, it is important to remember that not only is the field extremely emotional and reaction driven, but that it is also only about 40 years old. The field of child sex abuse investigation has gone through three essential phases:

• An arguably “true believer,” histrionic, sex-abuse junk-science period.

• A reactive period, tamped down to a degree by medical–behavioral science research.

• A period of the rebirth of junk science in deference to victimology-advocacy.

III. The Development of “Experts” on Child Sex Abuse

Since the initiation of child abuse investigation, there have been seminal developments that define expertise in the field. One is in the field of pediatrics. Initially, it was thought that pediatric assessment could determine a significant number of child sexual abuse cases. Prior to normative research being conducted, it was believed that observed conditions—particularly in female preadolescent children in the hymen and vaginal area—were beyond-a-reasonable-doubt proof of sexual abuse.

Additionally, certain precepts, such as “children never lie about abuse,” caused investigation techniques to focus on assisting the child in revealing abuse. Techniques were developed to contribute to the comfortable reinforcement of a child’s retelling of abuse and not on analysis of whether or not abuse occurred.

The behavioral sciences (psychology, child psychology, child psychiatry, behavioral and developmental pediatrics, counseling, and social welfare) began researching factors that impact child sex abuse reporting. That research essentially began in the mid-1970s with perhaps the best known work produced by David Finkelhor and Lucy Berliner. Additional early researchers and writers important to behavioral science analysis of child sex abuse reporting include John Conte, John Briere, Gail Goodman, William Friedrich, and Sandra K. Hewitt. By the 1980s, in addition to these individuals, Stephen Ceci, Maggie Bruck, Kathleen Faller and others contributed to a significant body of research relevant to how children should be questioned in sex abuse investigations and the developing field of suggestibility. Suggestibility is defined as the vulnerability of human beings, and particularly young children, to be influenced by others in perceiving facts that then influence their subsequent reporting based on external influences remembered and reported.

The low point of child abuse investigation and prosecution—in terms of abhorrent methodology, extremely biased expert analysis, and testimony and convictions based on incompetent methodology and bias—was the 1980s.

A byproduct of the 1980s was Dr. Roland Summit’s Child Sex Abuse Accommodation Syndrome.7 Dr. Summit was then a psychiatrist enmeshed with the Los Angeles forensic community as a clinical assistant professor of psychiatry at Harbor UCLA Medical Center in Torrance, California. He came up with a list of typical reactions of children to sex abuse that was then adopted, incorporated, and testified to as a basis for expert identification of children who had been victims of sex abuse. This was tragic. It is well accepted in the behavioral sciences that there is no inherently predictable manner in which a human being will react to a particular stimulus. Dr. Summit himself ultimately disdained the use of the Child Abuse Accommodation Syndrome as a guidepost of red flags of concern for defining whether a particular child may have been sexually abused.

Child abuse investigators and prosecutors commonly misused Summit’s work to establish that if children had certain conditions or symptoms, it meant beyond a reasonable doubt that they had been sexually abused. Much of this happened even after Middleton and Milbradt were decided and their prohibition against the use of syndrome evidence. Unfortunately this misuse, combined with even sloppier and more inaccurate perceptions held by the pediatric community, led to the profiling of abusers and victims. None of the profiling was scientific; it was experiential and subjective. This was devastating to fair hearings and created tragic outcomes.

Most existing child abuse assessment centers in Oregon date back to the late 1980s or early 1990s. Unfortunately, they were founded at the height of the bias, incompetence and junk science that invaded both the pediatric and behaviorist approaches to child sex abuse investigation.

“Junk science” became a popular term for defense lawyers and defense experts in the 1980s because defense experts and many counsel understood that a substantial amount of medical and behavioral science evidence that led to the conviction of individuals accused of child sex abuse was based on invalid expert assertions rather than legitimate scientific principles.

The 1980s and early 1990s also saw an increase in the number of therapists who viewed it as their mission to assist children and adults in recovering repressed memories that the therapist presupposed were the source of trauma-driven behaviors in their clients. Therefore, the therapist’s presupposition that the child or adult had been sexually abused created a highly leading approach to help their patient get in touch with that abuse and report it.

Also during this time, state experts and prosecutors in Oregon held a significant belief that Satanic Ritualistic Abuse (SRA) existed as a prevalent societal problem leading to the abuse of children. In the early 1990s, DHS and others responsible for training around sex offender issues presented a conference about SRA. Behavioral scientists today believe that SRA as a common societal problem has the same scientific validity as the theory that the moon is made up of Swiss cheese. Most long-term psychiatrists and psychologists have never encountered a SRA situation. Again, the MDT shows no signs of reflective humility or lessons learned based on the idiocy of some of its early presuppositions. We who defend in the area of child sex abuse allegations are up against the reality that endorses the maxim that “those who do not learn from history repeat it.”

Research from the 1980s and 1990s that current MDT advocates sometimes ignore revealed the following:

• Documentation of normative genital findings in non-abused prepubescent girls. That research by Dr. John McCann from 1989 to 1992 debunked common forensic pediatric representations and testimony about preadolescent female anogenital conditions medically proving sex abuse.

• Guidelines for normative and concerning conditions in children regarding the connection between physical conditions of children and the likelihood of sex abuse, developed in the early 1990s by Dr. Joyce Adams.

• The lack of reliability of repressed memory. Dr. Elizabeth Loftus conducted substantial research in this area in the 1980s and early 1990s. Her publication, The Myth of Repressed Memory, had a national impact. Repressed memory remains a debatable issue, but it is not debated by mental health professions with licensing standards that stipulate a therapist may not presuppose a condition and then, in essence, brainwash a subject or patient into belief in that condition. In the early 1990s, at least two psychologists and one social worker lost their licenses in Oregon for such conduct.

• A relatively small percentage of children (two to six percent) actually self-generate a lie or a bad motive to report sex abuse, as shown in studies of mass subjects conducted by Jones and McGraw in 1987 and Oates in 2000.

• In the same studies, a bottom-line percentage of 33–35 percent of sex abuse reports by children were invalidated, reflecting confusion, influence, secondary gain motivations by people in powerful positions over the child, and suggestive, misleading, contaminating investigation procedures.

IV. The Multidisciplinary Team (MDT)

Under federal influence from 1989 to 1995, Oregon enacted the MDT statutes, ORS 418.746-418.800.

The MDT statutes create what, in function, appear to many defense lawyers as a conspiracy between different power-holding entities of government. It is not a conspiracy. It is a statute that provides for coordinated case-by-case implementation of practice standards for child sex abuse investigation pursuant to the establishment of a team approach for child abuse investigation in Oregon. However, the lack of sophistication of many individuals and the variables of weakness between different partners within the MDT create several problems. This section will describe how the MDT works.

A. Law Enforcement and DHS

The MDT functions in essentially three ways:

• On a statewide level, the attorney general’s office at the Department of Justice (DOJ) is empowered by statute to engage in funding and oversight of child abuse investigation in Oregon.

• The Child Abuse Multidisciplinary Intervention Advisory Council (CAMI) of the Oregon DOJ is responsible for that oversight and for applications from community providers, generally child abuse centers for assessment, and the administration of funds.

• Defense lawyers encounter the practical functions of the MDT at the county level. By statute, the district attorney in each county is supposed to be the head of the MDT. In most counties, the head of the MDT is the district attorney or an individual to whom the district attorney has delegated that responsibility. In large district attorneys’ offices, there are often MDT units that focus on the prosecution of child abuse cases. In Multnomah County, a deputy district attorney on the MDT unit will prosecute both the criminal and the juvenile court dependency facet of a child abuse case when both exist. In most large counties, a MDT deputy will be responsible for the prosecution in criminal court, but a deputy assigned to juvenile court will be responsible for the prosecution of a dependency matter.

The MDT at the county level is, by statute, made up of many individuals from predominantly local government entities involved in working with children. The critical members of the MDT are the district attorney’s office, law enforcement, DHS Child Welfare Division, and the local child abuse assessment center. Representatives from school districts and mental health organizations can be other members of the MDT, but generally they do not play a significant role in the formation and structure of investigation and prosecution of child abuse cases in Oregon.

The district attorney’s office by statute is supposed to establish a protocol for the investigation of child abuse cases in each county. The defense should have a copy of the protocol for each county relevant to that attorney’s sex abuse defense practice.

The roles of the essential members of the MDT involved in a prosecution of child sex abuse are as follows:

1. The District Attorney’s Office

Following reports from law enforcement and DHS, the district attorney’s office is responsible for the consideration and possible presentation to the grand jury for indictment in
a child sex abuse prosecution. In several counties the district attorney’s office is also responsible for the prosecution of child sex abuse cases in dependency matters in juvenile court. In many counties the attorney general’s office handles dependency matters in juvenile court.

2. Law Enforcement

Law enforcement is responsible for the initial investigative response into matters where there is a reasonable basis to believe child sex abuse has occurred. Most large departments have detectives who receive the initial reports from deputies and police officers who respond to initial reports of abuse, and from DHS, as a matter of cross-reporting. Cross-
reporting between DHS and law enforcement is required by statute and the relevant
OAR in Chapter 413. Most frequently, detectives are responsible for the evidence, the documents, and the case ultimately presented to the district attorney’s office for review and consideration for prosecution. The DA then decides if the case will be presented to grand jury. If it is unlikely to be a criminal case, more often than not DHS Child Protective Services (CPS) will be in charge of case investigation where there is a concern that sex abuse occurred.

Many detectives in Oregon have substantial experience in attending MDT meetings, attending trainings put on by regional assessment centers or large law enforcement agencies. They have engaged, for many years, in work in which their focus is investigation for testimony that can be used at trial in child sex abuse cases.

Some smaller law enforcement departments will, by practice, refer cases to larger departments so that a detective who has more expertise in the investigation and presentation of child sex abuse cases is in charge of the matter. All larger counties have detectives whose primary work is the investigation of child abuse.

Multnomah County has had for many years a CAT Team (Child Abuse Team), which focused on the investigation and presentation of child abuse cases.

In venues with smaller populations, follow-up on child sex abuse reporting tends to be assigned to the more experienced officers who often have established a long-term working relationship with the DHS branch in their county and with the prosecutor.

3. DHS

The recipient of child abuse reporting is by statute (ORS 419B.015) either the local law enforcement agency or DHS.

DHS may play a primary role in the initial investigation of child sex abuse cases. The general rules of DHS are the following:

DHS has a screening section, which responds to initial reports of abuse and determines follow-up assignments. Child Protective Services (CPS) is the section of DHS responsible for investigation of child abuse, including child sex abuse. Screening workers receive calls and make determinations about whether or not the call merits a follow-up, a short-term follow-up, or an immediate follow-up.

DHS makes an attempt to ascertain, during the early stages of involvement, whether the primary focus is going to be criminal investigation of the suspect for child sex abuse, or child welfare–oriented, where there may well not be a criminal prosecution but there is a sufficient level of concern connected to the report to merit investigation, follow-up, and protective action being taken. Per OAR Chapter 413, from the inception of the case DHS refers to the accused person as the alleged perpetrator.8

In the initial weeks of an MDT investigation, the degree of focus on potential criminal prosecution may increase or lessen, and the degree of focus on child welfare and child protection may vary. As the distinct and overlapping roles of DHS and law enforcement are better defined during the initial stages, this is when law enforcement or DHS is more likely to be primarily responsible for investigation.

The array of responses by law enforcement to a situation may range from immediate arrest of a suspect, to ultimate arrest of a suspect for prosecution, to a suspect first being arrested on a warrant post-presentation to grand jury, to a no-prosecution determination, or to an early-stage determination that the level of evidence will not rise to probable cause or an eventual successful opportunity for prosecution.

As we know, police involvement is supposed to range from probable cause assessment to the capacity to prove the case beyond a reasonable doubt. Regarding DHS, the range of assessment connected to standards of proof is much broader. Even in situations in which law enforcement does not find sufficient cause to enable prosecution, DHS may decide that it can meet a more-likely-than-not or preponderance-of-the-evidence standard in juvenile court. Additionally, DHS has the option of safety planning or entering founded dispositions based on a reasonable cause, or a reasonable conclusion based on a substantial facts standard, which is below the standard for probable cause.

Both DHS and law enforcement have cross-reporting requirements as a matter of statute (ORS 419B.0175) and policy. Cross-reporting means that DHS and law enforcement report to one another when one is the recipient of an initial call of child abuse and regarding essential developments in the progress of an investigation.

The importance of communication between DHS and law enforcement is underscored by the fact that DHS, by administrative rule, has a responsibility to interview the suspect of a child sex abuse allegation, but DHS may defer or avoid that responsibility if law enforcement has conducted an interview of a suspect or made reasonable efforts to interview a suspect.

To summarize, the negative outcomes for a suspect in a child sex abuse allegation are
listed below:

  • Law enforcement may arrest based on probable cause or the district attorney may prosecute.
  • DHS may file a petition in juvenile court and remove the child or children from the custody of their parent or parents or caregiver on a preponderance-of-the-evidence standard.
  • DHS may enter an administrative conclusion of a Founded Disposition for sex abuse, which can have sweeping ramifications on employment and opportunities to work with and volunteer with children. These conclusions are formed on “a reasonable basis in substantial fact” standard and are subject to challenge under the Oregon Administrative Procedures Act.

4. Local Child Abuse Assessment Center

The fourth critical member of the MDT is the local child abuse assessment center.

There is a formal structure by statute for child abuse assessment centers in Oregon. CARES Northwest, founded in 1987, was the first such center in Oregon.

By statutory structure, regional assessment centers conduct assessments and provide education and training to fellow MDT members, including other child abuse assessment centers.

There are five regional assessment centers in Oregon:

• CARES Northwest, Portland

• Kids’ FIRST Center, Eugene

• KIDS Center, Bend

• The Children’s Advocacy Center of Jackson County, Medford

• Mt. Emily Safe Center, La Grande

All five regional assessment centers have child abuse assessment centers or interviewers and medical examiners who are part of (for lack of a better term) satellite educational and training systems connected to other child abuse assessment centers. Historically, children were often subjected to multiple in-depth forensic interviews in the initial stages of a child sex abuse assessment. Unfortunately, that still can occur. One role of the centers is to lessen the need for multiple in-depth interviews of children. This is both for the child comfort’s and the recognition that multiple interviews contaminate the value of a child’s report.

The general role of the center is a physical assessment of and an interview assessment of a child. Regional assessment centers and most child abuse assessment centers are predicated on a medical model. Kids’ FIRST in Eugene is not a medical model program.

Record-keeping, document-keeping, and the quality of documentation varies from program to program. However, programs generally follow current guideline recommendations during interviews of children regarding sex abuse. The role of a center
is to:

• Assess the context and factors attendant to a sex abuse complaint.

• Form an opinion or diagnosis about the level of concern.

• Make treatment recommendations and refer a treatment resource for the child.

• Interact with law enforcement and DHS regarding the conduct of and make recommendations coming out of the assessment.

• Provide expert testimony supporting prosecution in criminal and dependency prosecutions.

Practice Tip

Many prosecutors and DHS will discover only the assessment report from a child abuse assessment. It is important to subpoena the entire file. Studying intake notes and background information received by the center, as well as having an expert study the interviews and the entire file, is essential for the defense. (Kids’ FIRST in Eugene does not keep records of an interview other than a DVR of the interview itself.) To get the entire file, submit a motion and order for a pretrial subpoena pursuant to ORS 136.580 and file an appropriate protective order to allow you to receive the DVR of the child’s interview.

V. MDT and Other Guidelines for Interviewing and Assessing Child Sex Abuse

Since 1998, the CAMI section of the DOJ has published guidelines regarding recommended practices for interviewing children in child sex case cases. The original Oregon Interviewing Guidelines were published in 1998 and amended in 2004. The current Oregon Interview Guidelines were published in October of 2012.

The CAMI interview guidelines make recommendations about the manner in which an assessment should be conducted and how children should be questioned regarding concern of sex abuse. Unfortunately, the October 2012 Guidelines eliminate a significant majority of the research that was the basis of the 1998 and 2004 guidelines. Much of that research had to do with how children are suggestible and the impact of complex content, such as custody and parenting time disputes. In fact, the 2004 guidelines had a specific chapter dealing with the impact of children’s reporting on conflict in custody and parenting time cases. With that chapter eliminated, the current guidelines emphasize helping children be comfortable reporting abuse. They ignore guidance regarding how to engage in objective questioning or interviewer-investigator analysis to help understand different explanations for a child’s report.

Practice Tip

The 2012 Guidelines eliminate about 75 percent of the previously contained research and national expert guidance, which makes it harder to impeach state experts for failing to follow much of their own published theory and research regarding good practice. Eliminating nationally recognized research and practice from the guidelines weakens the standards interviewers are required to uphold, and be held accountable to, when interviewing children.

The current Guidelines still pay homage to well-recognized national guidelines, which do discuss such things as context and alternative theories for a child’s report. The vast majority of Oregon MDT interviewers will be ignorant both of the specifics of the national guidelines and the concepts they embrace.

Published national guidelines include the Guidelines for Psychological Evaluations and Child Protection Matters, published initially by the APA in 1999 and revised in 2013; the American Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Guidelines for Responding to Children Who Say They Have Been Sexually Abused, published in 1996; the APSAC (American Professional Society on the Abuse of Children) Guidelines regarding the Commercial Sexual Exploitation of Children, published in 2013; the National Children’s Alliance Standards for the Interviewing of Children in Child Sex Abuse Cases, published in 2011; and the National Institute of Child Health Protocols Interviewing Guidelines for Evidence Based Investigation and Interviews of Children, published
in 2013.

Additionally, there are medical guidelines for assessing the physical conditions of children relevant to child sex abuse. These guidelines reflect knowledge of pediatricians and physicians that debunks early-stage beliefs referenced earlier in this chapter. These include the CAMI-sponsored DOJ Oregon Medical Guidelines, initially published in 1998 and revised in 2004; the American Academy of Pediatrics Guidelines on the Evaluation of Children in the Primary Care Setting When Sexual Abuse Is Suspected, Carole Jenny and the Committee on Child Abuse and Neglect, 2013; the AAP standards on Evaluation of Sexual Abuse in Children by Nancy Kellogg and the Committee on Child Abuse and Neglect, 2005; previous AAP standards were published in 1999 and 1991; and Updated Guidelines for the Medical Assessment of Children Who May Have Been Sexually Abused, published in 2015, Adams, Kellogg, et. al., in the Journal of Adolescent Gynecology.

Interview Suggestions

When encountering a government expert, defense questioning should include:

• Where they received the background information that guided their assessment and interaction with the child.

• If they self-generated any effort to receive background materials.

• If they are open to receiving background information from the suspect or from individuals who are not connected to the alleged abuse or the MDT.

• If they have any knowledge about the history of the relationship between the accuser and the accused.

• If they made any inquiry into the history of the relationship between the accuser and
the accused.

• If they know to whom the child first reported.

• If they know the history of the relationship between the person reported to and
the accused.

• If they made any effort to be informed about the history of the relationship between the person reported to and the accused.

• Whether they are collecting preliminary data based on a part of the whole picture,
data that excludes input from the suspect and others who may not support the allegation of abuse.

• If they are familiar with the concept of “secondary gain.” (Secondary gain is when there is a benefit or a motive connected to a report that is not self-evident in the report.)

• What effort they made in interviewing the child to analyze the influence of others on the child’s report.

• Whether they made recommendations that impact the child’s relationship with the suspect even though they did not have any direct information from the suspect or sources connected to the suspect. (This is different than a Southard or Lupoli issue because you are not asking about a specific conclusion, you are pointing out that your client is being impacted without the expert being informed about or hearing the other side of the story.)

• If, as a pediatrician or nurse practitioner, interviewer, detective or caseworker, they operate as a team or as community partners with prosecution, law enforcement, DHS or the local child abuse assessment facility.

As a matter of law, ORS 418.747, they are part of a county team headed by the district attorney in each county. Pursuant to ORS 418.783 and 418.793, child abuse investigation is subject to the oversight of the attorney general’s office and particularly so in the case of the child abuse assessment center answerable to the attorney general’s office. Generally, experts who are members of the MDT established by statute are ignorant of the legal structure of the MDT. Often, and particularly in the case of child abuse assessment facility experts, they will try to claim that they are independent or based in the medical or behavioral sciences. In Lane County and Deschutes County the district attorney delegates their statutory authority to the regional assessment center. The Oregon Supreme Court clearly recognizes the role of proxy or teammate of the prosecution and law enforcement as vital to the function of a child abuse assessment center, be it medical or behavioral in structure, as any other function. [https://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?case=1689712124358304376&q=State+ex+rel.+Juv.+Dept.+v.+S.P.,+346+Or+592&hl=en&as_sdt=6,48 State ex rel. Juv. Dept. v. S.P.], 346 Or 592, 215 P3d 847 (2009).

• Whether the expert is familiar with the 2012 edition of the Oregon Interviewing Guidelines published by the DOJ.

• Whether the expert is aware that the DOJ or the attorney general’s office is in charge or in an oversight position regarding the quality of child abuse investigation
in Oregon.

• Whether the interviewer is aware of the recommendation that children not be subjected to duplicate interviews.

• If they know how many times the child has been engaged in conversation about the facts involving the allegation. (MDT experts and interviewers like to misconstrue what an interview is. They generally try and restrict its definition to a formal structured conversation that addresses the allegations. A better definition is any conversation in which someone is talking to the child and the allegations are a topic of conversation.)

• Whether they made any inquiry or are aware whether law enforcement or DHS has made any inquiry into the motives of individuals who engaged in conversation with the child about the allegations.

• Whether the goal of a competent professional interview is to elicit a child’s free recall as opposed to the influenced statements of a child.

• What questions they asked the child to determine whether the child’s information had been influenced by someone else.

• If they are familiar with the term “contamination” as used as a professional term relevant to a child’s report.

• Whether they are familiar with the use of the term “contamination” and its interrelationship with suggestibility per the work of Dr. Stephen Ceci. (Dr. Ceci is to the field of child sex abuse reporting and assessing the accuracy of child’s reports as to what Einstein is to physics. Many contemporary MDT experts are unfamiliar with him.)

• Experts often refer to studies in general, which is a tipoff that they do not have research or expert knowledge regarding the studies they refer to. If that happens, make specific inquiry about what specific studies and researchers they are referring.

• Whether the expert is familiar with the significant body of research that addresses human memory and its relationship to the perception of and reporting of facts. Most MDT experts lack knowledge in this area, and generally there is a complete lack of knowledge relevant to the impact of the brain’s function on memory. There is a general lack of knowledge about how human memory functions. That means there is a general lack of knowledge about how children’s memory functions. In recent years, law and science have developed a more sophisticated understanding of human memory and its relationship to fact reporting. The Oregon Supreme Court demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of the issue relevant to eyewitness testimony in State v. Lawson, 352 Or 724, 291 P3d
673 (2012).

• If they understand how children’s memory differs from adult memory. (Be prepared to make hay with the fact that if they do not understand how memory works, they probably cannot helpfully address this question.)

• If they can name any researcher in the medical or behavioral sciences who focuses on the relationship between the function of the brain and the retention and reporting of accurate information.

• If it is a case where the reporting is in any way connected to custody, parenting time, or relationship disputes, ask if they are familiar with any of the research that addresses high-conflict relationships and the impact on children’s reporting.

Be prepared for the fact that most experts affiliated with the government have knowledge that comes from attending seminars and in-house trainings that narrow the scope and focus of information they are exposed to.

VI. The Role and Scope and Availability of Experts

When analyzing how to use your own expert, anticipating the state’s use of an expert, and integrating law and research for the scope of what you will be able to present and defend against, here are some essential elements to bear in mind:

A. Evidence

Understand the traditional concept that expert testimony can only come in under OEC 702, 703, and 705, and whether it falls into one of three categories:

• It is scientifically valid.

• It is reflective of specialized knowledge on the part of the expert that will be helpful to the trier of fact.

• Impeachment of state experts.

Essential cases that focus on the concept of scientific validity are Brown in 1984 and O’Key in 1995. An essential difference between those two cases is that Brown is predicated on federal standards under Fry, and O’Key is predicated on federal standards under Daubert. Brown and O’Key articulate standards by which proffered expert testimony can meet scientifically valid standards. (Note: See the OCDLA manual, Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys, for more on these cases and the issue generally.)

A significant development regarding the use of experts in Oregon is the Lupoli decision of 2010, which bars expert testimony that provides a direct commentary on the credibility of a witness (vouching).

A significant case limiting the ability of medical experts, interviewers, or others to comment on their conclusions in a child sex abuse case in the form of diagnosis or degree of concern is the Southard case.

As noted earlier in this chapter, the long established rulings of the Oregon Supreme Court that syndrome evidence is inadmissible are often ignored when it comes to MDT and government testimony but invoked when it comes to defense witnesses. The critical cases in Oregon addressing the lack of admissibility of syndrome evidence are State v. Milbradt, 305 Or 621, 756 P2d 620 (1988), and State v. Middleton, 294 Or 427, 657, P2d 215 (1983).

The contention that child abuse victims or child sex abuse victims can be identified by
certain behaviors or that they react to sex abuse in particular ways is called syndrome evidence, which is to say that an individual behaves a certain way because that individual falls into a particular classification. Syndrome evidence may generally be attacked as not being subject to scientific validation under Brown and O’Key. It can also be attacked under Lupoli because it is a way of asserting that an individual’s claim is true based on observable conditions that are based in turn on the expert’s opinion.

However, since Lupoli, the Court of Appeals has allowed forms of syndrome evidence to come into evidence. For example, see State v. Althof, 273 Or App 342 (2015), rev. den., 358 Or 550 (2016) (detective allowed to testify on reasons why victims of sexual abuse might delay reporting); State v. Swinney, 269 Or App 548, rev. den., 357 Or 743 (2015) (detective allowed to testify that the events described by victim constituted grooming); State v. Criswell, 282 Or App 146 (2016) (MDT interviewer allowed to testify on phrases abused children may use when they first disclose abuse to parents, even though the victim used nearly identical wording in disclosing to mother; stating rule as “an expert witness may describe characteristics typical of an abuse victim and then apply that understanding to the case at hand, so long as that testimony does not rely on an assessment of the victim’s credibility”). The better ruling is that such testimony should not be allowed into evidence because it is conclusory and indirect vouching, is syndrome evidence, is not subject to scientific validation, and is often subject to the biases and predisposition of the expert who is describing such behavior or statements.

Practice Tip

Defense counsel should make and preserve objections on all of these grounds, make continuing objections, make offers of proof, and consider new approaches. For example, in State v. Thomas, 279 Or App 98, rev. den., 360 Or 423 (2016), the Court of Appeals affirmed the trial court’s exclusion of testimony by a defense expert concerning a particular study where false memories of sex abuse were created in children following a physical examination, on hearsay grounds. At a 104 hearing, police and other child abuse investigators will acknowledge that their training and experience is learned anecdotally through discussion with others, listening to speakers at seminars and from reading various studies, then applied by them in the field. The argument can then be made that testimony about common characteristics or behaviors of abused children simply parrots those hearsay sources.

B. Experts for the Defense

Defense experts in child sex abuse cases generally come in two categories:

1. Pediatricians, pathologists, gynecologists, and emergency room doctors.

These are experts in the physical symptomatology and conditions of children relevant to concerns of child sex abuse.

The need for experts knowledgeable about the physical condition of children significantly lessened a quarter century ago with the elimination of government experts’ false beliefs about anogenital conditions wrongly presented as proof of sex abuse of children. Unfortunately, in recent years the number of MDT experts misrepresenting the physical symptomatology connected to the sex abuse of children is on the rise. Conditions that are clearly not attributable to sex abuse are sometimes so attributed. More common is an expert who misuses medical testimony to assert that a condition could be symptomatic of sex abuse and therefore is concerning for sex abuse.

There are some instances in which a medical expert may still prove necessary and helpful. Such instances may include situations in which a poorly informed medical provider claims that a condition is symptomatic of child sex abuse when it is not. There may also be instances where a child reports severe intrusive penetrating rape suffered to a painful degree on multiple occasions at a preadolescent age, and medical experts differ on the likelihood of whether or not such victimization will create long-term physical trauma such as ancient healing, scarring, and transections. Most studies relevant to the subject are not helpful as they include all categories of child sex abuse when in the vast majority of cases there is no physical evidence of sex abuse. Most forms of sexual victimization of children are not likely to create physical trauma. Exceptions include acute trauma, STD transfer, and very painful trauma.

There are anogenital studies involving early-stage adolescents who claim not to have been otherwise involved in sexual activity where, generally speaking, there was observable variance caused by trauma as a result of the abuse.

National experts on the topic of the anatomical condition of children relevant to sex abuse include John McCann, Nancy Kellogg, Shireen Atabaki, Joyce Adams, Carole Jenny, Theodore Hariton, and Jan Paradise.

2. Behavioral science experts, such as child psychiatrists, behavioral/developmental pediatricians (psychologist and child psychologists), licensed clinical social workers and professional counselors.

These are professionals with an expertise about interviewing and understanding the context relevant to child sex abuse allegations who can be helpful in the defense of child sex abuse cases. Use such experts in two primary categories:

a. Exploring the method in which children were asked questions in interviews.

Were those methodologies appropriate? Did the methodology create contamination of reports? Experts in this vein can be helpful in underscoring some of the biases of the MDT members involved in the investigation that led to the prosecution.

b. The field of human memory.

This is a developing field in child sex abuse reporting.

C. Suggestibility

The merger between individuals who have an understanding about appropriate methodologies on how to interview children and investigate child sex abuse, and who have
an expertise on memory leads to experts who are knowledgeable about the fields of suggestibility as they apply to children’s reporting. Suggestibility is a body of research, as mentioned previously, which goes back nearly 40 years. Suggestibility studies the degree to which children can be influenced and how they are influenced in their reporting of sex abuse and other matters.

Here are the most common sources of suggestibility:

  • Parents, caregivers and relatives who are hostile to the adult accused of sex abuse.
  • Former romantic partners who are hostile to the recipient of a child sex abuse complaint, where the child of the former romantic partner is the purported victim.
  • First responders, such as school counselors, DHS workers, and law enforcement officers who may be encountering a child confused about a boundary or a fact subject to interpretation and who is then redirected into a report of sex abuse with inaccurate details from the predispositions, biases, and contaminating nature of the interview.
  • Therapists and counselors. In addition to the problem of therapists and counselors who, based on their own experience or minimal training, suspect abuse when it has not occurred and draw out abuse reporting through inappropriate methodology, there is a more common approach engaged in by therapists. Parents who are poorly motivated or who simply may misunderstand normal childhood behavior and boundaries commonly bring their children to a therapist who will assist a child in reporting sexual abuse.

Therapy is not a competent setting for the elicitation of disclosures of sexual abuse. There are distinct differences between the therapeutic role and the forensic role. Therapists respond to emotions, yet it is not their job to make forensic determinations about the occurrence of facts. Beyond that, even many licensed providers with Master’s degrees in Oregon are extremely poorly trained about appropriate methodology for interacting with children with concerns for sex abuse and all too often use blatantly inappropriate methods. Methods are used that are contaminating and can be emotionally abusive to children or can aid and abet the emotional abuse by others of a child. Unfortunately, the ethics standards for a LCSW, LPC, and LMFT are only six pages long and woefully inadequate. Psychiatrists and psychologists are constrained by having to engage in methods that are connected to the formation of conclusions based on objective data. All this begs the question that many counselors and therapists to whom parents and DHS refer are not licensed or adequately licensed, and they may lack both practical skills and ethical constraints on their interaction with children who may have been sexually abused.

Practice Tip

If a child’s report of sexual abuse is a product of inept therapeutic intervention, the use of a well-qualified mental health expert familiar with appropriate forensic methodology, the appropriate limits of therapy, and the ethical requirements of the relevant profession can be extremely helpful to the defense.

Suggestibility is a risk for all human beings and particularly young children. Research has well established that adults, let alone children, subjected to multiple presentations of data will, when that data is repeatedly or intensely presented, come to adopt it as true even when it is not. Significant amounts of the research on suggestibility relevant to child sex abuse reporting are listed in the articles at the end of this chapter.

Some nationally and internationally renowned contemporary experts on suggestibility and inappropriate methodology for interacting with children in the investigation of sexual abuse include psychologists Michael Lamb, Steven Lindsay, Deborah Ann Poole, and Tom Lyon. Several of the researchers mentioned previously in this chapter, such as Drs. Faller, Goodman, Ceci and Loftus, remain active.

There are substantial challenges to the use of experts by defense lawyers in criminal and dependency child sex abuse defense cases.

Practice Tip

While there are exceptions, particularly in the more rural areas of Oregon, most interviewers at child abuse assessment centers engage in appropriate technical formations of questions with children. This is often not the case with field responders, who will excuse their contaminating impact by claiming it is not actually a forensic interview. A more sophisticated approach understands that each time a child is interviewed about a concern for sex abuse, it has a potential to influence and contaminate the child’s report. Most experts available to the defense will agree with this.

Oregon Guidelines provide a reasonably good structure for the type of questions that a child should be asked. Of course, questions should vary in detail and complexity depending on the child’s age and developmental stage. Generally speaking, a competent interviewer should never ask a child below the age of six about a date or timeframe for an event. Concepts of time and event sequencing are beyond the developmental capacity of young children. Sometimes interviewers violate that and contaminate the report of a child.

As noted, Oregon’s Guidelines are generally good in terms of their advice regarding how an interviewer should form questions. Dr. Kathleen Coulborn Faller of the University of Michigan Family Assessment Clinic and School of Social Work is a leading research-clinician in the United States on the formation of questioning and the impact of formulated questions on children in child sex abuse cases. Dr. Faller’s work bridges the generations of child sex abuse assessment and is contemporary in function and value. What quality and comprehensive research and experience in the field of child sex abuse assessment reveals is the Oregon practice is less deficient in questions asked and extremely deficient in the scope of investigation.

Dr. Faller recommends that guidelines or principles for questioning children about possible sex abuse include:

  • Gather information from the child rather than asking the child to confirm information from a caretaker or other source. (This recommendation is commonly violated by MDT interviewers in Oregon.)

  • Use as many open-ended questions as possible. (This recommendation is generally followed by MDT interviewers in Oregon.)

  • Attempt to obtain a narrative account of what happened from the child. (This is followed with mixed results in Oregon. Often the structure of MDT interviews in Oregon seeks to narrow the narrative account to the suspected concern.)

  • If open-ended questions are not effective, do not give up. Use a more close-ended question. (This recommendation is generally followed by the MDT in Oregon. However, once a leading question or a close-ended question is introduced, MDT interviews are often slow to get back to an open-ended inquiry process.)

  • If you use a more close-ended question, place less confidence in the information obtained. (Here, law enforcement, DHS, and prosecutors are often deficient because they often do not differentiate in the quality of information received.)

  • Close-ended questions that elicit a positive response should be followed by a more open-ended one (e.g., Tell me more about that). (This recommendation is frequently violated by MDT interviewers in Oregon as more leading questions or close-ended questions often follow the use of a close-ended question.)
  • Explore alternative explanations for the concern about sexual abuse (e.g., no sexual abuse occurred, child care behavior mistaken for sexual behavior, advanced sexual knowledge from other experiences). (This recommendation is generally grossly violated by MDT practice in Oregon. Now, per Southard, the defense cannot attack diagnoses purporting to be based on a differential process that engage in no differential analysis, so exposing this deficiency has lesser impact.)

As Dr. Faller has noted, “The appropriateness of a question depends a great deal on its context.”

Dr. Faller has created a continuum of questions from preferred to not preferred. Preferred questions include:

• Open-ended inquiry about a child’s well-being.

• Open abuse-related directive questions such as, “Do you know why I’m talking to you?”

• Invitational questions like, “Tell me all you remember” or “Tell me what happened.”

• Facilitating cueing such as, “Okay, anything else?” or “What happened next?”

• Acute invitations such as, “What room were you in?” or “Where was your mom?”

Less preferred questions that Dr. Faller identifies as problems include:

• Multiple-choice questions, as in presenting a child with multiple opportunities to choose. (In my experience, these types of questions are confusing to children, particularly young children, and provide crapshoot data.)

• Forced restricted choice questions are very contaminating. Examples of these kinds of questions are: “Did he do it one time or more than one time?” “Did the abuse happen during the day or night or both?” “When did your mom talk to you about your daddy hurting you?”

• Direct questions can be highly leading and contaminating such as a statement the child is asked to confirm like: “Did Ralph touch your peepee?” “Did Ralph hurt your peepee?” “Did he put his finger inside you?”

We all know that leading questions carry a danger of narrowing a response to the information presented. A leading question can also suggest the answer. With children, the concerns for contamination about such questions heightens. A grossly contaminating form of a leading question is one in which a tag follows the lead. Dr. Faller provides a couple of good examples of those types of questions: “Isn’t it true that your brother put his penis in your mouth?” (an example of a grossly leading question) or “Your stepfather is the one who hurt you, isn’t he?” (an example of a leading question with a tag).

The worst questions, and ones we fortunately rarely see, are coercive. Parents, caregivers, and individuals with a motive to influence a child’s statement may engage in this form of questioning, though it is rare for a MDT investigator to do so. An example of this would be: “If you don’t tell me what I want you to, you won’t be able to see your brother.” A rare MDT example might be: “I know this is taking a long time but if you tell me what I need to know, I’ll stop questioning you.”

Remember that in Oregon, and particularly at the larger assessment facilities, errors occur more with the scope and narrowness of the inquiry and not in the use of grossly leading questions. What you may see with some frequency are the following:

• Failure to return to open-ended questions after using a leading prompt.

• Engaging in grossly leading questions such as, “I heard you told your mommy about what Ronnie did to you,” or “Can you tell me all about what Ronnie did to you?”

• The use of multiple-choice questions.

• The use of words, time sequence concepts, etc. that are beyond the child’s developmental abilities.

• Inappropriate cueing. Following an appropriate question like “Do you know why you’re here today?” with a structuring statement like “I heard somebody did something bad to you, can you tell me about that?” combines appropriate methods and inappropriate methods and undermines the usefulness of the information received.

A very limited number of medical experts are available to the defense. Almost all forensic pediatricians are or have been affiliated with child abuse assessment centers and are reluctant or generally outright unwilling to work with the defense.

To find a physician outside forensic pediatrics with expertise about trauma to the anal or genital area who can compete with a government forensic pediatrician or nurse practitioner, look to emergency room physicians (both current and former), pediatric gynecologists, and pathologists.

There are a few psychologists and child psychologists in the Pacific Northwest with substantial relevant experience who are deeply conversant in the research and literature about proper methods to interview and investigate child sex abuse. These experts can address suggestibility of children and methodologies that contaminate children’s reports. These experts can also address, relevant to age and development, how children receive, store and report experiences and what factors can impact, in a general sense and not a specific sense, on that reporting. This line of questioning will generally survive a Lupoli challenge.

Post-Southard, very few cases present an opportunity for a physical condition debate in child sex abuse cases; that does not apply to dependency cases, however.

Post-Lupoli, the state’s experts are frequently allowed to comment on things like grooming and delayed reporting and how they generally apply in child sex abuse cases and reporting. Experts for the defense can be used to rebut generalized surface syndrome expertise, help challenge whether those concepts are valid as OEC 702 specialized knowledge, clinical observations, or valid as OEC 702 scientifically valid conclusions. Defense experts can help underscore that the state’s experts are operating in a milieu of presumptive bias.

Almost all government experts called by the state in criminal and juvenile cases come, not surprisingly, from within the MDT itself. When dealing with experts affiliated with medical child abuse assessment facilities, defense counsel should be aware that they work in a team concept. Pediatricians or nurse practitioners may somewhat creatively construe knowledge and studies in ways beneficial to the team cause. Regarding interviews and generalized concepts about children who are sexually abused, the state all too often presents the Master’s-level LPC or LCSW interviewer as an “expert” about the child’s statements, factors common to sex abuse, investigation and interview methodologies engaged in, and the significance of questions and answers received from a child. The state may try—and unfortunately all too often is allowed by the court—to present testimony of detectives or DHS as experts on: 1) how abused children present, 2) how they commonly represent facts, and 3) how they find a particular response matches up with a category for victims per the experience of the observer. How that gets past prohibitions on syndrome evidence and comments on credibility may strike some as bizarre, but we work in a state where many trial courts appear to bend over backwards to ignore restrictions on the state’s evidence
and to protect child victims.

Practice Tip

The selection and use of your own expert:

  1. Ascertain whether the medical or behavioral sciences will be the most fruitful source of expertise to assist your case. In some cases, it will be both.

  2. The Oregon Medical Guidelines, although published in 2004 by the DOJ, have generally up-to-date information that can be used against misconstruing and misrepresentation by state experts regarding the anogenital condition of a child.

  3. If your expert is a behavioral scientist such as a psychologist, clinical social worker, or professional counselor, they should be familiar with the Oregon and national guidelines standards regarding the interviewing and assessment of children where there is a concern for sex abuse.

  4. While the rules of evidence do not allow research and treatises during direct examination, if you properly laid the foundation by demonstrating the ignorance of the state’s expert, you can then for the purposes of impeachment introduce evidence through learned treatises about seminal researchers and scientifically accepted conclusions of which the expert was ignorant.

  5. Make sure that all material available to the MDT is available to your expert. While your expert, per Lupoli, cannot comment on conclusions reached by the MDT, they can comment on process and how distorted process can create a distorted product.

  6. Ask your expert whether certain actions and inquiries were necessary to a fair and objective investigation or inquiry into the present matter.

  7. Once your expert has an opportunity to review materials and if your expert is sufficiently sophisticated, make sure you inquire in detail about their observations regarding flaws in process and interviews that occurred during the MDT investigation.

  8. Make sure your expert is familiar with the MDT function in Oregon. If not, supply them with the statutes from ORS 418.746 to 418.800. That way, not only will your expert be knowledgeable, they will impeach the child abuse assessment center’s representations, which are most often misrepresentations about their role and function. For example, in Multnomah County and Washington County, experts affiliated with CARES most often describe themselves as a medical center focused on medical assessment and protection of the safety and welfare of children. That mantra is generally repeated in various different forms throughout Oregon. While it is not untrue, it is highly misleading because it presents a picture that is narrow and self-serving.

  9. If your experience is not sufficient to recognize the issues, consult with your expert before trial to see if secondary gain, suggestibility, and contamination present as legitimate concerns in the interviewing and investigation relevant to the child’s report.
