Defending Sex Cases

Defending Sex Cases: Strategies from Allegation to Resolution, 2018 edition

Introduction

“How can you defend those people?”1

At some time or another we have to fend off this question. It’s so much a part of our lives that we probably have a repertoire of stock replies. Mine have changed over time, from the younger, idealistic, über- aggressive public defender:

“How? With vigor!!!”

Which really wasn’t an answer to the question asked but was about as accurate as I could be at the time. I really didn’t know how or why, I just did it, and I really didn’t have the time to think I about it, my practice was similar to an ER doctor, my clients were having a legal emergency and I was ER lawyer, triaging.

As I got older, maybe a little more jaded, I continued with still more avoidant, but maybe a bit more nuanced answers. From the pragmatic:

“I have a job to do, and somebody has to do it.”

“Because I was appointed,” or “It pays the bills.”

“Better ten guilty walk than one innocent convicted.”

“If everyone refused…”

“There but for the grace of God go I…”

To more career-focused:

“I love trial work.”

“Never a dull day… So many stories.”

To (maybe) more noble thoughts:

“I’m quality control for the government.”

“I’m defending the Constitution.”

“I’m called to it.”

To questioning the questioner:

“Who am I to judge?”

“What is truth?”

“Isn’t there a difference between moral guilt and legal guilt?”

These stock answers were all good and fine for the average case, but sex cases are different. The cast of characters and drama presented is so much more amplified. Put those stock answers under glass and they crack, they shatter. Why? Why do those stock answers that allowed me to avoid judgment, avoid introspection so effectively for the shootings, stabbings and homicides fail so miserably in sex cases?

At times, the idea of defending sex crimes just felt dirty. Gross. Just the written description of child porn images has made me feel horrible, wondering who are these despicable people who have stolen the magic of childhood and propagated these images and videos for the world to see? And to do horrible things with? When I couldn’t avoid it anymore and had to go and look at the images? Well, it is fair to say that some of those images are indelibly seared into my memory. Deviants and perverts and degenerates, oh my!

When we try these cases the drama is amplified. Amplified by the extreme stress attendant to sex crime allegations, caused when you have to confront extremely vulnerable complainants – which the DA will, with relish, call “Vulnerable Victims.” Add to that the high stakes of potential lengthy prison sentences and possible lifetime sex offender registration (the modern day Scarlet Letter), the pejorative nature of the mere allegation, and what seems like the incredibly biased perspectives of the detectives, CPS workers, and child interviewers, and the drama and stress are over the redline.

So why do we, why do I, represent those accused of sex crimes?

Partly because now it’s different. Partly because now I’m different. Over the past ten years I started to take on more and more of these cases, enmeshing myself in the law, enmeshing myself in the science, the psychology of accusers and the accused, unraveling all of the things I used to know, the things I had accepted, like doctrine, like an ideology, as to the manner in which people interact socially, emotionally and, yes, of course, sexually.

So as I dug in, these cases, these types of charges, the research and literature I’ve reviewed, and most of all, the relationships I have had with my investigators, with psychologists, and most importantly with clients have changed me. I started to actually like, and now I’d say love, doing this type of work.

Clarence Darrow, the famed criminal defense lawyer, once wrote,

Strange as it may seem, I grew to like to defend men and women charged with crime. It soon came to be something more than winning or losing a case. I sought to learn why one man goes one way and another takes an entirely different road. I became vitally interested in the causes of human conduct... I was dealing with life, with its hopes and fears, its aspirations and despairs. With me it was going to the foundations of motive and conduct and adjustments for human beings, instead of blindly talking of hatred and vengeance, and that subtle, indefinable quality that men call “justice” and of which nothing is really known…

Sometimes we represent the innocent accused. We know this happens. Could be the result of a broken marriage, a parent trying to get an advantage in court and using their child as a weapon of revenge or planting files on a hard drive. It could be innocent behavior, mistaken as frottage or a quick grope. Maybe it’s two adults, both fueled by too many cocktails and a foggy morning after shrouded in regret, rejection and, then, blame. It might be a peer-to-peer mass download of images or videos, of which just a few were contraband, never opened.

Sometimes we represent the overcharged, those whose criminal exposure is just too great, unsupported by common sense and unsupported by the risk assessment tools. Maybe boundaries have been crossed, but they are not so serious that incarceration or registry should be at issue. Or maybe a question should have been asked, “Really, are you 18? Can I see your ID?” Do people really do stop and think to ask such a question? Not in the reality of current culture, unless it is in hindsight by those sitting in the box of judgment. Sex and sexuality is already confusing enough. To stop the banter to ask for ID would kill the mood, wouldn’t it? If my partner said they were in school, or I met them at community college, or maybe on a dating service where you have to be 18, why isn’t that good enough? But we know it’s not. So sometimes we represent the 22-year-old with a 17-year-old sexual partner, the high school students exchanging files over Instagram or iChat, or the adult non-production child porn offender—the most common profile of which is a middle-aged, professional male with no criminal history and who, if he gets appropriate treatment, is very unlikely to reoffend and exceedingly unlikely ever to go hands-on.

Sometimes we represent the truly deviant. Personality disorders. Those who maybe should be warehoused for our safety, the safety of our children, the safety of any of us. Violent offenses. Kidnapping. Production or trading child porn for profit where the most terrible things have been done to children. Often, these clients have been victims as children themselves, victims of all kinds of horribles, scarred by poverty, instability, parental vacuums, and emotional, physical and sexual abuse.

None of these are just clients. They are humans. Humans who, like ourselves, are accused of horrible things, but unlike us (we hope), their worst moments will be played out in a court of law, and if that happens, it may destroy their life as they formerly knew it, no matter the true actuarial risk of reoffense. No one cares what happens to this person in the “system” except us.

In the system, the criminal accused is all alone. Especially those accused of sex offenses. The deck is stacked.

In court all they have is their defender. Us.

During this period of extreme risk and vulnerability, our clients—these legally fragile human beings—expose themselves to us and, but for our best work, maybe in spite of our best work, they will crash and burn, incarcerated and branded with a scarlet SO, adorning their lives like a tattoo for life. It is for us to understand them, be their voice, fight for their freedom, and expose the failures of the government. For me the very best part of it is the honor bestowed up on me, not in court but in private, to actually see someone for who they are, try to figure out how to tell their story, defend their truth, and put their best foot forward, knowing that there is a tenuous balance of maintaining my cool and supporting this fragile, legally vulnerable person who is under vastly more stress than I will ever know.

I hope I’m up for the battle. This book is written for those who are up for it too.

—Steven J. Sherlag

June 2018
