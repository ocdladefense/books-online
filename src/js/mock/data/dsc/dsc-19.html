I. Introduction

In 1989, Oregon initiated its sex offender reporting requirements. The reporting laws have changed over time. The history of the reporting requirements and the amendments to the original 1989 law can be found on the Oregon State Police website.

This chapter cannot cover all the changes that have been made over time. Given the frequent changes to statutes of limitation, it is conceivable that an individual could be charged with an offense where different registration requirements apply.

In State v. MacNab, 334 Or 469, 51 P3d 1249 (2002), the Oregon Supreme Court (DeMuniz, J) held that the sex offender registration requirement did not increase punishment and did not violate ex post facto clause.1 The MacNab case was examined, and its reasoning affirmed, in State v. Vazquez-Escobar, 211 Or App 115, 153 P3d 168 (2007).

Applying that formulation, the court observed that the registration statute’s express purpose was to preclude future sex offenses and that, “[a]s a practical matter,” the statute’s operation “conforms to the legislature’s declared purpose.” Id. at 480, 51 P3d 1249. Accordingly, the court concluded that the registration requirement “does not impose any significant detriment, restraint, or deprivation” on defendants constituting increased “punishment” for purposes of Article I, section 21. Id. at 481, 51 P3d 1249.

State v. Vazquez-Escobar, 211 Or App 115, 119, 153 P3d 168, 170 (2007).

Although our courts may not consider sex offender registration a form of punishment, our clients most certainly do consider it to be punishment, so always be aware of what requirements were in place at the time of the alleged crime and what the court is trying to impose at the time of conviction which, now, could be decades later. Do not just accept MacNab but try and determine if the multiple changes to the statute make the requirement a form of punishment when considering “detriment, restraint, or deprivation intended primarily to deter the offender and others from committing future criminal acts.” However, although not necessarily defined as ex post facto, the timing of the enactment of a particular statute and the actions of the person who may be required to report may provide relief. See State v. Clum, 216 Or App 1 (2007).

II. Who must report?

A person is required to report if the person falls into a statutorily defined class of persons required to report and a statutorily defined triggering event occurs. ORS 163A.010-163A.0252; see Clum, supra. The Clum court held that although the defendant fell within the class of persons required to report under former ORS 181.597(2)(b)3 (i.e., juvenile adjudication in another state that would constitute a sex crime in Oregon), the defendant was not required to report because the relevant triggering event (moving into the state) occurred prior to the enactment of the statute. Because the statute uses the present tense (i.e., “moves” into the state), a person who moved into the state prior to the enactment of the statute is not subject to its reporting requirements. In other words, if the triggering event occurred prior to the relevant reporting statute’s enactment, the person is not required to report.

Practice Tip

In light of Clum, if a person moved into the State of Oregon prior to the enactment of former ORS 181.597(2)(b) who had committed a crime in a “foreign jurisdiction” that if committed in Oregon would constitute a sex crime, that individual should avoid moving out of and then back into the State of Oregon, because moving back into the state would trigger the reporting requirement upon the move back into the State of Oregon.

Similarly, in State v. Driver/Collins, 143 Or App 17 (1996), the court held the defendant was not required to report under former ORS 181.518 (1989)4 because he was released from supervision prior to the enactment of the statute. “[T]he court construed the words ‘[w]hen a person is discharged, paroled or released’ to be the triggering event and, if the defendant had been discharged from supervised release before the statute’s enactment, he or she would not be subject to that trigger.” Clum, 216 Or App at 13.

Practice Tip

Arguably the Driver/Collins analysis may not apply to the current version of ORS 163A.0105 because, unlike former ORS 181.518(1)(a) (1989), paragraph (3)(a) does not include the phrase “[w]hen a person is discharged, paroled or released.” Therefore, it could be read to apply to a person who was discharged, paroled or released prior to the enactment of the statute. However, the inclusion of subparagraph (3)(a)(A), which requires a person to report within 10 days following discharge or release, supports an interpretation consistent with Driver/Collins.

On the other hand, both the Oregon Supreme Court and United States Supreme Court have held that applying sex offender registration statutes to persons who committed their crimes prior to the enactment of the statutes does not violate the state or federal ex post facto clauses. MacNab, supra; see also Smith v. Doe, 538 US 84 (2003).

III. What happens if a person fails to report?

A. State Penalties

A person who knowingly fails to report commits either a Class A misdemeanor or a Class C felony, depending on the severity of the underlying crime and specific reporting requirement violated.6 A person who fails to sign and return an address verification form as required by ORS 163A.035(4)7 commits a violation.8

As discussed with regard to Clum, it is important to pay particular attention to the express language of the applicable reporting statute to ensure that the person is actually subject to the reporting obligation. In State v. Hiner, 269 Or App 447 (2015), the court held that a defendant who changes his residence is not required to report until the defendant also obtains a new residence. See also State v. Williams, 278 Or App 620 (2016). In Hiner, a homeless defendant left a shelter and failed to report the departure. The court held the express language of the statute provided that the reporting obligation was not triggered upon the defendant’s departure, but would be triggered once the defendant obtained a new residence.

However, if the person is subject to the reporting obligation, there are several affirmative defenses available.9 The affirmative defenses apply to circumstances in which the defendant reported but to the wrong agency. It is the defendant’s burden to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the affirmative defense applies.10

B. Federal Penalties

A person who is required to report due to conviction for a federal sex crime and who knowingly fails to report commits a Class C felony.11 A state-convicted sex offender may
also be prosecuted in federal court for failure to report if the person engaged in interstate travel, foreign travel, or enters, leaves, or resides on an Indian reservation.12

IV. What happens if a person moves to another state?

The state police will notify law enforcement in the other state of the person’s move, and is no longer responsible for registering and tracking the person.13

V. How is a person classified?14

All sex offenders living in the state are classified into one of three levels.

Level 1 offenders present the lowest risk of reoffending and require a limited range of notification.15

Level 2 offenders present a moderate risk of reoffending and require a moderate range of notification.16

Level 3 offenders present a high risk or reoffending and require a wide range of notification.17 For general information see the BOPPPS website.

Adult male offenders, except Category B offenders, are classified by using the Static-99R. All other offenders, females, males under 18 and Category B offenders are classified using the Level of Services/Case Management Inventory (LS/CMI).18

Category B offenders are those offenders required to report as a result of the following:

1) consenting sex with other adults in public places

2) crimes relating to child pornography

3) indecent behavior without a sexual motive e.g., urinating in public)

4) prostitution-related offenses

5) polygamy, and

6) revenge or anger motivated behaviors with a sexual aspect (e.g., distributing obscene images without consent, such as “revenge porn,” “sexting,” and statutory rape offenses.

See OAR 255-085-0020; OAR 255-085-0010(2), (3); Static-99R Coding Rules, pgs. 21–24.

The Static-99R is the tool used by the State of Oregon Board of Post Prison Supervision and Parole to assess and classify individuals convicted of sex offenses and required to register as a sex offender. The Static-99 is a ten-item actuarial assessment that purports to be created and “normed” for adult male sex offenders who are at least 18 at the time of release into the community See www.static99.org. Several documents used by the Board of Post Prison Supervision and Parole are:

  • The Static-99R “Tally Sheet.”

  • The 2016 coding rules manual.

  • The age chart.

  • A person can also challenge the LS/CMI scoring.

If an offender has a Static-99R score of -3 to 3, that person is determined to be a Level 1, which indicates the lowest risk of reoffending and the state is limited in its range of notification to the public. If the offender has a Static-99R score of 4–5, that person is determined to be a Level 2, which indicates a moderate risk of reoffending and greater notification to the public requirements apply. If an offender has a Static-99R score of 6 or above, the offender is determined to be a Level 3, is considered the highest risk to reoffend, and the state has a wide range for notification requirements they must implement including publication on the Oregon State Police website. See OAR 255-085-020.

An offender is automatically determined to be a Level 3 if:

1) the offender was previously designated as a predatory sex offender between February 1, 2005, and December 31, 2013

2) is designated as a sexually violent dangerous offender or

3) refuses or fails to participate in the assessment process.

VI. When is a person classified?

The timing for the risk assessment depends on when and where (i.e., in or out of state) the person was convicted and whether the person is sentenced to a prison sentence.19 All existing registrants must be classified (reclassified) by December 1, 2018, and all new registrants must be classified within 60 days of release or initial obligation to report.

VI. Can a person challenge an initial classification?

A person may challenge the initial classification by filing written objections with the Board.20 The current review process is set forth in OAR 255-085-0040 and 255-085-0050. These OARs are continually updated and should be reviewed prior to petitioning for a review of a classification.

Documents for filing your written objections to the classification are located on the BOPPPS website at http://www.oregon.gov/BOPPPS/docs/Exhibits/SO1-SO3.pdf.

VII. Can a person petition for reclassification?

Yes, an offender may petition for reclassification, the process for which is provided in ORS 163A.125. Beginning in 2019 certain offenders will be able to petition for reclassification to a lower classification level.

For example, an offender with a Level 3 classification can apply to be reclassified to a Level 2 but can never seek to be reclassified to a Level 1 and cannot be relieved of the requirement to register.

Offenders previously designated as a Level 2 can be reclassified to a Level 1 and, if successful as a Level 1, after five years can petition for relief from registration. Any petition for reclassification cannot be filed until at least ten years after discharge from supervision for any registerable offense (or five years from reclassification from Level 2 to Level 1). Petitions for reclassification will be denied if the petitioner is convicted of a new person crime after the conviction for the registerable offense.

The BOPPPS must hold a hearing on a petition for reclassification and shall reclassify the petitioner under the rules if the new risk assessment shows a lower risk level and the BOPPPS determines that the lower level risk assessment is sufficient to protect public safety.

The factors that the BOPPPS will consider include the nature and degree of violence in the offense, the age and number of victims, the age of petitioner at the time of the offense, the length of time spent crime-free in the community since the time of the offense, the offender’s performance on supervision, successful completion of treatment, offender’s stability (employment, housing, etc.), any community or personal support system developed by the offender, criminal and non-criminal behavior and (the catchall) any other relevant factors.

VIII. Can a person obtain relief from reporting?

The timing and process to obtain relief from reporting varies depending on whether the reporting requirement is due to an adult conviction or juvenile adjudication and the type of conviction or adjudication.

A. Relief from Reporting for “Statutory” Sex Crime Convictions/Adjudications

A person is relieved of the reporting obligation if

1) the conviction or adjudication was for rape III, sodomy III, sexual abuse III, contributing to the sexual delinquency of a minor, sexual misconduct, or an attempt to commit one of those offenses

2) the person is less than five years older than the victim

3) the lack of consent was due solely to incapacity to consent by reason of being less than a specified age

4) the victim was at least 14 years of age at the time of the offense

5) the person has not been convicted or adjudicated for any unrelated sex crimes, and

6) there was only one victim.21

ORS 163A.150 provides the process for obtaining relief from reporting under these circumstances.22

Practice Tip

If a defendant is charged with some other sexual offense (e.g., sexual abuse in the second degree), counsel should consider a plea to one of the above listed offenses if the facts support the elements for relief.

B. Court Relief from Reporting

Until December 31, 2022, a person with only one misdemeanor or Class C felony sex crime conviction may petition the court for relief from reporting no sooner than ten years after termination of supervision, the process and factors to be considered by the court are provided in ORS 163A.120.

C. Board Relief from Reporting

Beginning January 1, 2023, a person classified as a Level 1 offender may petition the board for relief from reporting no sooner than five years after the date supervision terminated.23 A person who was reclassified from a Level 2 to a Level 1may petition the board for relief from reporting no sooner than five years after the date of reclassification.24 The process and factors to be considered by the board are provided in ORS 163A.125.

D. Relief from Reporting After Juvenile Adjudication

ORS 163A.130 and 163A.135 (foreign adjudication) provide the process for obtaining relief from reporting due to a juvenile adjudication. A person adjudicated for a Class A or B felony must wait two years from the date jurisdiction terminated before petitioning the court for relief.25 For a Class C felony, a person cannot file the petition sooner than 30 days before jurisdiction terminates.

X. What sex offender information is released to the public?

ORS 163A.215 relates to the release of sex offender information to the public. Only a Level 3 offender’s information may be published on the state police website.26

XI. Failure to Register

Offender is required to notify the authorities within ten days of moving, including moving out of state. State v. Wigglesworth, 186 Or App 374, 63 P3d 1185 (2008); State v. Cox, 219 Or App 319, 182 P3d 259 (2008) (change of residence broadly construed to include any change, including “couch surfing”).

State may have had to previously prove “venue” under State v. Depeche, 242 Or App 155, 164, 255 P3d 502 (2011), under a previous version of former ORS 181.599 but that is no longer the case as the legislature amended the statute. Or. Laws 2011, ch 675, and under State v. Mills, 354 Or 350, 312 P3d 515 (2013), the state is no longer required to prove venue as it is not a material allegation unless the accused raises the issue before trial. See State v. Streeter, 270 Or App 441, 348 P3d 290 (2015).

Requirement to report within ten days of “change of residence” not unconstitutionally vague. State v. Reigard, 243 Or App 442, 259 P3d 966 (2011).

Failure to register in Oregon was a felony where underlying sexual offense for which the accused had been adjudicated as a juvenile in Washington would have been a felony in Oregon had it been committed by an adult. State v. Hinkle, 287 Or App 786, 404 P3d 986 (2017) (very long discussion of the history of the reporting requirements in Oregon under the various statutes that have been renumbered several times); see State v. Rogers, 288 Or App 769, 407 P3d 907 (2017) (relying on Hinkle, the court held that juvenile adjudications are considered “crimes” for the purposes of the reporting requirement).

XII. Conclusion

The sex offender registration statutory scheme has a tortured history with multiple amendments throughout the years. Although the full scheme can now be found in Oregon Revised Chapter 163A, the statutes regarding SOR were codified in ORS Chapter 181 and cases may begin to more frequently refer to former statutes in addition to the statutes now codified in Chapter 163A.

The statutes are complicated to read and, at best, confusing. Therefore, if dealing with older cases that have now bubbled up based upon late disclosures or newly discovered evidence, it is very important to go back and trace the history of these statutes and the timelines that would be applied and ask whether or not there are exceptions or exemptions based upon your client’s unique set of circumstances. In addition, in all new cases alleging sexual misconduct, it is advisable to have your client complete an “independent” Static-99R test prior to any final resolution.
