Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys

2012 edition

What Is Scientific Evidence?

I. Why It Matters

As discussed in Chapters 3 and 4, there is a special admissibility analysis for “scientific evidence” under Oregon law. The proponent of such evidence needs to lay a fairly substantial foundation in order for the evidence to be admitted. Indeed, this admissibility test may be the most stringent for any type of evidence in Oregon.

Importantly, “scientific evidence” is not synonymous with “expert testimony.” Unlike federal law, which applies the Dauberttest to all expert testimony,1

Oregon law distinguishes between “scientific evidence” and other expert testimony for purposes of the admissibility analysis.2

Although the admissibility analysis for nonscientific expert testimony is not yet clearly established in Oregon,3it is almost certainly less demanding than the test for scientific evidence.

Accordingly, the proponent of a particular type of evidence will generally want to avoid classification as “scientific,” while the opponent will want that classification. If the state is the proponent, you have little to lose by arguing that the evidence is “scientific.” If you win that point, the state will have to lay the demanding Brown/O’Keyfoundation4in order to support admissibility. If the trial court concludes that the evidence is not “scientific” and admits it without a Brown/O’Keyanalysis, and an appellate court ultimately concludes the evidence was in fact “scientific,” that is grounds for reversal.5

This is true even if the evidence presented at trial may have been sufficient for a Brown/O’Keyfoundation.6

Before going further, it is worth noting that the significance of the “scientific evidence” definition is primarily in the admissibility context. In other contexts, that definition is much less important. For example, Chapter 1 addresses the importance of gaining a mastery of the relevant field, and Chapter 6 offers strategies for minimizing the impact of an opposing expert’s testimony. In those contexts, it is largely irrelevant whether a court ultimately decides that the evidence is “scientific.”

II. Definition of “Scientific Evidence”

The Oregon Supreme Court has described scientific evidence as “evidence that draws its convincing force from some principle of science, mathematics and the like.” 7

Unfortunately, this definition is not an easy one to apply in practice. As then-Judge Landau noted in a 1998 dissent: “[I]t is a bald tautology—scientific evidence is what draws its convincing force from science—and is essentially meaningless. I can only imagine the difficulty the bench and practicing bar will have in applying the holding of [the] lead opinion in future cases.”8

The best way to understand the “scientific evidence” definition is through a review of its application in particular cases. O’Key itself provides a useful starting point. The type of evidence at issue inO’Key is related to the horizontal gaze nystagmus (“HGN”) test, in which officers monitor a suspect’s eye movements in an attempt to detect alcohol impairment. Applying theBrown ''definition, the court concluded that HGN evidence is “scientific,” explaining:

The HGN test provides evidence that purports to draw its convincing force from a principle of science, namely, the asserted scientific proposition that there is a causal relationship between consumption of alcohol and the type of nystagmus measured by the HGN test. The value of HGN testing depends critically on the demonstrated scientific validity of that proposition. Moreover, the proposition that alcohol consumption causes nystagmus possesses significantly increased potential to influence the trier of fact as a “scientific” assertion.9

Note that the court expressly considered not only whether the evidence was in factgrounded in scientific principles, but also whether it would appearto be so grounded and thus carry extra weight with factfinders.10

This latter principle—that jurors may give particular deference to evidence perceivedto be scientific, and that as a result the admissibility analysis for such evidence must be relatively strict—is frequently noted in Oregon case law.11

The O’Keycourt’s distinction between HGN and other field sobriety test evidence which it did notconsider “scientific” is also helpful:

[HGN] is different from other field sobriety tests because it rests on a manifestation of alcohol consumption not easily recognized or understood by most people. The relationship between the effects of alcohol on the central nervous system, the nystagmus phenomenon, and the HGN test is not within the realm of common knowledge of the average person. Other field sobriety tests, such as the walk-and-turn test, the one-leg-stand test, and the modified finger-to-nose test, obtain their legitimacy from effects of intoxication based on propositions of common knowledge.12

In other words, a legally significant distinction between the HGN test and other field sobriety tests is that HGN evidence purports to rest on scientific principles that are not “common knowledge.” This is a critical point when litigating the question of whether a particular type of evidence is “scientific.” Consider whether the progression from observed facts (e.g., the eye movements observed during the HGN test) to the proffered conclusion (alcohol impairment) is one that could be made based on common knowledge. If that progression requires explanatory testimony by someone with specialized knowledge, then the evidence may well qualify as “scientific.”13

In State v. Stafford,14 the Oregon Court of Appeals further explained the difference between “scientific” and other evidence under the Oregon standard. The court noted, “[i]n drawing a distinction between ‘scientific evidence’ and ‘technical or other specialized knowledge,’ the determining factor is the source of the evidence’s reliability or accuracy.”15

For example, a medical doctor’s testimony regarding a diagnosis of a fracture “by palpation and observation or by resorting to an x-ray” would not be “scientific evidence.”16

By contrast, “evidence of a ‘syndrome’ could be ‘scientific evidence’ under some circumstances,” because “[t]he relation- ship between the aggregate of signs and symptoms may be what gives reliability to the conclusion that a syndrome exists, and that relationship may be demonstrable only by a comparative analysis of test data.”17

Note that cases decided since Staffordmay call into question the Court of Appeals’ statement in that case that the hypothetical medical doctor’s testimony would not constitute scientific evidence.18

Oregon courts have also considered whether the witness refers to studies, technical literature, or other traditional hallmarks of “scientific” knowledge, or testifies regarding her background in a scientific field.19

The “scientific evidence” definition encompasses more than the “hard” sciences; the Oregon Supreme Court “has made it clear that expert testimony concerning matters within the sphere of the behavioral sciences possesses the increased potential to influence the trier of fact as scientific assertions, just as expert testimony dealing with the ‘hard’ sciences does.”20

In addition to the cases discussed above, the following cases help illustrate the line Oregon courts have drawn between “scientific” and other evidence:

• Jennings v. Baxter Healthcare Corp.21Doctor’s testimony about medical causation, based on examinations of various patients, constituted scientific evidence. “A jury is likely to believe that a doctor’s testimony about medicine is a scientific assertion and, therefore, the proponent of the testimony must show that it is scientifically valid.”22

•State v. Sampson23

Drug recognition expert (“DRE”) testimony was scientific evidence. Among other things, the court noted that the testimony “draws its authority from scientific principles,” that each step “produces a test result that compares with results established through scientific research that purport to show the subject to be more or less likely under the influence of a controlled substance,” that “the DRE officer’s final analysis is heavily informed by data derived from the scientific portions of the protocol,” and that it was the type of evidence that could “exert a significantly greater influence on the fact finder than nonscientific evidence.”24

•State v. Branch25Lidar evidence, a type of laser-based distance measurement, is scientific evidence. It is “based on the premise that measurements of distance can be derived through the lidar device’s use of a certain scientific principle, viz., the speed of light,” with “[d]ata generated by the computer in the device .

. . then analyzed through the use of a mathematical algorithm based on that principle”; accordingly, “that distance evidence draws its convincing force from a scientific principle and would be more persuasive to the trier of fact due to its scientific nature.”26

•State v. Owens27

...Evidence regarding the manner in which blood was drawn for subsequent blood-alcohol content analysis was not scientific evidence. It was “not the type of evidence that can be presented and explained only by an expert,” and “[l]ay factfinders can reasonably be expected to understand and weigh the credibility and strength of evidence about who performed a blood draw by syringe and how the person did so.”28

III. Non-scientific Expert Testimony

If the evidence is not determined to be “scientific,” that is not the end of the inquiry. If the witness is testifying based on nonscientific specialized knowledge, the proponent should still have to lay an OEC 702 (“Testimony by experts”) foundation, even if not the specific Brown/O’Key foundation.

As discussed above, unlike federal law, Oregon law distinguishes between “scientific” evidence and other ex- pert testimony for purposes of the applicable admissibility analysis. The test for nonscientific expert testimony is not as well developed as the test for scientific evidence. At the very least, the opponent should insist on a foundation incorporating the literal text of OEC 702: the purported “technical or other specialized knowledge” at issue must be something that “will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue, and the witness must be “qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training or education.” Accordingly, the proponent should have to demonstrate not only that this witness has legitimate “specialized knowledge” but also that her testimony based on this knowledge will enlighten jurors beyond what they would be able to figure out based on factual testimony alone.

Finally, if the court concludes that the evidence is not even based on “specialized knowledge,” if that evidence is anything other than pure factual testimony the proponent should still have to satisfy OEC 701. That rule provides that lay opinion testimony must be “[r]ationally based on the perception of the witness” and “[h]elpful to a clear understanding of testimony of the witness or the determination of a fact in issue.”29

Look closely at the purported “lay opinion” testimony to see how the witness moves from “perception” to “opinion.” Does this process include any inferential step that the average person would not understand based on common knowledge? If so, the opinion testimony should be subject to the heightened OEC 702 standard. Conversely, if the witness’s testimony is notbased on any specialized knowledge, you may have an argument that it does not add anything legitimate to what the jurors could figure out on their own, and thus should be excluded on that basis.30
