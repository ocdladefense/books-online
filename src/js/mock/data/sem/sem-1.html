Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys

2012 edition

Learning the Science

“A lawyer who comes into court without knowing the full extent of the science surrounding his case is a well-coiffed charlatan.”1

{Note: Gerry Spence, Win Your Case (2005).

Your first task in any case involving scientific evidence is to master the relevant principles underlying that evidence. This chapter addresses such questions as:

• Is it really necessary to learn the science underlying the evidence?

• Canyou realistically hope to do so—especially if you do not have a scientific background?

• How do you go about doing this?

I. Do you need to learn the science?

In a word, yes. To represent your client effectively, you need to be the authoritative, credible author of the story you will be telling in the courtroom. This requires a mastery of all facts relevant to your case. If a piece of scientific evidence is part of that factual universe, you need to understand it.

This is true whether the scientific evidence is offered by the prosecution or the defense. In confronting the state’s evidence, you have a vital role in policing that evidence and preventing it from carrying any more weight than it deserves. You need to understand the evidence well enough to perform that policing function—to know, for example, when the expert is testifying to something beyond what the state of knowledge in the field supports. Otherwise, you will be at the mercy of the state’s expert, especially if she perceives, and is emboldened by, the absence of any meaningful check on her testimony.

Understanding the science is just as important when you want to offer your own expert. Again, you are the author of your story, and you will be most effective if you craft each chapter yourself to fit smoothly into the finished work. This does not mean, of course, that you shape your expert’s actual opinions. As with any other witness, you only want your expert to testify to what he truly believes. But only you ultimately know the particular aspects of the expert’s knowledge that need to be woven into your story and how that can be done most persuasively. If you end up essentially delegating that task to your expert—if, for example, you carefully compose all other parts of your story but rely primarily on your expert to fill in “his part”—your case will suffer.

Of course, you may not end up extensively cross-examining the state’s expert on intricate scientific details, or calling your own expert. As discussed in later chapters, both of these tactics carry risks. But you cannot make informed decisions regarding such tactics without understanding the type of evidence at issue. What- ever decisions you end up making, you owe it to your client to make them intelligently—and that includes learning the science.

II. Can you learn the science?

Again, the answer is almost certainly yes. It is highly likely that you, as a competent, intelligent attorney, can master a scientific topic to the degree necessary for a particular case. This may be the hardest thing for many attorneys—especially those without any formal scientific background or education—to believe. But it is true. The key is to figure out what it is about the purported experts in the relevant field that enables them to claim to have specialized knowledge, and then attempt to recreate that for yourself.

Some expert testimony is based on knowledge that has been accumulated in a particular field, usually by a number of researchers. For example, an expert might testify that the probability of two unrelated people having the same DNA profile is one in some large number, or that horizontal gaze nystagmus (“HGN”) is associated with alcohol impairment.2 {Note: Such testimony may accompany testimony about case-specific activities conducted by the expert or by someone under her supervision. For example, an expert may testify regarding how DNA testing was performed and what the computer-generated results were, or about the fact that an allegedly impaired driver’s eyes moved jerkily instead of smoothly. This is essentially fact testimony. The key contribution of the expert is in explaining the significance of such observations—for example, that the DNA at issue probably belongs to the defendant, or that the driver was impaired. This latter type of testimony is the focus of this discussion.}

Such an expert’s role is often simply to transmit to the jury some portion of objective knowledge from what is usually publicly available literature.3 {Note: Under federal law, such evidence is directly admissible under certain circumstances. SeeFed R Evid 803(18) (allowing a “statement contained in a treatise, periodical, or pamphlet” to be “read into evidence” where certain foundational requirements are met). Oregon has specifically rejected this rule. SeeOEC 803(18a) cmt. Oregon law does, however, allow the use of such materials for impeachment if certain foundational requirements are met. SeeOEC 706; see also infraChapter 6, Part III.}

This means that you can develop a level of comfort with the relevant topic by familiarizing yourself with that literature (perhaps with the aid of your own consulting expert, as discussed below).

That may seem unrealistic. How can you possibly bridge the gap between your knowledge and an expert’s just by reading a few articles? If this is your response, part of your lack of confidence may come from over- estimating the expert’s qualifications and your particular task. Even if the expert is highly educated and credentialed—not, by the way, a given—you should not assume that she spent all those years of training and experience building up knowledge specific to the testimony she will be giving in your case. On the contrary, you will often find that the particular scientific evidence issues in your case center on a relatively small number of identifiable principles that can be grasped with a manageable degree of time and effort.

So take heart, and make an effort to learn the science underlying the evidence at issue in your case. Remember, you do not need to know it as well as a true expert in the field does. You only need to understand it well enough to track what the opposing expert is saying, to know when she is straying beyond the boundaries of what the field allows, and to be able to work the evidence into your own case if you choose. You can do that.

Of course, not all expert testimony is based on published research or other widely acknowledged, publicly available sources. Some testimony is based more on personally derived observations and experiences. For example, a veteran detective may testify that in her experience child abuse victims often delay reporting. It may be more difficult to prepare for this type of expert as her purportedly specialized knowledge is largely in her own mind and thus inaccessible to you. Still, you are not without recourse. Start by educating yourself in the relevant field. Do published authors in the field believe that their discipline supports the types of assessments, predictions or inferences that the expert claims to be able to offer? If not, you may be able to set up an attack on the validity of the witness’s purported expertise. When someone in a scientific field claims to have discovered something, or to be able to draw conclusions from certain observations, the normal process is to submit these findings for publication so that the broader field can evaluate those findings and, if they are valid, ultimately benefit from them. Particularly for predictive or analytical tools that would seem to have high societal value—for example, in assessing the likelihood that child abuse has taken place—it is fair to assume that if this were legitimate, there would be literature on the topic. If the expert claims to be able to draw conclusions through a process that is not found in any technical literature, one may question how valid this supposed expertise really is.

Many experts will mix elements of both of the types of expertise discussed above. The DNA expert may include remarks based on her own experience, or the detective may attempt to refer to published results. Your mastery of the relevant field is particularly important in such cases. If a witness is allowed to move seamlessly between the common knowledge of her field and her own personal impressions, the former’s aura of objectivity and general applicability may improperly bolster the latter. If you know what the common body of knowledge in the field supports, you will know (and can point out to the judge or jury) when the witness is merely passing along knowledge widely accepted in the field, and when she is offering her own—more subjective and often less reliable—thoughts.

III. How to Learn the Science

The following sections discuss how to go about learning what you need to learn (again, with a focus on preparing to meet an adverse expert). One common theme is that this process takes time, not only to do the actual research, but to gather all of the materials and (perhaps) people you need to get started. So start early.

A. Gathering Documents

Much of what you will learn will be from documents, which you can obtain through the various processes discussed later in this chapter. The types of documents you will be interested in include:

Standard discovery materials. These will often include discussion of the science at issue and will help you understand background issues, certain procedural steps (for example, how an item of physical evidence was collected, handled, and stored), and what significance the state attaches to the scientific evidence in your case.

Lab reports. The results of scientific analyses are often expressed in final reports, which may include statements regarding the alleged significance of these results. These will be a useful starting point, although as discussed below you should not rely solely on them.

Bench notes and other notes or writings from anyone involved at any stage of the process. These can be critical. Even if the method at issue is generally valid, there may have been irregularities in the way it was applied in your case. Do not rely solely on the final lab report. Get these underlying materials, which will help you to spot such irregularities and also to understand the process as you recreate it in your mind.

Procedural materials. These go by a variety of names—for example, manuals, protocols, or standard operating procedures (“SOPs”). Whatever they are called, you want them. These will establish the standard for how the method should have been applied (at least according to the agency or entity at issue). They may also include explanations of why particular procedures are followed and of the skewed results or other con- sequences that can flow if they are not.

Cited materials. Procedural manuals and related materials often cite underlying research materials or other documents. These too can be very helpful. They provide additional education regarding the underlying scientific principles and may also discuss the limitations of the method or the associated field.

“Standard” methods. If the method at issue is an established one, there may be a standard method or protocol promulgated by a government agency or other entity. The method may have been derived from such a standard method. If so, the standard method may be cited in some of the procedural materials or other documents discussed above. In any event, you should compare the standard method to the specific protocol used in your case.

Other materials. There may be other materials that will be relevant to your case. This may include technical articles or textbooks relating to the method or its underlying principles. In addition, if there are letters, reports, emails, or other communications relating either to the development or evaluation of the method in general or to the specific analysis performed in your case, you may want to get these. If qualifications or error rates specific to a particular analyst or laboratory may be relevant to your case, consider getting documents relating to these issues as well.

How do you go about getting all of these materials? There are several procedural options.

Statutory and constitutional discovery. Some of these materials may be covered by the prosecutor’s statutory discovery obligations. For example, some would constitute witness statements,4... {Note: SeeORS 135.815(a).}

...or “reports or statements of experts.”5 {Note: ORS 135.815(c).}

Others may constitute Brady/Giglio material—i.e., defense-favorable material that the state must turn over pursuant to its constitutional obligations.6 {Note: ''See Brady v. Maryland, 373 US 83 (1963); Giglio v. United States, 405 US 150 (1972).

This would include materials evidencing, for example, a degree of uncertainty in the results or analyses at issue, any deviation from established protocols, or any possible alternative explanation for any observations or results. Any manuals or other procedural materials may also constitute Brady/Gigliomaterials on the basis that cross-examining against a governing manual is a well-established trial tactic.

The state is required to produce all of these materials regardless of whether you request them. Still, it is often worthwhile to make specific requests. The prosecutor’s response will let you know what, if any, portions of your requests you may need to litigate. Also, if the prosecutor is later determined to have withheld discovery materials, a prior written request will make it more difficult for the state to claim ignorance or good faith inadvertence.

Find out early on whether the prosecutor agrees to give you everything you are seeking. If not, consider litigating this issue at an early stage. Remember, it takes time to litigate discovery issues, to gather the materials once the court has ruled, and then to review and digest those materials. Think ahead and start soon.

To the extent some of these materials may arguably be outside the state’s discovery obligations, consider asking the prosecutor to treat them as being within those obligations anyway. If you make it clear that you will be getting these materials from the state and its witnesses (or associated government agencies) one way or another—for example, through the alternative routes discussed below—the prosecutor may prefer to treat this as a discovery matter and thus retain control over the process. The benefit to you is the efficiency of dealing with one source instead of several.

Public records requests. Oregon’s public records law, at ORS 192.410 to .510, is a formidable and underutilized tool for gathering documents. If the documents are in the control of any “public body”—a term defined broadly by statute, at ORS 192.410(3)—you have a right to their prompt production unless some exception applies.7 {Note: SeeORS 192.420.}

Case-specific materials may be withheld under the exemption in ORS 192.501(3) (“[i]nvestigatory information compiled for criminal law purposes”). More generally applicable materials, however—for example, procedural manuals, or documents relating to the development or evaluation of a method—should be available through this route.

Find out whether the public body at issue has a standard form for records requests; if not, you can simply write a letter citing the public records law. If the public body does not respond promptly, you can invoke the appeal process, which may involve a separate court action.8 {Note: SeeORS 192.450-.490.}

Again, get an early start so that if you need to demonstrate diligence, you can do so.

Subpoenas. You can also serve a subpoena on any person, agency, or entity that you believe has relevant records. Because you will generally need these materials well in advance of the trial or pretrial hearing in order to make effective use of them, you may want to use the early production procedure set forth in ORS

136.580(2)and discussed in State v. Cartwright.9 {Note: 336 Or 408 (2004).}

Other options. Finally, some relevant materials will be publicly available at university libraries or online bookstores, for example. If you retain a consulting expert, that expert may also have useful reference materials. If you find these materials on your own, you will not only avoid tipping your hand to the prosecutor, but also potentially end up with information that the state and its expert do not have.

In many cases you will want to pursue some or all of these strategies in combination, instead of relying on just one. Once you have determined what you want and put your request in writing, it is usually not much of an additional burden to repeat it in other forms. In addition, some procedural hurdles applicable to one route may not apply to another. Finally, if you seek relief for a discovery violation based on the prosecutor’s failure to turn over required materials, you may be denied such relief if the court finds that you could have obtained the materials through some other means.10 {Note: ''See State v. Spada, 286 Or 305 (1979).}

The downside to the comprehensive approach. The previous sections proceeded on the assumption that you want to seek all of the potentially relevant materials you can get your hands on. Often, that will be the case. The more mastery you have of the topic, the better, and reviewing these materials will often be the best way to develop that mastery.

It is worth noting, however, that there are some risks associated with the comprehensive approach. One that may not be immediately apparent relates to the admissibility determination for any scientific evidence that the state wants to introduce. As discussed in Chapters 3 and 4, the proponent of scientific evidence—at least evidence of a type not previously approved by an Oregon appellate court—needs to lay a substantial foundation in order for such evidence to be admitted. Not all prosecutors will anticipate the need to lay such a foundation and, absent advance warning, the prosecutor may show up without materials that could be helpful or even necessary for that task. You should consider the possibility that by making advance demands for the types of materials discussed above, you will inadvertently prompt the prosecutor to gather the necessary materials and otherwise prepare for the admissibility hearing.

B. Retaining a Consulting Expert

Whether or not you ultimately decide to offer the testimony of a defense expert witness, you may want to engage an expert as a consultant to help you understand the relevant principles, identify any significant issues, and prepare for trial or a pretrial hearing. Along with personally educating you, a consulting expert can also point you to the most relevant and useful reference materials.

If you decide to retain a consulting expert, there are certain considerations you will want to keep in mind.

1. Attorney-client privilege, work product, andState v. Riddle.

When you retain an expert in a non-testifying (i.e., consulting) capacity, that expert is a “representative of the lawyer” for purposes of Oregon’s attorney-client privilege rule.11 {Note: SeeOEC 503(1)-(2), 503(1)(e)cmt.; State v. Riddle, 330 Or 471, 476-77 (2000).}

Accordingly, your communications with that expert are privileged as long as the standard elements of the privilege (confidentiality, made for the purpose of facilitating legal services, etc.) are present.12 {Note: In the criminal context, attorney-client privilege claims, including those involving communications with consulting experts, implicate constitutional concerns along with the generally applicable statutory and common-law issues. See, e.g.,Smithv. McCormick, 914 F2d 1153, 1159-60 (9th Cir 1990). Be sure to note and preserve this point if you find yourself litigating privilege issues in this context.}

A version of the work product doctrine may also apply to your consulting expert’s work, but this is a relatively weak protection in Oregon. The statutory basis for work product protection in a criminal case is ORS 135.855(1)(a), which exempts from pretrial discovery obligations “[w]ork product, legal research, records, correspondence, reports or memoranda to the extent that they contain the opinions, theories or conclusions of the attorneys, peace officers or their agents in connection with the investigation, prosecution or defense of a criminal action.” The Oregon Supreme Court has held that this is only a limitation on discovery obligations, and does not provide an independent basis for exclusion if, for example, a consulting expert is subpoenaed to testify.13 {Note: Riddle, 330 Or at 483-84.}

Nor was the court hospitable to the invocation of common-law work product principles to exclude such evidence.14 {Note: Id.at 484-86.}

In short, you should not rely on the work product protection as a significant independent barrier to the ultimate disclosure of your communications with your consulting expert or that expert’s own work or opinions.

This determination led to the primary Riddle holding, which is that under certain circumstances a consulting expert retained by the defense can later be forced to testify on the state’s behalf regarding the expert’s opinions or conclusions. This is a serious matter and could undermine many of the traditional benefits of hiring a consulting expert—particularly, the ability to communicate frankly and seek an objective, unvarnished opinion. Fortunately, the court’s holding was a limited one, expressly relying on the unusual fact that the expert’s opinion was completely independent of and segregable from any confidences learned in the course of the consulting relationship. The court explained:

[U]nder OEC 503(2)(a), the lawyer/expert relationship does not automatically disqualify an expert who was retained by one party from testifying for some other party. That expert is disqualified from testifying, however, if his or her opinion discloses, either directly or indirectly, or is based on, any confidential communication between the lawyer, the client, and/or the expert. If an expert’s opinion is so bound up with any such communication that the expert cannot, in the view of the trial court, segregate his or her opinion from some part of the confidential communication, then the expert should not be permitted to testify.15 {Note: Id. at 487. The consulting expert in Riddle was an accident reconstructionist whose trial testimony was found to have been based on his own observations, measurements, and conclusions reached independently of any confidences learned from the defense attorney. Id. at 473-75.}

Accordingly, if you manage your relationship with your consulting expert in a manner that will prevent a Riddle-type factual showing from being made, you should be able to avoid Riddle’s result.16 {Note: 16 Even the end result in Riddle itself was not as damaging as it could have been. The trial court had ruled that the expert “could not mention any prior connection with the defense, . . . could not refer to any statements made by defendant or to information that came from defendant, and . . . could not refer to any statements that he had made to [defense counsel].” Id. at 475. In other words, the court shielded the defense from the special harm that could have been caused by the jury hearing that “the defendant’s own expert” supported the state’s theory.}

2. Thinking ahead to trial

You may end up deciding to offer defense expert testimony at trial. The role of the testifying expert is quite different from that of the consulting expert. Aside from the difference in skill sets and at- tributes—for example, you do not need to worry about how a consulting expert will come across to a jury—the confidentiality of your communications depends on the expert’s role. In particular, you should assume that all of your communications with a testifying expert will be treated as nonprivileged and subject to disclosure if, for example, the state serves a subpoena duces tecumon your expert.17 {Note: See OEC 503(1)(e) cmt. (“The definition [of ‘representative of the lawyer’ [for purposes of the attorney-client privilege] includes an expert who is hired to assist in rendering legal advice or to help in the planning and conduct of litigation, but not one employed to testify as a witness.”).

In an ideal situation, you might retain two experts—one consulting expert and one expert to testify at trial. Where resources allow, this is worth considering. It allows you to communicate freely with your consulting expert, thus maximizing the educational and preparatory benefits of your consultation, with- out generating material that can be used to impeach your testifying expert.

Realistically, however, resource constraints will often prevent the hiring of multiple experts within a single field of expertise, and a single expert will often end up serving in both roles. There is nothing wrong with this, but it means you need to plan ahead. If there is a possibility that you will end up calling the expert as a witness, then from the outset you need to conduct all communications with the assumption that they will be disclosed to the state. You should explain this in your first conversation with the expert, especially if he is unfamiliar with the legal system. The same is true for any notes or other writings your expert creates while working on your case. These can be subpoenaed by the state, and although you may have arguments that some or all of them should be withheld, you should proceed on the assumption that your expert will have to produce them. Again, the expert should know that, and understand the implications, before beginning work on the case.18 {Note: 18 Of course, you have the same right of access to any such materials relating to the state’s expert and should consider obtaining them through a discovery request or subpoena duces tecum.}
