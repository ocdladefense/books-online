Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys

Scientific evidence can be a critical and challenging element of a criminal case. Attorneys and judges universally acknowledge the profound effect such evidence can have on factfinders1—an effect that is not always commensurate with the evidence’s real value.2 Accordingly, a criminal defense attorney must be able to work effectively with scientific evidence, both offensively and defensively.

This manual is intended to help Oregon defense attorneys become as comfortable as possible with scientific evidence, and to assist them in addressing some of the unique factual and legal issues that can be helpful in using or attacking it.

  • Chapter 1 discusses how to learn as much as possible about the scientific evidence in your case—both the general underlying principles and their specific application to your situation.
  • Chapter 2 addresses the threshold question of what types of evidence are deemed “scientific” and therefore subject to the specific admissibility analysis applicable to such evidence.
  • Chapter 3 outlines the procedural issues associated with the admissibility analysis.
  • Chapter 4 discusses Oregon’s admissibility standard for scientific evidence, as set forth in State v. O’Key3 and other cases.
  • Chapter 5discusses evidentiary and other rules, separate from the specific scientific evidence analysis addressed in Chapter 4, that can also affect the admissibility determination.
  • Chapter 6 sets forth some basic principles that can be useful in cross-examining prosecution experts.
  • Chapter 7 addresses procedural and strategic issues that arise when the defense is considering calling its own expert witness.

Sample materials

Various court filings and other materials from some of my cases are included. Of course, these materials are case-specific and may not be suitable as templates for use in other cases. In addition, some of the legal positions and procedural approaches set forth in those materials have yet to be judicially tested, so I cannot represent that they will be accepted in any particular case. In essence, these materials are offered simply as samples that may serve as useful starting points for attorneys that have not previously prepared the types of materials at issue.

Gender conventions

Much of the discussion in this manual relates to hypothetical experts retained either by the state or the defense. For convenience, I have arbitrarily chosen to use the female gender when referring to a state expert and the male gender when referring to a defense expert.

Additional reference materials

There are many other reference materials that can be useful to attorneys dealing with scientific evidence issues. Some that may be particularly helpful are:

  • Federal Judicial Center, National Research Council of the National Academies, Reference Manual on Scientific Evidence (3d ed. 2011). This publication was designed primarily for federal judges but has been cited in several Oregon cases.4 It includes discussions of science and scientific evidence in general, along with chapters addressing various specific types of scientific evidence.
  • National Research Council of the National Academies, Strengthening Forensic Science in the United States: A Path Forward (2009). This publication addresses the current state of forensic science in the United States, including criticisms of certain types of evidence and of how courts have dealt with evidence of questionable quality. It has been cited by the United States Supreme Court5 and many other courts considering issues relating to forensic science.
  • John William Strong, Language and Logic in Expert Testimony: Limiting Expert Testimony by Restrictions of Function, Reliability, and Form.6 This law review article appears to have been quite persuasive to the Oregon Supreme Court, which cited it multiple times in O’Key. Accordingly, language in this article that supports your position may be persuasive to Oregon trial or appellate courts.
