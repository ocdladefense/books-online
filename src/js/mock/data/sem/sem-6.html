Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys

2012 edition

Cross-Examining the State’s Expert

Cross-examining an expert is widely considered to be one of the most challenging tasks faced by a trial lawyer. The following sections outline certain principles that may assist you in the planning and execution of your cross-examination. This is not intended to be a treatise on cross-examination per se. Instead, it is intended to help practitioners treat scientific evidence like all other types of evidence, and make the best possible use of their own individual cross-examination styles and skill sets.

I. Should you cross-examine the expert?

This is a real question in many cases. Again, cross-examining an expert is not easy, and an unsuccessful cross can do much more harm than good. This is of course true for any witness, but is especially so with an expert witness. If she is able to fend off your attacks with ease or condescension, her aura of reliability will be enhanced and your credibility will suffer.

Accordingly, some lawyers recommend the exercise of significant caution in deciding whether to engage in a substantive battle with an adverse expert. There is indeed an “if you aim for the king, don’t miss” element to these crosses. So before going forward with a substantial cross-examination, think carefully about whether the expert truly needs to be weakened or defeated in order to win your case.

II. Choosing Your Strategy

If you have decided that you are going to enter the fray, the next step is to think through your objectives for the cross-examination. Again, this is true for all witnesses, but especially so for experts in light of the complexity of the task and the particular risks involved.

The fact that you have decided to fight this battle means that the expert’s ultimate analysis, if accepted, will harm your case. So how do you go about finding the weak points in that analysis? Your strategy will of course be case specific, but below are some potential points to consider exploring. Oregon law provides that

“the cross-examiner is to be given great latitude in eliciting testimony to vitiate [an expert’s] opinion,”1  so it is worthwhile to think about how to make the best use of this latitude.

A. Underlying Facts, Observations, or Data Points

Scientific evidence generally appears in the form of expert opinion testimony, but the expert’s opinion will typically be based on certain underlying facts, observations, or data points. For example, a psychiatric evaluation may be based in part on the subject’s answers to certain questions, or a reconstructionist’s conclusion may rely on the locations at which certain items were found after a collision.

If you can establish that any of these “building blocks” are incorrect or at least subject to uncertainty, you may be able to call into question the expert’s ultimate conclusions. With respect to the examples above, you may be able to raise questions regarding whether the subject understood the questions asked by the examiner, or whether all of the items mapped by the reconstructionist were in fact found in the locations where they were immediately after the collision. In other cases, a fact relied upon by the expert in reaching her conclusion may be based on the testimony of a fact witness who is susceptible to cross-examination on the point in question.

If your strategy involves calling into question one or more of these “building blocks,” it may be useful to set up your cross-examination by establishing the significance of those facts. You may be able to extract an admission that the expert could not come to a trustworthy conclusion without knowing a particular fact, or that if a fact were different from what the expert understands it to be her conclusion would be different. Again, consider the examples above. If the psychiatrist’s evaluation is based on the subject’s responses to certain questions, would the psychiatrist’s ultimate conclusion be different (or impossible to reach confidently) if the psychiatrist had reason to believe that the subject had not understood the questions? If the reconstructionist’s conclusion relied on the positions of certain objects, would the conclusion (or the reconstructionist’s level of confidence in her conclusion) be different if in fact one of the objects had been moved between the collision and the analysis?

If the expert agrees that her conclusion, or her level of confidence in her conclusion, is dependent on certain facts, you have set yourself up to undermine that conclusion if you can call any of those facts into question. Conversely, if the expert refuses to concede the significance of these underlying facts, you may be able to use this to call the expert’s credibility and objectivity into question. The more the expert insists that her conclusion would have been identical regardless of variations in the underlying facts, the more she looks like a hired gun committed to a predetermined conclusion, instead of an objective analyst following the data wherever it leads.

This approach of focusing on underlying facts is easy to overlook, but it can be a vital part of the cross- examination of an expert. By shifting the battle from the expert’s “home turf ” of interpretation to ordinary facts, a lawyer can neutralize some of the expert’s advantage in substantive expertise. The lawyer can also undermine the ultimate conclusion without questioning the expertise or credibility of a witness that may seem impressive to jurors. A “building block” attack enables jurors to discount the expert’s conclusion despite accepting most or all of her specialized testimony: “She’s very intelligent and well-qualified, but her conclusion is only as good as the facts she’s been given, and we have reason to question those facts.”

This type of attack is also available where certain information was withheld from, or not available to, an expert as she was performing her analysis and coming to her opinion.2

B. Validity of Interpretive Steps

This is now the “science part”—the process of translating the basic facts discussed above into conclusions relevant to a particular case. The HGN evidence from O’Keyprovides a useful example. Assuming that the officer observed the requisite number of “clues” (e.g., lack of smooth pursuit when the subject’s eye movements were observed), how trustworthy is the resulting expert opinion of impairment?

The answer will implicate the factors discussed in Chapter 4, which involved scientific validity as assessed for purposes of admissibility. Of course, the fact that an expert’s proffered testimony survives that analysis does not mean her conclusions are correct—only that, in the court’s opinion, the evidence is of sufficient quality to present to a jury. In appropriate cases, attacks on such factors as the quality of the underlying validation studies, or the method’s error rate, may be useful at trial even if not sufficient to have the evidence excluded.

One strategy that can be fruitful relates to the reluctance of some experts to set forth defined, objective criteria that determine their ultimate conclusions. Some methods rely on such criteria—for example, when an officer performs the HGN test, observing four or more “clues” allegedly supports a conclusion of im- pairment. Other methods lack such definitive guideposts; experts who testify regarding these methods may testify more generally that (for example) measured values were “high” or “low.” An expert may, for example, testify that elevated levels of certain substances in the water downstream of a factory prove that the factory is polluting the water.

With the latter type of expert, it may be fruitful to attempt to pin down just what constitutes a “high” or “low” value. The state is attempting to use the “high” or “low” value to prove some fact of consequence (e.g., pollution from the factory). What is the normal range that would be expected absent that fact? What is the cut-off point above or below which the expert generally concludes that that fact must exist? Above or below that cut-off point, is the expert reasonably certain that that fact exists, or does the value at that level provide some lesser level of certainty?3

If the latter, is there another, more extreme cut-off point that doeslead to reasonable certainty? And for all these questions, how did the expert come up with these numbers? Has she used them consistently before? Are they published or written anywhere? Are they standardized throughout her field? 4

You may find that the state’s expert is reluctant to commit to any such criteria. Such reluctance may be based on an understanding of the risks created by such line-drawing. If you can establish that the correct value may have been on the other side of the cut-off point, that obviously undermines the state’s case. The expert may recognize this and try to retain the flexibility to adjust her analysis if necessary. In addition, if up to that point you have been exacting with your cross-examination, the expert may realize that any answer setting forth a specific number will be scrutinized—“What is your basis for using 25 as your cut-off? So you can say to a reasonable degree of certainty that whenever the value is more than 25, Fact X is present?” If she knows she cannot set forth a scientifically respectable basis for such a number, she may not be willing to subject herself to that line of inquiry.

The expert’s refusal to be pinned down can be frustrating, but in appropriate cases it can also be used to undermine her conclusions. Almost by definition, a method that is not governed by predetermined criteria is in essence an exercise in “I know it when I see it,” with the corresponding subjectivity and susceptibility to outcome-based analysis. Accordingly, you may be able to portray such a method as inherently less reliable than one in which conclusions are based on objective, preexisting standards.

C. Interpretive Limitations

Often, a method will stop short of proving everything the state would like to prove. The method may be nonspecific, meaning that a certain result may be consistent with multiple explanations or conclusions. For example, as the Oregon Supreme Court acknowledged in O’Key, factors other than alcohol impairment can cause nystagmus.5 Alternatively, the method may provide only part of a necessary logical chain. DNA ex- perts, for example, will generally admit that, although they can match a sample to a suspect, DNA analysis does not itself reveal how the DNA got to the place in which it was found.

D. Quality of Application

As discussed in Chapter 4, many methods are conducted according to detailed, generally recognized procedures. Indeed, the proponent will often emphasize the existence of such procedures during the admissibility phase as one way of demonstrating the trustworthiness of the method.

If the application of the method in your case involved any deviations from established procedures or other defects, that can be a subject of cross-examination. If you are going to pursue such an attack, it will frequently be helpful to set it up by demonstrating the significance of the procedures at issue, and the possible skewing or uncertainty that can result if those procedures are not properly followed. You may find helpful statements in the protocols themselves or in other literature relating to the method. In addition, as noted above, the expert herself may have provided helpful statements in her admissibility-phase testimony.

The expert may, of course, attempt to downplay the importance of any particular step, especially if she knows that that step was not properly carried out. If she does so, it may be appropriate to inquire into her qualifications to opine on the relative importance of various procedural requirements. Many state experts are essentially technicians who have been trained to perform a certain method but lack the background and expertise necessary to testify competently on such issues. Published protocols will often have been prepared by, or with the guidance of, people with significant expertise. If that is the case, and if neither those protocols nor any other published materials discount the importance of a particular step, you may be able to portray the contrary testimony of the state’s expert as carrying relatively little weight.

E. Qualifications

Are the expert’s qualifications sufficient to justify the weight that the state wants jurors to give to her testimony? This is a frequently used avenue of cross-examination.

There are, of course, risks and limitations involved with cross-examining an expert regarding her qualifications. For example, the more objective the scientific evidence is, the less significant a particular expert’s qualifications are. As discussed in Chapter 1, many experts essentially perform a “pass-through” function, testifying about widely known information that is often published in technical literature. If that is the form of the testimony, virtually any “expert” would presumably say the same thing, and an attack on the particular witness’s qualifications may not be very effective. By contrast, where the expert’s supposed specialized knowledge is largely dependent on her own personal experience and background, an inquiry into her qualifications may be more appropriate.

Even for experts testifying on supposedly objective topics, an attack on qualifications may be helpful with respect to any aspects of the testimony that rely on the expert’s own judgment. For example, there may be competing schools of thought within a field regarding the validity of a particular test. Even if the expert has learned to perform the test and can testify to its application and results, is she qualified to explain why the test’s proponents within the field have a better case than its opponents? As another example, is she qualified to testify regarding the significance of any deviation from established procedures?

In general, be alert for any effort by the expert to stray outside the boundaries of her legitimate expertise, or the boundaries set by the court at the admissibility hearing. Similarly, when an expert combines testimony about objective, generally known facts with more subjective testimony based on her own experience, it may be helpful to highlight the difference between those portions of her testimony. The enhanced credibility often associated with the former will then be less likely to spill over into the latter.

F. “Traditional” Cross-examination

Like any other witnesses, experts are subject to cross-examination on generally applicable topics such as bias, interest, and motive. As with cross-examination regarding the expert’s qualifications, here too the usefulness of this approach will depend on the nature of the testimony and, in particular, how subjective it is. To the extent that the expert is relying on her own judgment or experience, as opposed to passing along objectively verifiable information or principles, this line of cross-examination may be appropriate. It can be especially useful in the case of an expert who is employed by, or testifies regularly for, the state. As Upton Sinclair said, “[i]t is difficult to get a man to understand something when his salary depends upon his not understanding it.”6

G. Constructive Cross-examination

Finally, do not overlook the possibility of a constructive cross-examination in which you attempt to turn the state’s expert, at least in part, into your own. Obviously, the entire logical chain as presented by the state’s expert probably does not favor the defense. However, there may be critical “turning points” or “forks in the road” at which the subsequent direction of the analysis depends on something that the expert accepts, but that you can call into question. This may be an underlying fact, as discussed above. Alternatively, it could be the selection of a particular method, formula, or analytical approach from among multiple alternatives that have legitimate support within the field.

The expert may admit—especially if you have objective support from learned treatises or other materials— that taking a different “turn” at one of these points would ultimately have led either to a different, defense-favorable conclusion or to an inability to arrive at any reliable conclusion. If so, and if you can at least raise a question as to which way the analysis should have “turned” at one of those points, you can use the state expert’s testimony to your advantage.

III. Using Learned Treatises

OEC 706 (“Impeachment of expert witness by learned treatise”) provides that:

Upon cross-examination, an expert witness may be questioned concerning statements contained in a published treatise, periodical or pamphlet on a subject of history, medicine or other science or art if the treatise, periodical or pamphlet is established as a reliable authority. A treatise, periodical or pamphlet may be established as a reliable authority by the testimony or admission of the witness, by other expert testimony or by judicial notice. Statements contained in a treatise, periodical or pamphlet established as a reliable authority may be used for purposes of impeachment but may not be introduced as substantive evidence.

This can be a particularly effective cross-examination tool. Unlike expert testimony, pre-existing printed materials have fixed content that cannot be adjusted to fit the state’s needs in a particular case. Often, these materials will reference limitations, uncertainties, or necessary procedural safeguards that may be relevant to your case. Chapter 1 outlines some of the various types of materials that can used for this purpose, including laboratory protocols, “standard” methods, technical articles, and textbooks.

A publication must be “established as a reliable authority” before you can use it for cross-examination under OEC 706. The rule provides three ways to lay this foundation: through the expert you are cross-examining, through the testimony of another expert witness, or by judicial notice. Of course, the state expert’s own admission of reliability is particularly valuable. She may, at the admissibility phase, have acknowledged or even affirmatively relied on certain materials that she treats as trustworthy authorities with respect to the topic at issue; if so, she may find it awkward to distance herself from defense-favorable statements in those materials. Conversely, if she repeatedly refuses to acknowledge any publication as a reliable authority, this may open up an attack on her credibility or objectivity—especially if her qualifications are less impressive than those held by the publications’ authors—or the basis of her purported expertise.

If you are not confident that the state’s expert will acknowledge the reliability of the material you want to use for cross-examination, be prepared to use one of the other two options listed in OEC 706. If you rely on the “judicial notice” option, be prepared to make a convincing case for the acceptance of the publication as a reliable authority. One option is to present evidence that the publication is frequently cited in other materials in the field.
