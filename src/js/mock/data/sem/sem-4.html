Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys

2012 edition

The Scientific Evidence Admissibility Analysis

“[T]rial judges have an important role to play as ‘gatekeepers,’ charged with the responsibility of ensuring that proffered expert scientific testimony must be ‘not only relevant, but reliable.’”1

This chapter discusses Oregon’s admissibility analysis for scientific evidence, with emphasis on the multifactor test set forth in Statev. O’Key.2

I. The Applicable Evidence Rules

Although many lawyers and judges instinctively jump to the O’Keytest when evaluating scientific evidence, it is worthwhile to begin with the key evidence rules underlying that test. The primary rules specific to ex-pert testimony are the following:

• OEC 702(“Testimony by experts”): “If scientific, technical or other specialized knowledge will assist the trier of fact to understand the evidence or to determine a fact in issue, a witness qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training or education may testify thereto in the form of an opinion or otherwise.”

• OEC 703(“Bases of opinion testimony by experts”): “The facts or data in the particular case upon which an expert bases an opinion or inference may be those perceived by or made known to the expert at or before the hearing. If of a type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field in forming opinions or inferences upon the subject, the facts or data need not be admissible in evidence.”

In addition, the generally applicable OEC 401(“Definition of ‘relevant evidence’”) and OEC 403(“Exclusion of relevant evidence on grounds of prejudice, confusion or undue delay”) also apply to scientific evidence.3 These rules should not be overlooked. It is entirely possible for evidence to satisfy the scientific evidence standard but still be excluded on OEC 401 or 403grounds.4

Finally, OEC 701 (“Opinion testimony by lay witnesses”) will also be implicated in some cases. By its terms, it does not apply to expert testimony. However, if, as the opponent of a certain type of evidence, you lose on the threshold question of whether that evidence constitutes expert testimony, consider making the alternative argument that the evidence does not meet the helpfulness and “[r]ationally based on the perception of the witness” requirements of OEC 701.5

II. State v. O’Key

A. Background and Significance

In State v. O’Key,6

... the Oregon Supreme Court set forth a multifactor analysis for scientific evidence admissibility determinations in Oregon.7

O’Keyaddressed the admissibility of evidence relating to the horizontal gaze nystagmus (HGN) test, which involves examining the eye movements of drivers suspected of being under the influence of intoxicants. The court, after considering extensive testimonial and documentary evidence, held that under certain circumstances HGN evidence is admissible to prove that a defendant was under the influence of intoxicating liquor.8

Attorneys litigating scientific evidence issues in Oregon should be thoroughly familiar with the O’Keyopinion, and should understand the nature of the HGN evidence at issue along with the evidence and arguments presented concerning its admissibility. The elements of the O’Keyanalysis are best understood in the context of the evidence at issue in that case, and familiarity with that context can be useful in applying O’Key’s analysis to other types of evidence. For example, it may be helpful to compare the validation studies relied upon in O’Keyto any less impressive studies relied upon by the state in support of the evidence at issue in your case.

B. Relationship to Daubert

The federal evidence rules relating to scientific evidence are similar to Oregon’s.9 The leading case governing scientific evidence admissibility in federal courts is Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals,Inc.10 Daubertwas decided on statutory, not constitutional, grounds. Accordingly, it does not control the application of Oregon’s evidence rules.11 The Oregon Supreme Court nonetheless found at least some “aspects of the Daubertdecision” to be “persuasive,” and “adopt[ed] them,” noting that Oregon trial courts considering proffered scientific evidence should “find Daubertinstructive.”12

With this announced correlation between the two jurisdiction’s standards, Oregon attorneys and judges may find guidance not only in Daubertitself but in the decisions of other federal courts applying its analysis. Opinions from other states, many of which have adopted the Daubertanalysis, may also be helpful. 13

Be aware, though, that Oregon law has diverged from federal law in some potentially significant respects. For example, while federal courts apply the Daubertanalysis to all expert testimony, not only that which is deemed “scientific,” Oregon courts have limited O’Key’s scope to “scientific” evidence.14

Additionally, at least some federal courts have held that defects in the applicationof an otherwise valid method go only to the weight of the evidence, not its admissibility.15
By contrast, Oregon courts have repeatedly held that proper application is part of the admissibility analysis.16

Another difference is that federal appellate courts consider expert testimony admissibility issues under an abuse of discretion standard, while Oregon courts treat the admissibility analysis as a question of law.17

III. Preliminary Questions

Certain preliminary questions must be considered at the outset, as the answers to these affect whether the full multifactor analysis will be applied.

A.Prior Appellate Authority?

The Oregon Supreme Court has held that “[o]nce a trial court has decided that proffered expert scientific testimony is scientifically valid and has admitted such evidence for the particular purpose to which it is directed, and that decision is affirmed by this court in a published opinion, it will become precedent controlling subsequent trials.”18 This is significant. Although the proponent of a previously approved type of evidence will still need to lay a foundation, that foundation is much more limited than that required for evidence lacking prior appellate approval. Despite the reference to “this court,” a trial court will also likely truncate the admissibility analysis for types of evidence previously approved by the Oregon Court of Appeals.19

Accordingly, one of your first tasks is to determine whether the type of evidence at issue has been previously approved by any Oregon appellate court. Carefully scrutinize any potentially relevant appellate authority. The questions to consider include:

• Is the method in your case precisely the same as that which was previously approved? The question is not whether some generic category of evidence has been approved. Instead, it should be whether an appellate court has found a specific method, conducted a specific way, to be admissible.20 In this regard, pay close attention to State v. Aman.21 In that case, the Court of Appeals rejected the state’s attempt to rely on prior appellate approval because the method applied in the later case omitted a key step from the previously approved method.22

•Is the method being used for the same purpose for which it was previously approved? “[S]cientific va- lidity for one purpose is not necessarily scientific validity for other, unrelated purposes.”23 Accordingly, the prior approval rule applies only where the evidence is being offered for the same purpose as that for which it was offered in the previous case.24

•Do you have any evidence, not presented to the previous courts, that undermines the earlier decision? “[N]o particular reason of logic or good sense exists to immunize particular areas or principles simply on the basis of longevity . . . . Supposedly valid ‘science’ has not infrequently been unmasked.”25 If you have such evidence, consider presenting it to the trial court. Even if that court considers itself bound by precedent, you will have laid the groundwork for possible appellate reconsideration. This may be particularly fruitful if the prior appellate authority predates O’Key.26

B. Exceptions

In O’Key, the Oregon Supreme Court carved out certain exceptions to the general applicability of its multifactor analysis—a “clear case,” a “case for judicial notice,” and “a case of prima facielegislative recognition.”27

Clear case/judicial notice

The “clear case” and “judicial notice” exceptions appear to be closely related. Both appear to apply when the validity of the proffered evidence is sufficiently obvious that no extensive analysis is required. Because the difference between these two exceptions may not be especially significant for practical purposes, 28

they will be discussed together.29

The contours of the clear case/judicial notice exception are not entirely clear. As of the date of this publication, it has only been applied to one type of evidence, in State v. Branch30 and the closely related State v. Jaffe.31Branch ''involved “lidar” (“light detection and ranging”) devices, which are used to measure distances. The Court of Appeals did not offer a specific standard of what constitutes a “clear case” (or a “case for judicial notice”).32 In holding that the lidar evidence had been properly admitted, the court noted the following: “the basic scientific principle underlying the conclusions [at issue]—is the speed of light, one of the fundamental constants of physics and chemistry in our universe”;33 “using lidar devices to measure distances” is “universal[ly] accept[ed]” and used by “law enforcement depart- ments nationwide”;34 and the defendant did not “substantively challenge on appeal [the relevant scientific] principles or the scientific validity of using lidar devices to measure distances.”35

It remains to be seen how courts will apply the clear case/judicial notice exception following Branch.

Opponents of proffered scientific evidence can point out the relatively unique nature of the lidar evi- dence as perceived by the Court of Appeals—for example, the court compared that evidence to “body temperature readings from a state-of-the-art digital thermometer.”36 Additionally, opponents should thoroughly research the literature for materials that, even if they do not undermine the basic principles underlying the method at issue, at least call into question its supposed inerrancy. It is the rare method that has no identified uncertainties, interferences, or potential sources of error, and pointing these out to the court may at least move your case out of the clear case/judicial notice realm and into the stan- dard O’Keyanalysis.37

C. Prima FacieLegislative Recognition

This exception applies where the legislature has essentially preempted or overridden what would other- wise be a court’s role in assessing scientific evidence admissibility, and has itself determined that a cer- tain category of evidence should be admitted. This exception has been applied to admit blood alcohol testing evidence, in light of the Oregon legislature’s enactment of “statutes that have allowed for blood testing and the admission of such results in court proceedings.”38

The exception does not apply simply because a method is referenced in some legislative provision, but requires some indication of the legislature’s specific intention to make the evidence admissible in a judicial proceeding.39

IV. The Framework

For any scientific evidence to be admissible, three basic requirements must be met: the method must be valid, the particular witness must be qualified as an “expert,” and the method must have been properly applied in the case.40 These requirements are discussed below in the admissibility context. Keep in mind, though, that much of what follows can also be used to attack the weight of the evidence if some or all of it is admitted.

A. Validity

Scientific evidence must be “scientifically valid.”41 “[V]alidity describes how well the scientific method reasons to its conclusions”; in other words, “[t]he validity of the test is the extent to which the test measures what it claims to measure.”42 It is different from “reliability,” which is “the ability of the . . . method to produce consistent results when replicated,” i.e., “the consistency with which the test mea- sures whatever it is that it measures.”43

This distinction—similar to that between “accuracy” and “precision”—was explained in O’Keyas follows: “For example, a new test for blood alcohol may be invalid in that it grossly underestimates the amount of alcohol in one’s bloodstream, and yet be reliable in that it underestimates the blood alcohol level in one’s bloodstream by the same amount every time.”44 Pay close attention to this difference, as proponents of evidence will often speak primarily in terms of reliability or precision instead of validity or accuracy.

Always keep in mind that the validity analysis does not take place in the abstract, but in the context of the specific purpose for which the proponent seeks to offer the evidence. Again, “scientific validity for one purpose is not necessarily scientific validity for other, unrelated purposes.”45 Oregon appellate decisions considering scientific evidence regularly begin with discussions of the purpose for which the evidence is being offered,46 and this is an appropriate point of emphasis at the trial court level too.

In O’Key, as discussed above, the Oregon Supreme Court set forth a series of factors to be considered in the validity analysis. Those factors are discussed below. At the outset, however, it is worth noting that Oregon appellate courts have not been entirely consistent in their post-O’Keyarticulation of these factors.

Before O’Key, the leading Oregon case on the issue was State v. Brown,47 which set forth what have since been described as “seven primary and 11 additional factors” to be considered in the admissibility analysis.48 In O’Key, the court essentially combined the seven “primary” Brownfactors with the four

factors discussed in Daubert; the resulting list includes the 11 factors discussed below.49 Since O’Key, however, Oregon courts have not always applied the specific factors listed in that opinion. Some opinions continue to cite Brownas the governing case; of these, some refer only to the seven factors discussed above as “primary,”50 and others list both these and the “additional” 11 factors.51 Other opinions refer to O’Keyas having added the four Daubertfactors to the eighteen Brownfactors, while acknowledging some “overlap[]” between these sets.52

The remainder of this chapter takes a broad approach and assumes that an Oregon court’s analysis can encompass the 11 factors addressed in the section of O’Keyassessing HGN evidence,53 the 11 “additional” Brownfactors, and finally, as discussed below, any additional factors that may bear on a court’s overall assessment of validity and admissibility.

1. “Testability or falsifiability.”

54

This is the first listed O’Keyfactor:

Ordinarily, a key question to be answered in determining whether a theory or technique is scientific knowledge *** will be whether it can be (and has been) tested. Scientific methodology today is based on generating hypotheses and testing them to see if they can be falsified; indeed, this methodology is what distinguishes science from other fields of human inquiry.55

This is the nature of the traditional scientific method in which hypotheses are proposed and experiments designed to prove or disprove those hypotheses.56

For example, in a case involving the admissibility of a doctor’s testimony on medical causation, the Oregon Supreme Court explained:

On this record, the “testability,” or “falsifiability,” factor is met. Any scientist can check Grimm’s testing methods and the clinical history of each of his patients. Moreover, Grimm’s results also can be tested (another neurologist could test women with silicone breast implants to see if they had the same neurological conditions identified by Grimm). Grimm’s hypothesis is capable of being falsified; that is, evidence may be introduced to disprove his hypothesis.”57

The first part of this inquiry is whether the method at issue canbe tested.58

“The main premise of falsifiability is that a hypothesis that cannot be subject to the possibility of rejection by observation and experiment cannot be regarded as scientific.”59

For most evidence that finds its way into courtrooms, this is a largely academic question, as most such evidence can, at least in theory, be subjected to experiments that would tend to prove or disprove its validity. The primary question will generally be whether the method hasbeen tested, and if so whether such testing is sufficient to establish the validity of the method for the evidentiary purpose for which it is offered. “This consideration includes an evaluation of the testing procedures used, the number of studies undertaken, and criticisms of those procedures.”60

Imagine, for example, that you have a hypothesis that a particular additive will cause a plant to grow more quickly. You can imagine a relatively simple experiment in which a set of identical plants is split into two groups. These groups are treated identically with respect to all factors that could influence their growth (water amount, soil type, sunlight exposure, etc.), except that one group (the “experimental group”) is given the additive and the other (the “control group”) is not. If the experimental group grows more quickly than the second, and the difference is greater than would be expected from random variation, that is evidence in favor of your hypothesis.

That does not mean that you have definitively proven that hypothesis. It may be, for example, that you did not precisely control the experiment, and factors other than the presence or absence of the additive could have differed between the two groups and caused the observed difference.61

Or you may not have used enough plants, or repeated the experiment enough times, to demonstrate a sufficient probability that the observed difference is due to the additive and not random chance.

Still, your experiment is a step in the direction of proving your hypothesis and validating your method. By contrast, if the growth rates were not significantly different between the two groups, that would tend to disprove your hypothesis. In any event, that is the nature of your hypothesis—it is susceptible to being empirically proven or disproven.62

At the core of any scientific evidence, there will be some hypothesis that, if correct, makes the witness’s testimony relevant to a particular case. Your task, at this point in the O’Keyanalysis, is to identify that hypothesis and evaluate the degree to which it has been validated empirically (i.e., through testing and data).

Consider, for example, the scientific evidence in O’Key. In that case, the state sought the admission of HGN evidence (evidence relating to certain eye movements) to prove that a driver is impaired. What might be the core hypothesis in such a case? Perhaps something like this:

“A trained police officer can accurately identify alcohol impairment by using a test in which eye movements are tracked as a driver is instructed to follow a stimulus.”

Why did I suggest this as the hypothesis? Because it encompasses both the underlying science and the ultimate question of whether the evidence is useful to the factfinder. We might have chosen, for example, “A person’s eye movements become jerkier when that person is under the influence of alcohol.” But whether that is true or not, it does not answer the ultimate question for the court, which is: Is this evidence going to accurately and reliably help the trier of fact decide whether someone was impaired? If, for example, alcohol did in fact cause jerky eye movements but the effect was too gradual or variable for officers to make consistently accurate judgments, the second sample hypothesis could be validated but the evidence would still not be useful to the factfinder.63

This may seem like a laborious exercise, but it can be critical. Remember, what matters is not whether scientific principles are accurate in the abstract, but whether they can be applied to help a factfinder make a factual determination in a real case.

Once you have identified the state’s functional hypothesis, the next step is to evaluate whether it has been sufficiently validated. Do not be intimidated or distracted by scientific terminology—this is, at bottom, an exercise in ordinary logic. Think of it as just another one of the proof issues attorneys work with every day. Does the evidence presented by the state in support of the validity of the method support a conclusion of validity for purposes of your case? 64

For example, in O’Key, the state had to prove that the particular version of the HGN test at issue could

be effectively used by officers to distinguish impaired from unimpaired drivers. The evidence presented in favor of admissibility was of a type that might have been expected. Volunteers were given enough alcohol to give them blood-alcohol contents (“BACs”) at various levels both above and below 0.10 percent.65 After being trained in the test’s administration, officers administered the tests to the volunteers and attempted to identify them as being above or below that threshold. According to the evidence presented by the state, trained officers were able to classify accurately the volunteers as above or below 0.10 percent BAC approximately 80 percent of the time. These results were viewed favorably by the Oregon Supreme Court.66

There is no universally applicable “checklist” for assessing the quality of proposed scientific evidence. As discussed above, the validation of a method is at bottom a matter of proof and logic, and both the mode of proof and the key “soft spots” will vary from one method to another. The following is a list of (partially overlapping) issues that frequently arise in the evaluation of scientific reports or findings. Whether or not any of these relate to the evidence in your case, they may illustrate some of the logical principles involved in assessing scientific evidence.67

a. Failure to isolate variables. This is a common problem in experiments designed to determine whether one variable (the independent variable) has an effect on a second variable (the dependent variable). For example, in the plant additive hypothetical above, the independent variable is whether the additive is added or not (or the amount of additive added), and the dependent variable is the plant’s growth rate. In order for the experiment to accurately measure the additive’s effect on growth, the only condition that should differ between plants is whether, or in what amount, the additive is added. If any other condition differs between plants—for example, if the plants to which additive is added also are exposed to more sunlight—it will be difficult, and perhaps impossible, to ascribe any observed changes in growth rates to the additive. Some of the other issues discussed below are essentially specific examples of this issue.

Not all questions lend themselves to controlled experimental studies such as the plant additive hypothetical discussed above. Sometimes researchers must resort to investigating broad “real world” trends outside of a controlled setting. For example, researchers studying potential health effects of certain environmental conditions (e.g., proximity to power lines) cannot always set up experiments in which a group of subjects is split into two subgroups—one of which is exposed to the condition, the other of which is not—with the subjects’ health then monitored. As an alternative, they may try to compare the rate of certain medical conditions between people naturally exposed to the condition and others. Such a comparison, however, is subject to the problem of “confounding variables”—variables, other than the variable under investigation (here, proximity to power lines), that can affect the observed results. For example, in this hypothetical it may be that property values of homes close to power lines are lower than those of the comparison homes. If so, people living near power lines may, on average, be less wealthy and have lower-quality medical care than those in the comparison group.

Good researchers will do their best to “control for” such confounding variables. They might, for example, try to pick a comparison group that is as similar as possible to the “power line” group in every possible way other than proximity to power lines. Alternatively, statistical tools such as regression models may help to “tease out” the effects of particular variables in multivariable settings.

Of course, such solutions are not perfect, even in well-designed experiments. Ultimately, the persuasiveness of any research, including the degree to which it addresses concerns such as those discussed above, is based on ordinary logical principles. When confronted with experimental data that purportedly supports a point the state wants to make, assess that data carefully and critically—with the help, where appropriate, of your own expert—and see how convincing it really is.68

b. Sample issues. When researchers take a defined number of data points using a defined number of subjects, the objective is ultimately to generalize their findings beyond that group. For example, imagine a study designed to determine what percentage of Americans have red hair, conducted by analyzing the hair color of one hundred participants, of whom two are found to have red hair. That information only becomes useful if it can be generalized to a conclusion that a corresponding percentage of Americans have red hair. Whether such generalization is possible depends critically on the nature of the sample (i.e., the hundred people studied). Sampling issues can be complex, and even a brief overview would be beyond the scope of this manual, but a few common issues can be highlighted.

One such issue is the sizeof the sample. Generally speaking, the greater the sample size, the better the argument for validation. For example, imagine that in an HGN validation study, a single officer performed the test on a single subject and correctly identified the subject as having a BAC greater or lower than 0.10 per- cent. So far, that would not be terribly convincing—that correct result could easily have been obtained by chance. By contrast, if a large number of subjects are tested and the overall success rate is greater than what can reasonably be explained by chance alone, the validation case grows stronger.69

Accordingly, consider whether the experiments underlying the scientific evidence in your case involved a large enough sample size to support the conclusions sought to be drawn. You may not need to undertake this analysis from scratch.

If the experimental results have been subjected to peer review, the adequacy of the sample size will likely have been one of the factors considered by the reviewers.

Other issues relate to the representativenessof the sample. Generalization requires that the sample be representative—sometimes described as a “cross-section”—of the population to which the study’s conclusions are intended to apply. For example, using the hypothetical red hair study, imagine that the experimental

subjects were drawn from a state with a higher-than-average Scottish population. With red hair being more common in people of Scottish descent, this sample would not be representative of Americans in general, and the study would be affected by this “selection bias.”

Bias can creep into experiments in many ways. In telephone surveys, for example, bias enters in at various points. One of these points relates to telephone access itself. If researchers use a call list randomly picked from a land-line telephone book, the survey will only be representative of that portion of the community that has land-line telephones. This may not be representative of the community as a whole. In the early 20th century, such a survey would have resulted in overrepresentation of wealthy people (who would be more likely to own telephones); today, it may result in underrepresentation of sectors of society in which people are more likely to rely exclusively on mobile phones. Then, an additional level of bias can arise based on the fact that responses are voluntary, and not everyone is equally likely to be willing to answer the survey questions. This might result, for example, in overrepresentation of retired or unemployed people.

The foregoing discussion, of course, barely scratches the surface of the huge area of sampling issues. In general, analyze the sample as part of your assessment of any experimental design and consider whether there are any issues that call the proffered conclusions into question.

c. Unwarranted extrapolation or extension. Experiments conducted within certain ranges or under certain conditions may not validate the subject methods for use in different ranges or under different conditions. For example, if a technique for measuring blood-alcohol content has been demonstrated to be valid for BACs between 0 and 0.15 percent, that may not support the use of that technique for determining specific BACs over that amount. Similarly, research studies may indicate that officers can use certain field sobriety tests to accurately distinguish impaired from unimpaired drivers in a controlled indoor laboratory setting using volunteer subjects. That does not necessarily translate into validation for making impairment deter- minations in real-world roadside settings, which involve significant additional variables such as the psycho- logical stress experienced by the “subject.”70

One common variation of this problem occurs when, as discussed above, samples are not representative of the broader group to which the research’s conclusions are intended to apply. For example, one commonly acknowledged problem in psychological research is that in many studies, for practical reasons, subjects are drawn heavily or exclusively from the ranks of undergraduate students (or, even more narrowly, undergraduate psychology students). There are, of course, real questions regarding the degree to which findings from such studies can be generalized to more heterogeneous groups.71

A related issue arises when physical materials are sampled. If the point of analyzing a sample is to learn something or draw conclusions about the material as a whole, the samples must collectively be representative of that broader material.72

d. Confusion of correlation and causation. A frequent error is the assumption that because one variable “tracks” or correlates with another, the first must be causing the changes in the second. In fact, two variables can be correlated (i.e., there may be some statistically significant relationship between them) without there being any causal relationship. One commonly used example is the fact that over the course of a year, murder rates tend to be correlated with ice cream consumption rates. Even if the correlation is not coincidental, that does not mean that eating ice cream causes people to kill, or that a high number of murders causes people to eat ice cream. It could mean that a third variable—for example, temperature—is affecting both ice cream consumption and murder rates.

e. Observer effect. The very fact that a process is being observed by a researcher can cause that process to play out differently than it would naturally. Consider, for example, typical juror studies involving mock jurors who are informed that their “deliberations” are being recorded. In addition to the obvious question of whether “jurors” in such studies will approach their “cases” the way they would if real consequences would result, those jurors may modify their behavior because they know they are being observed.73

f. Analyst bias or influence. Sometimes a participant in an experiment has the potential to influence the apparent results based on previously acquired knowledge or bias. As an obvious example, imagine a drug-testing procedure in which the physician who administers the drug to the subjects and assesses their progress knows which subjects are receiving the drug (the experimental group) and which are receiving a placebo (the control group). Especially if the physician has an interest in seeing the drug succeed, she may influence the apparent results at multiple levels. She may display more enthusiasm and a warmer attitude towards experimental group patients, which may itself affect their progress independent of any effect of the drug. Similarly, she may be inclined to see more progress in experimental group patients—especially if the measure of “progress” has subjective aspects (as opposed to, for example, objectively measurable aspects such as blood pressure or heart rate).

The Oregon Supreme Court has recognized the potential for both analyst influence and biased interpretation to affect ultimate conclusions. For example, in considering polygraph evidence in State v. Brown,74the court explained:

Polygraph test results may be altered by the examiner’s preconceptions as to factual elements of the case or the guilt or innocence of the subject. If the examiner has an accusatory tone of voice, rather than a neutral tone when asking the questions, the subject’s response may be altered. If the examiner is biased as to the subject’s guilt or innocence, the questions may be framed differently. Furthermore, any preconceptions or biases are likely to influence the exam- iner’s final interpretation of the polygraph charts.75

Good studies will include procedures to minimize these effects. For example, in a “double blind” study, neither the subjects nor the experimenters know, during the progress of the experiment, which subjects are receiving the drug and which are receiving a placebo.76 When analyzing the research underlying the method in your case, be on the lookout for any points at which these effects could creep in.77

g. Inexact or misleading terminology. Particularly when a method has a potential forensic application, its proponents may use terminology that misstates or overstates the inferences that the method can legitimately support:

[M]any terms are used by forensic examiners in reports and in court testimony to describe findings, conclusions, and the degrees of association between evidentiary materials (e.g., hairs, fingerprints, fibers) and particular people or objects. Such terms include but are not limited to “match,” “consistent with,” “identical,” “similar in all respects tested,” and “cannot be excluded as the source of.” The use of such terms can have a profound effect on how the trier of fact in a criminal or civil matter perceives and evaluates evidence. Yet the forensic science disciplines have not reached agreement or consensus on the precise meaning of any of these terms.78

Accordingly, the terminology used by expert witnesses deserves special scrutiny. For example, whenever some version of “matching” terminology is used, it is worth examining how probative such a “match” really is. The significance of a “match” is inversely proportional to the uniqueness of the particular characteristic at issue.

For example, consider the following three characteristics of a hypothetical suspect: (a) two legs, (b) blood type A-positive, and (c) a particular DNA profile. Assume that an expert witness is attempting to demonstrate that the characteristics of a person determined to have been at the crime scene “match” those of the suspect. If the “match” is that the person has two legs, that is obviously of little significance—that characteristic is shared by virtually everyone. If it is that the person has A-positive blood, that is a little more significant, but that still describes approximately a third of the population and therefore is relatively insignificant. But if the “match” is a particular DNA profile—something that is believed to be present in only one in several billion people—it is much more meaningful.79

Accordingly, whenever “matching” evidence is involved, be sure to look into the rarity of a “match” and thus the significance when one is found. If the expert does not know, or if it turns out that a “match” is relatively common and thus minimally probative, you can argue that the “matching” evidence is effectively meaningless. Even if there is some slight relevance, such evidence should be excluded under OEC 403, especially in light of the significance that jurors may attach to an expert’s dramatic reporting of a “match.”80

2.“Peer review and publication (the existence of specialized literature).”

81

This is a particularly significant factor—both directly and in the ways that it can bear on a court’s analysis of other factors. Peer review is a critical part of the process of testing the validity of a researcher’s work and ultimately making the benefits of that work available to the field and to society at large.

Peer review will generally take place in something like the following manner. At the conclusion of a segment of research, a researcher who believes that her research has yielded valuable results will prepare a draft article summarizing her work. That draft will discuss the way that the experiment was designed and conducted, describe or summarize the data obtained, and set forth the researcher’s conclusions. The draft will be sent to an established technical journal, which will in turn pass it along to a panel of other researchers in the field. Those other researchers will analyze the draft to determine whether the cited experimental work supports the proposed conclusions. They can make recommendations in favor of or in opposition to publication. They may also interact with the author, asking clarifying questions or suggesting additional experiments to firm up the analysis.

There are two major benefits of this process of peer review and publication. First, if implemented correctly it is a particularly effective way to evaluate the quality of a researcher’s work. After all, the people most qualified to spot potential flaws will be other experts in the same field.82

(Of course, there are drawbacks to a narrowly focused approach; it is not uncommon for researchers within a field to succumb to a type of “groupthink” that can lead to blind spots and uncritical acceptance of the field’s conventional wisdom.)

Second, the process is a good way of ultimately communicating the researcher’s findings—perhaps sharpened or clarified through the peer review process—to the rest of the world. At least in theory, most research (at least nonproprietary research) is done for the eventual benefit of society or some portion of it. If the research is published, others can begin to make use of it, either by applying it directly or by using it as a starting point for further research.

When evaluating the state’s scientific evidence in your case, pay close attention to whether it has gone through any peer review process, and if so, what the quality of that process was. As with other aspects of the overall admissibility analysis, be picky here. Do not simply accept generalized testimony that the method has been “subjected to peer review” or “published in peer-reviewed literature.” Ask about the peer review process (including who the reviewers were) and the particular publications.83

In addition, do not just look at whether the broad principles underlying a particular method have been validated through peer review. Investigate whether any peer-reviewed literature supports the particular application of the method for the purposes for which the state intends to use it in your case.84

If the evidence in your case has not been subjected to rigorous peer review with respect to the particular implications relevant to your case, this is a potentially fruitful line of attack.85

The state will likely emphasize that, according to the Oregon Supreme Court, peer review and publication are not a “sine qua non of admissibility.”86 That is true; the lack of peer review and publication do not per serender an expert’s analysis invalid. Still, a purported expert who has not chosen to subject her analysis to this process should be questioned closely to find out why not. This is particularly true where the expert’s work, if in fact valid, could be of significant value to the field and to society if subjected to the normal review process and published.

The lack or poor quality of peer review can also be useful in challenging the state and its expert with respect to other factors. Although it is ultimately up to the court to assess the validity of the evidence for purposes of your case, the judge may feel less than fully qualified to evaluate technical matters such as, for example, sample size or other aspects of the experimental design. In such a case, consider emphasizing that there is in fact a recognized process through which true experts in the field make these evaluations. If that process was available and the state’s witness chose not to subject her work to it, the court can rely on the obvious inferences that can be drawn from that choice.87

Another concept to keep in mind is that of reproducibility. Although not necessarily part of the peer review process, reproducibility is an essential element of good science. It means that if a researcher’s work is valid, another researcher should be able to obtain the same results. If this is not the case—for example, if subsequent researchers cannot duplicate the results obtained by the first—there is probably something wrong with the first researcher’s work.88

Consider investigating whether any other researchers have attempted to reproduce the work underlying the evidence in your case, and if so, what the results have been. If the work is inherently not susceptible to replication, this weighs against a validity finding.89

3. “The known or potential rate of error.”

90

Every method or technique is subject to some degree of error or uncertainty. Legitimate scientists acknowledge this, and regularly include expressions of uncertainty when they report their results. Indeed, it is generally misleading for an analyst to record a measurement using only a single number—for example, to say that a suspect’s blood-alcohol content (BAC) was “0.065.” It would be more appropriate for the analyst to say she is, for example, 95 percent confident that the suspect’s BAC was between 0.060 and 0.070.91

Error rate (or uncertainty, or whatever term is used to express the concept) is something you should research carefully. Some techniques have fairly low uncertainties, such as a few percent of measured values. Others have much greater uncertainties—I have had cases involving experimental data with uncertainties several times greater than the measured values.

Uncertainty is not something an analyst should simply estimate off the top of her head. Determined correctly, it is itself the product of significant experimental analysis, often based on well-established mathematical formulas.92 Be alert for the expert who will simply cite an error rate of, for example, “plus or minus ten percent.” Find out how she got that number. Is it simply a “gut feel,” or has she (or anyone else) done any actual analysis? If an analysis was done, is the reported uncertainty based on controlled laboratory studies? If so, and if (as will usually be the case) real-world applications will introduce additional elements of uncertainty, how has the analyst accounted for those additional elements in her ultimate determination of uncertainty?

Even if the expert purports to cite data in support of her uncertainty testimony, find out whether in your case she performed the method the same way it was performed when the uncertainty data was generated. If not, that uncertainty data has little relevance to the admissibility analysis.93

As explained by the Oregon Supreme Court, this factor includes not only the quantitative analysis dis- cussed above, but also qualitative considerations of the types of influences that could skew an analysis and lead to a misleading result. For example, in O’Keythe court specifically discussed several conditions, other than alcohol impairment, that can cause nystagmus.94 As you study the method or technique at issue in your case, look for these potential sources of error and be ready to demonstrate them to the court.

Keep in mind that the concept of error rate or uncertainty, at least in its quantitative aspects, will be more significant in some cases than in others. Some experts do not offer specific measured values but instead testify to provide background or context—for example, a commonly litigated form of testimony involves allegedly common behavior (such as delayed reporting) by sexual abuse victims. Such testimony may not be particularly susceptible to a quantitative analysis of error rate (unless, for example, the witness attempts to speak in quantitative terms or rely on specific data). It should still, however, be appropriate to investigate potential complicating factors that could undermine the validity of the witness’s testimony. Similarly, even in more quantitative applications, the significance of any particular error rate will depend on how that error rate affects the usefulness of the data for its intended use in the case.95

The Oregon Supreme Court has held that “reasonable certainty” is the appropriate standard under this factor.96 Accordingly, consider asking the state’s expert if she believes she is able to opine on the relevant issues to a level of “reasonable certainty”; if not, this may be sufficient for exclusion.

Finally, in appropriate cases you may want to link the uncertainty discussion to your argument for exclusion under OEC 403. This will be particularly appropriate in cases in which the uncertainty is quantitatively significant in light of the specific numbers involved. Consider, for example, a hypothetical case in which a “normal” measured value is 50, the measurement in your case is 54, and the uncertainty is plus or minus ten percent of the measured value. If the state attempts to attach significance to the “high” measurement, point out that any apparent deviation from the “normal” value is within the margin of error such that the probative value is minimal at best, whereas the potential prejudice is great.97

===4.“Existence and maintenance of standards governing the use of the [method].” 98

Find out if there are detailed protocols, guidelines, or other generally applicable materials underlying the evidence in your case. In addition to being marks of good science in general, these sorts of “standards” help to make the application of a method fairer and more objective.

As to the latter point, the importance of pre-existing standards can be considered by analogy to legal prohibitions against ex post factoapplication of criminal laws. In the legal realm, a significant purpose of requiring pre-existing legal rules is to limit the government’s ability to tailor the legal regime to suit a particular target. The same concern is present with scientific evidence. If the application of a method can be adjusted from one case to another, the “scientist” can make adjustments designed to support a preferred result. By contrast, at least in theory, pre-existing standards force analysts to accept the results yielded by the standardized procedures, whether those results support the preferred result or not. For example, for the HGN evidence approved in O’Key, the standard protocol provides that BAC above 0.10% is indicated only if four or more “clues,” out of a possible six, are observed. Setting aside the subjectivity of the observation of “clues”—a topic discussed infra—this standard provides for better “science” than if each officer could individually determine, after each HGN test, whether the number of “clues” indicated high BAC.

Consider cross-examining the state’s expert closely on the question of whether any pre-existing standards were considered or consulted. In other words, going into the analysis, was there some standard governing whether the observed results would or would not support the expert’s ultimate conclusion—for example, a “match,” or a conclusion of drug impairment? Where did those standards come from? Were there other available standards in the field—perhaps more stringent, or otherwise less favorable to the state’s ultimate theory—that the expert chose not to use? On what basis was the selection of standards made?

If the expert testifies that legitimate standards did exist and were consulted, this will generally help the state in the admissibility analysis, but it may also help set up other avenues of attack, either at the admissibility stage or at trial. For example, the expert agrees that the ultimate conclusion would have been different if a different value had been obtained for a particular measurement. This can be a valuable admission if you are ultimately able to demonstrate—based on, for example, flaws in the method’s application, or additional data not known to the expert—that that other value may in fact have been accurate.

By contrast, if the expert fails or refuses to acknowledge any pre-existing standards—saying, for example, “I do these analyses on a case-by-case basis, and I draw my conclusions based on my training and experience”—this can also be helpful to you. For one thing, it should weigh against admissibility. For another, if the expert refuses to acknowledge any specific data or conclusions that would have led to a different conclusion, that can set up an attack on that expert’s objectivity.99

Focusing on this factor may also set up an argument based on evidence that certain established procedures were notfollowed. As discussed infraat Part IV.C., under Oregon law proper application is part of the admissibility analysis, and flaws in the application of an otherwise valid method can be grounds for exclusion. This can end up forcing the proponent of scientific evidence to walk a fine line. The proponent may need to emphasize the importance of protocols and other guidelines for purposes of the “existence and maintenance of standards” factor. Doing so, however, can end up undermining the evidence if in fact those protocols and guidelines were not carefully followed.

5.“The degree of . . . acceptance in the relevant scientific community.”

100

“Widespread acceptance can be an important factor in ruling particular evidence admissible, and a known technique that has been able to attract only minimal support within the community may properly be viewed with skepticism.”101

As the Oregon Supreme Court recognized, this factor requires that a court first “identify the relevant scientific community to which the [type of evidence in question] belongs.”102 The law enforcement community itself generally should not be the “relevant scientific community.”103

Instead, look at all of the fields that could potentially have an interest in, or connection to, the type of evidence in your case. For example, in O’Keythe court concluded that “the scientific disciplines of pharmacology, ophthalmology, and to a lesser extent optometry should be included with behavioral psychology, highway safety, neurology, and criminalistics in the relevant scientific community” for the evaluation of HGN evidence.104

As you look into what experts and literature in these fields have to say about the evidence in your case, keep in mind that the question is not whether the method or technique is valid in the abstract, but whether it is valid for the purposes for which the state wants to introduce it in your case.105

For example, there may be consensus that a method is generally useful to provide approximate indications of some quality or characteristic, but no consensus (or consensus limited to the law enforcement community) that the method produces results at the level of accuracy and precision required for your case.106 Similarly, general acceptance does not aid the proponent when the method was not applied in the way that the field generally accepts.107

6.“The Expert’s Qualifications.”

108

A discussion of expert qualifications can arise at different points in the admissibility analysis. In one sense, this factor relates to the particular qualifications of the specific expert whose testimony is being offered; that is addressed infraat Part IV.B.. There are also suggestions, however, that this factor can be treated as a type of “standards” or “safeguards” issue in the overall validity analysis. Specifically, if certain training, education, or credentialing is required before someone can present herself as an expert in a particular field, this can support an overall conclusion of validity.109

At the same time, if a particular proffered expert lacks qualifications deemed necessary, that can be a basis for excluding that expert’s testimony.110

7.“The use that has been made of the [method].”

111

This factor has been interpreted in multiple ways. In State v. Sampson, for example, the court noted that as of the time of that case “[t]he case law ha[d] not analyzed this factor in detail and [was], in fact, not entirely clear” as to whether it referred to “how widely the protocol ha[d] been used” or “the goal of the protocol.”112 In any event, “[t]his factor includes non-judicial uses and experience with the [method at issue].”113 The significance of this factor is obvious. If no one outside of law enforcement uses the method, that can be quite telling—especially if the method would, if valid, be highly useful in other fields.

If the state presents evidence of non-judicial applications, consider whether those applications require the type of accuracy and precision that courts should demand for forensic applications. People routinely use “rough cut” methods for approximations, or to help them make decisions that are preliminary or comparatively unimportant. Methods appropriate for such applications may not be appropriate for forensic use.114

Watch for the circular argument that a type of evidence should be approved for criminal prosecutions because it is widely used in law enforcement. As the Oregon Supreme Court explained with respect to polygraph evidence:

[I]t is common knowledge that police and law enforcement agencies utilize polygraph exami- nations routinely in the investigation of criminal activity. Extensive use of polygraphy is not equivalent to general acceptance by the relevant scientific community . . . . Widespread use may have some bearing on probity, but is certainly not determinative.115

===8.“The extent to which other courts admit [the type of evidence at issue].” 116

This factor is self-explanatory. Of course, you will want to pay attention to whether any out-of-state cases cited by the state involve the same use of the evidence as is proposed in your case, and whether the cited cases are from states with admissibility analyses similar to Oregon’s.117

===9.“The novelty of the [method].” 118

Generally speaking, novelty is of course a negative in the admissibility analysis. A courtroom is not the appropriate place to test new theories. In addition, novel types of evidence are less likely to bear the other hallmarks of good science—for example, a body of technical literature in which experts in the field have probed the method’s validity, or a set of widely established protocols and safeguards.

Conversely, the fact that a type of evidence has been around for a while does not necessarily mean that it isvalid. Indeed, the absence of validity-supporting elements—for example, a record of other jurisdictions’ admission of the evidence—can be especially striking with respect to a type of evidence that has been around long enough to have gained traction if genuinely valid.119

Even evidence that supposedly has been validated at some previous point is not immune from attack. “[N]o particular reason of logic or good sense exists to immunize particular areas or principles simply on the basis of longevity or the fact that their in- troduction antedated imposition of the new standard. Supposedly valid ‘science’ has not infrequently been unmasked.”120

===10. “The extent to which the [method] relies on the subjective interpretation of the [analyst].” 121

Different types of scientific evidence will fall on a spectrum from the primarily objective to the primarily subjective, based on the nature and degree of independent judgment exercised by the analyst in any particular case. On the far “objective” end would be evidence generated mechanically or electronically by some sort of device that does not rely on any substantive input from the analyst. For example, when a police officer administers a breath test, the subject breathes into the instrument and the instrument puts out a number allegedly representing the subject’s BAC. Absent outright tampering, the officer has little ability to influence the outcome, whether deliberately, subconsciously, or inadvertently. At least theoretically, any officer (or other person) administering the test should obtain the same result.

Other types of evidence do rely to a significant extent on the analyst’s subjective input or assessment. In O’Key, for example, the court acknowledged that “[t]he HGN test is based on subjective visual examination.”122 The determination of, for example, whether a subject’s eyes are moving smoothly or jerkily is made not by a machine or instrument, but by an individual officer. Different officers may have different standards for what “counts” as smooth or jerky; accordingly, different officers may come to different conclusions.123

One obvious problem with subjective methods is the degree to which they allow infiltration of the analyst’s pre-existing views into the analysis. For example, an officer who has already decided that a driver is impaired may be more likely to “see” nystagmus than an officer who believes that driver is sober. In addition, in the courtroom, it can be difficult for an adversary to counter evidence based on a subjective test, as the basis for the witness’s opinion is largely within the witness’s own mind. The Oregon Supreme Court acknowledged this as a problem with HGN evidence, albeit not one sufficient to render the evidence invalid: “The officer who administers the test has no physical sample to take to a laboratory. A defendant is not able to have an expert examine the evidence. Test conditions cannot be duplicated, and the test results can- not be verified. Thus, a defendant cannot contradict much of the officer’s testimony.”124

Accordingly, look for any ways in which the ultimate conclusions from the evidence in your case rely on the subjective interpretations of the state’s expert. Under O’Key, an element of subjectivity by itself may not be sufficient to block the admission of evidence that otherwise bears strong indicia of validity, but in a closer case subjectivity may tip the balance in the direction of exclusion.

===11. “Presence of safeguards in the [method].”125

This factor has not been significantly elaborated upon by Oregon appellate courts. As expressed by the Oregon Supreme Court, it appears to treat the term “safeguards” as referring to elements designed to minimize the potential for deliberate or inadvertent corruption of the evidence, and to maximize the adversary’s ability to assess the evidence and probe its weaknesses.126

12. Other factors

The factors listed above are not exhaustive. In synthesizing prior case law into its admissibility analysis, the court in O’Keynoted that “[b]oth Daubertand Brownreject a ‘bright line test,’ and the multifactor observations they offer suggest a flexible approach to admission or exclusion of scientific evidence.”127 Accordingly, a court considering scientific evidence is not limited to marching in lockstep through the specific factors listed in O’Key. In O’Keyitself, after listing the primary factors from the Browntest (all of which were expressly incorporated into the O’Keyanalysis), the court noted that Brownhad also “mentioned” a series of “somewhat overlapping additional factors to consider.”128 These “additional” factors included, for example, “[a]nalogy to other scientific techniques whose results are admissible,” and “[t]he clarity and simplicity with which the technique can be described and its results explained.”129

These factors continue to be cited after O’Key—which, after all, did not displace Brownbut instead supplemented its analysis—and therefore can still be part of the admissibility analysis in appropriate cases.130

If your analysis of the evidence in your case has revealed flaws, you should be able to fit your argument within one or more of the factors set forth above. And even if you cannot do that, remember that ultimately the analysis of any specific factor serves as a means to the overall end of assessing validity.131

Oregon courts tend to emphasize the nonmechanical nature of the admissibility analysis,132

so you can argue that even a factor not expressly mentioned in Brown, O’Key, or their progeny can legitimately be considered.133

B. Witness Qualifications

Along with establishing the validity of the method in general, the proponent must demonstrate that the particular witness to be called is “qualified as an expert by knowledge, skill, experience, training or education”134 to testify on the pertinent topic.

The qualifications required of a purported expert depend in part on whether the type of testimony at issue has previously been approved by an Oregon appellate court. If it has, the testimony will probably be admitted if the witness can demonstrate some level of expertise in the application of the method. Even in such cases, however, there needs to be something “special” about the witness that the proponent wants to offer as an expert. In State v. Dunning,135 the Court of Appeals reversed a conviction in a case in which a police officer had been allowed to testify “on the subject of memory after traumatic events.”136 Noting that the officer’s supposed qualifications consisted largely of having read a few publications, the court explained: “That is not the stuff of expertise; if it were, any literate person with access to a library or an Internet connection could become an expert in anything over one long weekend. Our standards are higher.”137

When the proponent is attempting to lay the foundation for a type of evidence that has not previously been approved at the appellate level, the qualifications demanded of the expert are higher. This distinction is demonstrated by the cases leading up to O’Key. In State v. Reed,138 the trial court had admitted HGN evidence—which, at the time, had not yet been approved by any Oregon appellate court—based on the foundational testimony of a police officer who had been trained to administer the test and interpret the results. The Court of Appeals held that the officer was not sufficiently qualified to give that foundational testimony, noting that “[a]lthough [the officer] may [have been] qualified as an expert in giving the test, she was not qualified as an expert on the reliability of the relationship, if any, between alcohol consumption and nystagmus.”139

Only years later, when the state finally came forward with experts with substantial expertise in the relevant fields—including a “professor of optometry,” a “research psychologist,” and a “consulting pathologist”—did the Oregon Supreme Court conclude that an adequate foundation had been laid for HGN evidence.140 This distinction between the two levels of required expertise has continued to be applied following O’Key.141

This distinction is important to keep in mind whenever the state has to lay a full O’Keyfoundation. Pay close attention to the qualifications of the witness the state puts forward at the admissibility hearing. Is that witness a true “foundation-level” expert? Is she qualified to explain the scientific principles underlying the test, how it works, what the potential sources of error are, and all of the other factors that the court must consider? Or is she just someone who knows how to do the test—like the officer whose testimony was deemed insufficient in Reed? Remember that what matters is not whether the wit- ness givesfoundation-level testimony—the officer in Reedtried to do so 142 —but whether she is qualifiedto do so.

When arguing that a witness is insufficiently qualified to lay an initial O’Keyfoundation, watch for attempts by the state to rely on superficial “qualifications” that may appear significant but ultimately do not convey foundation-level expertise. For example, perhaps the witness has a degree in some scientific discipline. Did anything in her undergraduate experience relate to the test she is trying to validate? If not, how is she any different than the Reedofficer? Similarly, perhaps your witness is not just a technician but a supervisor or lab manager. If she is in that position because she has done some underlying research in relevant fields, that may make a difference. But if she is there because she learned the test, did it some number of times, and now can teach others to do it, that should not qualify her as a foundation-level expert.143

By comparison, the result in Reedprobably would not have been any different if the officer had been a field sobriety test instructor.

C.Proper Application and OEC 703

Even if the method is generally valid and the witness is properly qualified, the proponent must demonstrate that the method was properly applied in the particular case. The state may argue that defects in the application of an otherwise valid test go to weight, not admissibility. This may be true in some jurisdictions, but Oregon courts have repeatedly held that proper application is part of the admissibility analysis.144 At the same time, aspects of the application that are “remote” from the critical portions may not be deemed relevant to the O’Keyanalysis.145

Issues may arise regarding the nature and quality of the facts and data underlying an expert’s opinion. Whether or not technically part of the “application” portion of the O’Keyanalysis, these issues are ad- dressed here because they often relate to the development of specific expert testimony in a particular case.

OEC 703provides that:

The facts or data in the particular case upon which an expert bases an opinion or inference may be those perceived by or made known to the expert at or before the hearing. If of a type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field in forming opinions or inferences upon the subject, the facts or data need not be admissible in evidence.

As the Oregon Court of Appeals has explained:

The legislative commentary to [this] rule explains that it permits an expert to base an opinion on any of three sources: the expert’s personal observation; the testimony of other witnesses at trial; and . . . “data that have been made known to the expert outside of court and other than by the expert’s own perception.”146

The “type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field” language may provide a basis to challenge proffered expert testimony. That language was intended to “bring judicial practice into line with the practice of experts themselves when not in court.”147 The commentary gives the example of a physician, who “bases a diagnosis on information gathered from a number of sources, including statements by patients and relatives, opinions from nurses, technicians and other doctors, reports, hospital records and X-rays.”148 Because “[t]he physician makes life and death decisions in reliance upon these sources,” that same “validation, expertly performed and subject to cross-examination, ought to suffice for judicial purposes.”149 This is true even if those sources might otherwise be deemed inadmissible— for example, as hearsay.150

This is not, however, a “rubber stamp” allowing experts to give opinions based on any underlying facts on which they claim to have relied, regardless of the nature or quality of those facts. The proponent must demonstrate that the underlying facts or data are in fact “of a type reasonably relied upon by experts in the particular field in forming opinions or inferences upon the subject,” and this is a “preliminary question of fact to be decided by the trial court under OEC 104(1).”151 Where that foundation is not established, the expert may not give an opinion that relies on those facts or data.152

Accordingly, look carefully at any facts or data relied upon by the state’s expert in forming her opinion, and consider arguing that they do not meet the requirements of OEC 703. Watch out for a “bootstrap- ping” approach, in which the state attempts to satisfy the OEC 703 standard by having law enforcement officials testify that theyregularly rely on certain types of facts or data. Such “reliance” should not qualify for purposes of Rule 703’s “reasonably relied upon” standard, which is based on the inherent reliability of evidence regularly relied upon outside ofthe adversarial litigation process.153

Similar skepticism may be warranted where civilian expert witnesses attempt to rely on materials generated by law enforcement personnel.154

Where the otherwise admissible evidence relied upon by the expert is some form of oral or written out-of-court statement, the Oregon and federal Confrontation Clauses may impose additional constraints. As of the date of this publication, neither the United States Supreme Court nor any Oregon appellate court has specifically addressed the question of whether the opinion of a prosecution expert in a criminal case may be based on testimonial statements that would be barred on confrontation grounds if offered independently.155

Accordingly, a confrontation argument may be appropriate if the underlying facts or data relied upon by the state’s expert meet constitutional standards for “testimonial” statements subject to confrontation analysis.156

The question of whether an expert may give an opinion that is basedon otherwise inadmissible evidence is different from the question of whether she can testifyabout that evidence itself. The latter question is addressed in Chapter 5, at Part VI.

V. Relevance and OEC 403

OEC 401(“Definition of ‘relevant evidence’”) and OEC 403(“Exclusion of relevant evidence on grounds of prejudice, confusion or undue delay”) are not, of course, specific to scientific evidence. Both rules have, however, been treated as part of the scientific evidence admissibility analysis,157 and it is worth considering the particular ways in which those rules have been applied in that context.

The relevance threshold under OEC 401 is, of course, a low one. Scientific evidence may nonetheless be excluded on relevance grounds if, for example, the state fails to establish a logical connection between the proffered scientific evidence and the particular case. For example, in State v. Ogden,158 the state had offered the testimony of an expert on “battered women syndrome (BWS)” to explain certain aspects of the complainant’s behavior.159 The expert testified as to common behaviors exhibited by women with BWS, but there was no testimony that the complainant had suffered from that syndrome. The Court of Appeals deemed this a “fail[ure] to establish a critical link between the expert’s testimony about [behaviors consistent with BWS] and why this complainant [behaved in such a manner],” noting that “[i]f complainant does not suffer from BWS, then testimony about that subject seemingly has no bearing on complainant’s behavior.”160

OEC 403 has also been invoked to exclude scientific evidence. Importantly, such evidence has been excluded on OEC 403 grounds even where it had been found to meet the validity standard.161

Accordingly, OEC 403 should generally be argued as a basis for exclusion separate from any validity issues.

The “unfair prejudice” aspect of the OEC 403 analysis is significantly influenced by the universal recognition that jurors tend to give substantial weight to purportedly scientific evidence.162

This can support exclusion, especially if the evidence does not in fact support the conclusions that jurors are likely to draw from it.163

If the evidence submitted in support of the O’Key ''foundation, while legally sufficient to lay that foundation, is nonetheless comparatively weak, that weakness can weigh in favor of exclusion under OEC 403.164

Finally, consider whether the expert’s testimony will truly help jurors figure out something they could not under- stand on their own. The less true this is, the less probative the evidence is and the more the balance is tipped towards exclusion.165

VI. Enforcing the Boundaries

The trial court’s initial determination on admissibility is not the end of the matter. Even if the court deter- mines that some or all of the proffered evidence can come in, you need to watch for testimony that transgresses any boundaries set by the court, or is inadmissible for any other reason. “A general ruling that a certain type of evidence (even scientific evidence) is minimally relevant under OEC 401 and OEC 702does not relieve a party of the obligation to make specific objections to discrete pieces of that evidence at trial, if the dynamics of the trial process reveal other grounds for objection.”166 Be prepared to object (and, where appropriate, ask for a limiting instruction) if, for example, the expert’s testimony strays beyond the scope of her expertise,167 or if it appears that the state is attempting to use the testimony for an improper or unapproved purpose.168

Similarly, be prepared to object if the expert’s “basis for opinion” testimony crosses the line into improper presentation of hearsay, testimonial statements violative of the Oregon or federal Confrontation Clause, or other inadmissible evidence.169

Finally, consider seeking to preclude the use of the word “expert” by the prosecutor, the witness, or the judge, as that word may have an improper bolstering effect.170
