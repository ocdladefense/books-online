Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys

2012 edition

Other Admissibility Issues

The previous chapter focused on the specific admissibility analysis applicable to scientific evidence. Evidence that satisfies that standard may nonetheless be inadmissible under more generally applicable rules of exclusion. In addition, issues relating to scientific evidence may provide grounds to exclude other, nonscientific evidence. Accordingly, this chapter focuses on principles, separate from those set forth in the O’Keyanalysis, that may support exclusion of scientific or other evidence.

I. Search and Seizure

Of course, whenever any portion of the physical, documentary, or other evidence underlying any expert testimony was obtained illegally or was the fruit of such illegality, suppression on traditional constitutional grounds may be available. If you have a suppression issue in your case, look closely to see if any anticipated expert testimony may rely, even implicitly or indirectly, on illegally obtained evidence. Especially if you can establish that the state’s witness was or could have been exposed to the tainted evidence at some point, you may have an argument that her testimony constitutes fruit of the illegality, even if she claims to have ignored the tainted evidence in arriving at her opinion.

In addition, just as constitutional violations can be used to exclude “downstream” scientific evidence, defects in “upstream” scientific evidence may provide grounds for suppression. This may occur where scientific evidence is relied upon to support probable cause for a search. The O’Keytest may not apply to the use of scientific evidence in that context—that test is an application of the evidence rules which do not apply in probable cause determinations. Regardless, scientific evidence must pass some threshold of reliability even in the probable cause context.1

Similarly, where the conclusion allegedly supported by the proffered scientific evidence is less clear than the state contends—for example, because of flaws in application, or inherent uncertainty in the method used—this may support suppression. The probable cause inquiry must include both those facts that support probable cause and those that tend to negate it.2

Accordingly, it may be helpful to demonstrate any gaps or uncertainty in the scientific evidence portion of the state’s purported probable cause showing. In the case of a warranted search, the affiant’s failure to include such limiting language may support a motion to controvert.3

II. Loss or Destruction of Evidence

Another issue that arises with some frequency in cases involving scientific evidence relates to the loss or destruction of evidence. This implicates “what might loosely be called the area of constitutionally guaranteed access to evidence,”4 and may provide a basis for suppression or even dismissal.

Courts have described the constitutional roots of this doctrine in various ways. The United States Supreme Court has generally treated it as part of the “fundamental fairness” guarantee protected by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.5

The Oregon Court of Appeals has indicated that the compulsory process clauses of the Oregon and United States Constitutions are also implicated, although it has treated the analysis under those clauses as being “identical” to the due process analysis.6

The analysis for loss/destruction cases is set forth in State v. Zinsli,7 which synthesizes United States Supreme Court precedent and also, as noted above, adds the Oregon Compulsory Process Clause into the analysis.8

Under Zinsli, the loss or destruction of “constitutionally material” evidence is a constitutional violation if a defendant can “make some showing” that either: (1) “the state acted in bad faith in failing to preserve the evidence”, or(2) “the evidence sought to be discovered [would have been] favorable,” and that “comparable evidence cannot be obtained ‘by other reasonably available means.’”9

The “bad faith” standard is extremely difficult to meet. There are very few cases finding constitutional violations under this standard.10

Among other things, this standard has been construed to require, at a minimum, that the exculpatory value of the evidence was apparent to the government before its loss or destruction.11

Other courts have also interpreted the test as requiring that the defendant be unable to obtain comparable evidence by other reasonably available means.12

The Oregon Court of Appeals, however, has indicated that this requirement does not apply in bad faith cases.13

The alternative showing—that the evidence would have been favorable—is generally an easier one to make. In particular, although “the defendant must show that the claim of favorableness is genuine, not speculation,” this requires only a showing of a “reasonable likelihood” that the missing evidence would have been favorable and material.14 Accordingly, under the second prong of the Zinsli ''analysis, there is a constitutional violation whenever the government has lost or destroyed evidence, there is a reasonable likelihood that that evidence would have been favorable and material to the defendant, and the defendant cannot reasonably obtain comparable evidence. The good faith or bad faith of government agents in losing or destroying the evidence is irrelevant under this second-prong test.

If a violation is established, the court has several remedies from which to choose. In appropriate cases, such a violation supports dismissal of the indictment.15

If the court considers this too extreme, suppression is another potential remedy.16

There may also be additional, case-specific remedies. For example, in State v. Lance,17 the defendant argued that the state’s negligent destruction of brake parts prevented him from conducting tests that may have shown that his brakes failed at the time of the accident underlying his manslaughter charges. The Court of Appeals held that the appropriate remedy was, in addition to exclusion of the testimony of the state’s expert (who had testified that the brakes had been working properly), a stipulation on retrial that the brakes were not functioning at the time of the collision.18

Although motions based on lost or destroyed evidence usually involve evidence that would have been favorable to the defense at trial, similar constitutional issues may arise where the evidence could have been used to challenge statements in a search warrant affidavit.19

III. Chain of custody/preservation/change in condition

The proponent of physical evidence must establish that it is what it purports to be and that its condition has not substantially changed since the relevant time. These issues are frequently litigated under the label “chain of custody.”

With respect to evidence subjected to some type of forensic analysis, “ordinarily when an object is taken from the defendant [or, presumably, obtained through other means] for the purpose of testing and passes through the hands of several persons, a foundation for its admission into evidence must be laid by the testimony of each person who had possession or custody of the article.”20

This, however, is “not an inexorable rule of law,” and the “ultimate question” is “whether the proof shows with reasonable certainty that the article offered is the identical article taken from the accused,”21 and, as discussed below, whether there has been any substantial change in condition.

The standard, which is essentially the same regardless of whether the case is civil or criminal,22 has been described as follows:

The exactness of a proponent’s accounting for the custody of exhibits must, necessarily, rest in the sound discretion of the trial judge. If the exhibits are of a questionable type, or if the environment from whence they come suggests reasons that would cause the court to have more than a mere captious doubt about the authenticity of the exhibits, or about their identity, or about changes in their condition, then the trial court may very well require a proponent to lay a substantial foundation for the receipt of the evidence.23

Perhaps not surprisingly in light of this flexible standard, Oregon appellate courts have not been especially hospitable to criminal defendants’ chain-of-custody arguments.24

Nonetheless, a significant gap can support exclusion.25

Defense attorneys may have more success focusing on actual or possible changes in the conditionof the tested evidence, as opposed to attacks on identity or authenticity. “It is elementary that before a material object may be admitted in evidence, it must be shown that there has been no substantial change in condition since the time in issue.”26

Consider the nature of the forensic analysis at issue, and whether it is of a type as to which the passage of time, exposure to the elements or other conditions, or any other events between collection and analysis could affect the validity or probative value of the eventual results.27

In addition to being a basis for exclusion, if the deleterious change in condition occurred before the defense had an opportunity to conduct its own testing, this may support a Zinslior speedy trial argument.

Finally, if the collection, storage, or treatment of the physical evidence violated established standards or protocols applicable to the method at issue, exclusion may be warranted under the O’Keyanalysis. Depending on the nature of the method, such “upstream” events can be critical to the ultimate validity of any testing results.28

As discussed in Chapter 4, under O’Key, defects in the application of an otherwise valid method support exclusion. Accordingly, it may be advisable to cast a chain-of-custody argument both in its traditional form and as a basis for finding that the evidence does not satisfy the standard for scientific evidence.

IV. Discovery Violations

As with other types of evidence, discovery violations—for example, a lack of disclosure, or untimely disclosure, of witnesses or witness statements—can be grounds for excluding scientific evidence.29

Carefully consider all materials that could be discoverable in your case. For example, the discovery statutes require disclosure of all “reports or statements of experts, made in connection with the particular case, including results of physical or mental examinations and of scientific tests, experiments or comparisons which the district attorney intends to offer in evidence at the trial.”30 In addition, the state’s expert is a “witness”; she, along with her “relevant written or recorded statements or memoranda of [her] oral statements,” must be disclosed.31 Physical evidence underlying expert testimony may constitute “tangible objects . . . [w]hich the district attorney intends to offer in evidence at the trial; or . . . [w]hich were obtained from or belong to the defendant.”32 If any such evidence was obtained via seizure, ORS 135.825will be implicated.

The state’s discovery obligations are not limited to that which is in the prosecutor’s physical possession. Instead, “the prosecutor is [also] responsible for evidence in the possession of the police,” as well as “information that the prosecutor may obtain directly.”33

This may be especially significant if the state’s expert is an employee of the Oregon State Police or another government agency. For purposes of the state’s statutory discovery obligations, “relevant” statements of a witness are “statements pertaining to testimony that the district attorney intends to elicit from a witness at trial”34; this category has also been described as statements that “can be used for purposes of impeachment.”35

There are many types of “statements” that could potentially be used to impeach a prosecution expert. For example, the expert may have made previous statements setting forth her views regarding limitations in the method, possible alternative explanations for apparent results, or other matters that might be useful in impeaching her testimony. These statements may be embodied in, for example, notes, correspondence, or prior testimony. Under the statutes and case law discussed above, all of those statements should be discoverable, regardless of whether the prosecutor has previously attempted to review or gather them.

Although infrequently granted, exclusion of related evidence is an expressly available remedy for the state’s failure to comply with its discovery obligations.36 The “presence or absence of prejudice” to the defense from the violation is a “most important factor to be considered by a trial court” considering the exclusion remedy, but an “absence of prejudice” does not preclude that remedy.37

Prejudice may be established where, for example, late disclosure of an expert hinders the defense attorney’s ability to locate and retain a corresponding defense expert.38

Although a continuance is frequently considered as an alternative remedy, this may not be a sufficient remedy even under the discovery statutes,39 without considering the additional statutory and constitutional issues that arise when the state’s violation results in the delay.40

Separate and apart from the discovery statutes, the Oregon and United States Constitutions require the government to disclose to the defense “Bradymaterial,” i.e., information and material that is favorable to the accused and material either to guilt or to punishment.41

This obligation imposes an affirmative duty on the part of prosecutors to collect and assess such material in the possession of government agencies outside of their offices.42

With respect to timing, Bradymaterials must “be disclosed by the prosecution at such time as will allow the defendant to use [them] effectively in his own defense.”43

As with statutory discovery violations, nondisclosure or late disclosure of Bradymaterials can be grounds for the exclusion of related evidence, among other available remedies.44

Unless tactical considerations weigh in favor of secrecy, it may be advisable to make specific requests, well in advance of trial (or any pretrial hearing to which the materials may be pertinent), for the types of materials discussed in this section. Even if such requests do not succeed in getting you the materials you need in a timely manner, a record of your persistent requests coupled with state inaction may support a more severe remedy than might otherwise be imposed for any ultimate discovery violations.45

V. Other Evidentiary Bases for Exclusion

Consider whether the scientific evidence in your case violates some evidentiary rule other than the specific rules applicable to expert testimony. The following are examples of grounds, separate from the O’Keystandard, on which courts have excluded scientific evidence:

• Vouching. “[O]ne witness may not give an opinion on whether he or she believes another witness is telling the truth.”46 This rule is not limited to direct statements of belief or disbelief. “[S]tatements that fall short of such overt vouching also may be impermissible,” and this can encompass certain types of purported expert testimony—for example, testimony in child abuse cases regarding whether alleged victims’ statements have characteristics consistent with truthfulness.47 Accordingly, if the proffered testimony can be characterized as commentary on any other witness’s credibility, it may be subject to exclusion as improper vouching.48

• Usurping the jury’s function. On a similar note, the Oregon Supreme Court has expressed concerns regarding scientific evidence that may effectively usurp the jury’s role by purporting to answer definitively the ultimate question of guilt or innocence.49

This was one of the court’s concerns in State v. Brown,50 in which it held that polygraph evidence was inadmissible under OEC 403even though such evidence could satisfy the scientific evidence standard in at least some contexts.51 According to the court, polygraph evidence is subject to being overvalued by jurors, and “may well divert the trier of fact from the direct and circumstantial evidence presented in a case to a distorted valuation of the polygraph evidence.”52

• Specific requirements for certain types of evidence. If the substantive law requires that an analysis be done in a certain way, evidence generated in a different way may be inadmissible even if scientifically valid. For example, in an Oregon DUII case, the state may not use HGN evidence to prove that a defendant’s blood-alcohol content (“BAC”) is greater than 0.08 percent, because the pertinent statute specifically requires that BAC be established by “chemical analysis of breath or blood.”53

There may be other grounds for the exclusion of even those types of scientific evidence that generally satisfy the O’Keystandard. The point to keep in mind is that the O’Keyanalysis sets forth a rule of exclusion, not inclusion. In other words, satisfaction of the general scientific evidence standard does not mean that the evidence is admissible. It means only that it cannot be excluded on the basis that it fails theO’Key test. The evidence remains subject to all other potential grounds of exclusion.

VI. OEC 703, hearsay, and confrontation

Most of the foregoing discussion relates to the admissibility of expert opinion testimony itself. A separate question arises with respect to the underlying “facts or data” relied upon by the expert in reaching her opinion.

As discussed in Chapter 4, OEC 703provides that an expert may base an “opinion or inference” on “facts or data” that are not themselves “admissible in evidence,” as long as those “facts or data” are “of a type reason- ably relied upon by experts in the particular field in forming opinions or inferences.” By its terms, this rule does not necessarily mean that the expert may testifyabout otherwise inadmissible “facts or data”—only that she may give her opinion notwithstanding the fact that it is basedon such “facts or data.”

Some early cases highlighted this distinction and refused to allow Rule 703 to be used as a vehicle for the presentation to jurors of otherwise inadmissible evidence.54

These cases may need to be reevaluated, however, in light of McCathern v. Toyota Motor Corp.55 InMcCathern'', a product liability case involving an allegedly defective vehicle, the plaintiff called an expert accident reconstructionist whom she had retained to review prior accidents involving the model at issue “to determine whether those accidents were ‘substantially similar’ to plaintiff ’s accident.”56 “The majority of [the expert’s] testimony was based on information that he had reviewed from lawsuits or investigation reports involving those incidents, including police reports, police photographs, and witness depositions.”57 The defendant argued that this factual information was inadmissible hearsay, but the trial court overruled this objection and allowed the expert to “give a brief summary of each accident for the jury and then to offer his opinion” based on what he had reviewed.58

On review, the Oregon Supreme Court held that the admission of the underlying factual testimony was not erroneous. The court acknowledged that OEC 703“does not render otherwise inadmissible evidence admissible merely because it was the basis for the expert’s opinion.”59 It concluded, however, that the plaintiff had “offered the evidence of the details surrounding the [other accidents] only to provide the foundation necessary to explain [the expert’s] opinions, not for its truth,” and that the trial court had “admitted the evidence solely for that purpose.”60 “Consequently, by definition, that information was not ‘hearsay.’”61

Under McCathern, therefore, it appears that evidence may be presented to jurors, even if otherwise inadmissible, to explain the basis for an expert’s opinion testimony. Where evidence is admitted for this purpose, a limiting instruction may be appropriate.

Notwithstanding McCathern, there will likely be situations in which evidence will be deemed inadmissible even if relied upon by the expert and offered to explain the basis of the expert’s opinion. If the otherwise inadmissible evidence is highly prejudicial compared to its probative value—which, again, should be considered solelywith respect to its value in explaining the expert’s opinion—the evidence should be excluded, either under OEC 403or through a court’s exercise of discretion within OEC 703.62

For example, in State v. Knepper,63 a blood test had been deemed inadmissible for purposes of the defendant’s DUII trial, but the state’s expert was allowed to tell the jury about the test anyway, purportedly under OEC 703 as part of the basis for his opinion that the defendant was under the influence of intoxicants. The Court of Appeals reversed, stating that “[i]f OEC 703 were construed to permit an expert both to rely on a blood test subject to exclusion andto reveal the results of the test to the jury, then it indeed would be a ‘drastic’ change in the law.”64

Knepperdoes not cite OEC 403, and contains some language that is arguably inconsistent with the later McCathernopinion.65 Elsewhere, however, the opinion appears to anticipate McCathern’s rule of limited admissibility—while at the same time recognizing that under such a rule there will be circumstances in which even such limited admissibility would be improper: “The disclosure of the basis of the expert’s opinion may not be substantive evidence and may be only a means to evaluate the soundness of the opinion, but a precautionary instruction to this effect may not be sufficient to prevent the use of the information for substantive purposes.”66 Accordingly, Kneppermay still support the argument that certain evidence should be withheld from the jury even if the expert purports to have relied on it.

The reference in OEC 703 to “facts or data in the particular case” arguably implies that the rule should be limited to case-specific sources—for example, specific medical data such as that discussed in the commentary—and does not extend to more general material such as previous research or technical literature. Oregon courts, however, appear to have extended the rule’s reach to the latter in at least some circumstances.67

Constitutional issues may be implicated when testimonial statements that would otherwise be excluded under the Oregon or federal Confrontation Clause are admitted to explain the basis of an expert’s testimony. As of the date of this publication, neither the United States Supreme Court nor any Oregon appellate court has definitively addressed this issue.68

Accordingly, under appropriate circumstances it may be appropriate to raise confrontation challenges to such evidence. Case-specific assertions will typically be the most likely to constitute “testimonial” evidence for confrontation purposes.69

Conceivably, however, even general “background” information—for example, descriptions of underlying research studies—might be “testimonial.” For example, if the studies were designed and conducted by law enforcement personnel to support the eventual introduction of forensic evidence in criminal prosecutions, the researchers’ statements describing their work may have been “made under circumstances which would lead an objective witness reasonably to believe that the statement[s] would be available for use at a later trial.”70

Sometimes, of course, it is the cross-examiner who wants to expose the basis of the expert’s opinion. OEC705gives the proponent the option of having the expert “testify in terms of opinion or inference and give reasons therefore without prior disclosure of the underlying facts or data,” but “[t]he expert may in any event be required to disclose the underlying facts or data on cross-examination.”71
