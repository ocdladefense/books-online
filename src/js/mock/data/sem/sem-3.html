Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys

2012 edition

Procedural Issues

Chapter 4 explains the standard under Oregon law for the admissibility of scientific evidence. This chapter discusses procedural issues associated with the admissibility determination, along with some strategic considerations. It is written from the perspective of a defense attorney challenging the state’s evidence, but the discussion is also relevant to defense attorneys seeking to admit their own scientific evidence.

I. Challenging the Evidence

A. Right to Challenge

When the state seeks to admit any form of scientific evidence, you have a right to insist that a foundation first be laid. You do not have the burden of identifying some flaw in the evidence; the burden is on the proponent.1

This is true even for types of evidence that have previously been held admissible by Oregon appellate courts (although, as discussed in Chapter 4, the foundational requirement in that situation is less rigorous). Note that the proponent’s burden is not merely to present evidence sufficientfor a finding of validity, but to persuade the court by a preponderance of the evidence that the evidence isvalid.2

Although certain tactical decisions depend on the circumstances, you have much to gain and little to lose by challenging the state’s scientific evidence at some point. In addition, if there is a dispute over whether some portion of the state’s evidence requires the laying of a scientific evidence foundation, it is generally in your interests to argue that it does. If the trial court determines that it does not and admits it without the foundational inquiry, and an appellate court concludes that this determination was wrong, that is grounds for reversal—even if the evidence, as presented at trial, would have satisfied the necessary foundational requirement.3

B. Timing of Challenge

You have two basic options with respect to timing—you can file a pretrial motion to exclude the evidence, or wait until the state attempts to introduce the evidence at trial. Both strategies have advantages and disadvantages.

If you want the admissibility determination made at a pretrial hearing, you should have that right. In State v. O’Key,4 the leading Oregon case on scientific evidence admissibility, the court expressed a preference for pretrial determinations.5

In addition, ORS 135.037gives you the right to a pretrial omnibus hearing to determine issues including “[s]uppression of evidence.” “Suppression” has been interpreted quite broadly for purposes of another statute (ORS 138.060, governing the state’s right to take interlocutory appeals); at least in that context, it is not limited to constitutionally based suppression motions.6

You may want to include a separate motion for an omnibus hearing along with your substantive motion.

The advantages of a pretrial determination of admissibility include:

• Predictability and enhanced ability to prepare.A pretrial determination allows you to prepare for trial knowing whether the state’s scientific evidence, which may be critical evidence, is coming in. Of course, the same is true for the state, so this factor cuts both ways.

• Better decision making environment for the court.Again, the admissibility determination can be a highly complex one. It may involve extensive, intricate testimony on complicated topics and lengthy documentary exhibits. Accordingly, it may be a difficult determination for a trial judge to make in a well- reasoned manner “on the fly.” If you have good arguments for admissibility that you want the court to consider carefully, a pretrial hearing may be the best option. 7

• No references to inadmissible evidence.If you wait until the attempted introduction of the evidence to mount your challenge, the jury may hear about evidence that is ultimately deemed inadmissible. This exposure could occur during, for example, voir dire or opening statements. You may conclude that this potential tainting requires a pretrial determination.8

The disadvantages to a pretrial motion include:

• Appealability of a favorable decision.If you win a pretrial motion to exclude scientific evidence, the state has a right to appeal that decision.9

By contrast, the state cannot appeal a mid-trial ruling. This is obviously a key consideration.10

Indeed, for this reason the state itself may ask for a pretrial hearing on the admissibility of its evidence, especially if you have alerted the prosecutor to the fact that you intend to challenge it.

• Enhanced state preparation.The prosecutor may not anticipate a serious admissibility challenge, or may not fully think through all of the testimonial and documentary evidence that could be required to lay the necessary foundation. Putting the state on notice through a pretrial motion makes it more likely that the prosecutor will show up prepared.

Ultimately, there is no universally applicable rule. The decision whether to challenge the state’s evidence before or during trial is a case-by-case decision.

If you decide to make a mid-trial challenge, you should have a right to insist that the admissibility hearing, including the presentation of evidence in support of and in opposition to admissibility, be held outside the jury’s presence.11

Generally, this is what you want. The admissibility inquiry will often be a searching one. You want the freedom to ask probing questions that will build your record and set up your argument for exclusion, without having to worry about any effect on the jury. In addition, the rules of evidence do not apply at an admissibility hearing, and some of the otherwise inadmissible evidence that will be offered by the state in support of admissibility could improperly bolster the expert’s testimony in the jury’s eyes.12

If you do file a pretrial motion and some or all of the state’s evidence is determined to be admissible, you may need to make additional objections during trial for preservation purposes.13

II. Framing the Pretrial Motion

A. Substantive Issues

Substantively, the first step is identifying the evidence that you want to exclude. The interplay between substance and procedure can be somewhat tricky here.

Before trial, you may not know exactly what scientific evidence the state intends to use. The state is obliged to disclose all expert reports and related materials, as well as all statements (or memoranda of statements) of witnesses, including expert witnesses.14

Not all expert testimony, however, will be associated with discoverable reports or statements, and you may not be able to predict the substance of the expert’s testimony from the discovery materials.15

Moreover, the admissibility of scientific evidence is not considered in the abstract, but in light of the purpose for which the proponent intends to offer it.16

The discovery statutes do not, by themselves, require the state to identify the purpose for which any type of evidence will be offered.

Accordingly, you need to think carefully before identifying the evidence that you want to exclude. One option is to move for the exclusion of all scientific evidence as to which the state fails to lay a sufficient foundation at the hearing. If you take this route, you should probably also identify the specific categories of evidence that you know about, as broadly as possible. This will make it difficult for the state to claim, at trial, that it did not realize that a certain category of evidence needed to be screened at the pretrial hearing and that it should have an additional opportunity to lay a foundation during the trial.17

With respect to the purpose for which the evidence is offered, you can draft your motion broadly enough to seek preclusion of the evidence for any purpose, and then deal with any specifically identified purposes at the hearing. You may want to ask the court to require the prosecutor to identify one or more purposes for the evidence at some point, whether or not such identification is required by the discovery statutes. As discussed above, the purpose for which the evidence is offered is relevant to the admissibility analysis, and without one or more identified purposes the court cannot perform its gatekeeping function.

With respect to your supporting brief (or “points and authorities”), you need to balance the competing concerns common to many pretrial motions—the benefit of identifying specific issues to focus the court’s attention, weighed against the risk of offering the prosecutor a roadmap outlining all of the evidence necessary to defeat your motion. Again, this is a case-by-case decision. One thing to be careful of—if you do go with a more minimalist approach, you need to ensure that at some point you specifically identify all of the factors within the admissibility analysis that you want the court to consider. Otherwise, you may be deemed to have waived any argument with respect to any factors not so identified.18

B. Procedural Issues

The issued raised in a scientific evidence admissibility hearing may be more complex, and may involve more extensive and technical evidence, than those involved in the more common pretrial motions. In light of the nature and amount of evidence related to admissibility, it may be unrealistic to expect that you will be able to process and analyze the relevant materials as quickly as you can in other contexts. With such materials, you may need time not only to review them yourself, but also to discuss them with a consulting expert.19

Accordingly, if new evidence or discovery is disclosed for the first time at or shortly before the admissibility hearing, you may find it appropriate to ask for a continuance—or, in the alternative, for exclusion of some portion of the state’s foundational evidence as a remedy for the untimely disclosure. To make a record for either type of request, you may want to outline, in your moving papers, the specific reasons making timely production important and a proposed schedule. If you are seeking some materials through subpoenas, this can be worked out in your proposed schedule.20

Even if the court declines to adopt your proposal, the record will show that the state is responsible for any delay if its untimely disclosure necessitates a continuance.

III. The Hearing

A. Evidence

The rules of evidence, other than those involving privileges, do not apply at an admissibility hearing.21

This has several implications, particularly with respect to documentary evidence. Especially if the method or technique at issue has not previously been approved by any Oregon appellate court, much of the evidence at the hearing may consist of documents such as reference books, technical articles, and laboratory reports.22

The hearsay rule does not apply at this stage (although it does apply if, after the admissibility determination, the proponent attempts to introduce such materials as trial evidence).

Oregon law provides little guidance regarding the standards governing admissibility at hearings not governed by the evidence rules. Documentary and other evidence should be allowed in if there is some plausible way that it could assist the court in the admissibility determination. With respect to authentication, no set procedure has been established. A representation of counsel—for example, that you are submitting true and accurate copies of identified materials—may or may not suffice. To be certain, absent a prior stipulation you may want to bring authenticating affidavits to the hearing.

The trial court is also entitled to take judicial notice of facts or materials that are relevant to the admissibility determination. This is considered judicial notice of “legislative facts,” and the standard is more relaxed than the standard applicable to judicial notice of “adjudicative facts.”23

Be prepared to take full advantage of these generally lenient rules for the admissibility of documents. For scientific evidence in particular, documentary evidence will often be more persuasive and authoritative than testimony. Even the materials that generally support the method at issue—for example, standardized protocols or research articles—may include statements about limitations or uncertainties. Your research may also turn up articles or other publications critical of the method, or materials that call into question the method’s usefulness in the context of your case. If so, consider getting these materials into the record.

Be careful, though, that you do not unwittingly fill in gaps in the state’s foundation. It bears repeating that your prosecutor may not have fully prepared for the hearing or anticipated all of the issues that you will raise. If you have a good argument that the state’s foundational evidence is insufficient and if your proposed exhibits include material that could cure that insufficiency, it may be best to leave well enough alone. You may also consider the intermediate route of asking the state’s expert, on cross-examination, to agree with certain statements in your materials. The expert may do so, in which case you will have established your points without bolstering the state’s case in the process.

B. The State’s Expert

When planning your cross-examination of the state’s expert, consider the same issue discussed above with respect to documents—the possibility that your cross-examination will end up filling in any gaps in the state’s foundation. Both at trial and in any appeal, the state should be limited to the foundational record it establishes at the admissibility hearing. Accordingly, before cross-examining the state’s expert at length think carefully about the record as it stands and whether a probing cross-examination is more likely to make the overall record better or worse for the state.

If you decide to cross-examine the state’s expert, it is often useful to probe beneath the surface of the witness’s direct examination testimony, which is often quite conclusory. Chapter 4 discusses the factors the proponent must address in a hearing on scientific evidence admissibility; these include, for exam- ple, the degree to which the method is accepted in the relevant scientific community and the method’s error rate.24

The prosecutor may attempt to develop this evidence through a series of perfunctory yes/ no questions: “Is this method generally accepted in the scientific community?” “Yes.” “Is the error rate considered acceptable?” “Yes.”

If you decide that the benefits of a thorough cross outweigh the risks, this is the sort of testimony on which you should focus. For example, as to the “acceptance in the community” question: what is the witness’s basis for stating that the method is generally accepted in the relevant scientific community? What sorts of contacts does that witness have in the scientific community (particularly outside the laboratory in which the witness works)? Have they discussed this method? When? What was discussed? Has the witness been to conferences at which the validity or usefulness of this method was discussed? Read publications? Which ones? What did they say? What is the citation information (author, journal title, etc.) for these references? The answers to such questions can be especially revealing when the purported expert is in reality essentially a technician, trained in the application of a method but not an authority on the underlying science.

You should also be allowed to ask questions that go to the witness’s credibility, including any motivations that may affect the witness’s testimony, or personal or professional biases in favor of the state or against your client or similarly situated persons. As discussed above, the admissibility determination is not governed by a “sufficiency of the evidence” standard; instead, the court must decide whether validity has been established by a preponderance of the evidence.25 Accordingly, credibility questions are entirely proper.

Because the evidence rules do not apply, the state is not obliged to present supporting testimony through live witnesses; it may submit such testimony through affidavits.26

If it is important to you to examine any witness, you should subpoena that witness to the admissibility hearing.

C. Your Own Expert

The defense, of course, has the right to call its own expert at the admissibility hearing. Think carefully, however, before doing so. The state will have an opportunity to conduct the same searching, comprehensive cross-examination of your expert that you will be conducting of the state’s. This pretrial opportunity can be a significant tactical advantage, one that should not be lightly handed over to the state.

This is especially true in light of the particular function of the admissibility hearing. The court, in its “gatekeeping” function, is not charged with determining which side’s scientific evidence is “better,” but only whether either side’s evidence is sufficiently valid and legitimate to satisfy the admissibility standard.27

A court facing a “battle of the experts” at the admissibility stage may simply let all of the evidence in and let the jury sort it out, which may indeed be the correct approach under the governing standard.28

The result is that you will have given the state a shot at your expert without gaining much in the process.

Of course, there are circumstances in which this basic calculus will shift. For example, your expert may be in a position to testify about virtually indisputable scientific principles, ignored or misapplied by the state’s expert, that seriously undermine the validity of the state’s proffered evidence. Alternatively, if you have disclosed your expert as a trial witness and the state has successfully moved for a pretrial hearing on the admissibility of that expert’s testimony, the state will have an opportunity to examine your expert before trial in any event. In that situation, your expert’s pretrial testimony in support of your challenge to the state’s evidence may come at little additional cost.
