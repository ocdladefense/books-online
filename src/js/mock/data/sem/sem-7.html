Scientific Evidence: A Manual for Oregon Defense Attorneys

2012 edition

The Defense's Scientific Evidence

I. Do you want to offer expert testimony?

Think carefully before deciding to offer your own expert testimony, as that decision carries real risks. If, for example, your theory is that a method or scientific discipline relied upon by the state is generally not worthy of credence, presenting your own witness with a background in that area might be seen as a concession of validity. It may be awkward, after all, for an expert to testify that he has spent a significant portion of his career in a field that does not produce trustworthy conclusions.1

In addition, just as you will often be able to extract valuable admissions from the state’s expert,2 the state may be able to do the same with your expert. For example, in a relatively objective field with extensive published literature, the state will be able to use the “learned treatise” rule (OEC 706) to cross-examine your expert, who may find it difficult to disagree credibly with the statements in that literature. When dealing with such evidence, consider whether the literature you can use for cross-examination might be a sufficient proxy for whatever live expert testimony you might otherwise offer.

II. Laying the Groundwork

From the time you first begin selecting an expert, think not only about the helpful testimony the expert can provide, but also about any negatives that could be used to impeach his testimony or otherwise be used against you at trial. Your assessment should include not only the expert’s qualifications, curriculum vitae, and testimonial style, but also factors such as whether the expert has a record that could be used to demonstrate bias (for example, a history of testifying predominantly for one side or the other); the degree to which the expert has been found qualified, or not qualified, in other cases; and whether his published works or prior testimony include statements that could harm your case.

Additionally, from the beginning of the relationship proceed on the assumption that all written communications between you and the expert, and all reports, drafts, and notes made by the expert, will be subject to dis- closure pursuant to your discovery obligations or a state subpoena. You do not necessarily want to discourage the expert from taking notes or otherwise generating written work product. Especially in complex matters, it can be difficult for an expert to conduct a high-quality analysis without leaving a paper trail, and the absence of any written record may be exploited by the prosecutor in challenging the quality of your expert’s work. Still, the expert should be made aware that his materials will likely end up being perused by the state, and perhaps given some guidance regarding the types of notations that could end up making things uncomfortable for him or for you.

Similarly, keep in mind that experts are commonly asked what materials they reviewed, or what materials were given to them to review, in connection with their analysis or in preparation for their testimony. When deciding, for example, which discovery materials to give to your expert for review, you may want to err on the side of inclusion, even as to materials that you do not believe are relevant or useful to the expert’s task. This will make it more difficult for the state to portray you as having attempted to shape the expert’s conclusions by presenting only materials favorable to the defense.3

At the same time, be careful about providing the expert with any of your work product or other confidential materials. Although contrary arguments can be made, you should assume that any such materials will lose any otherwise applicable privilege status if shared with an expert who ends up testifying.4

III. Defense Analysis of Physical Evidence

If your case involves physical evidence that has been or could be the subject of expert analysis, you may want to have your own expert independently analyze that evidence. Neither Oregon nor federal law unequivocally provides a right to do so, but trial courts may be receptive to requests for such access, and the prosecutor in your case may provide it voluntarily.

As discussed in Chapter 1, Oregon law at present is not hospitable to work product claims in this context. Accordingly, be careful not to turn your expert into a potential prosecution witness. Under State v. Riddle,5 the state may be able to require an expert to testify about work done while engaged by the defense. There are certain precautions you can take to minimize the risk of this happening. First, you can try to obtain the evidence and have it analyzed in a way that will not reveal to the state the identity of your expert. If the prosecutor attempts to force you to reveal that information as a condition of obtaining the evidence, you can emphasize the constitutional significance of a defense attorney’s ability to secure expert assistance 6 and the confidentiality safeguarded by the attorney-client privilege and work product doctrine.7

Second, you can manage communications with your expert in such a way that you will be able to prove, if the need arises, that any testimony the expert could give would be inextricably linked to your confidential communications with the expert.8

Although the foregoing discussion focuses on written materials, oral communications are also a potential concern in multiple respects. Any oral communications between you and your testifying expert, with the possible exception of those irrelevant to the topics on which the expert will testify, will likely be fair topics of inquiry for the state. In addition, no legal or ethical prohibition currently prevents the prosecutor or any other state agent from contacting your expert once you have identified him as a testifying witness; in fact, a Formal Ethics Opinion expressly blesses this practice.9

Although you cannot direct your expert not to talk to the government, it is permissible to remind him that he is under no obligation to do so, and that he should not in any case divulge communications or information that maintain privileged status notwithstanding the anticipated testimony.10

IV. The Admissibility Hearing

If you have an expert who could make a real difference for you at trial, the last thing you want is to have that expert’s testimony deemed inadmissible. Accordingly, do your homework and show up prepared to make a strong case for admissibility. This is essentially the flip side of the analysis in Chapters 3 and 4, which focus on how to exclude testimony proffered by the state.

At the outset, consider whether you can argue that your evidence is not in fact “scientific” evidence subject to the demanding O’Keystandard. As discussed in Chapter 2, the answer to this question is often less than clear, and it can be a real advantage to the proponent to have evidence deemed nonscientific. Where appropriate, consider arguing, for example, that your expert’s testimony will not be perceived by jurors as scientific in nature,11or that your expert will not be using “the vocabulary of scientific research.”12

As you prepare, work through all of the O’Keyfactors and consider what evidence you want to present in favor of each one. Your expert’s testimony will be part of this foundational showing, but there may be other elements as well. Remember that the rules of evidence do not apply at the admissibility hearing, so technical literature and other written materials may be part of your proof.

Arm yourself with as much judicial precedent as possible. If there is Oregon appellate precedent affirming the type of evidence at issue, that will significantly reduce your foundational burden.13

If there is some difference between the specific method used in your case and that which was previously approved, consider citing State v. Herzog,14 in which the Oregon Supreme Court summarily affirmed the admissibility of one type of DNA evidence based on its simultaneous acceptance of a distinct type. Even if the evidence blessed by prior Oregon case law is sufficiently dissimilar that a full foundational showing is required in your case, that precedent may still be useful with respect to one of the “additional” admissibility factors, e.g., “[a]nalogy to other scientific techniques whose results are admissible.”15

Cases from other jurisdictions may also be helpful, as “[t]he extent to which other courts admit” the type of evidence at issue is explicitly part of the O’Keyanalysis.16 Finally, even a record of previous admissibility determinations at the trial court level may be persuasive to the court in your case (especially if the expert can testify that no court has ever excluded his testimony).

Remember that the “flexibility” of the O’Keyanalysis can be exploited by the proponent of evidence as well as by the opponent.17

If there is something about your expert’s evidence that supports a conclusion of validity and admissibility but does not fit neatly into any of the primary admissibility factors, try to fit it into one of the “additional” factors referenced in O’Key. If there is no good fit at that level, remind the court that the factors are simply means to the end of assessing validity, and that anything bearing on that ultimate determination should be considered.18

Where appropriate, remind the court that the focus of the admissibility inquiry “must be solely on principles and methodology, not on the conclusions that they generate.”19

Accordingly, if your expert’s method is valid and was properly applied in your case, the fact that others in the field might disagree with his conclusions should not affect admissibility.20

Consider citing some of the following cases, with language and analysis tending towards leniency in the admissibility determination:

• Jennings v. Baxter Healthcare Corp.21: Doctor’s testimony regarding medical causation should have been admitted even though it was novel and lacked some of the usual hallmarks of validity such as publication, peer review, and a statistically determined rate of error, and doctor could not explain the mechanism underlying the alleged causation.22

• Kennedy v. Eden Advanced Pest Technologies23: Testimony of expert diagnosing plaintiff ’s condition should have been admitted notwithstanding significant dispute within the field concerning the methodology at issue; “[w]hen qualified experts disagree about the validity of medical diagnoses or other scientific evidence, judges are in no better position to resolve that dispute than are juries.”24

•Marcum v. Adventist Health System/West25 Suggesting a more lenient analysis may be appropriate in a context in which, “because of ethical or other considerations, studies and comparisons may not be possible.”26

•State v. Lyons27: Explaining that the United States Supreme Court’s decision in Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals,28which the Oregon Supreme Court found “instructive” in O’Key, “cautions lower courts not to confuse the role of judge and jury by forgetting that [v]igorous cross-examination, presentation of contrary evidence, and careful instruction on the burden of proof,’ rather than exclusion ‘are the traditional and appropriate means of attacking shaky but admissible evidence.’”29

V. Presenting Your Witness

All of the foregoing is, of course, preparatory to the ultimate objective of presenting persuasive expert testimony at trial. One critical point to keep in mind is that it needs to be you, not the expert, who crafts and shapes your presentation. That does not necessarily mean that you will be the dominant figure in the direct examination itself. The best approach may consist of counsel playing a minor role with the expert taking center stage. But the planning—including decisions such as the nature and extent of background or explanatory testimony, which points to highlight, and whether and how to address preemptively any foreseeable attacks by the state—needs to be driven by you.

If your expert is an experienced testifier, he may expect to essentially direct his own presentation. As the lawyer, however, you are the expert in crafting your story and in fitting every bit of evidence effectively and persuasively into your overall presentation. You will know, in a way that your expert may not, the ways in which your case differs from other cases in which the expert has testified. And if you have taken the time to master the principles underlying the proposed testimony, you will be in a position to direct a presentation that effectively accomplishes your objectives. So, while you will always want to listen carefully to all of your expert’s thoughts and suggestions, you are ultimately responsible for deciding how to present the evidence.

If your objective is to help the listeners understand the evidence as clearly as possible, there are certain tactics that may help in that regard. I spent several years as a high school science teacher, and during that time I came to believe that the most effective teaching strategy was often to focus on a logical progression of principles, instead of emphasizing specific facts.30

Just as you should not underestimate your own ability to understand the scientific principles relevant to your case, you should not underestimate a judge’s or a juror’s ability to understand those same principles. Remember, you will not need to replicate in your audience the full breadth of knowledge possessed by a career expert in the field. Most forensic applications will include a relatively small number of principles constituting only a fraction of what an expert will have learned or studied. You may be surprised at how frequently you will be able to learn, and teach, those principles to the extent necessary for your case.

In your role as “science teacher,” make use of the various tools and techniques educators use to make scientific principles understandable. Visual aids and other demonstrative evidence can be invaluable in this process. Teachers, and particularly science teachers, regularly quote the proverb: “What I hear, I forget; what I see, I remember; what I do, I understand.”31 Although you will often be limited to the first two of those options, certain cases may present opportunities for the third.32 Even where it is not feasible to have jurors experience the evidence by “doing,” alternative approaches may serve as acceptable substitutes. For example, you and the expert may be able to build a demonstrative exhibit gradually as the testimony progresses, giving jurors the sense of participating in the progression towards the expert’s ultimate conclusion.
