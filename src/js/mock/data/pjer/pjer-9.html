Post Judgment and Extraordinary Remedies

2012 edition

Judicial Review of Parole Board Decisions

Ryan O'Connor, Irene Taylor, and Pamela Deutsch

I. Introduction

A. General Historical Information

Parole law in Oregon primarily involves constitutional and administrative law principles. DeYoung/Thomas v. Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 332 Or 266, 281–82, 27 P3d 110 (2001)(“[P] etitioners’ status as prisoners or parolees may be the result of a criminal action that is completed, but administrative proceedings respecting that status, and judicial review of those administrative proceedings, are so far removed from the criminal actions that they cannot be said to be ‘connected with’ them.”).

Prior to 1977, the Oregon Board of Parole (Board) operated under procedures called “the Discretionary System.” As indicated by its name, under this system, the Board held considerable discretion when making parole decisions regarding an inmate. SeeSmith v. Board of Parole, 297 Or 184, 683 P2d 990 (1984), and Ritchie v. Board of Parole, 35 Or App 711, 715–16, 583 P2d 1 (1978)(discussing the Board’s discretion).

In 1973, the Board became subject to the rule-making procedures of the Administrative Procedures Act in Chapter 183 of the Oregon Revised Statutes. Or Laws 1971, chap. 734. Although the Board is a state administrative agency, it is not bound by all provisions of the Administrative Procedures Act. SeeORS 183.315(listing those provisions not applicable to the Board).

The Board is not subject to the contested case provisions of Chapter 183. SeeORS 183.315(1) (stating that the Board generally is not subject to certain provisions of ORS Chapter 183). The provisions of the Administrative Procedures Act to which the Board is subject are those involving the promulgation of rules and murder review hearings under ORS 163.115. For a complete copy of both past and present Board rules and exhibits, go to the Board’s website at: http://www.oregon.gov/BOPPPS/adminrules.shtml(last accessed on January 6, 2012).

As an administrative agency, the standards of review applied to judicial review of a Board order are those applied in administrative law cases pursuant to ORS 144.335(3)(“The Court of Appeals may affirm, reverse or remand the order on the same basis as provided in ORS 183.482(8).”). See alsoCalderon-Pacheco v. State Board of Parole, 309 Or 454, 457, 788 P2d 1001 (1990)(applying the standard). However, the length of an offender’s incarceration and supervision terms are affected by the laws and Board rules in effect on the offender’s crime commission date. Therefore, the adoption of new rules or laws that are substantive in nature or infringe upon an offender’s liberties may violate the offender’s constitutional rights against the application of ex post factolaws the same as in criminal cases. SeeChristenson v. Thompson, 143 Or App 483, 484, 923 P 2d 1316 (1996)(concluding that applying a version of ORS 144.125to a prisoner whose offense was committed before the effective date of the statute violated ex post factoprotections). In response to the United States Supreme Court’s decisions in Morrissey v. Brewer, 408 US 471,

92 S Ct 2593, 33 L Ed 2d 484 (1972), and Gagnon v. Scarpelli, 411 US 778, 93 S Ct 1756, 36 LEd 2d 656 (1973), the Board adopted rules for conducting hearings and for appointment of counsel to represent offenders at Board expense. Ritchie, 35 Or App at 715-16(discussing impact of the decisions in Morrisseyand Gagnonon Oregon parole law).

In Morrissey,the Court held that due process under the Fourteenth Amendment required at the minimum, “an informal hearing structured to assure that the finding of a parole violation will be based on verified facts and that the exercise of discretion will be informed by an accurate knowledge of the parolee’s behavior.” 408 US at 484. In Gagnon, the Court held that the Morrisseyhearing requirement also applied to probationers facing potential revocation of their probation. 411 US at 782. The Court further held that the agency conducting the hearing should follow guidelines for exercising its discretion to grant counsel for the probationer or parolee. The Court held that counsel should be provided where “the probationer or parolee makes such a request, based on a timely and colorable claim (1) that he has not committed the alleged violation of the conditions upon which he is at liberty, or (2) that, even if the violation is a matter of public record or is uncontested, there are substantial reasons which justified or mitigated the violation and make revocation inappropriate, and that the reasons are complex or otherwise difficult to develop or present.” Id.at 790.

B. The Matrix System

In an effort to create more uniform sentencing, the 1977 legislature adopted the “matrix” system for the parole Board. Moore v. Board of Parole, 54 Or App 369, 272-73, 635 P2d 3 (1981)(“[W] hile one of the purposes of the act was to safeguard against arbitrary and inconsistent sentences for similar criminal conduct, another legislative concern was to structure rather than eliminate the Parole Board’s discretion.”). The matrix system had a complex formula for determining the length of time each offender would serve. SeeORS 144.780. The Board adopted rules to determine each offender’s sentence based on the severity of the crime or crimes of conviction, the age of the offender, and the criminal history of the offender. SeeHarris v. Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 288 Or 495, 605 P2d 1181 (1980)(addressing the legislation implementing the matrix system as reflecting a plan to eliminate disproportionate sentences), and Anderson v. State Board of Parole, 303 Or 618, 740 P2d 760 (1987)(discussing the application of the matrix system). Under the matrix system, the trial court imposed the maximum sentence an offender was required to serve, but the Board determined the actual prison term for an offender serving a sentence under the matrix system. See Harris,288 Or at 503 (holding that under ORS 144.780 and ORS 144.785 the parole board set the actual duration of punishment within the maximum and minimum sentencing parameters set by the trial court). This system is similar to the current sentencing guidelines in use today, except that the trial court sets the actual duration of punishment for the offender.

For those offenders whose crimes were committed prior to the enactment of ORS 144.245(3), they could choose to refuse parole, and serve out their prison sentence to their statutory good time date. Bollinger v. Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 329 Or 505, 515, 992 P2d 445 (1999). The Board, however, retained authority to impose six months of parolelike supervision without offending ex post factoproscriptions. Day v. Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 184 Or App

409, 413-14, 56 P3d 495 (2002). For offenders whose crime dates were committed before December 4, 1986, and who were released on parole, the Board could review their behavior while on parole, issue a certificate of completion, and allow the sentence to be completed early. Those offenders whose crimes were committed on or after December 4, 1986, and before November 4, 1989, are required to serve a term of parole until the maximum imprisonment time imposed by the trial court. SeeformerORS 144.305, repealed by Or Laws 1993, chap 680, § 7(extending parole for the entire time of an offender’s sentence); see alsoDavidson v. Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 139 Or App 289, 293, 911 P2d 973 (1996)(applying the statute).

C. Oregon Sentencing Guidelines

On November 4, 1989, the Oregon legislature passed the Oregon Sentencing Guidelines. Or Laws 1989, chap 790, § 87. See alsoState v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 486-89, 847 P2d 834 (1993)(summarizing the sentencing guidelines legislation). The Board was renamed the Oregon Board of Parole and Post- Prison Supervision. The guidelines removed the trial court’s discretion to sentence an offender to an indeterminate length of time in prison, and also removed the Board’s authority to determine when an inmate would be released from prison. Under the Sentencing Guidelines, the trial courts are required to impose determinate prison terms and determinate terms of post-prison supervision. OAR 213-005- 0005; OAR 213-005-0002(4). SeeState v. Mitchell, 236 Or App. 248, 252-53, 235 P3d 725 (2010).

In 1991, the legislature passed legislation allowing at least three, but not more than five members on the Board. ORS 144.005(1). The Board currently has three board members.

D. Board Hearings

The Board conducts a variety of types of hearings and takes actions that result in a decision an inmate may wish to appeal. (See Hearings Chart attached as Appendix A). Unfortunately, there are few hearings where an inmate is represented by legal counsel. Therefore, the majority of appeal issues are based on what an inmate is able to “preserve” in his request for administrative review. That is the first step for an inmate in seeking review of a Board decision.

The Board generally issues decisions using a Board Action Form (BAF), an Order of Revocation, an Order of Supervision Conditions, or an Order to Continue/Amend Supervision Conditions. However, these decisions do not need to be in any special form. ORS 144.335(3). If an inmate is dissatisfied with the Board’s decision, he or she may request administrative review of the Board’s order by filing an Exhibit O form. This form provides an outline for raising specific issues in the manner outlined by the Board rule. OAR 255-080-0005. The inmate submits a Request for Administrative Review/Exhibit O. Generally, only issues raised in the administrative review request can later be presented for judicial review in the Court of Appeals. However, issues involving how a hearing is conducted need to be preserved at the hearing itself, in addition to being raised in the request for administrative review. SeeO’Hara v. Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 346 Or41, 46-48, 203 P3d 213 (2009)(holding that petitioner properly preserved his request for the Board to subpoena his witnesses when he requested the presence of witnesses at his hearing and explained the relevance of their testimony).

After receipt of an administrative review request, the Board responds with an Administrative Review Response [ARR]. The Board’s criteria for granting review of a timely administrative review request are set out in OAR 213-080-0010. The response may grant relief, deny relief, deny review, or grant or deny in part either review or relief. If the decision does not grant the inmate the review or the relief sought, and the inmate is adversely affected or aggrieved by the Board’s decision, then judicial review may be sought. ORS 144.335.

II. Appellate Jurisdiction

The Court of Appeals’ authority to review an order of the Board derives from ORS 144.335. A person may seek judicial review of a final order of the Board if “(a) the person is adversely affected or aggrieved by a final order of the Board” and “(b) the person has exhausted administrative review as provided by Board rule.” ORS 144.335(1). 1 {Note:

1 The legislature frequently amends parole statutes, and the Board frequently amends its rules. The authors of this chapter cite to the statutes and rules in effect in October 2011. Practitioners should carefully review the applicable statutes and rules because they may have changed from this writing. Also, practitioners should bear in mind that the state and federal ex post facto clauses require the Board and the courts to apply the statutes and rules in effect on a person’s crime commission date if the application of the current statutes and rules would increase the person’s punishment. See Meadows v. Schiedler, 143 OrApp 213, 218–19, 924 P2d 314 (1996)(explaining ex post facto provisions of state and federal constitutions and applying them to a change in parole statutes).}

A. Adversely Affected or Aggrieved by a Final Order

Generally, when a person timely files for administrative review of a Board order, that order becomes final for the purpose of judicial review after the Board issues an administrative review response (ARR). SeeOAR 255-080-0001(describing requirements). In some circumstances, the Board may expressly or constructively “reopen” a prior order. This decision can adversely affect and aggrieve a person anew, and may constitute a final order subject to judicial review. See Dawson/Fletcher v. Board of Parole andPost-Prison Supervision, 346 Or 643, 656, 217 P3d 1055 (2009)(“If the Board alters a factual finding or legal conclusion in an earlier order, and the change is material to a party’s legal rights under that order, then we would conclude that the Board had reconsidered the order even if the order purports to deny reconsideration.”). However, “a board order denying reopening and reconsideration of an earlier final order is not subject to judicial review.”Dawson'', 346 Or at 649–50. Whether a Board order reopens and reconsiders an earlier order depends on the substance of the Board order, not the title or the tag line. Thus, even if the Board order purports to deny reconsideration, practitioners should carefully review the body of the Board order and relevant case law to determine whether the Board has constructively reopened an earlier order.

In most cases on judicial review of a Board decision, a person is adversely affected or aggrieved when the Board denies the person the relief that he or she seeks in the administrative review proceeding. Richards, 339 Or at 182–83. To determine whether a person is “adversely affected or aggrieved” by a Board order, the Oregon Supreme Court adopted the definition of “adversely affected and aggrieved” from ORS 183.480(1)for purposes of ORS 144.335. The definition applies a three- part test:

(1) the person has suffered an injury to a substantial interest resulting directly from the challenged governmental action; (2) the person seeks to further an interest that the legislature expressly wished to have considered; or (3) the person has such a personal stake in the outcome of the controversy as to assure concrete adverseness to the proceeding. Richards, 339 Or at 182(quoting People for Ethical Treatment v. Inst. Animal Care, 312 Or 95, 101– 02, 817 P2d 1299 (1991)).

B. Exhausting Administrative Remedies

As noted above, a person must exhaust administrative remedies as provided by Board rule to invoke the jurisdiction of the Court of Appeals. ORS 144.335(1)(b). The Board’s rules require a person to follow specific procedures to exhaust administrative remedies. OAR 255-080-0001(2)(“An inmate/ offender has exhausted his or her administrative remedies after complying with OAR 255-080-0005and after the Board denies review, or grants review and either denies or grants relief.”) The procedures for administrative review are set out in OAR 255-080-0005.

All cases begin when the Board engages in some action or refuses to engage in an action, typically memorialized in a Board Action Form (BAF), an order of revocation of parole or PPS, or an order of supervision conditions. An aggrieved person must then challenge the Board action through an administrative review request received bythe Board within 45 days of the date the BAF was mailed to the person or mailed by the 45th day so long as the envelope bears evidence of the mailing date. OAR 255-080-0005(2). For offenders in custody, the Board will consider a request timely if an inmate whose request for administrative review does not bear a legible “postal mailing verification as defined in” OAR 255-080-0005(2)(b) signs and dates the request and places “it in the institutional mailing system, following all applicable Department of Corrections rules, on or before the 45th day after the mailing date on the board’s final action.” OAR 255-080-0005(2)(c). A separate subsection, OAR 255- 080-0005(3), governs the timeliness of a request for administrative review of an Order of Supervision, but the rules still require a person to request administrative review within 45 days of receiving the Board’s order. OAR 255-080-0005(3)(a).

The receipt rule has been the subject of litigation, and the Oregon Court of Appeals has generally upheld it. Woodroffe v. Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 219 Or App 87, 182 P3d 202(2008). But it is possible to successfully challenge the receipt rule in a particular case if, for example, the person’s receipt of the order was delayed or the institutional mail system delayed in sending the inmate’s mail. In extraordinary circumstances outside of an inmate’s control, due process may require the Board to excuse the late filing of the request for administrative review. Id.at 96–98.

The Board created an exhibit to its rule for submitting the administrative review request, the “Exhibit O, Administrative Review Request Form.” The Oregon Department of Corrections provides copies of the Exhibit O to inmates in Oregon prisons. 2 {Note: 2 The Exhibit O form is also available on the board’s website. http://www.oregon.gov/BOPPPS/exhibits.shtml(last accessed on January 6, 2012).}

The Board’s rules require a request for administrative review to be “substantially in form” of the Exhibit O. OAR 255-080-0008(1). For example, the request must set forth particular challenges to the Board action set forth in OAR 255- 080-0010, including citations to pertinent authority. OAR 255-080-0008(1)(d)-(e). Practitioners should review the Board’s rules for all the requirements of request for administrative review. A failure to “preserve” a challenge in an administrative review request can deprive the Court of Appeals of jurisdiction, particularly if the only issue raised on judicial review was not exhausted in the administrative review process.

To properly exhaust state administrative remedies, and to preserve state and federal constitutional issues for judicial and federal habeas review, constitutional issues must also be raised before the Board on administrative review.

The administrative review request may not exceed eight pages. OAR 255-080-0008(3)(a). Rarely, the Board has granted permission to submit requests exceeding eight pages in some cases. A person should submit a written request to the Board for an extended administrative review request well in advance of the 45th day after the order’s mailing date and explain why he or she needs to file an over-length administrative review request.

In response to the administrative review request, the Board may hold a hearing, may withdraw and amend its order, or may grant or deny the person relief. OAR 255-080-0012. Typically, the Board responds in an Administrative Review Response (ARR). The ARR is a brief written opinion by the Board explaining its actions. The ARR marks the end of the administrative review process.

In some cases, the Board has taken 12 months or longer to issue an ARR in response to a person’s request for administrative review. During 2010, several people successfully petitioned the Court of Appeals for judicial review by explaining that the Board had not issued the ARR after 12 months or more had passed since the person submitted the request for administrative review. A person filing a petition for judicial review in such circumstances should set out the pertinent dates in the petition. Pertinent dates include the date the Board issued its order, the date the person submitted the request for administrative review, and the time elapsed since the request for administrative review was submitted. The offender should provide copies of the relevant documents when possible. See Filing for Judicial Review, below, for more information.

III. Filing for Judicial Review

The requirements for filing a petition for judicial review are the same as those established for appeal from most state agencies in the Oregon Rules of Appellate Procedure (ORAP). ORAP 4.10. A sample petition is provided in the ORAP Appendix at App. 4.15-1.

As a practice tip, make sure that you include the statement in the petition: “Petitioner is not willing to stipulate that the record may be shortened.” Otherwise, the Board may omit valuable information, usually older reports from the Department of Corrections and psychological evaluations, that may be helpful to your client. ORS 144.335(5)(a). It is easier to obtain the missing information if you have not agreed to a shortened record.

IV. The Agency Record on Review

Thirty days after the petition for judicial review is filed the agency record is due to be filed. ORS

144.335(5). Occasionally the Board will take an extension of time to file the agency record or, depending on the procedural posture of the case, file a motion to dismiss the case. Such motions are discussed later in this chapter.

The agency record should contain all the information and documentation relied upon by the Board when making the determination issued in its order. Here are some of the standard things you will find in an agency record:

• The notice of rights (for the hearing).

• Pre- or Post-Sentence Investigation Reports 3. {Note: 3 It is imperative for criminal defense attorneys to ensure that they carefully review the pre-sentence report (PSI) and have any errors corrected in writing on the document at the time of sentencing. ORS 137.077and ORS 137.079. The board relies very heavily upon this document.}

• Judgments or sentencing orders.

• Psychological evaluations.

• Transcript of the hearing.

• Prior Board action forms, orders, and administrative review responses.

• Certificates of accomplishments (from inmates).

• Nondisclosed confidential documents (usually submitted from victims).

• The Board action form or Board order being reviewed.

• The related Request for Administrative Review or Exhibit O filed by the client.

•Any subsequent Administrative Review Responses, including the one under review.

The record should be reviewed carefully for any errors, omissions or confidential documents. You have 15 days before record settlement to file a motion to correct the agency record. If the error is found after that time period, you may request leave of the court to file a motion to correct the record.

The Board will routinely withhold documents from the agency record that it relied upon in making its decision. A notice should be included in the record when a document has been withheld. In such circumstances, you may need to file a motion to inspect confidential documents or, in the alternative, request an in camerareview. SeeFisher/Gordon v. Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 239 Or App 603, 245 P3d 671 (2010). Relying on the authority granted in ORS 1.160and ORAP 3.07(8), the appellate court in Fisherheld that disclosure of public records is the norm, and that the Board, a state agency, has the burden to justify nondisclosure. 239 Or App at 608–611.

V. Motions

Judicial review of parole cases tends to involve more motion practice than direct criminal appeals. This section is not an exhaustive list of motions in parole appeals. Instead, it highlights two of the most common motions.

A. Motion to Dismiss

Typically, the Board files a motion to dismiss if it believes the Court of Appeals lacks jurisdiction. The jurisdictional challenge arises most often in cases where the inmate failed to timely exhaust administrative remedies and the Board issued a written reponse that it claims did not reopen and reconsider its prior action. SeeDawson/Fletcher, 346 Or at 656(establishing the analysis for determining whether the Board reopened and reconsidered a prior action).

B. Motion to Inspect Sealed or Confidential Material

The Board frequently seals materials during the administrative hearing process and refuses to disclose the materials to an inmate or the person’s attorney. The sealed materials are usually documents submitted by the victim of the inmate’s crime or the victim’s family.

On judicial review, the petitioner should move to inspect the sealed or confidential documents under ORAP 3.07(5), particularly if the Board appears to have relied on the documents in rendering its decision. Absent an exceptional showing by the Board demonstrating why the public and systemic interests—including due process—in disclosure are outweighed by the need to keep the material sealed, the court should permit the petitioner’s attorney to inspect the material. ''See generally Fisher/Gordon, 239 Or App at 603. The Court of Appeals may be more likely to permit the petitioner’s attorney to inspect the sealed material if the attorney submits an affidavit promising not to disclose the material to anyone, including the petitioner. In the author’s view, such a limitation is not justified absent a showing by the Board demonstrating that the need for nondisclosure to the petitioner outweighs the significant public and systemic interests in disclosure. But the Court of Appeals often orders counsel not to disclose the contents of sealed documents to the petitioners without requiring such a showing from the Board.

The Board may withdraw its ARR instead of allowing the petitioner’s counsel to inspect sealed materials. Withdrawing the ARR suspends the judicial review. When the Board files a new ARR, the petitioner must decide whether to proceed with the judicial review by filing a Notice of Intent to Proceed or to file a new administrative review request. ORS 144.335(7); ORAP 4.35(4)(a)(ii).

This decision by the petitioner is determined by the action taken by the Board in the new ARR. If the Board has effectively issued a new order or BAF as opposed to explaining the basis for the earlier BAF or order, then the petition may want to file a new Exhibit O. If the new ARR just explains its reasoning in more detail or clarity, then a Notice of Intent to Proceed should suffice. Of course, the petitioner need not pursue further litigation if the new ARR grants the relief the petitioner had been seeking.

VI. Briefing

Once the record has been settled, the standard opening brief due date is set by the court for 49 days from record settlement. The respondent’s answering brief is due 49 days from the date the opening brief has been filed. Because judicial review of a parole Board order is administrative, the petitioner does not have to seek leave of the court to file a reply brief. The reply brief is due 14 days after the filing of the answering brief.

Briefing a parole case on judicial review involves the same skills and rules outlined in the briefing chapter. See Chapter 5, “Briefing.” The main differences between briefing a parole case and a direct criminal appeal are the vocabulary (e.g., petitioner instead of appellant or defendant) and the application of administrative law in parole cases. For example, a petitioner on judicial review in a parole case may file a reply brief as matter of right, without asking for leave from the Court of Appeals. ORAP 5.70.

Once the briefs have been filed, the Court of Appeals sets the case for oral argument and submission of the case. This may take anywhere from three to six months. The case may be submitted on the briefs or after the court hears oral argument.

Practitioners should bear in mind that parole cases comprise a relatively small percentage of the court’s docket and often involve arcane administrative rules or statutes from the 1970s or 1980s. Thus, the judges are likely to be unfamiliar with the subject of your brief. That makes clarity of writing and analysis even more important than in a direct criminal appeal. If the writing is unclear or the argument unfocused, one risks losing a case because the judges simply do not understand the argument.

In particular, practitioners should take care to use the proper standard of review from ORS 183.482(8)and clearly explain the alleged error, the authorities upon which the legal arguments rely, and the proper remedy.

The appellate court “may affirm, reverse or remand the order on the same basis as provided in ORS 183.482(8).” ORS 144.335(3). Because many of the Board’s decisions are discretionary decisions, after a win by the petitioner the case is usually remanded back to the Board for the proper application of the law. The Board then gets another opportunity to correctly apply the law and rules. Unfortunately, the petitioner often ends up with the same end decision, but this time properly supported with express findings from the Board.

VII. Remedies

The appellate courts’ authority to impose a remedy in a judicial review of a Board order is governed by ORS 183.482(8), which is made applicable to the Board by ORS 144.335(3). ORS 183.482(8) provides:

(a) The court may affirm, reverse or remand the order. If the court finds that the agency has erroneously inter- preted a provision of law and that a correct interpretation compels a particular action, the court shall:

(A) Set aside or modify the order; or

(B) Remand the case to the agency for further action under a correct interpretation of the provision of law.

(b) The court shall remand the order to the agency if the court finds the agency’s exercise of discretion to be: (A) Outside the range of discretion delegated to the agency by law;

(B) Inconsistent with an agency rule, an officially stated agency position, or a prior agency practice, if the inconsistency is not explained by the agency; or

(C) Otherwise in violation of a constitutional or statutory provision.

(c) The court shall set aside or remand the order if the court finds that the order is not supported by substan- tial evidence in the record. Substantial evidence exists to support a finding of fact when the record, viewed as a whole, would permit a reasonable person to make that finding.

The most common remedy for a petitioner who prevails on appeal is a remand to the Board for the Board to reconsider its decision under a proper understanding of the law or using proper procedures. The relatively limited scope of the remedy may affect a person’s decision whether to pursue judicial review. The Board holds many hearings at regular intervals. For example, an inmate under the matrix system typically receives an exit interview every two years. ORS 144.125(3)(a). The judicial review process usually takes one to two years. If the best case remedy for a client is reversal for a new hearing, the client may have had a new hearing by the time the appellate court decides the judicial review. Of course, there are many reasons why a client might want to pursue relief on judicial review even if he or she will have had a new hearing by the time the case is decided. One example is that the Board may have applied an incorrect legal standard to the inmate, and it might continue to do so unless the petitioner prevails on judicial review. Another example is that the original order may be held invalid by the appellate court, causing the continued confinement of the petitioner to be held unlawful. SeeHamel v. Johnson, 330 Or 180, 187-88, 998 P2d 661 (2000)(holding that judicial review from an earlier hearing was not moot because if petitioner could establish that the Board did not have a valid basis to postpone his release date, then petitioner should be released on parole).

VIII. Costs and Fees

The state appellate courts may award the prevailing party costs and fees on judicial review of a parole Board decision. SeeBlacknall v. Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, 348 Or 131, 229 P3d 595(2010)(describing appellate court’s authority to impose costs and fees on judicial review of an order of the Board). ORS 20.310(1), 20.190(1)(a), and 20.120together establish that “on review of an agency order, unless another statute provides to the contrary, the reviewing court has authority to allow the prevailing party to recover an award of costs and disbursements, including a prevailing party fee of $100.” Blacknall, 348 Or at 136.

Additionally, the Court of Appeals may order a petitioner to pay up to $100 in attorney fees (in addition to the costs and fees discussed above) to the Board if the court “determines that a brief filed by the petitioner, when liberally construed, fails to state a colorable claim for review[.]” ORS 144.335(9); Blacknall, 348 Or at 141. However, the court may not impose the $100 fee if the petitioner moves to dismiss before court summarily affirms Board’s order under ORS 144.335(6). In practice, the court usually does not impose any costs or fees when a petitioner moves to dismiss the judicial review, regardless of whether the Board has filed for summary affirmance under ORS 144.335(6).

Many people seeking judicial review of Board decisions are indigent. Accordingly, the imposition of a $100 prevailing party fee and additional costs may constitute a significant financial burden. An attorney should advise a client of the possibility that an appellate court will impose costs and fees. Furthermore,

if an attorney determines that the client has little likelihood of prevailing on judicial review, the attorney should advise the client to weigh the remote possibility of prevailing against the possibility that the person will owe the Board costs and fees. In the authors’ experience, many clients elect to move to dismiss their judicial review in such circumstances.
