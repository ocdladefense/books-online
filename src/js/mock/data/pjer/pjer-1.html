Direct Appeal

Eric Johnson

This section on direct appeal is primarily intended for beginners, novices in the “Art” of the direct appeal. Although much of it is procedural and based on statutory requirements, the “meat” of the direct appeal is truly an art, not a science. The authors hope that when a client walks into the office of a novice appellate practitioner and says, “I was just convicted—I want to appeal,” the practitioner can pick up this manual and walk through the steps of filing a notice of appeal, obtaining the transcript, completing the record, filing an appellant’s brief, reviewing the respondent’s brief, deciding whether to file a reply brief, effectively arguing the case in the Oregon Court of Appeals, petitioning for reconsideration or review when appropriate, filing a brief on the merits in the Oregon Supreme Courtwhen requested, effectively arguing the case in the Supreme Court, and petitioning for a writ of certiorari in the US Supreme Courtwhen appropriate.

This section is not only for the novice, however. The authors hope to provide a “nugget” or two for the seasoned veteran, to assist him or her in becoming an exceptional appellate practitioner.

I. Constitutional and Statutory Authority

The right to appeal in criminal cases is authorized by Article VII (Amended), section 3, of the OregonConstitution(appeals in criminal cases are to be heard by the Oregon Supreme Court).

Statutory authority for criminal appeals is provided in Chapter 138of the Oregon Revised Statutes. ORS 138.010 states that the onlymode of reviewing a judgment or order in a criminal action is that prescribed by ORS 138.010 to 138.310. ORS 138.020 states that the state and the defendant may appeal as a matter of right from a judgment in a criminal action as prescribed in ORS 138.010 to 138.310, and not otherwise.

Many of the provisions of Chapter 19of the Oregon Revised Statutes (relating to appeals in civil proceedings) are referenced by the criminal appeal statutes and are therefore also applicable to criminal appeals.

Additionally, a crime victim may, as a matter of right, appeal to the Oregon Supreme Court an order adversely resolving a claim that the state has violated such victim’s rights under Article I, section 42, of the Oregon Constitution, and ORS 147.500et seq., if (1) the criminal proceeding charged defendant with either a felony or a “person Class A misdemeanor,” and (2) the alleged victim’s rights violation occurred before the trial court pronounced its sentence or disposition in open court. ORS 147.535(4)(a); ORS 147.537(1). SeeState v. Barrett, 350 Or 390, 255 P3d 472 (2011).

II.Scope of Direct Appeal by a Defendant in Criminal Cases {1 Appeal from a judgment involving a violation is governed by ORS 138.057 and will not be discussed in this manual. Appeal by the state in a criminal case is governed by ORS 138.060 and also will not be discussed. Appeal by any person who is a party to a juvenile court proceeding is governed by ORS 419A.200and will likewise not be discussed.}

The scope of direct appeal by a defendant in a criminal case is generally covered by ORS 138.040, ORS 138.050, ORS 138.053, and ORS 138.222 (Chapter 138)

A. ORS 138.040

Appeal by a defendant in a criminal case notinvolving a guilty or no contest plea is generally governed by ORS 138.040. The defendant may appeal to the Court of Appeals from a judgment or order described under ORS 138.053. The appellate court may review any decision of the court in an intermediate order or proceeding, and any disposition described under ORS 138.053 as to whether such disposition exceeds the maximum allowable by law or is unconstitutionally cruel and unusual.

B. ORS 138.050

Appeal by a defendant in a criminal case involving a guilty or no contest plea is generally governed by ORS 138.050. The defendant may appeal to the Court of Appeals from a judgment or order described in ORS 138.053 onlywhen the defendant makes a colorable showing that the disposition exceeds the maximum allowable by law, or is unconstitutionally cruel and unusual. In such an appeal, the appellate court considers onlywhether the disposition exceeds the maximum allowable by law or is unconstitutionally cruel and unusual.

C. ORS 138.053

{2 A judgment or order imposed after judgment is not subject to appeal after the expiration of the time specified in ORS 138.071. See ORS 138.053(2). Additionally, a probation judgment may be reviewed only if the defendant shows a colorable claim of error in the proceeding from which the appeal is taken. See ORS 138.053(3).}

ORS 138.053discusses the types of judgments and orders that are subject to appeal. A judgment (or an order imposed after judgment) is appeallable if the disposition imposes a sentence on conviction, suspends imposition or execution of any part of a sentence, extends a period of probation, imposes or modifies a condition of probation or of sentence suspension, or imposes or executes a sentence upon revocation of probation or sentence suspension.

D. ORS 138.222

ORS 138.222limits review of sentences imposed for any felony committed on or after November 1, 1989 (the date of the implementation of the sentencing guidelines). The appellate court may not review any sentence that is within the range of a presumptive sentence (ORS 138.222 (2)(a)), a sentence of probation orimprisonment when optional probation is authorized (ORS 138.222 (2)(b) and (c)), any sentence resulting from a “stipulated sentencing agreement” between the state and the defendant which the sentencing court approves on the record (ORS 138.222 (2)(d)), or any other issue related to sentencing, except as authorized in ORS 138.222 (3) and (4).

The appellate court may review a sentence that departs from the presumptive sentence only as to whether the sentencing court’s findings of fact and reasons justifying a departure are supported by the evidence in the record and constitute substantial and compelling reasons for departure.

SeeORS 138.222 (3).

The appellate court also may review a claim that the sentencing court failed to comply with requirements of law in imposing or failing to impose a sentence (ORS 138.222 (4)(a)), a claim that the sentencing court erred in ranking the crime seriousness classification of the current crime or in determining the appropriate classification of a prior conviction or juvenile adjudication for criminal history purposes (ORS 138.222 (4)(b)), or a claim that the sentencing court erred in failing to impose a minimum sentence that is prescribed by ORS 137.700 or 137.707(ORS 138.222 (4)(c)).

The defendant must show a colorable claim of error in a proceeding if the appeal is from a proceeding in which a sentence was entered subsequent to a plea of guilty or no contest, or a proceeding in which probation was revoked, the period of probation was extended, a new condition of probation was imposed, an existing condition of probation was modified or a sentence suspension was revoked. SeeORS 138.222 (7)(a) and (b).

The defendant must also show a colorable claim of error in a proceeding if the appeal is from a proceeding in which a sentence was entered subsequent to a resentencing ordered by an appellate court or a post-conviction relief court. SeeORS 138.222 (7)(c).
