Post Judgment and Extraordinary Remedies

2012 edition

Section III

Mandamus and Habeas Corpus in the Oregon Supreme Court

Laura Graser

Overview

This chapter will cover when a criminal practitioner can file a petition for a writ of mandamus or a petition for a writ of habeas corpus in the Oregon Supreme Court. In the court’s practice, jurisdiction to file a petition for a writ of mandamus can sometimes be confused with, or can overlap, jurisdiction to file a petition for a writ of habeas corpus. The distinction will be addressed.

First, the chapter will address filing for a writ of mandamus in the Oregon Supreme Court, as an original matter. The chapter will then summarily address filing in the circuit court, as an original matter. The rules are similar—and distinctive to Oregon state practice.

Second, the chapter will address when a habeas corpus petition can be filed in the Oregon Supreme Court. The chapter will not cover when to file a habeas corpus petition in the circuit court, which is generally a different matter, and is covered in the previous section of this chapter. Also, the procedure described here is unrelated to filing a habeas corpus petition in the federal court.

I.Seeking a Writ of Mandamus in the Oregon Supreme Court

A. Introduction

A petition for a writ of mandamus is a very powerful tool, with very limited and specific usefulness. This chapter will discuss the law in the area, but knowing the law is not necessarily enough. This remedy is discretionary and “extra-ordinary,” and an understanding of the culture of the Oregon Supreme Court is important. While the culture of the court changes with its personnel, there is

an overriding preference for civility. In this forum, courtesy to the opposing party and courteous references to the trial judge are tools for effective advocacy.

Similarly, the practitioner can be most effective when he is mindful of the specific requirements for a writ, and seeks one only when issuing a writ would be appropriate.

B. The Terms, and Some Context

“Mandamus” is Latin for “We command.” If the history of the writ is of interest, it is outlined in Keith Garza’s “Advanced Mandamus Practice”(2004) on the Oregon State Bar’s Appellate PracticeSection’s website1.1 http://osbappellate.homestead.com/CLE_Secondary_files/garza_mandamus.pdf

The writ goes back to medieval England. In this country, the foundational case, Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (Cranch) 137, 2 L. Ed. 60 (1803), involved a writ of mandamus. Oregon’s own quirky procedure is not the same as the federal procedure, nor is it similar to other states’. The concept—but not the specific procedure and quirks—applies to other jurisdictions, so case law from other jurisdictions is unlikely to be helpful.

Note that Oregon Land Use Board of Appeals cases have a distinctive mandamus procedure, and mandamus is not so unusual here. This is a completely different context controlled by different law, so the criminal practitioner should not be distracted by the LUBA cases.

A peremptory writ of mandamus orders the lower court to “Do it now” (or be in contempt of court).

An alternative writ of mandamus orders the lower court to “Do it now, or show cause why you are not doing it.” SeeORS 34.150. The lower court shows cause by filing an answer to the writ, or by filing a motion to dismiss the writ, with legal argument in either case. If the lower court complies, the lower court files a certificate of compliance. ORAP 11.10(4).

The statute sets out the distinction between a peremptory writ and an alternative writ, but in terms which may assume the answer. ORS 34.160 provides:

When the right to require the performance of the act is clear, and it is apparent that no valid excuse can be given for not performing it, a peremptory mandamus shall be allowed in the first instance; in all other cases, the alternative writ shall be first issued.

Unless the practitioner is very confident that the “right to require the performance of the act is clear” and that there is “no valid excuse,” deciding which writ to seek is mostly a matter of strategy. If the relator asks for an alternative writ, the court has the power to issue a peremptory writ, so asking for a peremptory in this context may be more aggressive than effective. As discussed throughout this chapter, getting any writ is rare, and relator is asking the court to use its discretion. Treading softly is generally the best approach.

The party seeking the writ is the “relator” (not “petitioner”). The relator is “the beneficially interested party on whose relation a mandamus proceeding is brought.” ORS 34.105(4).

The other side is the “defendant” or “adverse party” (ORAP 11.05(1)). Under the old statute (pre-1997), the judge was the defendant; but now the real opposing party is in the caption. Old cases were captioned, “State ex rel Criminal Defendant vs. Judge.” Now the case name for the petition is the same as the case name in the trial court, “State v. Criminal Defendant.” Occasionally the criminal defendant is not involved; rather, the fight is between the judge and (for example) a member of the media, where the media member is seeking sealed information. If so, the caption will read: “SER Media Member v. Judge.” The criminal defendant can intervene.2 {Note: 2 Although a criminal defendant can intervene to seek to keep a document sealed, the law (particularly the Oregon Constitu-tion’s open courts clause, Or Const. Art I , sec 10), is in the media’s favor. See, e.g.,[../../../../Publications/post%20judgment%20appeals%20pcr%20habeas%20manual/section%20III%20-%20mandamus/ex%20rel%20davey%20v%20frankel State ex rel Davey v. Frankel, 312 Or 286, ][../../../../Publications/post%20judgment%20appeals%20pcr%20habeas%20manual/section%20III%20-%20mandamus/ex%20rel%20davey%20v%20frankel 823 P2d 394 (1991) ](writ issued to overturn order excluding media from the courtroom during a 412 hearing, under “rape shield law”—the criminal defendant was the relator); State ex rel Oregonian Pub. Co. v. Diez, 289 Or 277, 613 P2d 23 (1980)(writ issued to overturn order excluding media from a juvenile proceeding allowed under a statute—a newspaper was the relator), see alsoOregonian Pub. Co. v. Given that the information is probably going to be unsealed and published eventually, it may be better for a criminal defendant if the publication is sooner, rather than later, so the information is in the media as much before trial as possible.

In a criminal case, if the dispute is between the criminal defendant and the state, typically the judge will ask the district attorney or Attorney General to defend, and typically the AG’s appellate division will handle the matter.

When the judge is not a party, he or she can intervene, as can others with standing. For example, the judge may intervene if a local rule is at issue. See e.g.,State v. Pena, 345 Or 198, 191 P3d 659(2008)(presiding judge intervened). In that case, of course, the judge has to have separate counsel. There is no specific time to intervene, but obviously it should be done promptly.

ORS 34.120 provides that the matter is filed in circuit court unless the decision to be contested was by a circuit court judge—which is typically the situation in a criminal context. Thus in criminal practice, the petition, when it is filed, is more frequently filed in the Supreme Court.

In unusual situations, a petition for a writ can be filed regarding a ruling by the Court of Appeals. See e.g.,State ex rel Hall v. Riggs, 319 Or 282, 877 P2d 56 (1994).

C. When in Doubt About Which Remedy Applies, Seek More Than One

If a criminal defendant is in custody because of a ruling by the circuit court, the remedy may be through a writ of habeas corpus, discussed later in this chapter. If it is not clear which remedy is the proper one, file both. See Priest v. Pearce, 314 Or 411, 840 P2d 62 (1992)(both sought; habeas writ issued).

Similarly, seeking a writ of mandamus does not toll the time for appealing. The practitioner should keep in mind that the relator may be right on the underlying issue, but the Supreme Court may decline to exercise its discretion to issue a writ of mandamus. Or, the relator may be right on the underlying issue, but the Supreme Court could conclude that the remedy was only through an appeal. (The court does not inform the parties why it denied a petition for a writ.) On this issue, see generally,State v. Salzmann, 119 Or App 217, 850 P2d 1122, rev denied, 317 Or 162 (1993), cert denied, 510 US 1040 (1994) (no right of interlocutory appeal to protect defendant from second prosecutions when defendant raises former jeopardy). (A writ of mandamus was simultaneously sought, and was denied without comment by the Supreme Court.)3 {Note: 3 For a discussion of the merits of the double jeopardy issue, seeState ex rel Turner v. Frankel, 322 Or 363, 908 P2d 293(1995), below.}

If judgment has been entered, and the practitioner wants to preserve any appellate issue in the case, he must file a notice of appeal, perhaps with a motion to determine appellate jurisdiction in the Court of Appeals under ORS 19.235.

D. Writs Are Rarely Granted

A writ of mandamus is rarely sought, but nevertheless it is sought too often. Even when it is properly sought, it is rarely granted. The statistics tell the tale.

These are the writs (peremptory and alternative) that were granted; the relator may or may not have won ultimately. If a peremptory writ was initially granted, the relator won. However, in the more likely case, an alternative writ was issued. The trial judge may have done what the alternative writ directed (and issued a certificate of compliance), or the trial judge may have decided to show cause as to why he did not do as the alternative writ directed. In such a case, the matter was briefed and argued before the Oregon Supreme Court. If the court then issued a peremptory writ, the relator won. If the court dismissed the alternative writ, the relator lost.

Thus the practitioner should keep in mind that seeking a writ will likely be unsuccessful. Consider the impact this will have on the case in the long run. It may delay the resolution of the case if the trial judge decided to issue a stay (more on this below), or it may annoy the prosecution or the judge.

E. Seeking a Writ Does Not Stay the Proceedings in the Trial Court

Seeking a writ does not stay the proceedings in the trial court. ORS 34.130(5). The trial court can issue a stay, although it may not be inclined to do so. If it declines to issue a stay, the Supreme Court can issue a stay. The Supreme Court generally will issue a stay if it issues an alternative writ, pending briefing before the court. To seek a stay in the Supreme Court, it must first be sought in the lower court. ORAP 11.05(2)(c).

The trial court continues to have jurisdiction while the mandamus matter is pending, absent a stay. For example, in one case the plaintiff refused to participate in the trial while a mandamus petition was pending. The trial judge dismissed the case for want of prosecution, and that ruling was upheld. Lee v.Brown, 264 Or 341, 505 P2d 924, cert denied, 414 US 830 (1973).

F. Sources of Authority

There are four sources of authority: the Oregon Constitution, the statute (ORS 34.105–.250), the appellate rules (ORAP 11.05–.17), and case law. The statute and the relevant appellate rules are short and should be read every time.

The Oregon Constitution, Art. VII (Am), § 2, provides:

The courts, jurisdiction, and judicial system of Oregon, except so far as expressly changed by this amendment, shall remain as at present constituted until otherwise provided by law. But the supreme court may, in its own discretion, take original jurisdiction in mandamus,quo warranto and habeas corpus proceedings.

ORS 34.105–.250 sets out the rules for both circuit court and the Supreme Court. The key is ORS 34.110:

A writ of mandamus may be issuedto any inferior court, corporation, board, officer or person, to compel the performance of an act which the law specially enjoins, as a duty resulting from an office, trust or station; but though the writ may require such court, corporation, board, officer or person to exercise judgment, or proceed to the discharge of any functions, it shall not control judicial discretion.The writ shall not be issued in any case where there is a plain, speedy and adequate remedy in the ordinary course of the law.

G. Relator Must Show Three Elements

Thus under ORS 34.110, the petition for a writ of mandamus must establish three necessary elements: 1) probable error by the trial judge, 2) the issue must not involve a discretionary call by the trial judge, and 3) there must be no effective remedy on appeal (or elsewhere). If all three elements are not present in the case, the Supreme Court does not have mandamus jurisdiction.

1. Factor One: “to compel the performance of an act which the law specifically enjoins...”

In theory, a mandamus case is not a forum to make new law. The trial judge must have made a ruling that was a clear breach of the law. However, case law seems to undercut the firmness of that rule. ORS 34.160 provides:

When the right to require the performance of the act is clear, and it is apparent that no valid excuse can be given for not performing it, a peremptory mandamus shall be allowed in the first instance; in all other cases, the alternative writ shall be first issued.

The relator does not have to show he has a “clear” right to win if relator is seeking an alternative (not peremptory) writ. ORS 34.160. The court has noted that “mandamus will lie to decide in a certain way an especially complicated question of law, the answer to which is in extreme doubt and the solution of which requires the use of legal judgment and acumen.” State exrel Maizels v. Juba, 254 Or 323, 327, 460 P2d 850 (1969).

In mandamus cases, the Supreme Court has addressed the constitutionality of a new statute—see State ex rel Pierce v. Slusher, 119 Or 141, 248 P 358 (1926) (a new tax statute)—and the constitutionality of the newly passed Measure 11 when the relator (the state) did not have an appellate remedy—State ex rel Huddleston v. Sawyer, 324 Or 597, 932 P2d 1145, cert denied,522 US 994 (1997). See alsoState v. Rogers, 334 Or 633, 55 P3d 488 (2002)(interpreting ORS 10.215regarding the jury records that must be produced to a party in a criminal case).

More typically, a successful relator has argued that the unambiguous words of a statute require a certain result. For example, a successful petition argued that a judge was required to recuse himself after a party had precisely followed the recusal statute, ORS 14.250. State ex relKafoury v. Jones, 315 Or 201, 843 P2d 932 (1992); compareState v. Pena, 345 Or 198, 191 P3d659 (2008)(counsel failed to follow the statute exactly, so no recusal); State ex rel Hopkins v.Schenck, 313 Or 529, 836 P2d 721 (1992)(interpreting recusal statute).

What a statute requires may, of course, be subject to disagreement. For example, in Statev. Petersen, 347 Or 199, 218 P3d 892 (2009), the relator argued—over the state’s vigorous objection—that the statute and unambiguous case law prohibited the state’s psychological expert from asking a defendant about his thoughts at the time of the underlying crime. The relator won on the merits. It is not entirely clear if the court simply reiterated the preexisting state of the law, or made new law.

2. Factor Two: a writ “shall not control judicial discretion.”

Mandamus does not lie to control judicial discretion. ORS 34.110. That is, if a matter could be decided either way, the Supreme Court will not interfere by issuing a writ. However, if the exercise of discretion has a prerequisite, it is not an exercise of discretion absent the prerequisite. For example, the rules of civil procedure allow a party to take a videotaped deposition, unless the trial judge finds that process would subject the witness to embarrassment or the like. Absent those findings, a party has a clear right to take a videotaped deposition. Thus in a case with no findings, the Supreme Court issued a writ requiring a videotaped deposition. State ex rel Anderson v. Miller,320 Or 316, 882 P2d 1109 (1994).

Refusing to exercise discretion is not an “abuse of discretion,” it is an error of law—the judge has a duty to rule. State ex rel Pearcy v. Long, 234 Or 630, 383 P2d 377 (1963); State ex rel Riccov. Biggs, 198 Or 413, 255 P2d 1055 (1953)(mandamus appropriate to compel a lower court to exercise its discretion, when it had refused to act).

Mandamus will lie if the court abused its discretion, that is, if it ruled outside the permissible range of a grant of discretion. State ex rel Keisling v. Norblad, 317 Or 615, 860 P2d 241 (1993)(court did not have authority to keep an initiative off the ballot).

The relator must provide the Supreme Court with all the relevant facts, as an appellant would in an appellate brief, because the court will look beyond the words of the statute to consider the factual context. For example, the statute provides that a decision to allow or deny a civil compromise is discretionary. ORS 135.705(1)(a). Yet, when the judge told the criminal defendant in one hearing that she would allow the civil compromise if he followed certain conditions and he did so, the judge no longer had the discretion in a later hearing to refuse to allow the compromise. The judge was required to allow the compromise and dismiss the indictment. The discretion the statute granted was exercised when the judge told the defendant in the first hearing that if he followed the conditions, she would allow the civil compromise.

This was true even though the trial judge had learned new facts about the case in the later hearing. (The Supreme Court noted there could be some new facts that would allow the trial judge to change her mind.) State v. Orians, 335 Or 257, 66 P3d 468 (2003).

3. Factor Three: “The writ shall not be issued in any case where there is a plain, speedy and adequateremedy in the ordinary course of the law.”

A writ of mandamus will issue only if no other remedy is possible, that is, if there is a “plain, speedy, and adequate remedy in the ordinary course of law.” ORS 34.110. The mere “burden of litigation,” including the burden of being convicted, is not enough to provide mandamus jurisdiction. SER Automotive Emporium v. Murchison, 289 Or 265, 611 P2d 1169, reh’g denied by opinion, 289 Or 673 (1980)(writ denied when a criminal defendant was denied a preliminary hearing, which under existing law was an appealable ruling). Seeking a writ is not a form of interlocutory appeal.

Mandamus is the correct remedy if the relator has no right to appeal, as, for example, the state does not after a guilty verdict. State ex rel Schrunk v. Bonebrake, 318 Or 312, 865 P2d 1289(1994)(writ issued after trial judge dismissed an indictment after a guilty verdict). SeeStateex rel Huddleston v. Sawyer, 324 Or 597, 932 P2d 1145 (1997)(when trial court declined to impose a Measure 11 sentence, state’s only remedy under then-existing law was to seek a writ of mandamus).

Mandamus was the correct remedy when a defendant claimed a right to be free from double jeopardy. One of the protections of that clause is to be free from a second trial, so an appeal would be too late to vindicate that right. State ex rel Turner v. Frankel, 322 Or 363, 908 P2d 293 (1995). See alsoState v. Sawatzky, 339 Or 689, 693 n.4, 125 P3d 722 (2005)(“significant former and double jeopardy issue presented” so writ issued. Relator later lost on the merits: sentencing jury empaneled).

The remedy must be adequate, that is, complete, giving relator all the relief the law gives him. In most cases the “adequate remedy” is an appeal, but the remedy can be something else, for example, the existence of new legislation passed since the petition was filed. ''See, e.g., State v.Staudinger, 332 Or 477, 31 P3d 426 (2001)(petition dismissed after new legislation passed).

The following are topics where the court has found mandamus jurisdiction. Included are the names of the trial lawyers in the unreported cases.

a.Discovery, Privacy, and Privilege Issues

An appeal is not a remedy if a breach of privacy or a privilege will occur immediately. For example, a trial court order allowed a defense investigator and defense counsel to enter a homicide scene to inspect it and to take measurements. The widow/homeowner objected, and the Supreme Court issued a writ to the circuit judge to prohibit the inspection. State exrel Beach v. Norblad, 308 Or 429, 781 P2d 349 (1989).

There are some civil cases (which have different discovery rights than criminal cases) holding that an order that is beyond statutory authority for discovery is appropriately addressed by a writ of mandamus. State ex rel S.P. Co. v. Duncan, 230 Or 179, 368 P2d733 (1962)(writ issued because there is no statutory authority to require a deposition of a corporate non-management employee). State ex rel Byers v. Crookham, 304 Or 268, 743 P2d1115 (1987)(writ issued to vacate discovery order that required a party to produce work product, absent a finding of need). Stevens v. Czerniak, 336 Or 392, 84 P3d 140 (2004)(in a post-conviction relief case, the criminal defendant did not have to disclose his experts).

Notwithstanding the difference in discovery law, some civil cases on privilege appear directly applicable to criminal cases. In Frease v. Glazer, 330 Or 364, 4 P3d 56 (2000)the court issues a writ of mandamus, holding that the attorney-client privilege protects even in camerareview. In State ex rel OHSU v. Haas, 325 Or 492, 497, 942 P2d 261, 264 (1997), the court interpreted the attorney-client privilege and waiver. The court wrote, “Mandamus is an appropriate remedy when a discovery order erroneously requires disclosure of a privileged communication. . . . [M]andamus is appropriate in such cases, because relators otherwise would ‘suffer[] an irretrievable loss of information and tactical advantage which could not be restored to them on direct appeal’ ” (citing State ex rel Automotive Emporiumv. Murchison, supra). See alsoState ex rel N. Pacific Lbr. v. Unis, 282 Or 457, 579 P2d 1291(1978)(attorney-client privilege in a tort case).

In State ex rel Grimm v. Ashmanskas, 298 Or 206, 690 P2d 1063 (1984), the court issued a writ interpreting the physician-patient privilege [which is different in a civil case, seeOEC504–1(2)].

In State v. Lobo, S058221 (2010), the Supreme Court issued an alternative writ, but no reported opinion. The ruling is described in the media release of February 16, 2010. The trial court ordered the state to provide material to the defense to test, but required the defense to disclose where the material would be stored, who would test it, and which tests would be done. The defense claimed this would reveal its strategy, and filed for an alternative writ. The Supreme Court issued the alternative writ, and the trial judge complied. Janet Hoffman and Sarah Adams were the trial attorneys.

b.Speech Issues

Given the protections in the Oregon Constitution, the Supreme Court is inclined to rule against prior restraints on speech. See, e.g.,State ex rel Sports Management News v. Nachtigal,324 Or 80, 921 P2d 1304 (1996)(pretrial order, issued under the terms of a constitutionally invalid statute, could not require the media to obtain court permission before publishing “trade secrets” learned from court proceedings).

c.Jurisdiction Issues

Mandamus is the correct remedy if the court is improperly exercising personal jurisdiction over a defendant. This comes up in civil cases, and conceivably could come up in a criminal case. SeeState ex rel Circus Circus Reno, Inc. v. Pope, 317 Or 151, 854 P2d 461 (1993)(long- arm jurisdiction did not reach to Nevada under these circumstances).

d.Court Administration Issues

In a series of cases, the Supreme Court issued writs directing the state court administrator to release records relating to the summoning of jurors. State ex rel. Click v. Brownhill, 331 Or 500, 15 P3d 990 (2000)(trial court had authority to order records released); State v. Rogers,334 Or 633, 55 P3d 488 (2002)(statute requires that certain records be released); State v.Staudinger, 332 Or 477, 31 P3d 426 (2001)(new statute was adequate remedy); State v. Gallatin, 334 Or 189, 47 P3d 488 (2002) (writ issued but then rendered moot by criminal defendant’s guilty plea).

In State ex rel Lasswell v. Sanders, 293 Or 41, 643 P2d 1273 (1982), the writ directed the trial court to make findings and then rule, depending on the findings, that a witness did or did not have to testify.

H. The Mechanics of Seeking a Writ of Mandamus

To seek a writ, the issue to be addressed by the Supreme Court must be preserved in a written document (typically an order issued by a circuit court), entered into the register, and accessible through the Oregon Judicial Information Network. In this regard, the practice is like an appeal.

If the trial court made findings or legal rulings orally, the practitioner must make certain they are incorporated into a document. ORS chapter 34and the appellate rules set out the details of the mechanics. As of the time of this writing, the ORAP 11.05provides as follows:

The caption of documents must include the words “Mandamus Proceeding,” and the petition must be entitled, as relevant, “petition for alternative writ of mandamus” or “petition for peremptory writ of mandamus.”

The petition must contain:

• a statement of the material facts and the relief sought;

• a statement why the petition is timely;

• a statement why application was not first made to the circuit court;

• a statement why appeal or any other potential remedy is not plain, speedy, adequate in the ordinary course of law; and

• the order (or other ruling) at issue must be attached to the petition.

The petition functions as a complaint, and relator will be held to its terms. Stevens v. Czerniak, 336

Or 392, 396, 84 P3d 140 (2004).

In addition to the petition, a relator:

• must file a copy of relevant portions of the record—eight copies plus the original if less than 50 pages; two copies plus the original if 50 pages or more;

• must pay the filing fee, or file a motion to waive it if the criminal defendant is indigent;

• must file a supporting memorandum complying with the rule for filing briefs, including the page limitations.

The memorandum is relator’s only opportunity to convince the court that the court should issue at least an alternative (if not a peremptory) writ. If the memorandum is longer than 20 pages, it must have an index and a list of authorities. ORAP 11.22.

The relator must include a proof of service on everyone who needs to be served. Service of the petition can be complicated and, as a practical matter, requires consulting with opposing counsel before the relator files the petition. The trial court judge must be served, as well as the opposing party. In a criminal case the Oregon Attorney General—if that office represents the judge—will generally agree to service by mail on itself and the judge. That means the relator can merely mail the materials to the judge and the Attorney General. The relator must serve all parties at the trial court level.

Note that current rules do not ask for a proposed form of the writ (as they did in prior times). (Now, if the court decides to issue a writ the court itself drafts it, often incorporating the petition.)

The opposing party has 14 days to file a memorandum in opposition. ORAP 11.10 (1). The AG usually does not respond in criminal cases, unless the court asks it to respond.

If the opposing party files a memorandum in opposition to the petition, relator may not file a reply unless the court asks for one. ORAP 11.10(1). If the AG brings up something new, the relator can ask to file a reply. If there is a reason to file a reply, it must be filed along with a motion for leave to reply. These should be filed promptly, as the case is at issue as soon as the opposing party files its material.

Once the case is at issue, the court either issues the writ or dismisses the petition. If the court issues an alternative writ, the trial judge is given time to act and issue a certificate of compliance, or to decline to act. In the author’s experience most, but not all, circuit judges respond in some way, but the judge may simply do nothing. If the judge makes a showing (or does nothing), the matter goes to the briefing stage. ORAP 11.15(1). Both sides get 28 days. ORAP 11.15(1)(a). Relator goes first. Note that the prior pleadings become “legally defunct,” and in essence disappear. State ex rel KashmirCorp. v. Schmidt, 291 Or 603, 606 n 1, 633 P2d 791 (1981). Therefore, relator’s brief must stand on its own. The adverse party can file an answering brief. Relator can ask to file a reply brief, but, again, should do so only if the adverse party made a new point in its answering brief. ORAP 11.15(3).

Oral argument is held, and the court decides thereafter. The court will either issue a peremptory writ (relator wins), or dismiss the petition (relator loses).

I. Preservation

As a practical matter, the ordinary rules of preservation apply. The relator must have made a complete record. In theory, because mandamus is under the Supreme Court’s original jurisdiction, the preservation rules may be more lax. See, e.g., Freeman v. Duffy, 328 Or 564, 566 n 1, 983 P2d 533(1999)(adverse party attempted to supplement the trial court record, but the extra material was not necessary for that party to win, so court did not rule about the supplemental material). As a matter of institutional culture, the Oregon Supreme Court will be very disinclined to step in and issue a writ if the issue was not squarely presented to the trial judge.

The practitioner should present the court with the entire relevant record, which may be bulky.

If the facts are in dispute, the Supreme Court will review the record in the light most favorable to the trial court order. State ex rel OHSU v. Haas, 325 Or 492, 942 P2d 261 (1997).

J. When to file? At least in 30 days

Mandamus is a “special proceeding,” neither legal nor equitable. Buell v. Jefferson County Court, 175 Or 402, 152 P2d 578, 154 P2d 188 (1944). Equitable rules generally apply, and the timing is governed by “laches,” rather than a statute of limitations. The court will look to the 30-day statute for notice of appeal as an analogy. State ex rel Redden v. Van Hoomissen, 281 Or 647, 576 P2d 355, reh’g den, 282 Or 415 (1978). Note that it is an analogy, not a rule. Thus, if the petition and related materials are filed within 30 days of the entry of the order at issue, that is probably a safe harbor, if relator is not seeking emergency relief (discussed below).

In some unusual cases, relator could perhaps file after 30 days. “In determining whether a petition for a writ of mandamus is barred by laches, the court considers whether any delay prejudiced the adverse party.” State v. Peekema, 328 Or 342, 976 P2d 1128 (1999); ''see also State ex rel. Kaino v. Or.Comm’n on Judicial Fitness & Disability, 335 Or 633, 74 P3d 1080 (2003).

In sum, it is probably safe to file within 30 days, and it is probably too late to file beyond 30 days. But this is flexible because equitable principles apply. It is best to file as early as possible.

Mootness:“The judicial power under the Oregon Constitution does not extend to moot cases that are ‘capable of repetition, yet evading review.’ Petitioner has requested this court to decide a matter that no longer is a controversy between the parties. As we have explained, Article VII (Amended), section 1, of the Oregon Constitution constrains us from doing so.” Yancy v. Shatzer, 337 Or 345, 363 (2004).

If the issue is going to disappear soon, it is unrealistic to file a petition for a writ. The court loses power to grant relief if there is not a live controversy.

As a practical matter, the justices typically meet in conference three times a month. The dates are a matter of public record, so the clerk will disclose the date. The justices can meet at any time.

To get on the next docket, material must come into the appellate records office, and be in a justice’s hands, at least a few days before the next conference.

The opposing party is given a chance to respond in 14 days. As noted, in a criminal case the AG’s office typically does not respond unless the Supreme Court asks.

The court typically decides whether or not to issue an alternative writ as soon as the case is at issue.

K. The Unwritten Factor: Are the facts and legal questions interesting and important?

As a practical matter, there should be an “oh my goodness!” component to the issue. The practitioner should review the criteria for granting a petition for review for guidance. ORAP 9.07.

The Supreme Court has broad discretion, which it exercises in a broad manner. In one case the court deemed a letter, written by counsel at the same time a petition was filed by a “next friend,” to be a properly filed petition for a writ of mandamus. The court ruled that the “next friend” did not have standing, but issued the alternative writ based on the letter. This was a capital case where the defendant was volunteering to be executed, and counsel’s letter asserted that the defendant was not competent. The matter was unpublished (although it received a good deal of media attention). Statev. Haugen, S059519 (June 29, 2011), seeState v. Haugen, 351 Or 325, 266 P3d 68 (2011)(related case). (Note that orders issued pursuant to death warrant hearings, and orders regarding defendants’ mental capacities, are not appealable. ORS 137.463(8), 137.466(3).)

There is a voodoo factor: some issues simply pique the court’s interest for reasons that are hard for an outsider to discern. The jury pool issue may have been such a situation. SER Click, State v. Rogers, State v. Staudinger, State v. Gallatin, supra. Also, some issues perhaps need to be decided sooner rather than later, such as the validity of sentencing juries. SeeState v. Sawatzky, 339 Or 689, 125 P3d 722(2005).

A century ago the Supreme Court described some of the factors it can consider, including public policy and public importance. Citing its own opinion in a habeas case, it noted it can consider its own case load, and the overall hardship to the relator.

We are of the opinion that it was not the intention of the framers of the late amendment [adding Amended Article VII to the Oregon Constitution] to thrust upon this court the burden of hear- ing, considering, and deciding in the first instance every application for quo warranto, manda- mus, and habeas corpus which should be presented to it. Such a construction would overwhelm us with a mass of original business, including the examination of witnesses, hearing arguments of counsel, and considering the merits of the causes presented, which would interfere seriously with those duties for which this court was primarily constituted, namely, the hearing and decision of cases coming here in the usual manner upon appeal. And we are also of the opinion that, before taking jurisdiction in any of the cases enumerated, we should carefully consider: First, the condi- tion of the business of this court; second, the hardships to the petitioner incident to a denial of the writ; third, whether the petitioner has any plain, speedy, adequate remedy in the circuit court; and, fourth, whether he has a remedy by appeal.

State ex rel Plummer, 97 Or 518, 189 P 401, 191 P 883 (1920), quoting Ex parte Jerman, 57 Or 387, 392,112 P 416 (1910).

L. An Emergency Motion

If relator needs relief in less than 21 days, ORAP 7.35 sets out the rules for filing an emergency petition. Filing an emergency petition is very disruptive to the court and to court staff and should be considered very carefully. If an emergency petition must be filed, try to minimize the disruption to increase the chances of winning. Because a writ is essentially in equity, and because of the culture of the Oregon Supreme Court, the court will expect to see early and full disclosure to the other side and to the trial judge. The appellate rules specifically require early notice.

If something is coming up on an emergency basis, inform the court what is coming. A supervisor in the AG’s office can assign a specific assistant AG, and that will allow relator’s counsel to provide early service of the transcript and order to the AAG immediately before formal filing.

Because of the doctrine of laches, even if the emergency petition is filed within 30 days, relator may have “slept on his rights” if the petition could have been filed earlier. It is unwise to expect the court to rule in an expedited manner if the relator did not file as soon as possible.

A mere quorum of the court can rule on the emergency motion. In fact, the Chief Justice alone can rule (ORS 2.111(6)), but in practice rarely does so. This applies to the motion seeking emergency relief and any emergency motion for a stay. The Chief Justice can rule without giving the other side an opportunity to respond, but this obviously would be rare.

When an emergency motion is pending, counsel should be available by telephone.

An “emergency” is less than 21 days. If the issue will become moot in less than 30 or 40 days, that fact should be noted conspicuously in the pleadings.

Sometimes a potential emergency can be anticipated, such as an anticipated ruling that will be made a few days before a trial is scheduled to begin. In that case, counsel can file a “precautionary” petition for a writ. For example, a renewed competency hearing may be set just before trial, defense counsel may continue to doubt the criminal defendant’s competency, and counsel may expect that the trial judge will rule the defendant is competent.

A “precautionary” petition is simply a petition with the word “precautionary” in the caption.

The “precautionary” petition (with all the supplemental documents) can be filed in the Supreme Court before the ruling is made in the circuit court. Then, if the ruling in the circuit court is favorable, counsel simply notifies the Supreme Court that the petition has become moot. If the ruling in the circuit court is adverse as expected, counsel can immediately file a petition with the ruling attached.

II. Seeking a Writ of Mandamus Before the Circuit Court

These cases tend not to generate opinions, so counsel will be guided by the statute. The Oregon Rules ofCivil Procedureapply.

If a state actor (e.g.,sheriff, medical examiner, county clerk) fails to act when the actor had a duty to act, a writ of mandamus can be filed in circuit court. The issue is whether the actor had a nondiscretionary “duty resulting from an office.” ORS 34.110. Mandamus is not available if the Administrative Procedures Act provides a remedy. ORS 183.310–.750.

The relator must choose the correct venue. For a public body or officer, jurisdiction is in the county where the officer “exercises functions.” ORS 34.120. Against a private individual or corporation, jurisdiction is in the county in which the individual “resides or may be found,” or where the corporation “might be sued in an action.” ORS 34.120.

Procedurally, the process is very different from mandamus proceedings before the Supreme Court. The circuit court does not have discretion to take the case or not. If there is a colorable petition filed, the circuit court must issue an alternative writ and set a hearing date. The relator then serves the alternative writ on the opposing party and the judge. In sum, before the Supreme Court the battle is—at least initially—to obtain the alternative writ. In the circuit court, obtaining it is relatively easy. The battle begins on the merits, after the alternative writ is issued and served.

The author is aware of two mandamus petitions filed in circuit court. In one, the medical examiner refused to give a copy of an autopsy to the victim’s family, reasoning that releasing the report would impede the investigation. However, ORS 146.035(5)provides that the family has a right to the autopsy The Circuit Court of Marion County ruled favorably, and the state released the report to the family. State ex rel Francke v. Lewman, Marion County 89C11476 (1989). Steve Krasik was the trial lawyer.

In the second case, four people found guilty except for insanity were under the jurisdiction of the Psychiatric Security Review Board. The time for their parolelike hearings arrived, and they had hearings. However, the PSRB failed to rule, notwithstanding that ORS 161.346 limits the number of continuances during a hearing, and ORS 161.346provides that the board must rule 15 days after the hearing. After many months passed after the hearing with no decision, the relators obtained alternative writs from the Circuit Court of Marion County. The PSRB then ruled, without a contested mandamus hearing. This was in 2006. Harris Matarazzo was the trial lawyer.

An adverse ruling by the circuit court can be appealed to the Court of Appeals.

III. Seeking a Writ of Habeas Corpus Before the Oregon Supreme Court

There are four sources of authority, the Oregon Constitution, the statute, the appellate rules (minimal), and case law (minimal). When the Supreme Court rules on a petition for a writ of habeas corpus, it rarely generates an opinion. If the writ was granted and no opinion was issued, there may be a description of the decision in the court’s media release. If the writ was denied, there is no opinion at all and just a line with the name of the case in the media release. Thus, this chapter will be somewhat anecdotal, based on the experience of the author.

ORS 34.310provides:

Every person imprisoned or otherwise restrained of liberty, within this state, except in the cases specified in ORS 34.330, may prosecute a writ of habeas corpus to inquire into the cause of such imprisonment or restraint, and if illegal, to be delivered therefrom.

The Oregon Constitutionhas several relevant provisions. Oregon Constitution, Art I, § 14:

Offences [sic], except murder, and treason, shall be bailable by sufficient sureties. Murder or treason, shall not be bailable, when the proof is evident, or the presumption strong.

Oregon Constitution, Art I, § 23:

The privilege of the writ of habeas corpus shall not be suspended unless in case of rebellion, or invasion the public safety require it.

Oregon Constitution, Art. VII (Am), § 2:

The courts, jurisdiction, and judicial system of Oregon, except so far as expressly changed by this amendment, shall remain as at present constituted until otherwise provided by law. But the supreme court may, in its own discretion, take original jurisdiction inmandamus, quo warranto and habeas corpus proceedings.

Should the petition be filed in the circuit court or Supreme Court?

If the sheriff is refusing to release a criminal defendant who should be released, the petition must be filed in circuit court. However, as with the law about mandamus writs, if the adverse ruling is from a circuit judge, the forum is an original proceeding before the Supreme Court. That is the focus of this chapter.

Sometimes selecting the remedy is complicated. If the issue is, for example, the calculation of good time for a jail sentence, jurisdiction is in circuit court, with an appeal to the Court of Appeals if there is an adverse ruling in the circuit court. If the criminal defendant would have been entitled to immediate release if the circuit court ruled in his favor, but it did not, counsel can appeal andfile an original habeas proceeding in the Supreme Court. If the criminal defendant is languishing in the appellate process and the time for release arrives, counsel can file a petition for habeas corpus in the Supreme Court. As with mandamus jurisdiction, the Supreme Court has discretion, and it will be disinclined to issue a writ if there is another mechanism to solve the petitioner’s concern.

The appellate rules which describe the procedure for seeking a writ of habeas corpus before the Supreme Court merely direct the habeas petitioner to follow the mandamus rules “insofar as practicable.” ORAP 11.20.

In a habeas petition the criminal defendant is the “plaintiff ” and the custodian is the “defendant.” It avoids confusion if counsel refers to the parties as “the criminal defendant” and “the sheriff ” in the body of the pleading.

A. The Difference Between Mandamus and Habeas Corpus Jurisdiction

If the desired remedy is release, the remedy is to seek a writ of habeas corpus. To put it another way: if the criminal defendant’s release will moot the issue, the remedy is habeas corpus.

The author is aware of one case where it did not seem to matter which remedy was selected. In State v. Chavez-Gutierrez, 346 Or 724, 217 P3d 182 (2009), the criminal defendant was entitled to release under the 60-day rule. The DA dismissed and reindicted; the trial judge ruled the 60-day period ran from the reindictment, rather than the time of the original arrest. That reading of the statute is flatly wrong.

Should the trial judge be directed to follow the statute setting out the 60-day rule? Mandamus. Should the criminal defendant be released because the statute requires release? Habeas. Does the remedy differ? Not in any obvious way. Perhaps in the abstract, seeking a writ of habeas corpus might get the court’s attention faster, but in this case the criminal defendant got prompt relief seeking a writ of mandamus.

The petition for a writ of mandamus was filed August 7, and the writ was issued September noted only, “Petition for peremptory writ of mandamus allowed.” The case is not further described anywhere, not even in the media release.

In the view of the author, this example does not mean that the Supreme Court is necessarily inclined to be forgiving if counsel picks the wrong remedy.

In a reported case, the Supreme Court held that when the sheriff had miscalculated an inmate’s good time but a correct calculation would not result in immediate release, the remedy was a writ of mandamus, not habeas corpus. Gage v. Maass, 306 Or 196, 203, 759 P2d 1049 (1988).

B. The Writ Is Not Available If There Is Another Remedy

Habeas corpus is unavailable if another “timely remedy is available to the prisoner.” Penrod/Brownv. Cupp, 283 Or 21, 28, 581 P2d 934 (1978), ORS 34.362(2). In theory, then, if mandamus is available, habeas should not be available, or so opine the authors of the “State Habeas Corpus” chapter 28, OSB Criminal Law (Judge Ochoa, David Kuhns, Kathleen Cegla). Mandamus is not available if there is another “plain, speedy and adequate remedy in the ordinary course of the law.” ORS 34.110. (Habeas is in equity.) The example the authors give is for a “credit for time served” issue. If giving the credit would result in immediate release, the remedy is a writ of habeas corpus; otherwise it is a writ of mandamus.

To avoid guessing wrong, when in doubt the practitioner can file two petitions (and one memorandum, excerpt, etc.).

C. The Procedure

It is unrealistic to seek a writ when there is a very short amount of time before the criminal defendant will be released, because once the criminal defendant/habeas plaintiff is released, the habeas case is moot and unreviewable. As noted earlier in this chapter, our Supreme Court will not take a case that is moot even if the issue is “evading review.” Yancy v. Shatzer, 337 Or 345, 363, 97 P3d 1161 (2004).

As a practical matter, if the criminal defendant is due to be released in a month, relief is probably not available for that reason. Also, the Supreme Court is disinclined to interfere at the trial court level if the problem is being solved, as the Garrison case described below illustrates, or, if the problem can be solved at the trial court level, as the Sierra-Sanchez case described below illustrates.

Getting the materials to the Supreme Court takes a little bit of time. The court will almost certainly ask the Attorney General to respond if the court is considering issuing a writ. In one case the court gave the AG only 24 hours (and then issued a writ of habeas corpus).

The Steps:Before seeking a writ in the Supreme Court, counsel must get a written order in the trial court, and have the order entered into the trial court register as reflected in OJIN. This is a rule from the mandamus context, but it stands to reason that it applies in the habeas context because a court can change its mind between an oral ruling and the written order, and the written order controls.4 {Note: 4 “The terms of the written order . . . control over the court’s earlier statements.” Stevens v. Czerniak, 336 Or 392, 397–98, 84P3d 140 (2004).}

To reiterate: if there is any way to fix the problem at the circuit court level, the Supreme Court will not exercise its original jurisdiction.

The practitioner needs to determine the identity of the defendant—usually the county sheriff if the criminal defendant is in jail. County counsel may agree to accept service.

Tip:The sheriff is a nominal defendant, and the AG usually represents the actual adverse party (the prosecutor). If you contact the appellate division first and identify the AAG who will have the case, the county counsel will be more likely to relax and allow service by mail on counsel (rather than personal service on the sheriff ).

Tip:Involve the appellate division of the AG’s office early. Although they will be your adversary, they appreciate the heads up, and the Supreme Court appreciates your courtesy to the AG.

To begin the process, the plaintiff files a petition and a memorandum of law supporting it. This is the plaintiff ’s only opportunity, and it should be done as soon as possible. As described above, mandamus procedure has a 30-day statutory analogy as a required time to file. Habeas involves an ongoing wrong, so there is no filing deadline; but the matter is in equity, so laches controls. The practitioner should make every effort to file within 30 days of the ruling being challenged.

The plaintiff must provide an “excerpt of record” to the Supreme Court, including the transcript of the oral proceeding at issue. Check the appellate rules for the requirements of the excerpt.

The excerpt must include a number of specific documents, including the order at issue and the indictment. See ORAP 11.05(3)(b).

The plaintiff must locate a certified transcriber and convince the transcriber to do the transcription as soon as possible.

Tip:To avoid delay, the practitioner should contact a transcriber directly, rather than rely on the trial court to provide the transcript as the court would do with an appeal.

The plaintiff can file a motion to waive the filing fee if the plaintiff is indigent. Plaintiff must provide the court with a statement from the inmate trust account, to verify that the plaintiff does not have funds for the filing fee ($355 after October 2011). ORS 30.643.

A motion seeking emergency relief is a mechanism to get the court’s attention before 21 days have passed. From the author’s observation, the court does not consider it an “emergency” when a criminal defendant is in custody without more than a pending habeas petition.

If counsel anticipates an emergency situation will come up, it is possible to file a “precautionary” petition for a writ of habeas corpus, as described in the mandamus portion of this chapter.

Counsel should follow the appellate rules to the letter (extrapolating from the mandamus rules). For example, counsel should attach a copy of the written order from the trial court to the petition. ORAP 11.05(2)(f ).

It is possible to calculate when the court will first consider the case, as described in the mandamus portion of this chapter. In theory the opponent gets 14 days to respond, although the court may give less. The case is assigned to a specific justice (but not the Chief Justice). Of course, just because the court is considering the case does not necessarily mean it will decide it then.

This is an extraordinary remedy. If there is any way the issue can be remedied in the circuit court, the Supreme Court will not take the case.

If there is a good issue, and counsel timely files a petition for a writ, good things may happen at the circuit court level. However, there is the danger that if the petition is weak or frivolous, credibility may suffer.

D. A Criminal Defendant Can Be Wrongfully in Custody for Any Number of Reasons

As previously noted, the Supreme Court does not issue opinions, typically, in habeas cases. For that reason, this portion of the chapter is necessarily anecdotal.

A number of cases involve the so-called 60-day rule, which provides that a defendant is entitled to a trial or to release 60 days after arrest, which in some cases can be extended to 180 days. ORS 136.290–.295.

1. The 60-Day Rule

There is a reported case where the trial court refused to release a criminal defendant under the because defense counsel developed a conflict and could no longer remain on the case. The criminal defendant filed a petition for a writ of habeas corpus before the Supreme Court, and that court released him. Brophy v. Burks, 307 Or 62, 762 P2d 1017 (1988). The court noted that the criminal defendant is not responsible for his lawyer’s ethical conflicts.

2. The 60-Day Rule When Late Discovery Causes Delay

Garrison v. Dotson, Sheriff of Lincoln County, S058069 (2010), presents a fairly extreme example of what the author suspects is a fairly common problem. At the trial court level in this case, just as the 180 days was about to run, the state provided 14 boxes of Department of Human Services documents for an in camerareview. Once that occurred, the defense lawyer had to seek more time to prepare, past the 60-day limit, indeed past the 180-day limit. ORS 136.290 to 136.295(the 60/180-day rule). The defense argued that because the delay was due to the late discovery, the criminal defendant should be released as the defense prepared for trial, even though it was the defense which needed the time. As the court turned documents over to the defense (and there were nearly 2,000 in the end), the defendant had to do more investigation. A petition for a writ of habeas corpus was filed in the Supreme Court.

As the petition was pending before the Supreme Court, the trial judge worked nights and weekends to do the in camerareview, but the trial court declined to release the defendant.

Before the Supreme Court, the habeas plaintiff/criminal defendant argued that ORS 136.290 to 136.295 provides that a defendant is entitled to a trial or release in 60 days, with two extensions (absent unusual circumstances, which were not present). A defendant must be tried or released in 180 days, absent his express consent. As noted, the defense needed time past the 180 days only because of delay, not attributable to the defendant, in providing constitutionally mandated discovery.

The question was whether the defense request for more time to get discovery, and then to investigate based on the new discovery, constituted statutory “consent” to the delay?

The habeas plaintiff/criminal defendant argued he had not consented to the delay. He claimed that he was entitled to release and, further, that he was entitled to bail he could actually post, or to be released with release conditions.

The Supreme Court denied the writ, and the trial judge finished the in camerareview fairly promptly thereafter and provided a great many documents to the defense. Trial occurred a few months later.

In the view of this author, if the judge had not finished the review, at some point the Supreme Court would have entertained a renewed petition. Elizabeth J.C. Baker was the trial attorney.

3. Extradition — The 60-Day Rule and the Time it Takes to Extradite the Criminal Defendant

In Owens v. Giusto, Sheriff of Multnomah County, S055188 (2007), the habeas plaintiff/criminal defendant was arrested in Minnesota on May 2, 2007, waived, and was extradited to Oregon. She remained in custody. Her trial date was set to be 72 days after she was arrested. The state argued it could try her within 60 days of her arrival in Oregon on July 12, 2007, because she had been extradited “with all practicable speed,” as required in the 60-day rule (quoted below), ORS136.295.

In other words, if it took a period of 72 days to move the plaintiff from Minneapolis to Portland, does that qualify as the use of “all practicable speed”?

The Supreme Court issued the writ releasing the plaintiff on August 28, 2007, about a week after the petition for habeas was filed. The matter is reported only in the court’s media release of August 27, 2007. Despite this author’s request, nothing was published.

After the case was over, the state sought legislation to change the statute to provide a “safe harbor.” The state partially succeeded.

The statute at issue in the Owenscase, ORS 136.295(a) (2008), provides:

(2)(a) If the defendant is extradited from another jurisdiction, the 60-day period shall not commence until the defendant enters the State of Oregon, provided that law enforcement authorities from the other jurisdiction and this state have conducted theextradition with all practicable speed''. The original 60-day period shall not be extended more than an additional 60 days, except where delay has been caused by the defendant in opposing the extradition.

The statutory change added ORS 136.295 (2)(b) (effective 1/1/10):

(b) For purposes of this subsection, an extradition is presumedto have been conducted with all practicable speed if it has been conducted within 90 daysafter the date the defendant has been delivered to an agent of this state.

This legislation, 2009 HB 2269 (ORS 136.295(2)(b)), above, was intended to be an Owens“fix” but, to quote Gail Meyer’s summary in her OCDLA 2009 Legislative Analysis, “Legislative history before the House Judiciary Committee on March 11, 2009 [sic? February 19?], indicates that the factual scenario at issue in Owens v. Giustowould not warrant application of the presumption.” (The bill was discussed on February 19, 2009, before the House Judiciary Committee (it was the first measure discussed, for 21 minutes). Audio of the hearings are on the legislature’s website.)

Oregon uses the US Marshall services to transport prisoners from out of state. There is no apparent source of the “90 days” that ultimately appeared in the statute (down from 180 originally proposed). It was clear that a defendant can challenge the time it takes to transport, even within 90 days. At the hearing, the AG and OCDLA lobbyists testified, as did Erin Kirkwood (Owens’s trial attorney). Kirkwood described Owens’s journey, which was circuitous. Owens had children, who needed care. Owens was released after the writ with restrictions and appeared for trial.

Representative Barton expressly stated that the new statute only sets out a presumption, and that the fact pattern in Owenswould not be “practicable speed” under the new law. He described Owens’s “fact pattern” as “tortured” and “wholly unreasonable.” Another representative expressly agreed.

Counsel may wish to cite this legislative history to the trial court if this statute is at issue. ORS 174.020.

4. The 60-Day Rule and a Hung Jury

Poppe v. Dotson, Lincoln County Sheriff, S057480 (2009), involved the 60-day rule and how to calculate the time after a trial with a hung jury.

The criminal defendant was arrested June 18, 2008, and he sought a speedy trial. There were various delays. Trial began March 2, 2009, and ended with an acquittal on two counts and a hung jury on the remaining counts, on April 10, 2009. The state stated its intention to retry the case, starting September 9, 2009. The criminal defendant sought an immediate trial date in the trial court, or an immediate release.

When both were denied, the criminal defendant filed a petition for a writ of habeas corpus on June 16, 2009. He argued that, not counting delays attributable to the defendant, and not counting the days in trial, as of May 1, 2009, 180 days had passed since his arrest. Thus, he was entitled to be released from custody immediately under ORS 136.290 to .295 (the 60/180 day rule).

He argued that after a hung jury he could not be held indefinitely pending retrial. That is, when a trial commenced before the ORS 136-290–.295 period had run, and there was a mistrial because the jury was unable to reach a verdict, the 180-day clock resumed running when the jury was discharged. Thus, he was entitled to an immediate retrial or release.

He also argued that the answer did not change because he was charged with “Measure 11” [ORS 137.700–.707] felony sex offenses. He was absolutely indigent and could not even post $50. He argued, without contradiction, that he fit all the statutory (ORS 135.230(7) and(11)) and common law release criteria, except that he was charged with serious offenses. The trial court declined to set bail under $200,000, because of the seriousness of the offenses. The plaintiff argued that the Oregon Constitution, Article I, sections 14 and 16, as applied in the case, prohibited the denial of release simply because the plaintiff could not post bail because he was indigent. Applying ORS 135.240(5) would violate the Oregon Constitution, Article I, section 14 and 16.

The Supreme Court never considered the petition because the day after the habeas petition arrived in Salem and Newport, the DA moved to dismiss the case, and Poppe was released. Susan Reese and Steve Lovejoy were the trial lawyers.

5. The Criminal Defendant Is Unable to Aid and Assist for More Than Three Years

As criminal courts increasingly deal with the seriously mentally ill, this problem may come up more often.

When a person is charged with a crime and is unable to aid and assist for over three years, ORS 161.370provides that the indictment must be dismissed without prejudice. If the statute of limitations has not run, the state may reindict.

In Sierra-Sanchez v. Skipper, Multnomah County Sheriff, S057647 (2009), the criminal defendant was developmentally delayed, brain-injured, and schizophrenic. He was charged with murdering his brother and raping his own girlfriend in 2003. He was unable to aid and assist for over three years. Both the Oregon State Hospital and Multnomah County were unwilling to civilly commit him. The 2003 indictment was dismissed without prejudice as required under ORS 161.370(6), and he was reindicted in June 2009. The question put to the Oregon Supreme Court thereafter was: must the defendant be released? Or, can he be held either in custody of the jail, or at the state hospital under a treat-until-fit order?

The habeas plantiff/criminal defendant argued that ORS 161.370 provides exactly two options regarding the person’s custody after three years have passed: he may be civilly committed or he may be discharged.

The Supreme Court declined to issue a writ of habeas corpus on September 17, 2009. Justice Kistler would have allowed the petition. There was no reported decision.

In the view of the author, the petition was dismissed because the Supreme Court thought the matter was not yet ripe. The trial judge had not ruled that the defendant would never be able to aid and assist. Lack-of-ripeness was the general thrust of the state’s memo in opposition.

Once the trial court ruled that there was no “substantial probability that the defendant, in the foreseeable future, will gain or regain the capacity to stand trial” [ORS 161.370(9)], Multnomah County could civilly commit him. That is, there was still room to fix this problem at the circuit court level.

The ultimate resolution was a GEI plea to manslaughter, after which the criminal defendant would be deported. Dave Audet and Chris Burris were the trial lawyers for years.

6. Other Possible Grounds for a Habeas Writ

a.Error During a Bail Hearing

If the trial judge made an error during the bail hearing that mattered to the outcome of the hearing, habeas would be the forum. For example, it is an open question as to whether a criminal defendant can present exculpatory material in a murder bail hearing, where the state must prove that the proof is evident, or the presumption is strong, that the defendant is guilty of murder. ORS 135.240. For that reason, one could argue that the defendant should be allowed to show he was not guilty of murder. The Supreme Court denied a petition raising the issue on March 8, 2012, although Justices De Muniz and Durham would have allowed it. Cockrell v. Roberts, S059831. Jenny Cooke and Robert L. Huggins, Jr, were the trial lawyers.

b.Release from Custody Pending a State’s Appeal

ORS 138.160; 135.230–.295. An appeal by the state will delay the trial. The regular release rules apply, and the same rules would continue to apply for seeking a writ of habeas corpus. One could argue that the criminal defendant should have the release rules applied more generously to him in this context, particularly if the crime is relatively minor and the state’s appeal is weak.

c.Release Pending the Defendant’s Appeal After Conviction

After conviction, the trial court has discretion to release pending appeal. State v. Wimber, 108

Or App 1, 814 P2d 169 (1991). ORS 138.135. ORS 135.285. There is no appeal from a denial of post-trial release; habeas may be a remedy.

Because we hold that this court has no statutory authority to review a trial court’s decision regarding release pending appeal, either in an appeal of the judgment of conviction and sentence or by way of an appeal of the order embodying the release decision, grounds may exist for the Supreme Court’s exercise of original habeas corpus jurisdiction under the Oregon Constitution, Article VII (amended), section 2. We need not discuss that issue.

Wimber, 108 Or App at 4 n.3.

The Court of Appeals has no authority to stay probation while appeal is pending. State v.Popp, 118 Or App 508, 848 P2d 134 (1993). But note that this is a pre-Guidelines case— now probation is a “sentence.”

There is no constitutional right to release or bail pending an appeal after a conviction. Priest v. Pearce, 314 Or 411, 840 P2d 65 (1992). It would have to be a very unusual fact pattern for the court to consider a habeas petition.

d.The Trial Court Set Excessive Bail

Except for cases of murder and treason, a criminal defendant has a constitutional right to release under the least onerous conditions, and, arguably, bail in an amount he cannot possibly post would be appropriate for a habeas writ. See, e.g.,Liberman v. Burks, 293 Or457, 650 P2d 83 (1982).

This is true even if the defendant is charged with a Measure 11 offense. ''See State v.Sutherland, 329 Or 359, 987 P2d 501 (1999)(mandamus).

The “total amount of security to be posted must be no more than is necessary to reasonably assure the attendance of the person charged at trial. . . . It is not to be set so as to make it impossible, as a practical matter, for a prisoner to secure release.” Gillmore v. Pearce,302 Or 572, 580, 731 P2d 1039 (1987). In setting the amount, the trial court may consider the seriousness of the charges and the potential sentence, but only to factor in the defendant’s incentive to flee. Oregon does not allow preventive detention. To the author’s knowledge, this point has not been explored in recent years.

The rule for release is different for a civil contemnor. Strain v. Griffin, 290 Or 535, 612P2d 106 (1981). “The issue is whether a civil contemnor [who failed to follow an order in a child custody dispute] may stay his imprisonment for the contempt by filing notice of appeal from the order of contempt, supported by an undertaking for costs on appeal. We hold that he may.”

e.Time in Custody Has Expired

SeeStrong v. Gladden, 225 Or 345, 358 P2d 520 (1961)(dicta) (credit for time served at issue).

f.(In Theory) Unreasonable Conditions of Release

If the condition keeps the criminal defendant in custody, the remedy is probably to seek a writ of habeas corpus. If the criminal defendant is out of custody, the remedy is probably mandamus.

As a matter of Supreme Court culture, and deference to the trial court’s discretion (and in any event discretion is not reviewable in mandamus), this probably has a very low likelihood of attracting the Supreme Court’s interest. Perhaps if the defendant was ordered to refrain from contacting a co-defendant, when such contact was necessary for trial preparation?

Any condition would have to be extremely onerous and prejudicial. Refraining from using lawfully prescribed medical marijuana?
