Oral Advocacy

Dave Ferry

I. Overview

Appellate oral arguments may be brief, but they can be a vital component of appellate advocacy. Oral arguments before the Oregon Court of Appealsand Oregon Supreme Courtare markedly different from the typical motions argument before a trial court. Practitioners new to appellate practice should take the opportunity to attend the arguments of other appellate practitioners to gain an understanding of the exercise. The Court of Appeals schedules one day each month for the arguments of clients represented by the Appellate Divisionof the Office of Public Defense Services(OPDS-AD). OPDS argument day consists entirely of criminal matters argued by full-time criminal appellate counsel on both sides. Thus, OPDS argument days provide excellent opportunities to observe criminal case oral advocacy. For those who cannot attend in person, the Oregon Supreme Court website now allows live streaming of oral arguments.

II. A Note on Style

Oregon appellate courts prefer a tone of cordial conversation. Formalistic recitation and impassioned pleading are disfavored and ineffective. Beyond that, there are as many styles of oral argument as there are attorneys who argue. However, this chapter will provide important guidelines for preparing and presenting your case effectively, whatever personal style you bring to the table.

As a general rule of style for Oregon appellate arguments, one should be prepared to have a discussionwith the court rather than to give a speech. Many appellate advocates draft speechlike presentations as part of their preparation. They don’t deliver them. Oral argument is a conversation requiring responsiveness to the court’s questions and interests.

Consequently, it may be more useful to imagine the argument as an important job interview rather than as a speaking engagement. Like the interview environment, the oral argument setting requires you to be flexible enough to follow the questioners’ lead while still finding opportunities to make those points that you believe to be critical to your case. You must also be prepared to read the court and respond with different perspectives that aid your client’s position when members of the court seem to be aligned against it. Oral argument should be about persuasive reasoning and offering the best perspective in a professional environment.

Thus, oral argument is neither about presenting a position to a passive audience nor confronting an opponent or hostile court. It is about having a conversation that serves the court’s need to understand your position, the strengths of your arguments, and your response to any perceived weaknesses in your case. To effectively perform that role, it is imperative that you endeavor to respondto every question. In doing so, you should seek the answer that best serves your client’s position. However, it is important to be measured and rational. If you do not know the answer to a question, admit it (and offer to find an answer after the argument if it seems appropriate). If your position has a weakness, do not refuse to acknowledge its existence—address it head on. If you cannot fully distinguish a case in your way, it may be necessary to explain why it should be overruled.

As an appellate advocate, your credibility is important. Thus, it is important that you not over sell your position, stretch or alter the “facts,” or mischaracterize the law. Overzealousness is self-defeating. Do not portray the narrowest question in the case—the point that could go either way—as obvious or beyond question. Do not trivialize cases that stand against you (often written by members of your panel) nor your opponent’s arguments. Explain why the pivotal questions should be answered in your favor, distinguish the bad cases, and point out the difference between your view and your opponent’s. Again, be careful notto stretch the facts or the law beyond credibility. Such practices are not only ineffective, but they may damage your credibility in the instant case andfuture cases.

III. Preparation

A. Introduction

Appellate argument is ninety percent preparation and ten percent presentation. Thorough preparation is required so you can maintain flexibility and effectively answer the questions posed. While you have some input over the direction of the argument, and the Court of Appeals often allows appellants to focus the argument, you must be fully informed regarding the facts, law, and standards of review applicable to all assignments of error in the briefs.

Preparation includes careful review of the file. Familiarize yourself again with the facts and arguments in the briefs. Clarify any ambiguities by returning to the original source documents and reviewing the precise text in your record. Reread the relevant motions and arguments made before the trial court. Reread the relevant cases, and update your research. For the complex case, practice sessions can be an invaluable tool. Moot courts can help you identify the critical pieces of your argument, the facts and legal positions you cannot concede without losing your case, and prepare for the court’s questions. Solo practitioners and others who do not have access to colleagues ready and able to assist may request assistance from OPDS in conducting moot court sessions. OPDSregularly holds mooting sessions for upcoming arguments, including cases to be argued by non-staff attorneys.

B. Targeting the Argument

The first step in the preparation process is to review the briefs and decide which assignments one intends to argue. Note that oral argument is no longer an automatic occurrence. Under the new ORAP 6.05,the Court of Appealswill notautomatically schedule a case for oral argument. Rather, cases generally will be submitted to the court without oral argument unless argument is specifically requested.

Because the period allowed for argument is brief, it is often impractical to argue all of the assignments of error. Generally, because of the time constraints, oral argument should be focused on the strongest arguments with the best chance of success.1 {Note: 1 Sometimes the difference in available remedies may complicate your analysis regarding which arguments to focus on. For example, when the strongest argument for error involves only a minor sentence element, it may be advisable to focus on a slightly weaker assignment that carries the possibility of a reversal of conviction.}

Further, it is usually advisable to create a list of the most critical points within those arguments and structure your argument around those points. The Court of Appeals will usually follow your lead when you inform them from the outset that you intend to narrow your argument to a particular assignment, or a particular issue within an assignment, and rely on the arguments in your brief for other issues.2 {Note: 2 This is only true to the extent your assessment of the strength and/or dispositive nature of particular assignments approximates the court’s. Thus, if the court believes that the argument you plan to focus on has a clear resolution, and that an assignment you wished to ignore presents a close and interesting question, then your announced plan is likely to be ignored.}

Remember, however, that regardless of the focus youdesire, all assignments of error are available to the court for questioning during oral argument. Accordingly, you must be familiar with the facts, law, and standards of review relevant to the entire case.

C. What to Know: The Facts

As noted elsewhere in the chapter, the facts relevant to any appeal are only those contained in the record. Reference to information not contained in the record is inappropriate and should be strictly avoided.

It is also important to regularly remind oneself that “facts” and “evidence” are different things. Some “evidence” may be considered as “fact” for purposes of appeal depending upon the standard of review that applies to the assigned error. For example, in reviewing the legality of a motion for judgment of acquittal (MJOA), all facts are taken in the light most favorable to the state (the prevailing party), including all reasonableinferences necessary to support the factual scenario behind conviction. Consequently, testimonial evidence that contradicts the state’s theory of liability is presumed to have been disbelieved—it cannot be taken as “fact” no matter how persuasive you found it. Thus, in appealing the denial of a MJOA, counsel points out missing “facts” needed to satisfy the elements of the crime, not contrary “evidence.” That is, counsel must be able to argue that, regarding at least one element of the offense, no reasonable inference can be drawn from the evidence in the record to support the existence of the element. Contrary evidence is irrelevant to that question.

In preparing for oral argument, counsel should also be prepared to cite to the relevant portions of the record that support the desired characterization of the evidence. It is generally good practice to bring the transcripts to oral argument, but time to search through them for relevant text during the argument is unlikely. Consequently, counsel should prepare lists of relevant citations, make notes that contain transcript pages, post flags in the transcripts themselves, or use some other method that allows quick access to important pages. Having transcript references is especially important if the state’s brief or argument contains “facts” that the defense contests. That is, it is far more helpful to the court to provide a transcript page for review of the actual evidence in the record (along with a quick recounting of what it states), than it is to merely assert that the state is mistaken in its representation.

D. The Law

The oral advocate is also expected to be knowledgeable about the law applicable to the case. This plainly includes the cases cited by both parties in support of the arguments at issue. Remember, as well, that while you may intend only to focus your argument on particular assignments of error, the court is free to consider any issues raised in the briefs. Consequently, it is risky to ignore the cases and law at issue in assignments of error that are not part of your primary focus.

Because there is always a lag between the filing of briefs and the argument date, additional research is imperative to preparation. Discovery of new cases relevant to your arguments that have been issued since the filing of the briefs can be provided to the court in a Memorandum of Additional Authorities filed in advance of the argument. Furthermore, appellate courts are often quite familiar with any just- issued cases and will ask about them. Thus, it is good practice to review the cases issued in the days preceding argument to consider whether such new cases should or should not impact yours.

E. The Standard of Review

The standard of review is a required component of the brief, so it will have been considered prior to oral argument.3 {Note: 3 For further discussion of what the standard of review is, see Chapter 5, “Briefing.”}

However, it is critical that the appellate advocate be very familiar with the appropriate standard of review for each of the legal issues presented, and be prepared to explain and advocate for standards that benefit the client whenever there reasonably could be a dispute. The appellate courts frequently focus questions on the standard of review and its impact on their resolution of the case and its issues.

The standard of review also provides the lens through which the court will view a case, and thus frames the issues for oral argument. For example, if a case is reviewed for an abuse of discretion, the advocate must be prepared to explain why the trial court ruling was not among the options available to a trial court with discretion. In urging reversal of the denial of a motion for judgment of acquittal, the advocate must be prepared to explain why the evidence was insufficient even if the state won every factual dispute in the evidence(i.e., the facts are taken in “the light most favorable to the state”). On the other hand, when arguing that the trial court erred in denying an instruction requested by the defense, the advocate must be prepared to characterize the facts in the light favorable to that instruction. This likely will entail fighting the state’s habitual assumption that the evidence should be viewed in its favor as the winning party below.

IV. The Argument

A. Procedures

Always arrive for oral argument at least twenty minutes before the start of the session during which your argument is scheduled. Call ahead if an emergency prevents this.

Upon arrival, you must check in with the clerk. When you are the appellant, you must indicate how much time you wish to reserve for rebuttal (up to five minutes). Most advocates find it advisable to request the full five minutes for rebuttal. Despite the reservation, however, going over time during the primary argument will cut into your rebuttal time. After you have checked in, defense attorneys sit to the left as you enter (to the judges’ right). If you are first on the docket, you may sit at the table near the podium on your side.

Both the Oregon Court of Appealsand Oregon Supreme Courtoperate with the same lighting system. When the white light on your podium is lit, you have two minutes of argument time remaining. When the red light comes on, your time is up. If you are in the middle of answering a question, you should ask the court’s permission to quickly conclude your response. Otherwise, you should complete your statement and stop.

B. Demeanor

At the argument, it is important to present a respectful and professional demeanor. You are an officer of the court, and the primary purpose of argument, in addition to advocating for the client, is to serve as a resource for the court. Do not talk over judges, avoid or ignore questions from the court, adopt a dismissive or disrespectful tone of voice, disparage opposing counsel, or distort the trial court record or precedent. Such actions are unprofessional, unlikely to advance the client’s cause, and will damage your reputation in the court.

A professional demeanor also requires presentation of arguments that are persuasive because of their logic, not because of your personal vouching or feelings. Thus phrases like “I think,” “I feel,” “it doesn’t seem fair to me,” and “in my opinion” should be stricken from your vocabulary as much as possible. When appropriate, such phrases should be replaced with: “the law requires,” “the legislature intended,” “the case dictates,” “the ruling violated the fundamental fairness protected by the Due Process Clause,” and “in the Supreme Court’s opinion in .”

C. Opening

While Oregon’s arguments are generally less formal in tone than in many appellate courts, all arguments traditionally begin with the same line: “May it please the court.” You should then introduce yourself by name and indicate the party you are representing.

From that point, there are many different views on how to begin your argument. This author recommends that you start with a pithy and concise opening line that indicates what you believe is the primary issue in your case. Before fleshing out the argument any further, you should address all procedural matters. That includes telling the court which assignments of error you wish to address and in which order, and informing the court of any recent filings (like memoranda of additional authority) or changes in position (recent mootness of any particular arguments, any concessions). An early statement summarizing the arguments you wish to present may be essential because the court’s questioning may take you in unexpected directions throughout much, if not all, of your argument time. Also, any arguments that are not at least mentioned in your argument (or your opponent’s) generally cannot be raised during rebuttal.

It is often unnecessary to provide a detailed factual account of the case at the beginning of the argument. In cases that revolve around plain legal disputes, it is best to go directly to the point and provide a factual summary only when necessary to the issue or when requested by the court. In some cases, like stop and suppression cases and others involving “totality of the circumstances” tests, the facts are central to the resolution of the dispute. In such cases you will need to provide a succinct summary of the necessary facts at or near the beginning of your argument.

D. Other Notes on Arguing

Remember, oral argument is an interactive process. It is critical that you observe and “read” the court while you argue. Thus, if the judges seem to “get it” on certain points from the get-go—if they have no questions about a point or make statements that demonstrate they fully understand that component of the argument—then you should move on and focus on the areas where they still have questions.

When the court has questions, and it always does, you must wait until a judge has finished the entire question before answering. This provides a measure of respect and insures that the question is properly understood. You should use the court’s questions as an opportunity to explain and advance your argument, but not at the expense of integrity and professionalism. If you do notunderstand the question, request clarification. If the court asks a question requiring a yes or no answer, you should answer “Yes” or “No” beforeattempting further explanation.

Rebuttal is provided to allow the appellant to rebut arguments raised by opposing counsel. Occasionally, you may feel a need to revisit a point mentioned in your initial argument that you did not have time to address.4 {Note: 4 Remember, if you did notmention an issue in your initial argument and it does not come up during your opponent’s argument, then it is not appropriate for rebuttal. For that reason you should at least try to highlight all the issues you wish to cover in your argument near the beginning.}

However, it is often the better course to keep rebuttal focused on the opposition’s arguments, especially those with which the court was most involved, and answering the court’s questions. Thus, use the court’s questions, especially its hardest questions to your opponent, to direct your rebuttal and act as “springboards” for your arguments.

Two books on appellate advocacy contain excellent advice on the preparation and delivery of oral argument. The first is Judge Ruggero Alderist’s, Winning on Appeal: Better Briefs and Oral Argument(NITA, 2d ed: 2003). The other is Bryan Garner and Justice Antonin Scalia’s, Making Your Case: The Art of Persuading Judges(Thomas West, 2008).
