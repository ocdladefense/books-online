Post-Conviction Relief

Noel Grefenson

I. History and Authority

A. Federal Mandate

In 1867, Congress substantially increased the jurisdiction of federal courts to issue the writ of habeas corpus by authorizing its issuance “in all cases,” state or federal, “where any person may be restrained of his or her liberty in violation of the constitution, or of any treaty or law of the United States.” In deference to state court jurisdiction, however, federal law required state prisoners to exhaust their state remedies before bringing them to federal court. In 1948, the United States Supreme Court warned the states that this exhaustion requirement presupposed the existence of adequate state remedies for raising federal claims. Young v. Ragen, 337 U.S. 235, 239, 69 S Ct 1073, 93 L Ed 1333 (1948). In an effort to simplify and standardize state post-conviction remedies to meet the federal mandate, the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws completed the Uniform Post- Conviction Procedure Act (UPCPA) in 1955.

B. The Oregon Post-Conviction Hearing Act

In 1959, Oregon responded to the federal mandate by passing the Oregon Post-Conviction Hearing Act (PCHA), codified at ORS 138.510–138.680. With the exception of habeas corpus, the PCHA abolishes all common law post-conviction remedies and provides a single detailed and unitary procedure to collaterally attack a criminal conviction. Strong v. Gladden, 225 Or 345, 348, 358P2d 520 (1961). The PCHA is taken from the UPCPA. SeeDatt v. Hill, 347 Or 672, 227 P3d 714(2010). See also Jack G. Collins and Carl R. Neil, “The Oregon Postconviction Hearing Act,” 39 Or. L. Rev. 337, 340–41 (1960).

II. Grounds for Relief

ORS 138.530(1)(a)–(d)establish four grounds for post-conviction relief: Denial of state and/or federal constitutional rights, lack of jurisdiction, excessive sentence, or the unconstitutionality of a statute. Datt v.Hill, 347 Or 672, 227 P3d 714 (2010).

A. Denial of State/Federal Rights

ORS 138.530(1)(a) provides relief upon proof of “[a] substantial denial in the proceedings resulting in petitioner’s conviction, or in the appellate review thereof, of petitioner’s rights under the Constitution of the United States, or under the Constitution of the State of Oregon, or both, and which denial rendered the conviction void.”

1. Ineffective Assistance of Trial Counsel

The vast majority of Oregon post-conviction claims allege ineffective assistance of trial counsel. The Oregon and federal constitutions each guarantee the right to effective assistance of trial counsel, and PCR counsel should always allege violations of both constitutions when pleading such a claim.

a.Oregon Constitution

To establish a violation of the right to “adequate” assistance of counsel under Article 1, sec. 11, of the Oregon Constitution, the petitioner must prove that counsel did not exercise reasonable professional judgment or skill, resulting in actual prejudice. Trujillo v. Maass, 312Or 431, 435, 822 P2d 703 (1991). Prejudice occurs where counsel’s acts or omissions have a tendency to affect the result of the prosecution. Lichau v. Baldwin, 333 Or 350, 359, 39 P3d851 (2002). A reviewing court, however, will not second-guess a lawyer’s tactical decisions unless those decisions reflect an absence or suspension of professional skill and judgment. Krummacher v. Gierloff, 290 Or 867, 875-876, 627 P2d 458 (1981).

b.Federal Constitution

To establish a violation of the right to “effective” assistance of counsel under the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution, the petitioner must prove that counsel’s performance was deficient and that the deficiency prejudiced his defense. Stricklandv. Washington, 466 US 668, 687, 104 S Ct 2052, 80 L Ed2d 674 (1984). Stricklandrequires a two-part inquiry, whether a) counsel’s representation fell below an objective standard of reasonableness, and b) there is a reasonable probability that, but for counsel’s errors, the result of the proceeding would have been different. A reasonable probability is a probability sufficient to undermine confidence in the outcome. Under Strickland,trial counsel’s tactical decisions are “virtually unchallengeable.” Id. at 690, 104 S Ct 2052.

c.Other Considerations

Specific errors by trial counsel held sufficient to warrant post-conviction relief are too numerous to list in this outline. The following represent some of the more common problem areas:

i) Failure to provide competent advice and representation during plea proceedings. Hartzogv. Keeney, 304 Or 57, 742 P2d 600 (1987), Long v. State, 130 Or App 198, 880 P2d 509(1994). Hill v. Lockhart, 474 US 52, 62, 106 S Ct 366, 88 L Ed2d 203 (1985).

ii) Failure to investigate: Stevens v. State, 322 Or 101, 902 P2d 1137, (1995), Mellem v. State,106 Or App 642, 809 P2d 1348 (1991). Wiggins v. Smith, 539 US 510, 123 S Ct 2527,156 L Ed2d 471 (2003).

iii) Errors at trial: Aquino v. Baldwin, 163 Or App 452, 991 P2d 41 (1999).

iv) Errors at sentencing: Gordon v. Hall, 232 Or App 174, 221 P3d 763 (2009), Layton v.Hall, 181 Or App 581, 591-92, 47 P3d 898 (2002),

2. Ineffective Assistance of Appellate Counsel

a.Oregon Constitution

Guinn v. Cupp, 304 Or 488, 747 P2d 984 (1987), sets forth the standard for violation of the right to adequate assistance of appellate counsel under Article 1, sec. 11, of the Oregon Constitution. Under Guinn, “[a] party seeking post-conviction relief stemming from a claim of inadequate assistance of appellate counsel for failing to assert claimed error must establish that competent appellate counsel would have asserted the claim and that, had the error been raised, it is more probable than not that the result would have been different.”

b.Federal Constitution

Strickland, supra, provides the standard for Fourteenth Amendment claims of ineffective assistance of counsel on appeal. A petitioner must show that counsel’s failure to find and raise meritorious issues on appeal was objectively unreasonable, and that absent this failure there is a reasonable probability of prevailing on appeal. Strickland, 466 U.S., at 694, Smith v.Robbins, 528 US 259, 285, 120 S Ct 746, 145 L Ed2d 756 (2000).

Practice Tip

The right to effective assistance of appellate counsel arises under the Fourteenth Amendment due process clause, not the Sixth Amendment right to counsel. Evitts v. Lucey, 469 US 387, 105 S Ct 830, 83 L E2d 821 (1985).

3. Invalid Guilty or No Contest Pleas

A plea of guilty or no contest necessarily waives multiple fundamental state and federal constitutional rights. These rights include confrontation of witnesses, compulsory process, self- incrimination and the presumption of innocence.

a.Oregon Constitution

To waive the substantive rights contained in Article 1, secs 11 and 12, of the OregonConstitution, a defendant must have knowledge of the legal consequences of the plea. Lyonsv. Pearce, 298 Or 554, 694 P2d 969 (1985).

b.Federal Constitution

Because guilty pleas implicate both the Fifth and Sixth Amendments of the federal constitution they must be knowing, voluntary and intelligent. Brady v. United States, 397US 742, 748, 90 S Ct 1463, 1468, 25 L Ed2d 747 (1970). Pleas which are not voluntary and knowing also violate the right to due process under the Fourteenth Amendment. McCarthy v. United States, 394 US at 465, 89 S Ct 1171, 22 L Ed 2d 425 (1969). Under the Fourteenth Amendment, the minimum standards for valid guilty pleas apply in state criminal proceedings. Boykin v. Alabama, 395 US 238, 89 S Ct 1709, 23 L Ed2d 274 (1969)

III.Northand Palmer

ORS 138.550(1)provides that a petitioner’s failure to seek appellate review or raise the matters alleged in the petition at trial shall not affect the availability of post-conviction relief. In Palmer v. State ofOregon, 318 Or 352, 354, 867 P2d 1368, 1370 (1994), the Oregon Supreme Court rejects a literal reading of this statutory provision, stating it would completely erode the long-standing procedural rule requiring a contemporaneous objection to the introduction of inadmissible evidence at trial. Instead, the court held that ORS 138.550(1) was designed to prevent assertion of the procedural rule requiring a contemporaneous objection to inadmissible evidence in very limited situations: “ ‘[W]here the right subsequently sought to be asserted was not generally recognized to be in existence at the time of trial; where counsel was excusably unaware of facts which would have disclosed a basis for the assertion of the right; and where duress or coercion prevented assertion of the right. Also, the failure to assert the right would not be a bar where counsel was incompetent or was guilty of bad faith.’ ” (quoting North v. Cupp,254 Or 451, 457, 461 P2d 271 (1969), cert. den.,397 U.S. 1054, 90 S Ct 1396, 25 L Ed 2d 670 (1970)).

The lessons of Northand Palmerare two-fold: First, for every post-conviction claim, PCR counsel must plead and prove a justification for failing to raise that claim at trial or on appeal. Second, PCR counsel should plead alternative claims wherever possible to cover the Palmerexceptions and prevent the PCR court from sidestepping the underlying constitutional claim. For example, consider an ironclad Bradyviolation where it is unclear whether trial counsel was excusably unaware of the Bradyevidence.

If PCR counsel pleads the Bradyviolation solely as a claim of ineffective assistance of counsel, the PCR court will never reach the merits of the Bradyviolation unless it first finds that trial counsel’s performance was deficient. To avoid this result, PCR counsel should alternatively plead that trial counsel was excusably unaware of the Bradyviolation because of governmental or prosecutorial misconduct. Although these alternative claims may appear inconsistent, ORCP16 C permits pleading inconsistent or alternative claims for relief so long as they meet the good-faith requirements of ORCP17 C.

IV. Remedy

A. Collateral Relief

Post-conviction proceedings are a form of collateral relief. The post-conviction remedy does not replace or supersede a motion for new trial, motion in arrest of judgment or direct appellate review of a conviction or sentence. Thus, a post-conviction petition cannot be filed while those remedies remain available. ORS 138.540.

Practice Tip

A PCR court lacks jurisdiction over a post-conviction case which is filed before entry of an appellate judgment on direct appeal. Gonzalez v. Nooth, 241 Or App 649, 240 P3d 1288 (2011).In this situation, PCR counsel should seek a stipulation for dismissal without prejudice from opposing counsel which includes the following terms:

• The state agrees to not move against or in any way challenge the new petition and claims (including claims which might later be added by amendment) on grounds that the petition or claims are successive or barred by the post-conviction statute of limitations.

• The state agrees that, for purposes of this litigation only, all post-conviction claims premised upon a violation of the petitioner’s federal constitutional rights (including claims which might later be added by amendment) are and will be considered “properly filed” on the date the first petition was filed for purposes of 21 U.S.C. § 2244(d)(2). As a result, the state will not challenge, move against or otherwise assert that such claims are barred by the federal habeas limitations period in any future federal habeas proceeding.

B. Forms of Relief

ORS 138.520permits the post-conviction court to grant relief to the successful petitioner, including “release, new trial, modification of sentence, and such other relief as may be proper and just.” This statute provides the court with broad authority to correct the actual prejudice suffered by a criminal defendant. Hinton v. Hill, 342 Or 222, 149 P3d 1205 (2006). The following are a few of the more significant rulings regarding ORS 138.520:

• Shipman v. Gladden, 253 Or 192, 453 P2d 921 (1969)– PCR court has authority to grant delayed appeal where trial counsel failed to file timely notice of appeal.

• Hinton v. Hill, 342 Or 222, 149 P3d 1205 (2006)– PCR court has authority to fashion a conditional remedy which depends on the actions of the state and trial court on remand. Thus, it was not error to vacate petitioner’s sentences and let stand her convictions so long as the state did not seek, and trial court did not impose, sentences in excess of those contemplated by petitioner when she pleaded no contest.

• Brock v. Baldwin, 171 Or App 188, 197, 14 P3d 651 (2000), rev den, 332 Or 56, 26 P3d 150 (2001) – PCR court has authority to remand entire case, including untainted convictions, to trial court for resentencing.

• Barnett v. State of Oregon, 72 Or App 585, 586, 695 P2d 991, rev den299 Or 583, 704 P2d 513 (1985) – ORS 138.520 does not authorize PCR court to award money damages or require the state to repay restitution paid by the petitioner.

Practice Tip

Make sure your clients appreciate the risk of receiving a greater sentence following remand to the trial court. There is no authority preventing a trial court from imposing a less favorable sentence on remand than that which was imposed initially. SeeState v. Partain, 349 Or 10, 239 P3d 232 (2010). This can be especially relevant to petitioners who 1) received concurrent sentences, but could face exposure to consecutive sentences upon remand; 2) received downward durational or dispositional departures from presumptive prison sentences, and could face exposure to the presumptive sentences upon remand; or 3) received dismissal of significant charges (e.g., charges subject to Ballot Measure 11) as the result of plea bargaining, but could face the refiling of those charges upon remand.

C. Judgment and Enforcement

ORS 138.640sets forth certain requirements for the post-conviction court’s judgment, and imposes upon petitioner a duty to provide notice to the trial court and district attorney.

ORS 138.640(1) provides: After deciding the issues raised in the proceeding, the court shall enter a judgment denying the petition or granting the appropriate relief. The judgment may include orders as provided in ORS 138.520. The judgment must clearly state the grounds on which the cause was determined, and whether a state or federal question was presented and decided.

It is important that post-conviction counsel ensure that the post-conviction court’s judgment complies with these requirements. Failure of a judgment to clearly state the grounds upon which the post-conviction case was determined can lead to repetition of the same infirmity after the case is remanded to the trial court. Inclusion of the grounds for relief serves to communicate both to the trial court and trial counsel the errors that need to be avoided. The requirement that a judgment clearly state whether a state or federal question was presented and decided is critically important to preservation of federal issues for purposes of federal habeas proceedings. SeeDatt v. Hill, 347 Or 672,227 P3d 714 (2010). If the post-conviction judgment does not indicate that a particular federal issue was addressed, that issue may be deemed procedurally defaulted when petitioner seeks federal habeas relief.

ORS 138.640(2) provides: If the court grants the petitioner relief, the judgment is not enforceable in the petitioner’s favor until:

(a) The petitioner causes a certified copy of the judgment to be entered in the circuit court in which the petitioner’s conviction and sentence were rendered; and

(b) The petitioner serves a certified copy of the judgment on the district attorney of the county in which the petitioner’s conviction and sentence were rendered.

It is important that post-conviction counsel ensure that the notice required by ORS 138.640(2) be provided to both the trial court and district attorney’s office in the county where petitioner was initially convicted. Failure to do so may cause petitioner to languish in county jail while the statutorily required notice makes its way to the appropriate parties.

Practice Tip

Post-conviction counsel should either consult the Oregon Judicial Information Network(OJIN) or contact the trial court directly to ensure that proceedings have in fact commenced after remand. Post-conviction counsel should also make immediate contact with petitioner’s newly appointed trial counsel after remand and provide as much assistance as possible. Post-conviction counsel should attempt to ensure that trial counsel has a copy of the post-conviction trial memorandum and the transcript from the initial trial court proceedings. It is imperative that post-conviction counsel clearly communicate to trial counsel the issues that entitled petitioner to post-conviction relief. If newly appointed trial counsel does not have a clear idea of the errors that resulted in post-conviction relief, then those errors may simply be repeated after remand—thus rendering post-conviction counsel’s hard-earned victory both short-lived and ultimately useless.

V. Limitations on Obtaining Post-Conviction Relief

A number of statutory provisions significantly limit the availability of post-conviction relief.

A. Statute of Limitations

ORS 138.510(3)requires that a petition for post-conviction relief be filed within two years of the following: 1) entry of the judgment of conviction if no appeal is taken, 2) if an appeal is taken, the date the appeal is final, or 3) if a petition for certiorari is taken to the United States Supreme Court, the date it is denied or entry of the final state court judgment following remand from that court, whichever is later. With the exception of claims that qualify under an “escape clause,” this statute precludes successive post-conviction proceedings. Ineffective assistance of prior post-conviction counsel is not a basis to avoid this bar. Church v. Gladden, 244 Or 308, 417 P2d 993 (1966), Miller v.Maass, 117 Or App 610, 845 P2d 933 (1993).

1. Escape Clause

ORS 138.510(3) contains an exception to the limitations period where “the court on hearing a subsequent petition finds grounds for relief asserted which could not reasonably have been raised in the original or amended petition.” This language is construed narrowly. The proper focus is whether the grounds for relief were reasonably available, not whether the petitioner was able to raise those grounds. [../../../../Publications/post%20judgment%20appeals%20pcr%20habeas%20manual/section%20IV%20-%20PCR/314%20Or%20353%2C%20839%20P2d%20217%20(1992) Bartz v. State of Oregon, 314 Or 353, 839 P2d 217 (1992)]. The following are several significant opinions which construe the escape clause:

•Bartz v. State of Oregon, 314 Or 353, 839 P2d 217 (1992) (under former 120-day limitations period, escape clause unavailable to petitioner who failed to discover statutory defense to his conviction offense because statutes were reasonably available during the limitations period).

•Fine v. Zenon, 114 Or App 183, 834 P2d 509 (1992)(escape clause available where appellate counsel failed to timely notify petitioner that direct appeal had been dismissed).

•Brown v. Baldwin, 131 Or App 356, 885 P2d 707 (1994)(escape clause unavailable to petitioner who could himself have read the limitations statute after attorney told him the limitations period was three years).

•Keerins v. Schiedler, 132 Or App 560, 889 P2d 385 (1995)(escape clause available to petitioner who missed deadline because attorney told him his appeal was still pending).

•Fisher v. Belleque, 237 Or App 405, 240 P3d 745 (2010)(escape clause unavailable to petitioner who claimed mental illness prevented him from timely filing his petition).

2. Waiver of Limitations Defense

The post-conviction statute of limitations is not jurisdictional. Therefore, a defendant’s failure to raise the statute of limitations as an affirmative defense in its answer or in a motion to dismiss waives that defense. ORCP21G(2), Palmer v. State, 121 Or App 377, 854 P2d 955 (1993), reversed on other grounds318 Or 352, 867 P2d 1368 (1994).

B. Failure to Raise Issue on Appeal

ORS 138.550(2)precludes grounds for relief which a petitioner did assert or could reasonably have asserted on direct appeal. Generally, matters which are not preserved by appropriate objection at trial are not preserved for appellate review. State v. Wyatt, 331 Or 335, 343, 15 P3d 22 (2000). It is unclear whether ORS 138.550(2) requires a petitioner to raise claims not preserved at trial as claims for ineffective assistance of appellate counsel, based on failure to assert the claim as plain error. To be safe, in such circumstances post-conviction counsel should allege alternative claims for ineffective assistance of trial counsel (failure to preserve the error) and ineffective assistance of appellate counsel (failure to raise the unpreserved error as error apparent on the face of the record).

VI. Pleading and Procedure

A. Pleadings

1. Special Pleading Requirements

ORS 138.580 sets forth special pleading requirements for post-conviction petitions. The petition must:

•Identify the relevant trial and appellate proceedings and provide the dates of judgment and sentence.

•Identify any prior post-conviction proceedings undertaken by the petitioner and the disposition of such proceedings.

•Specifically set forth the grounds for which post-conviction relief is claimed and clearly state the relief desired.

•Set forth all facts within the personal knowledge of the petitioner which the petitioner shall swear are true and correct.

•Include as attachments affidavits, records or other documentary evidence which support the allegations of the petition and which the petitioner shall authenticate as true upon sworn affirmation.

•Omit argument, citation and discussion of authorities.

•Bear the sworn certification of the petitioner.

2. How Much Is Enough

Although no Oregon case explains what quantum of evidence ORS 138.580requires, counsel should make out at least a prima facie showing for each claim. Harrison v. Hall, 211 Or App 697,700, 156 P3d 141 (2007)(state arguing prima facie standard for ORS 138.580). ORS 138.580 does not contemplate submission of all of the petitioner’s evidence. For example, ORS 138.620(2)provides in part: “If the petition states a ground for relief, the court shall decide the issues raised and may receive proof by affidavits, depositions, oral testimony or other competent evidence.” Moreover, in Stevens v. Czerniak, 336 Or 392, 84 P3d 140 (2004), the Oregon Supreme Court held that under the ORCPs, a trial court does not have the authority to require pretrial disclosure of the names of post-conviction petitioner’s experts and the substance of their testimony.

3. ORS 138.580 and the Amended Petition

It is also unclear whether the requirements of ORS 138.580extend to amended pleadings filed by post-conviction counsel. Counsel should, however, assume that ORS 138.580 does apply to amended pleadings. See ORS 138.590(5) (appointed counsel has right to amend a petition which is defective in form or substance by moving for leave to do so within 15 days of appointment).

4. Pretrial Motion Practice

a.Defense Motions

Where an original or amended petition fails to comply with the strict pleading requirements in ORS 138.580, zealous defense counsel occasionally file “Rule 21 motions” (ORCP21). Such motions are usually one or more of the following: motion to dismiss for failure to state ultimate facts sufficient to constitute a claim (ORCP 21A(8)); motion to make more definite and certain (ORCP 21D); or motion to strike (ORCP 21E). These motions seek to narrow petitioner’s grounds for relief and/or compel discovery. Post-conviction counsel can reduce the probability of Rule 21 litigation by pleading claims with particularity, identifying portions of transcripts from which claims arise and by attaching other supporting documentation as exhibits to the pleadings. Upon receipt of a Rule 21 motion, counsel should resist the argument that ORS 138.580 requires pretrial disclosure of all evidence which the petitioner intends to offer in support of a particular claim, citing to ORS 138.620(2) and Stevens v. Czerniak, 336 Or 392, 84 P3d 140 (2004).

b.Petitioner’s Motions

Because the ORCPs permit the defendant to answer petitioner’s claims with a general denial (see ORCP 19A), PCR counsel rarely file Rule 21 motions. PCR counsel can, however, move for summary judgment on particularly strong claims. See ORCP 47.

Practice Tip

Litigating Rule 21 motions can lead to a colossal waste of scarce resources, because such motions rarely dispose of the action. Even where a motion is successful, the trial court has broad discretion to grant leave to amend post-conviction pleadings and must exercise that discretion pursuant to ORCP 23A’s directive that leave to amend “shall be freely given when justice so requires.” Ramsey v. Thompson, 162 Or App 139, 986 P2d 54(1999). For this reason, counsel should confer and seek to informally resolve Rule 21 motions by agreement to amend pleadings or provide appropriate discovery as required by the ORCPs. For this reason, the OregonUniform Trial Court Rulesrequire a trial court to deny any motion to make more definite and certain or motion to strike absent certification of a good faith effort to confer with opposing counsel before filing the motion.3 {Note: 3 UTCR5.010(1) and (3) provide: “(1) The court will deny any motion made pursuant to ORCP 21 and 23, except a motion to dismiss: (a) for failure to state a claim; or, (b) for lack of jurisdiction, unless the moving party, before filing the motion, makes a good faith effort to confer with the other party(ies) concerning the issues in dispute. * * * (3) The moving party must file a certificate of compliance with the rule at the same time the motion is filed. The certificate will be sufficient if it states either that the parties conferred or contains facts showing good cause for not conferring.”}

5. Other Pleading Considerations

a.Federalize Claims

It is imperative that post-conviction counsel “federalize” all claims for relief by citing the federal constitutional violation (with specific reference to the involved amendment). For example, on an ineffective assistance of counsel claim under ORS 138.530(1)(a), allege both a violation of the petitioner’s right to counsel under Article 1, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution and the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments to the United States Constitution. The failure to fairly present a federal claim to the state post-conviction court, in the manner required by that court, will bar its later consideration in federal court. Rose v. Lundy, 455 US509, 519, 102 S Ct 1198, 71 L Ed 2d 379 (1982), Casey v. Moore, 386 F3d 896, 915–916(9th Cir 2004).

b.Church v. Gladden

ORS 138.550(3) permanently waives claims not raised in the original or amended petition. It is the petitioner’s personal obligation to ensure that all claims are raised and to notify the court if counsel refuses to raise a claim. Ineffective assistance of post-conviction counsel is not a legitimate basis for a successive petition in state court or an excuse for procedural default in federal court.4 {Note: 4 See Dotta v. Maass, 91 Or App 222, 754 P2d 36 (1988)(inadequate assistance of post-conviction counsel is not a ground for post-conviction relief ); Miller v. Baldwin, 176 Or App 500, 32 P3d 234 (2001)(no statutory mechanism exists for challenging the adequacy of post- conviction counsel, thus no remedy exists for the failure of counsel to timely file a notice of appeal from a judgment denying post-conviction relief ).}

Although the United States Supreme Court recently held that ineffective assistance of post- conviction counsel may constitute cause to excuse procedural default at the initial review stage for claims of ineffective assistance of trial counsel, PCR counsel should nonetheless assume that the failure to raise and fully litigate federal constitutional violations will preclude their later review in federal court. See Martinez v. Ryan, S Ct , 2012 WL 986816 (2012). There is no statutory or constitutional right to effective assistance or adequate assistance of post-conviction counsel. These harsh rules create a strong incentive for a petitioner to plead the proverbial “kitchen sink.”

On the other hand, post-conviction counsel cannot certify frivolous claims. ORCP 17. Where the petitioner insists on including claims which PCR counsel cannot certify, counsel can bring the matter to the court’s attention and/or seek to include those claims in a separate pro se section of the pleadings prefaced with a disclaimer sufficient to satisfy ORCP 17.

In this regard, consider using language such as: “The following claims for relief are made pursuant to Church v. Gladden, 241 Or 308, 417 P2d 993 (1966), McClure v. Maass, 110 OrApp 119, 821 P2d 1105, 1107 (1991), and Miller v. Maass, 117 Or App 610, 845 P2d 933(1993). Counsel has conducted a diligent and conscientious review and investigation of all aspects of the underlying criminal and appellate proceedings. Petitioner raises these claims pro se for the reason that counsel cannot certify that they are well grounded in fact and warranted by existing law or upon a good faith argument for the extension, modification or reversal of existing law.”

c.Waiver of Claims Not Pleaded By Post-Conviction Counsel

As previously stated, claims not pleaded are deemed waived under ORS 138.550(3). A post-conviction trial court thus has no authority to grant relief on a ground which is not specifically pleaded by the petitioner. Bowen v. Johnson, 999 P2d 1159, 166 Or App 89(2000). In this regard, it appears ORS 138.550(3) trumps ORCP 12A which provides that pleadings are to be “liberally construed with a view of substantial justice between the parties.” Leyva-Grave-De-Peralta v. Blacketter, 232 Or App 441, 223 P3d 411 (2009).

B. Procedure

1. Civil Proceedings

Post-conviction proceedings are civil in character and, in the absence of a more specific post- conviction statute, the ORCPs apply in post-conviction proceedings. Stevens v. Czerniak, 336Or 392, 84 P3d 140 (2004), citing Mueller v. Benning, 314 Or 615, 621 & n. 6, 841 P2d 640(1992). Accordingly, PCR counsel must develop a solid working knowledge of the ORCPs— including those provisions which govern motions and discovery.

2. Discovery; ORCP 1E

With the exception of ORS 138.5855 {Note: 5 Under ORS 138.585 a petitioner who seeks confidential jury records must either include a request to access those records or file a motion no later than 90 days before the post-conviction hearing date absent good cause shown. The request or motion must identify with particularity the type and time period of the jury records at issue and the purpose for the request to disclose them. The PCR court can release the records upon finding that they are likely to produce evidence relevant to a claim under ORS 138.530(1)(a) and that their production will not be unduly burdensome. This statute is relatively new and includes several requirements not addressed in this footnote. PCR counsel are cautioned to consult the statute directly before making a pleading request or filing a motion seeking such records.}

which governs discovery of confidential jury records, post- conviction discovery is largely controlled by the ORCPs. ORCP 36 through ORCP 46 govern civil discovery and are thus available to the post-conviction practitioner. ORCP 1E is not a discovery provision, but is an incredibly useful provision which avoids the necessity and hassle of obtaining sworn affidavits. Although PCR counsel should review all of the relevant ORCPs, the following are some useful pointers:

a.ORCP 1E (Declarations)

This rule permits use of a sworn declaration in lieu of an affidavit, thus avoiding the need for a notary public. The declaration must include the following statement above the declarant’s signature: “I hereby declare that the above statement is true to the best of my knowledge and belief, and that I understand it is made for use as evidence in court and is subject to penalty for perjury.”

b.ORCP 43 (Requests for Production)

This rule permits a party to discover relevant and nonprivileged documents and objects in the possession of the other party. It does not apply to non-parties. Note that the Department of Justice often takes the position that a production request does not require it to produce records possessed by other state agencies (or district attorneys’ offices). However, PCR counsel can use a production request to compel production of attorney affidavits obtained by opposing counsel, and other trial and appellate materials which opposing counsel gathers during defense of the case. The request should carefully define the exact nature of the requested items and should including a paragraph specifying that the production obligations are of a continuing nature to ensure production of after-acquired materials.

c.ORCP 55F(3) (Subpoenas)

This extremely useful rule allows PCR counsel to subpoena production of books, papers, documents, or tangible items other than individually identifiable health information at a specified place without requiring concurrent testimony at a deposition, hearing or trial.

The subpoena can be served by mail. ORCP 55D(4). PCR counsel must serve a copy of the proposed subpoena upon opposing counsel seven days prior to its service. ORCP 55D(1) Upon service, the recipient has at least 14 days to respond. ORCP 55B. The issuing party can require the recipient to explain why any items which are not produced were excluded. ORCP 55F(3). Uses for this subpoena include compelling production of DA files, police agency records, trial counsel’s files, and investigators’ files to list a few.

Practice Tip

Sexual Assault Cases: The rules governing disclosure of medical, counseling and treatment records which are required for cases challenging sexual assault convictions are too involved to address here. Generally, PCR counsel should seek to produce these materials using a court protective order governing their use and disposition.

d.ORCP 39 (Depositions)

Depositions are a common occurrence in post-conviction proceedings and are generally conducted with the agreement of opposing counsel. Petitioner’s counsel should always consider deposing trial counsel, particularly where counsel is uncooperative. Depositions are also useful to obtain testimony and other information from defense subproviders such as investigators and experts as well as for prosecutors. PCR counsel must subpoena a non-party witness for deposition in the county where that person resides, is employed, or transacts business in person or such other convenient place as the court may order. ORCP 55. With the agreement of all parties and the deponent, telephonic depositions can reduce travel expenses when witnesses are in distant locations. Special rules apply for deposing out-of-state witnesses. ORCP 55F(2).

Practice Tip

Attorney–client communications relevant to petitioner’s claims of ineffective assistance are not subject to the attorney-client privilege. OEC 503(4)(c), Petersen v. Palmateer, 172 Or App 537, 19 P3d 364 (2001). Also, it is unclear whether the privilege against self-incrimination applies in the post-conviction context. ''See Statev. Barone, 329 Or 210, 986 P2d 5 (1999). PCR counsel should consider moving the court for a protective order under ORCP 36C whenever there is a possibility that trial counsel might reveal damaging statements or materials which would otherwise be privileged and where defense counsel seek to obtain incriminating statements from the petitioner. In Bittaker v. Woodford, 331 F3d 715 (2003), the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed a federal protective order which precluded use of privileged materials for any purpose other than litigating a federal habeas petition, and barred the Attorney General from turning them over to any other persons or offices, including, in particular, law enforcement or prosecutorial agencies.

e.Expert Witnesses

Under ORCP 36 there is no right to pretrial disclosure of either an expert’s name or the substance of the expert’s testimony. Stevens v. Czerniak, 336 Or 392, 84 P3d 140 (2004). In the absence of an agreement to depose expert witnesses, PCR counsel should pay particular attention to local rules imposing time limits for notifying the court of the intent to call witnesses at trial.

VII. The Lifecycle of a Post-Conviction Case and Practical Considerations

The treatment of post-conviction cases differs widely from county to county. It is strongly advised that post-conviction counsel careful review the post-conviction court’s order appointing counsel, as well as any scheduling order provided by the post-conviction court. It is also advised that post-conviction counsel check OJINperiodically to check on the status of each of his post-conviction cases. In general, the progression of a post-conviction case is as follows:

1. Petitioner files a post-conviction case in the county where he is incarcerated at the time of filing. If the petitioner, then the defendant is the superintendent of the institution where the defendant is incarcerated. If the petitioner is in the custody of the Department of Corrections(DOC), then defense counsel is the Oregon Department of Justice (DOJ). If the petitioner is incarcerated elsewhere, then defense counsel is the district attorney for the county where petitioner is incarcerated. If petitioner is not incarcerated, then the defendant is the State of Oregon and defense counsel is the district attorney for the county where petitioner was convicted and sentenced. ORS 138.560.

2. Post-conviction counsel is appointed or retained. Post-conviction counsel may move to file an amended petition, as of right, within fifteen days or any other period allowed by the post- conviction court. If post-conviction counsel determines that the pro se petition does not state a claim for relief, and further concludes that the petition cannot be amended to state a claim for relief, then post-conviction counsel may file an affidavit to that effect with the trial court in lieu of moving to amend the pro se petition.

3. Immediately after appointment, post-conviction counsel should begin obtaining the materials necessary to prosecute petitioner’s case. These include:

•Transcript of the relevant trial court proceedings.

•OJIN printout of the trial court proceedings.

•Trial court file.

•Trial counsel’s file.

•Files from experts, investigators, or others hired by trial counsel.

•District attorney’s file.

•Appellate counsel’s file.

4. Defense counsel may move for dismissal (or other action against the pleadings) under ORCP 21, within the longer of 1) ten days from the date of service of the amended petition, or 2) the time remaining to respond to the pro se petition. Pursuant to UTCR 5.030(1), post-conviction counsel must respond within fourteen days, and any reply by defense counsel must be filed within seven days thereafter.

5. Either side may move for summary judgment pursuant to ORCP 47, although this is relatively rare. The response to a summary judgment motion must be filed and served within twenty days after service and filing of the motion. The reply is due within five days thereafter. ORCP 47C.

6. Depending upon the county, the post-conviction court may or may not schedule a status conference.

7. Trial is scheduled. Some counties (e.g., Umatilla, Marion) require all trial documents to be received by the post-conviction court 30 days prior to trial. Other counties do not have a hard- and-fast rule. Since the post-conviction statutes permit testimony by affidavit and/or declaration (ORS 138.630), testimony by live witnesses is not necessarily required. Thus, post-conviction trials are often “paper trials” where the evidence consists solely of the exhibits submitted along with other trial documents (primarily a trial memo drafted by post-conviction counsel and a copy of the transcript of the trial court proceedings). It is thus extremely important that all trial documents be timely submitted to the post-conviction court.

8. Trial. Some counties (e.g., Umatilla, Marion) hold trials at the State Court Administrator’s Office, on the 5th floor of the Department of Justice building located at 1162 Court Street, Salem, Oregon. In those trials, the post-conviction cases are heard by retired (“plan B”) judges who are present in the courtroom along with post-conviction counsel and defense counsel. In other counties (e.g., Jefferson), the trials are held in the county where the post-conviction case is proceeding. In those cases, post-conviction counsel may be expected to appear personally while defense counsel is permitted to appear by video. Post-conviction counsel would be prudent to ascertain in advance the place of trial and whether he is expected to appear in person. Post- conviction trials will generally be scheduled for 15–30 minutes, unless post-conviction counsel requests more time.

9. Post-conviction relief is either granted or denied. If relief is granted, post-conviction trial needs to ensure compliance with ORS 138.640. If post-conviction relief is denied, notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days from the date of entry of judgment. Post-conviction counsel should ensure that petitioner understands that his claims will be deemed procedurally defaulted for purposes of federal habeas relief, in the event that no appeal is pursued.

VIII. Conclusion

Indent

While post-conviction litigation is frequently grueling, thankless and seemingly hopeless work, it can be immensely rewarding. Perhaps no other practice area covers such a diverse range and provides a better opportunity to quickly gain valuable criminal, civil and appellate experience. Even more important is the vital role this remedy plays in our criminal justice system as a whole.

Since the passage of the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996, (AEDPA), it is increasingly difficult to obtain meaningful relief with a federal writ of habeas corpus. Cases construing the federal writ continue to narrow its scope and application, and the recent Supreme Court decision in Pinholsteressentially rang the death knell for evidentiary hearings in federal court. SeeCullen v. Pinholster,563 U S, 131 S Ct 1388, 179 L Ed 2d 557 (2011)(review under §2254(d)(1) is limited to the recordthat was before the state court that adjudicated the claim on the merits). With this erosion of federal rights, Oregon’s Post-Conviction Hearing Act truly provides the last opportunity to correct fundamental miscarriages of justice which cause very real human suffering in this state every day. In 2009, the Oregon State Bar Board of Governors approved the Principles and Standards for Post-Conviction Relief Practitioners in this state. In his preface to these standards, Chief Justice De Muniz sums it up best:

“Protecting the fundamental constitutional rights of all individuals is an essential element of our shared commitment to the rule of law. The importance of post conviction proceedings—the last opportunity in this state’s justice system to ensure fundamental fairness, accuracy, and transpar- ency in all criminal proceedings that impact individual liberty—cannot be understated.”
