Section III

State Habeas Corpus

Ginger Mooney

This chapter covers writ of habeas corpus proceedings under ORS 34.310–34.730and the OregonConstitution. The statutory and constitutional bases governing habeas corpus are found in ORS 34.310–

34.730, the Oregon Constitution Article 1, section 23, the Oregon Constitution amended Article VII, section 2, and the United States Constitution Article I, section 9.

I. Types of Relief

The Oregon Supreme Court in Colby v. Thompson, 183 Or App 311, 316, 52 P2d 1058 (2002), explained the types of relief a habeas corpus can challenge:

Habeas corpus relief is available where (1) an inmate challenges the authority for his or her original or continued confinement, see ORS 34.360(2) (“If the challenge is to the authority for confinement, the petition shall state, in substance … [t]hat such person is not imprisoned or restrained by virtue of any order, judgment, decree or process specified in ORS 34.330.”); see also ORS 34.610; (2) the person

is lawfully confined but is being subjected to further unlawful imprisonment, see Penrod/Brownv. Cupp, 283 Or 21, 28, 581 P2d 934 (1978); or (3) the person is lawfully confined but is being “deprived of a constitutional right that requires immediate judicial attention and for which no other timely remedy is practicably available”—often referred to as a “conditions of confinement” claim. ORS 34.362(2); see Penrod/Brown, 283 Or at 28, 581 P2d 934.

ORS 138.540(2)allows an action for habeas corpus relief only if the plaintiff asserts the illegality of the restraint. The challenge to illegality of the restraint must be based on grounds other than whether or not the underlying criminal conviction or proceeding was lawful. ORS 138.540(2) recognizes that such other grounds include but are not limited to unlawful revocation of parole or conditional pardon or completed service of the sentence imposed.1,2 {Note:

1 Despite the language of ORS 138.540(2), in cases where plaintiff is alleging a constitutional violation due to unlawful revo- cation of parole or conditional pardon, the plaintiff is generally required to seek appellate review of the challenged decision.

2 However, ORS 34.330(4–5) bars habeas corpus relief if the person challenges a final order of the Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision, regardless of whether the person failed to seek judicial review of the matter or if the person sought judicial review of the matter and the Court of Appeals resolved the case. (Between 1993 and 2001, certain parole board ac- tions were not appealable, thus many challenges were litigated under the habeas corpus statues. OR Laws 2001, c 661 § 2.) See Billings v. Maass, 86 Or App 66, 68, 738 P2d 222 (1987), Haskins v. Palmateer, 186 Or App 159, 163, 63 P3d31, rev. denied, 335 Or 510 (2003).}

A. Incarcerated Without Valid Authority

Claims that the plaintiff is not validly in custody, such as when an inmate’s sentence has been fully served but he is still in custody, are proper habeas corpus claims. This type of claim arises most often when the inmate claims the Oregon Department of Corrections has incorrectly computed his sentence. See Penrod/Brown v. Cupp, supra.''

B. Incarcerated With Valid Authority

Newton v. Cupp, 3 Or App 434, 474 P2d 532 (1970), defined the remaining two types of proper habeas corpus claims. Newtonheld that the writ of habeas corpus is available to challenge the constitutionality of prison conditions and practices and deprivations of a prisoner’s constitutional rights.

The Supreme Court in Penrod/Brown v. Cupp, 283 Or at 28, affirmed the holding in Newton v. Cupp:

The writ remains available to bring before a court the twokinds of cases we have described: (1)When a petition makes allegations which, if true, show that the prisoner, though validly in custody, is subjected to a further “imprisonment or restraint” of his person that would be unlawful if not justified to the court, and (2)when a petition alleges other deprivations of a prisoner’s legal rights of a kind which, if true, would require immediate judicial scrutiny, if it also appears to the court that no other timely remedy is available to the prisoner. (Emphasis added.)

Often in habeas corpus litigation courts refer to the two different types of habeas cases where the inmate is lawfullyconfined as “Penrodtype one” and “Penrodtype two” cases.

1. PenrodType One Cases

=

The first type of claim has been characterized as a “prison within a prison.” Armstrong v. Cupp, 67Or App 295, 299, 677 P2d 721 (1984), quoting Penrod/Brown v. Cupp(prisoner in segregation and isolation); Peters v. Renfro, 43 Or App 411, 602 P2d 1137 (1979)(juvenile in an adult facility). In this type of claim, the plaintiff is not required to show a need for immediate judicial attention or the inadequacy of all other remedies. Penrod/Brown v. Cupp,283 Or at 29''.

2. PenrodType Two Cases

Penrodtype two cases concern allegations of unlawful conditions of confinement. When the petition alleges unlawful conditions of confinement, the inmate must allege a deprivation of a constitutional right and,

[t]he petitioner must show (1) a need for immediate judicial attention either because of the “urgency of the harm to which the petitioner claims to be exposed or … [judicial scrutiny] is found to be required as a matter of constitutional law,” and (2) the lack of any other adequate and timely remedy. Penrod/Brown v. Cupp, supra,at 28 [581 P2d 934].

Mueller v. Cupp, 45 Or App 495, 498, 608 P2d 1203 (1980).

Penrodtype two cases include claims challenging for conditions of confinement as well as issues surrounding mental health and physical health treatment.

II. Types of Claims Not Cognizable in a Habeas Corpus

Allen v. Maass, 124 Or App 195, 859 P2d 1205 (1993), holds that a plaintiff cannot challenge the lawfulness of his criminal conviction and the proceedings on which it was based in a petition for habeas corpus. ORS 138.540(1). The exclusive means for challenging the lawfulness of a criminal conviction and the proceedings on which that conviction was based is a petition for post conviction relief. Mora v. Maass,

120 Or App 173, 851 P2d 1154 (1993), affd, 319 OR 570 (1994). Furthermore, post-conviction relief is available to those under the jurisdiction of the Psychiatric Security Review Board (PSRB), and thus habeas corpus relief is not available to patients in the Oregon State Hospital if they are challenging the lawfulness of their “conviction.” Mueller v. Benning, 314 Or 615, 619, 841 P2d 640 (1992), Allen v. Mass, supra.

If a plaintiff has mislabeled a challenge to the lawfulness of his conviction or sentence as a petition for habeas corpus, the trial court mayconvert it into a petition for post-conviction relief. Bates v. Czerniak,

187 Or App 8, 11–12, 66 P3d 519, rev den335 Or 422 (2003), Perry v. Zenon, 127 Or App 682, 685,

874 P2d 89 (1994).

Similarly, where a writ of mandamus is available to the plaintiff, a petition for habeas corpus is not proper.

III. Jurisdiction

A. Supreme Court

The Oregon Supreme Court has discretion to take original jurisdiction in habeas corpus proceedings. Or Const, Art VII (amended), § 2, ORAP 11.20.

B. Circuit Courts

1. County Where Plaintiff Is Imprisoned or Restrained

The circuit court of the judicial district wherein the party is imprisoned or restrained has jurisdiction to hear and decide all issues, and, if vested with power to exercise judicial func- tions, the county court and county judge of the county wherein the party is imprisoned or restrained, shall have concurrent jurisdiction of proceedings by habeas corpus.

ORS 34.320.

Practice Tip

To determine which county court has jurisdiction over the habeas corpus case, determine in which county the institution is located. For example, if the plaintiff is incarcerated in the Oregon State Penitentiary in Salem, Oregon, the proper court is the Circuit Court for the County of Marion.

ORS 34.320 grants jurisdiction over the plaintiff ’s habeas corpus action, and in the event that the plaintiff is transferred outside the jurisdiction of the court, the court is instructed to transfer plaintiff ’s case to the judi- cial district in which plaintiff is imprisoned or restrained. The final clause of ORS 34.320 requires the court to dismiss the plaintiff ’s claims if it determines that those claims do not require immediate judicial scrutiny, or are otherwise subject to dismissal, by virtue of the plaintiff ’s transfer. The Keenancourt held that the transfer of a prison inmate does not require dismissal of some habeas corpus claims, such as those challenging incarcera- tion or parole. Other claims challenging deprivations which, if not remedied, will continue to affect an inmate’s rights, may remain valid notwithstanding that the plaintiff has been transferred to another institution within the state. Keenan v. Hall, 202 Or App 571, 123 P 3d 812 (2005).

2. Justiciable Controversy

The court may lose jurisdiction because the writ not longer presents a justiciable controversy. For example, this may occur where the court’s decision on the matters will have no practical effect on the rights of the parties. Brumnett v. PSRB, 315 Or 402, 405, 848 P2d 1194 (1993).

3. Immediate Judicial Scrutiny

In deciding whether the plaintiff ’s claims require immediate judicial scrutiny, the court looks to whether the case involves allegations specific to the institution the plaintiff was incarcerated in when they filed the writ of habeas corpus. For example, if the plaintiff claims that the conditions of confinement were unconstitutional at the Oregon State Penitentiary and the plaintiff is transferred to the Two Rivers Correctional Institution, his writ would become moot because he is no longer an inmate at the Oregon State Penitentiary and no longer subject to those conditions. However, plaintiff can defend the motion to dismiss the writ of habeas corpus if counsel can find evidence that the former institution either directed or limited the medical care the prisoner received after his transfer, or sought to evade the prisoner’s claim by transferring him. See Keenanv. Maass, 138 Or App 576, 579, 911 P2d 331,vac’d and rem’d on other grounds'', 324 Or 230, 923 P2d 1200 (1996).

If a writ is based on allegations of circumstances that will effect the plaintiff regardless of where he is incarcerated, the court must transfer the properly filed case to the new judicial district where the plaintiff is imprisoned or restrained. Here, if the plaintiff is alleging that he is immediately releasable due to a miscalculation of his credit for time served, the case should be transferred to follow the plaintiff wherever he is imprisoned or restrained.

Most recently, the Supreme Court addressed this issue in Barrett v. Belleque, 344 Or 91, 176P3d 1272 (2008). In Barrett, the plaintiff ’s petition for writ of habeas corpus claimed he was entitled to a hearing before prison officials removed him from the general population and placed him in an intensive management unit (IMU). 344 Or at 93. This case was not rendered moot by an Interstate Corrections Compact (ICC) transfer of the plaintiff to Oklahoma’s prison system, Id. at 97–100, despite the general requirement that habeas plaintiffs be imprisoned “within this state.” Pursuant to the ICC, the decision to place the prisoner in IMU still had consequences for the prisoner’s treatment in the out-of-state prison. Id., ORS 34.310, ORS 421.245.

IV. Parties

The parties are referred to as plaintiffand defendant. Those who can prosecute a writ include those who are “imprisoned or otherwise restrained of liberty, within this state, except in the cases specified in ORS 34.330.…” ORS 34.310. The term “imprisoned or otherwise restrained of liberty” requires a level of “physical restraint within the state of Oregon and within some county or judicial district of the state.”3 {Note: 3 But seeBarrett v. Belleque, 344 Or 91, 176 P3d 1272 (2008), where plaintiff was incarcerated in Oklahoma per interstate compact, but because of the restrictions placed on him while incarcerated in Oregon, his habeas was cognizable in Oregon.}

This language has been interpreted to mean “physical restraint within the state of Oregon and within some county or judicial district of the state,” City of Salem v. Bruner, 299 Or 262, 267–268, 702 P2d 70 (1985)(citing White v. Gladden, 209 Or 53, 60, 303 P2d 226 (1956)); see alsoLaMarche v. State of Oregon, 81 OrApp 216, 220, 725 P2d 378, rev. denied,302 Or 299 (1986) habeas corpus was not available to plaintiff, who was not restrained, as method of challenging his conviction of violating municipal ordinance).

Under this interpretation, habeas corpus is not available to a person on parole because the plaintiff is not imprisoned. In addition, the plaintiff has a timely alternative remedy through an action for a writ of mandamus.

Notwithstanding White v. Gladden, in a very limited circumstance a plaintiff can successfully argue that although he is on parole and not imprisoned, he still meets the physical restraint requirement of ORS 34.310. Baty v. Slater, 161 Or App 653, 984 P2d 342 (1999), adhered to on recons. 164 Or App 779, 995P2d 1176 (2000). In Batythe court held that where the plaintiff was released on post-prison supervision, sought credit for period during which he was incarcerated, was not rendered moot by his release from incarceration to post-prison supervision during pendency of appeal. Baty, 161 Or App at 656–657. Habeas corpus is available where the collateral consequence of reduction of petitioner’s overall period of incarceration and post-prison supervision would result if petitioner prevailed. In Baty, the plaintiff ’s overall period of incarceration and post-prison supervision would have been reduced by 110 days if the plaintiff prevailed. Id. at 655.

ORS 34.330also dictates who cannotprosecute the writ. Discussed above, prisoners who are eligible to obtain post-conviction relief cannot do so. Also, those who are eligible but fail to seek judicial review of a final order of the State Board of Parole and Post-Prison Supervision under 144.335(ORS 34.330(3–5)) are not eligible.

V. Grounds for Relief

A. Pretrial

1. Excessive Bail, Delayed Commencement of Trial

A person may challenge the security, bail amount or confinement before trial longer than sixty days in a petition for habeas corpus. ORS 132.290. The Oregon Constitutionguarantees that “offenses, except murder, and treason, shall be bailable by sufficient sureties.” Or. Const. Art. I, § 14. The constitution further pro<nowiki>vides that “[e]xcessive bail shall not be required.” Or. Const., Art. I, § 16.

In Oregon, the total amount of security to be posted must be no more than is necessary to reasonably assure the attendance of the person charged at trial. See, e.g.,Cooper v. Burks, 299 Or449, 702 P2d 1107 (1985); State ex rel. Lowrey v. Merryman, 296 Or 254, 674 P2d 1173 (1984);

Sexson v. Merten, 291 Or 441, 443, 631 P2d 1367 (1981); Owens v. Duryee, 285 Or 75, 589 P2d

1115 (1979). The amount of bail cannot make it impossible, as a practical matter, for a pretrial detainee to secure release. State ex rel. Lowrey v. Merryman, supra, 296 Or at 258, 674 P2d 1173; Owens v. Duryee, supra, 285 Or at 80, 589 P2d 1115.

2. Speedy Trial

Both constitutional law and Oregon statutes govern the right to a speedy trial. However, there is no constitutional right to be released in sixty days, that right is governed by statutes. SeeORS132.290, 136.295, 135.240. Individuals charged with certain crimes may not be subject to release under Oregon’s “60-day” rule. ORS 132.290. As such, a plaintiff cannot simply claim that he is being held beyond the sixty days in his petition for habeas corpus relief. Instead the plaintiff must request a release hearing from the trial court, or the habeas court will hold that plaintiff has an alternative remedy in requesting a release hearing from the trial court judge.

Next, in order to survive the show cause motion, a motion for summary judgment or a motion to dismiss, plaintiff must allege the following: the crime he is charged with (specifically that the crimes are subject to the “60-day rule”) or why his incarceration violates his right to speedy trial, notwithstanding the statutory provisions; (2) whether he is represented by counsel; (3)whether he has requested a bail hearing; and (4) the date that he was taken into custody.

3. Other Pretrial Habeas Claims

The most common petition for habeas corpus comes from an inmate in state prison, however, an inmate awaiting trial in a county facility can make many of the same claims as a state inmate. The reality, though, is that once the petition for habeas corpus relief is filed, the county inmate is so close to either completing his sentence or to the resolution of his criminal trial that habeas corpus relief will evade him because he will either be released from the county facility or be sentenced and sent to a state facility.

B. Post Trial

1. Computation of Sentence: Credit for Time Served, Earned Time & Meritorious Time Credit

Habeas corpus relief is available for prisoners who claim their sentence has expired. Strong v. Gladden, supra. A claim that the prisoner was wrongly denied credit for time served must allege that, but for the improper computation of sentence, the plaintiff would be entitled to immediate release. Gage v. Maass, 306 Or 196, 204, 759 P2d 1049 (1988)(prisoner was granted habeas corpus relief to enforce his right to receive credit for time served.

Similarly, a claim for loss of earned time must show that but for the loss of the earned time, the inmate would be entitled to immediate release. Gage v. Mass, 306 Or at 204. Finally, if an inmate is alleging that he is entitled to credit for his participation in educational programs, the inmate must allege particular facts that, if true, would entitle the plaintiff to habeas corpus relief. See Bedell v. Scheidler, 307 Or 562, 770 P2d 909 (1989), ORS 34.362. However, the fact that the plaintiff might have to serve his full sentence, because programing is not available, is not grounds for habeas corpus relief. Bahrenfus v. Bachik, 106 Or App 46, 51, 806 P2d 170 (1991)(patients at the Oregon State Hospital are not entitled to habeas corpus relief on the ground that they might be released sooner than their original term if they were to receive diagnosis and treatment).

Inmates are not entitled to credit for time served if they are voluntarily absent from state prison, such as when they are serving a federal sentence. Wallett v. Thompson, 165 Or App 365, 998 P2d1273 (2000).

When the computation of time includes credit for time served in a county jail before proceeding to a writ of habeas corpus, the plaintiff should ensure that the sheriff has certified the correct amount of time spent in county corrections. If the sheriff has failed to correctly certify the amount of time served awaiting trial then a habeas corpus against a state correctional facility is improper. Instead, the inmate should file a writ of mandamus to mandate that the sheriff certify the amount of time served. If the Department of Corrections then fails to properly credit plaintiff for the certified time spent in county corrections and but for this miscalculation plaintiff would be entitled to immediate release, the inmate then has an arguable claim for habeas corpus relief, such as when they are serving a federal sentence or on abscond status.

A complicated set of administrative rules govern earned time, good time (statutory good time), and meritorious good time (sometimes referred to as extra good time). These rules must be read carefully with an eye toward the distinct differences in the applications of prison term modification depending on date of the crime. See OAR 291-097-0080.

2. Disciplinary Segregation Unit; Administrative Segregation

Disciplinary orders may be challenged in habeas corpus, however, the allegations must satisfy the general habeas corpus pleadings standards set forth in ORS 34.360and in Penrod/Brown v.Cupp, 283 Or at 28. As explained above, plaintiff must allege that his rights were violated in the disciplinary process (e.g., the plaintiff was denied due process in the hearing that resulted in the discipline being imposed) and that the restraint imposed triggered the need for immediate judicial scrutiny (e.g., but for the disciplinary process, plaintiff would be immediately releasable). Plaintiff must allege both further imprisonment or restraint and allege facts that the restraint is unlawful. Plaintiffs often claim that their constitutional rights were violated in the disciplinary process when they were sanctioned with time in segregation, loss of privileges, fines and retraction of good time. However, the imposition of a fine and extension of a potential parole release date, do not invoke the need for immediate judicial scrutiny. Vin Pham v. Thompson, 156 Or App 440, 965 P2d

482 (1998). Additionally, the plaintiff in Vin Phamhad alternative methods for challenging the sanctions other than by habeas corpus.

The case law suggests that while an inmate’s claim can prevail on the issue of whether or not the inmate’s rights were violated in the disciplinary and administrative segregation hearing, it is challenging to prove that but for the violation of the right, the inmate would be immediately releasable. In most if not all situations, an inmate’s disciplinary sanction will not impact his release date. For example, an inmate can be released from incarceration from disciplinary segregation without completing his sanction. It therefore appears that the only viable claim challenging constitutionality of a disciplinary hearing’s outcome is where, as part of the inmate’s sanction, the inmate loses his earned time or good time credits andbut for the loss of this credit the inmate would be immediately releasable.

If the inmate is alleging that the conditions of confinement in the disciplinary unit fall below constitutional standards, the inmate must be incarcerated within the disciplinary unit while litigating his claim. As such, in addition to alleging the unconstitutional condition, the inmate must also allege when his segregation began and that he is still in segregation.

3. Intensive Management Unit

Placement in the intensive management unit (IMU) is different than placement in DSU in that IMU is reserved for those inmates who demonstrate a need for increased security in a maximum security housing unit. Placement in IMU is not pursuant to a disciplinary violation per se, but often times, after an inmate has served his sanction in DSU, he will be assigned to IMU until he achieves a custody classification level that allows placement outside of IMU. OAR 291-055-

0019(1)defines which inmates are assigned to IMU:

Maximum custody inmates shall be assigned to an IMU or IMU status cell. Maximum custody inmates who have received a sentence of death (inmates on death row status) may be assigned housing in an IMU or IMU status cell. An inmate demonstrates the need for maximum custody housing by demonstrating behaviors that cannot be controlled in other housing as indicated by high severity and/or chronic misconduct sanctions, escape activity or security threat group activities causing serious management concerns. Inmates assigned to an IMU or IMU status cell may be temporarily assigned to other treatment, program or service units (i.e., Infirmary, Administrative Segregation, Special Management Unit) for treatment or programming as deemed necessary or advisable by the department.

An IMU inmate is entitled to administrative review of his or her maximum custody classification. OAR 291-104-0135. It appears that the outcome of the administrative review cannot be challenged in a habeas corpus.

Recently, in the context of a prison disciplinary matter, an inmate filed a petition for writ of habeas corpus alleging that his subsequent placement in IMU constituted “punishment” and that he was entitled to a hearing before being assigned to that unit. Barrett v. Belleque, supra. In Barrettthe plaintiff argued that he was entitled to raise a claim that his assignment to IMU violated his right to due process. The Court of Appeals held that he had another adequate remedy available to assert his challenge, i.e., a section 1983 action. 42 U.S.C.A. § 1983. The Oregon Supreme Court held that the availability of a § 1983 action did not constitute an adequate remedy, noting that the mere existence of a federalremedy does not deprive a prisoner of the protection of the writ of habeas corpus in state court. Barrett, 344 Or at 103.

On the merits of Barrett’s claims, the Supreme Court held that the Department of Correction’s procedures for administrative IMU placement were adequate and that the Due Process Clause does not require a pre-placement hearing.4 {Note: 4 In deciding Barrett, the Court of Appeals looked to Troxel v. Maass, 120 Or App 397, 853 P2d 294 (1993), where the Court held that placement in IMU is not punitive, that inmates do not have the right to rehabilitation or reformation,and that inmates have no constitutional right to placement outside of IMU. See Kent v. Cupp, 26 Or App 799, 802, 554 P2d 196(1976); Hewitt v. Helms, 459 US 460, 103 S Ct 864 (1983)(emphasis added).}

Id. at 104–08.

4. Conditions of Confinement

a. Medical, Dental, or Psychological Treatment

In Estelle v. Gamble, 429 US 97, 97 S Ct 285, 50 L Ed 2d 251 (1976), the Supreme Court held that deliberate indifference to serious medical needs of prisoners constitutes unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain in violation of the Eighth Amendment. Medical claims are valid habeas corpus matters for inmates confined to Oregon correctional institutions and county jails. Mueller v. Cupp, supra; Jorgenson v. Schiedler, 87 Or App 100, 741 P2d 528(1987); Billings v. Gates, 323 Or 167, 916 P2d 291 (1996).

The ultimate question before a habeas corpus court is whether or not the state is providing the prison with constitutionally sufficient medical care. The constitutions of Oregon and the United States require that the “prisoner must prove a deliberate indifference to the prisoner’s serious medical needs” in order to show that there has been a denial of constitutional rights. Estelle, 429 US at 104–05, Billings v. Gates, supra.

Medical habeas corpus claims have both an objective and subjective element. Id.at 180–81. The objective element requires proof that the prisoner has a serious medical need that is not being adequately treated. Id.at 180. The subjective element requires proof of “deliberate indifference” by the institution and its agents. Id.

To show that the institution has violated Article I, section 16, of the Oregon Constitution and the Eighth Amendment to the United States Constitution, the prisoner must prove that he has a serious medical condition. Estelle, 429 US at 106. A condition is a “serious medical condition” when, if untreated, it would (1) have a significantly adverse effect on a prisoner’s daily activities, resulting in substantial and reoccurring pain or discomfort, or (2) would create a significant risk of permanent disability or death. Billings, 323 Or at 181, Keenan v. Maass, 149 Or at 579. As an explanation, in Billingsthe inmate alleged that his high arches caused him so much pain he could not walk and the institution would not provide the required footwear. Billings, supra. The Supreme Court held that to be a serious medical need. Id. at 182.

An inmate can challenge the lack of mental health treatment in a habeas corpus. Villareal

v. Thompson, 142 Or App 29, 920 P2d 1108 (1996), Weidner v. Zenon, 124 Or App 314,

316, 862 P2d 550 (1993). In Villareal, an inmate’s allegations that he suffered from mental illness such that he’d committed several suicide attempts had, if true, established the existence of a serious medical condition that if untreated would create a significant risk of permanent disability or death within the meaning of the state constitutional provision prohibiting cruel and unusual punishment.

After a showing of a serious medical condition, the prisoner must further prove that the state or institution has been deliberately indifferent to that condition. 5 {Note: 5 While proving deliberate indifference is difficult, it is important to note that the deliberate indifference standard is not intended to insulate prison staff from judicial scrutiny of decisions made in the course of diagnosing and treating prison inmates.” Villarreal v. Thompson, supra, citing Billings, 323 Or at 181.}

Estelle, 429 US at 106, Billings, 323 Or at 180. Billingsillustrates the deliberate indifference standard, holding that deliberate indifference “constitutes the unnecessary and wanton infliction of pain, whether the indifference is manifested by prison doctors in their response to a prisoner’s serious medical needs or by prison guards in intentionally denying or delaying access to medical care, or in intentionally interfering with prescribed treatment.” Billings, 323 Or at 181.

b. Religious Access

Oregon has not developed case law addressing religious access in the context of a habeas corpus case. As such, the federal case law developed through litigation of 42 U.S.C. § 1983 claims guides the framework for arguing lack of religious access. Arguably, to establish a claim under the First Amendment, the prisoner must prove that the policies and practices of the institution substantially burden his right to the free exercise of his religion. Turner v.

Safely, 482 US 78, 89, 107 S Ct 2254, 96 L Ed 2d 64 (1987).6 {Note: 6 While in a 20 U.S.C § 1983 claim the burden shifts to the defendant to prove a valid, rational connection between the offending practice and a legitimate governmental interest, it is unclear if this remains the defendant’s burden in a habeas corpus.}

Additionally, the inmate must allege that burdens to his right to free exercise of religion pursuant to the requirements of thePenrodtype two” requirements (i.e., need for immediate judicial scrutiny).

c. Access to the Courts

Similar to the religious access claims, access to court is also controlled by federal law. For a prisoner to establish a claim of denial of access to the courts against a prison institution, he or she must allege that the institution’s conduct caused him to suffer an actual injury in a distinct legal proceeding. McGee v. Baldwin, 183 Or App 1, 5, 51 P3d 614 (2002); Boundsv. Smith, 430 US 817, 828, 97 S Ct 1491, 52 L Ed 2d 72 (1977), Lewis v. Casey 518 US

343, 116 S Ct 2174, 135 L Ed 2d 606 (1996). The prisoner may generally allege one of two kinds of distinct injuries: that a claim which he is currently litigating is being frustrated by the state’s actions, or that a claim which he could have raised or tried to raise has been lost as a result of the state’s actions and can no longer be pursued. Christopher v. Harbury, 536 US

403, 122 S Ct 2179, 153 L Ed 2d 413 (2002). In addition, the prisoner must also allege facts that will enable the court to determine whether the frustrated claims are non-frivolous— which involves alleging facts that show that the claim had arguable merit bearing more than a mere hope of success. Id.at 415–16, see also Barrett v. Williams, 247 Or App 309, 313–14,

270 P 3d 285 (2011). d.Personal Safety

In this context, the prisoner must allege and present evidence that he is subjected to ongoing and periodic assaults that rise to the level of a deprivation of the constitutional right to be

free from unnecessary physical abuse. Shaffer v. Maas, 122 Or App 518, 858 P2d 474 (1993). [S]he must also allege and present evidence that she presently faces a serious risk of harm

from other patients, other inmates or correctional staff and staff indicating a substantial risk of harm requiring immediate judicial intervention. In Feller v. Wright, 103 Or App 575, 577,

798 P2d 703 (1990), the prisoner failed to show that, despite allegations he was assaulted on one occasion by prison guards and that he feared he might be assaulted by guards some time in the future, he required immediate judicial attention. Instead, the court held that an injunction would be an adequate remedy. Id.

d. Physical Conditions of Confinement

Generally the writ of habeas corpus is not available to challenge “overcrowding, the quality of prison food, the opportunities for recreation or exercise, or similar conditions of imprisonment even when the challenge has merit.” Penrod/Brown v. Cupp, 283 Or at 27, Troxel v. Maass, 120 Or App at 399–400. However, the United States Supreme Court has held: “It is undisputed that the treatment a prisoner receives in prison and the conditions under which he is confined are subject to scrutiny under the Eighth Amendment.” Hellingv. McKinney, 509 US 25, 31, 113 S Ct 2475, 125 L Ed 2d 22 (1993). In DeShaney v.Winnebago County Dept. of Social Services, 489 US 189, 199–200, 109 S Ct 998, 103 L

Ed 2d 249 (1989), the Court held that where prison officials fail to provide for a prisoner’s basic human needs—including food, clothing, shelter, medical care, and reasonable safety— they “transgress[] the substantive limits on state action set by the Eighth Amendment.” Additionally, “Whether one characterizes the treatment received by [the prisoner] as inhuman conditions of confinement, failure to attend to his medical needs, or a combination of both, it is appropriate to apply the ‘deliberate indifference’ standard articulated in Estelle

v. Gamble,429 US 97 (1976)].” Wilson v. Seiter, 501 US 294, 111 S Ct 2321, 115 L Ed 2d

271 (1991).

As explained above, to prevail on a conditions of confinement claim, a prisoner must prove that the state or institution has been deliberately indifferent to that condition. Estelle,

429 US at 106, Billings, 323 Or at 180. Deliberate indifference is measured subjectively. Farmer v. Brennan, 511 US 825, 114 S Ct 1970, 128 L Ed 2d 811 (1994). However, this does not require a prisoner seeking a remedy from an unsafe condition to await a tragic event. Id. at 846.

5. Extraditions and Detainers

A writ of habeas corpus is an appropriate method to prevent an unlawful extradition or detainer. The writ is proper in the confining state, not in the state requesting extradition. Knowles v.Gladden, 227 Or 408, 413, 362 P2d 763 (1961). An example of an unlawful detainer is where the officials in Clark County, Nevada, send an extradition and detainer packet to Oregon, but the Oregon officials preparing the extradition paperwork assume that the extraditing state is Washington and the Governor then signs the extradition paperwork extraditing the plaintiff to Washington. Another example of an unlawful detainer is where the extradition paperwork does not properly identify the plaintiff; this situation arises where aliases are used.

The prisoner should first allege that without immediate judicial action, he will be illegally extradited, this extradition will be in violation of the his federal and Oregon constitutional rights, and his rights under the Uniform Criminal Extradition Act, codified at ORS 133.743–133.857and Agreements on Detainers, codified by ORS 135.775.

Next, the inmate should allege the extraditing [authority] has notified the State of Oregon of their intent to extradite him pursuant to an Interstate Agreement on Detainers.

Plaintiff should also allege that his imprisonment, incarceration, and restraint is illegal and unconstitutional because the state seeking extradition is: (1) seeking to immediately transport plaintiff, (2) the prison/jail officials will allow the state seeking extradition to transport plaintiff without due process of law in violation of plaintiff ’s constitutional and statutory rights because the papers that seek this transfer are insufficient, and (3) specifically the papers are not in order, do not identify plaintiff, and do not provide any due process for plaintiff. Michigan v. Doran, 439 US

282, 99 S Ct 530, 58 L Ed 2d 521 (1978), Cuyler v. Adams, 449 US 433, 101 S Ct 703, 66 L Ed

2d 641 (1981).

Finally, plaintiff should allege that the writ of habeas corpus should be granted because there is no other plain, adequate or speedy remedy available for plaintiff to seek review of the challenged extradition.

VI. Pleadings and Practice

Habeas corpus cases are civil proceedings and therefore the Oregon Rules of Civil Procedure apply, except where a different procedure is provided by the habeas corpus statutes. Holland v. Gladden, 229 Or 573,

574, 368 P2d 331 (1962)(habeas corpus is in nature of a civil proceeding).

A. Initial Filing and Filing Fees—ORS 34.340

The petition for writ of habeas corpus is usually filed by a pro se plaintiff, but in some circumstances where the inmate is represented by retained counsel prior to the initial filing of the petition, the attorney for plaintiff will file the initial petition. The petition must be signed and verified by oath (normally done in the form of a notary) of the plaintiff stating that he believes that the allegations in the petition are true. The petition must be accompanied by a filing fee of $250. This filing fee can be waived by the judge or drawn from the inmate’s trust account. ORS 34.365.

The required contents of the petition depend on the types of claims in the petition. If the petition is challenging the authority for confinement, then the petition must include the following requirements of ORS 34.360:

(1) That the party in whose behalf the writ is petitioned is imprisoned or restrained of liberty, the place where, and officer or person by whom the party is imprisoned or restrained, naming both parties if their names are known, or describing them if not known.7 {Note: 7 Usually this is the name of the correctional institution where the inmate is imprisoned and the name of the superintendent of the institution.}

(2) That such person is not imprisoned or restrained by virtue of any order, judgment or process specified in ORS 34.330.

(3) The cause or pretense of the imprisonment or restraint, according to the best knowledge or belief of the plaintiff.8 {Note: 8 Setting forth the name, case number and county for the inmates underlying conviction satisfies this requirement.}

(4) If the original imprisonment or restraint is by virtue of any order, warrant or process, a copy thereof shall be annexed to the petition, or it must be alleged that, by reason of the removal or concealment of the party before the application, a demand of such copy could not be made, or that the demand was made, and the legal fees therefore tendered to the person having the party in custody, and that a copy was refused.

(5) That the claim has not already been adjudged upon a prior writ of habeas corpus, to the knowledge or belief of the plaintiff.

If the petition is challenging the conditions of confinement or deprivation of rights, the petition must comply with the requirement set forth in ORS 24.360(1), (3), (4) and (5), and it must state facts supporting the claim that (1) the person is deprived of a constitutional right, (2) immediate judicial attention is necessary, and (3) no other timely remedy is practicably available. ORS 35.362.

B. Appointment of Counsel—ORS 34.355

Counsel can be appointed in a habeas corpus case after a showing of financial eligibility. Counsel can be appointed for representation at the trial and appellate levels. ORS 34.355. One way to prove financial eligibility is to provide the court with an affidavit of indigency and a printout of the inmate’s most recent trust account balance. While the inmate should attach the motion to appoint counsel and affidavit of indigency to his initial petition, the court will not normally appoint counsel until after the show cause proceedings are concluded.

C. Order to Show Cause—ORS 34.370

After it receives the initial filings, the court will either issue an order directing the defendant to show cause why the writ should not be allowed or, on its own, enter a judgment denying a meritless petition brought under ORS 34.310 to 34.730.9 {Note: 9 A meritless petition is one which, when liberally construed, fails to state a claim upon which habeas corpus relief may be granted.}

The defendant has 14 days to respond to the court’s order. If the defendant chooses not to respond to the court’s order, then the court will issue the writ and appoint counsel if the inmate is eligible. In court-appointed cases, counsel will not have an opportunity to respond to the defendant’s response to the court’s show cause order. In retained cases or cases where the attorney is appointed at the initial filing, the plaintiff can respond to the show cause order. After the defendant responds or the time period for responding has expired, the judge will make one of the following rulings:

(A) If the petition is a meritless petition, issue a judgment denying the petition and ordering the plaintiff to pay the cost of attorney fees incurred by the defendant. In no case shall the award of attorney fees exceed $100. The fees may be drawn from, or charged against, the inmate’s trust account.

(B) Issue a judgment granting appropriate habeas corpus relief. (C) Issue a writ of habeas corpus requiring that a return be made.

Next, the court will either require the defendant to file a return, ORS 34.370(2)(b)(C), or the parties may stipulate to a hearing described in ORS 34.670. If the court accepts the stipulation, the court will set the matter for an expedited hearing.

D. Defendant’s Return—ORS 34.421, ORS 34.540 (contents of writ)

The writ shall require the defendant to file a return, at a specified time and place, that states the time and cause of the plaintiff ’s imprisonment or restraint. Normally, the defendant’s return will state the plaintiff ’s underlying conviction, attach supporting documentation and state that the plaintiff is validly in custody. ORS 34.421, 34.540.

E. Plaintiff ’s Replication—ORS 34. 670

After the defendant files the return, the plaintiff will file a replication.

34.670Replication following return; hearing. '''The plaintiff in the proceeding, on the return of the writ, may, by replication, signed as in an action, controvert any of the material facts set forth in the return, or the plaintiff may allege therein any fact to show, either that imprisonment or restraint of the plaintiff is unlawful, or that the plaintiff is entitled to discharge. Thereupon the court or judge shall proceed in a summary way to hear such evidence as may be produced in sup- port of or against the imprisonment or restraint, and to dispose of the party as the law and justice of the case may require. [Amended by 1979 c.284 §73; 2005 c.22 §28]

Practice Tip

In most cases where counsel is appointed, the replication will be the first filing made by counsel. If counsel filed the original petition for writ of habeas corpus, the court may allow the plaintiff to rest on the original petition and not require the replication. However, this must be done by stipulation of both parties. In most jurisdictions after the writ issues the court will set a scheduling order establishing the due dates for the return, replication, motion to dismiss, and response to the motion to dismiss.

F. Defendant’s Motion to Dismiss—ORS 36.680

Motion to deny petition; motion to strike; controverting replication; time to plead; construction and effect of pleadings.(1) The defendant may, before the writ issues, move to deny the petition on the grounds that the petition fails to state a claim for habeas corpus relief. The defendant may, at any time after the writ issues, move to dismiss the writ on the grounds that the pleadings, including the petition, the return, the replication, if any, and any supporting evidence, demonstrate that plaintiff has failed to state or establish a claim for habeas corpus relief.

Practice Tip

To support their motion to dismiss, the defendant will gather evidence from institutional staff or Oregon Department of Corrections staff to disprove the claim. In medical habeas cases, the defendant will file as exhibits affidavits of medical practitioners employed by the Department of Corrections and will attach the inmate’s relevant medical file. In conditions of confinement cases, the defendant will attach an affidavit from the physical plant manager detailing the conditions of the institution. In cases involving improper calculation of an inmate’s sentence, the defendant will obtain an affidavit from the Offender Information and Sentence Computation division of the Oregon Department of Corrections explaining the sentence calculation.

G. Motion to Strike—ORS 34.680(2)

As discussed above, claims which collaterally attack a plaintiff ’s underlying criminal conviction are not proper habeas corpus claims. As such, if plaintiff pleads both facts that are cognizable in a habeas corpus case as well as facts which are not cognizable, the defendant will file a motion to strike the claims which are not cognizable.

The plaintiff may move to strike the return or any allegation or defense in the return. The defendant may move to strike the replication or any new matter in the replication, or by proof controvert the same, as upon a direct denial or avoidance.

H. Scheduling of Pleadings and Hearing—ORS 34.680(3)

The return and replication shall be made within such time as the court or judge shall direct, and the petition, return and replication shall be construed and have the same effect as in an action.

I. Hearing—ORS 34.695

If the matter proceeds to an evidentiary hearing, as described in ORS 34.670, the court shall decide the issues raised in the pleadings and may receive proof by affidavits, depositions, oral testimony or other competent evidence.

Practice Tip

The court will set the hearing for approximately thirty minutes unless otherwise indicated. The OregonRules of Civil Proceduredictate the terms of the hearing, unless the scheduling order indicates otherwise. Counsel should argue that the plaintiff should be transported to the hearing instead of appearing telephonically. If the court allows the plaintiff to appear in person, the court will direct the plaintiff to file the appropriate Order to Transport.

J. Judgment—ORS 34.700

(1) If it appears that the party detained is imprisoned or restrained illegally, judgment shall be given that the party be discharged forthwith; otherwise, judgment shall be given that the proceeding be dismissed and the party remanded. No officer or other person is liable to any action or proceeding for obeying such judgment of discharge.

(2) The court shall include in the judgment an order that the defendant pay the attorney fees incurred by the petition, not to exceed $100, if:

(a) The court enters a judgment requiring that the plaintiff be discharged; and

(b) The court finds that the allegations or defenses in the return were frivolous. [Amended by 1995 c.657 §9; 1999 c.114 §6]

Practice Tip

Counsel should request that the court’s staff send a copy of the signed judgment as soon as it is filed, this will expedite the filing of the appeal.

K. Appeal—ORS 34.710

Any party to a proceeding by habeas corpus, including the state when the district attorney appears therein, may appeal from the judgment of the court refusing to allow such writ or any judgment therein, either in term time or vacation, in like manner and with like effect as in an action. No question once finally determined upon a proceeding by habeas corpus shall be reexamined upon another proceeding of the same kind.

Practice Tip

Counsel for plaintiff should file the Notice of Appeal and Motion to Appoint Appellate Attorney/Production of Transcript. In court appointed cases, to identify the plaintiff ’s appellate attorney, counsel should contact OPDSC/CBC division.
