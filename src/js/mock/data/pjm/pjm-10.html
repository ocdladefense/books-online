Post Judgment and Extraordinary Remedies

2012 edition

Capital Cases

Shawn Wiley

I. Introduction

Capital appeals are a different breed than typical criminal appeals. The record is voluminous. Client communication is more extensive and incorporates different considerations than with the typical client. The legal issues are numerous, complex, and specialized. And, of course, the stakes are higher. The phrase “death is different” applies to all levels of litigation, including the direct appeal.

This chapter is not intended as a comprehensive guide to litigating a capital appeal. Such a guide would require its own manual. Instead, the chapter is designed to provide an overview of the practice.

II. Training and Qualifications of Attorneys

A. Minimum Requirements

The Office of Public Defense Services has adopted the ABA Guidelines for the Appointment andPerformance of Defense Counsel in Death Penalty Cases(2003) as the standards for representation of clients who are facing the death penalty or have been sentenced to death.

The qualification standards promulgated by the Public Defense Services Commission (PDSC), as well as the ABA guidelines, include specific training and experience for attorneys representing persons in capital appeals. See “Qualification Standards for Court-Appointed Counsel to RepresentFinancially Eligible Persons at State Expense,” Standard IV, section 9 (included in West’s Oregon Rules of Court); ABA Guidelines 5.1 and 8.1.

Attorneys working on capital appeals must perform their responsibilities consistently with those guidelines, and should pay particular attention to ABA Guideline 10.15.1, Duties of Post Conviction Counsel.

B. Mentorship and Resources

1. Mentorship

The requirements detailed in the sources listed above are a useful starting point. However, the complexities of capital appeals are such that even if an attorney meets those minimum requirements, he or she is unlikely to be prepared to litigate a capital appeal.

The most valuable advice this chapter could impart is to strongly urge attorneys to serve as second-chair to an experienced capital practitioner before attempting to litigate a capital appeal as the lead attorney. Familiarity with state and federal law regulating death penalty trials, as well as the current legal challenges to Oregon’s death penalty scheme, is fundamental to an effective practitioner in this field. In addition, an experienced attorney will have insight on issues related to the client, including effective communication techniques and assessing whether the client is depressed or considering abandoning his or her appeals. An experienced attorney will also have knowledge of the myriad levels of appeal in a capital case, and the role of the direct appeal in that context.

2. Resources

All attorneys litigating a capital appeal, regardless of experience level, benefit from taking advantage of the community of Oregon defense attorneys, and in particular the strong community of capital practitioners. The Oregon Criminal Defense Lawyers Associationmaintains a listserv for both defense attorneys as a whole (“The Pond”) and a more focused listserv devoted to capital practitioners (“The Capital Defender Listserv”). Both forums offer an opportunity to ask focused questions regarding legal issues, problems relating to the corrections system, etc. Participation in the listserv and attendance at the many defense-oriented capital CLEs held throughout the year by OCDLA, the federal public defender, and other organizations helps foster a sense of camaraderie and community, builds contacts and a support system, and reduces the sense of isolation that litigating a capital appeal can produce.

In addition, OPDS funds the Oregon Capital Resource Center (http://www.orcrc.org/), which provides support to capital practitioners at all levels. The resource center is an excellent source of information, pleadings, and advice. Moreover, one of the responsibilities of the center director is to keep tabs on all of the capital cases in Oregon, regardless of the stage of the case. It is important that anyone litigating a capital case contact the director.

III. Initial Stages of a Capital Appeal

A. Automatic Review before the Oregon Supreme Court

A criminal defendant who is sentenced to death has an automatic and direct appeal to the OregonSupreme Court, without filing a notice of appeal. ORS 138.012; Oregon Rules of AppellateProcedure(ORAP), Rule 12.10(1). The trial court administrator has the obligation to prepare and file a packet of information concerning the case with the state court administrator, who notifies the Chief Justice and serves a copy of the packet on the defendant and each trial attorney. ORAP 12.10(3) and(4); see alsoORAP 12.12(concerning notice to defendant of right to court-appointed counsel, if eligible).

The Supreme Court contacts the defendant and asks the client to notify the court if the client has retained representation for the appeal, or if the client would like counsel appointed to represent him, if eligible. In the past, the court would wait for the defendant to respond before appointing counsel. Under the current rule, which reflects the reality that death row inmates are overwhelmingly represented on appeal by court-appointed counsel, the court appoints counsel if the defendant does not respond within 28 days and appears to be eligible for court-appointed counsel. SeeORAP 12.12(3).

The Supreme Court contacts the Appellate Division of OPDS when it determines that court- appointed counsel is appropriate under ORAP 12.12(3). The Chief Defender informs the court whether the case should be assigned internally or to counsel on the Capital Appellate Panel. The Supreme Court typically appoints the attorney identified by OPDS.

B. Meetings with the Client

The lead attorney and, if possible, the second chair should visit the client on death row at the Oregon State Prison as soon as possible after being identified or appointed as counsel. Initial meetings with the client serve at least two very important purposes. First, the attorney(s) may begin establishing the trust and communication so vital to representing clients facing the death penalty. Second, during the initial meeting, counsel should provide the client with an overview of the appellate process (at all levels, state and federal) and the briefing schedule and inform the client that there will be regular written correspondence, telephone contact, and in-person consultation with the attorney during the course of the representation.

Note: ABA Guideline 10.15.1(E)instructs that appellate counsel has the obligation to “continually monitor the client’s mental, physical, and emotional condition for effects on the client’s legal position.” If counsel becomes concerned at any point during the representation that the client’s condition has so deteriorated as to become a danger to his own physical, psychological, or legal well-being, or that client is contemplating dismissing his appeals (i.e., “volunteering” for the death penalty), counsel should obtain professional assistance.

C. Meetings with Trial Counsel and Obtaining Case Files

The lead attorney should contact trial counsel immediately to gather information about the client, identify the major issues in the case, obtain copies of motions and memoranda, and ensure that the trial and appellate records are complete. ABA Guideline 10.7(B).

A capital direct appeal is a lengthy process, often stretching over several years. The trial attorneys’ physical and electronic case files, which are important for purposes of the client’s post-conviction relief (PCR) case, sit during that time in whatever storage is afforded them by the trial attorney. Documents could be lost, damaged, or reused. Obtaining, scanning, and storing case files protects the client’s interests and ensures that appellate and post-conviction counsel will have access to those materials. Accordingly, the current policy of the Appellate Division of OPDS, which was developed in conjunction with the Oregon Capital Resource Center, is to obtain the case files of trial counsel as soon as practicable after appointment on appeal.

Appellate Division attorneys use the following protocol. It is provided here as a suggested starting point; individual attorneys may want to develop different methods of collecting and storing case files.

1) Obtain a file release from the client.

2) Contact trial counsel and ask to come to his or her office and pick up all of the files and materials related to the case.

3) Photograph the boxes containing the files as they exist at trial counsel’s office. Remove the files and bring them to the Appellate Division.

4) Make at least a cursory index for each box.

5) Scan the files in the order received.

6) Send the scanned files to trial counsel on a CD or other electronic media.

D. Appellate Record

An accurate transcript of the proceedings of a capital trial is critical not only to the client’s direct appeal, but to any further effort to overturn the murder conviction and the death sentence. ABAGuidelines 10.7 and 10.15.1impose a duty on counsel at every stage to ensure that the record is complete and “supplement it as appropriate.”

The Oregon Rules of Appellate Procedure provide for the automatic preparation of a transcript of all trial proceedings, including jury selection. ORAP 12.10(5)(a).

The appellate attorney should contact the court reporter or transcriptionist and attempt to arrange for electronic receipt of transcript portions as they are prepared, as opposed to waiting for the entire paper copy of the transcript. Getting copies of the transcript as they are prepared will allow an opportunity to read ahead and complete as much of the transcript as possible before the transcript is filed and the record is settled.

The attorney must forward a copy of the transcript to the client.

Once the transcript has been prepared, the defense team should examine it carefully and compare it with information from the Oregon Judicial Information Network (OJIN), trial counsel’s records, and other sources of information to ensure that all proceedings have been transcribed. Trial counsel and the client should be asked to review the transcript for accuracy (however, given the duration of the trial and the frequently long period of time needed to prepare the transcript, counsel and the client may not be able to accurately recall specific details).

IV. Briefing and Arguing a Capital Case

A. Introduction

As noted above, this chapter cannot present a comprehensive guide to representing a death row client on appeal. Nor can a single chapter in a post-conviction manual completely instruct an attorney on how to prepare the opening brief in such an appeal.

The following materials touch on the “nuts and bolts” of drafting the opening and subsequent briefs, without discussing the vast body of substantive law surrounding capital cases.

A new practitioner should start with the statutory scheme authorizing the death penalty and articulating the procedures to be followed in capital cases, ORS 163.095, et. seq.In addition, practitioners may find the following material helpful in building an understanding of the history of and issues involving Oregon’s death penalty scheme. This is by no means an exhaustive list of the treatises and cases necessary for an adequate background in death penalty law. It is intended as a starting point for new practitioners.

1. Federalizing and United States Supreme Court

The following resources will prove particularly helpful. First, Randy Hertz and James Liebman, Federal Habeas Corpus Practice and Procedure, 6th Ed. (Lexis Nexis 2011) contains two especially useful chapters. Chapter 4 discusses the special needs of the capital client. In addition, the footnotes contain a significant bibliography on this topic. Second, Chapter 11 contains an extensive compilation of winning issues in capital cases. These include issues relating to mitigating circumstances, improper prosecutorial argument at the penalty phase and a variety of other issues relating to capital sentencing procedures.

Because capital appellate counsel has a responsibility to ensure proper exhaustion of federal claims, a thorough understanding of habeas corpus exhaustion principles is necessary. Federal Habeas Corpus Practice and Procedurecan provide the necessary background.

Next, the website of the Capital Defense Network, capdefnet.org, has several outlines and sections of note. One contains an overview of the Eighth Amendment framework governing the administration of capital punishment. The website also has case law outlines on a variety of relevant constitutional issues such as competency to stand trial, jury misconduct and exculpatory evidence.

The Georgetown Law Journal’s Annual Review of Criminal Proceduresummarizes the law governing the death penalty, with extensive citations to the decisions of the United States Supreme Court and the federal courts of appeal.

The following opinions of the United States Supreme Court provide a useful grounding in the Court’s jurisprudence in capital cases:

Penry v. Lynaugh, 492 U.S. 302 (1989) (requiring jury consideration of mitigating evidence at death sentencing).

Jurek v. Texas, 428 U.S. 262 (1976) (upholding Texas capital punishment statute against Eighth and Fourteenth Amendment attacks).

Oregon v. Guzek, 546 U.S. 517 (2006) (vacating and remanding a decision of the Oregon Supreme Court, which had held that the federal Constitution guaranteed defendant the right at resentencing to present live alibi evidence from his mother, U.S. Supreme Court held that the Constitution does not prohibit the state from limiting the innocence-related evidence a capital defendant may introduce at trial; explaining that the Supreme Court had not interpreted the Eighth Amendment as providing a capital defendant a right to introduce at sentencing alibi evidence designed to cast residual doubt on his guilt of basic crime of conviction; noting that any negative impact of the rule restricting defendant’s ability to introduce new alibi evidence was minimized by Oregon law giving defendant the right to present to the sentencing jury all the innocence evidence from original trial through transcripts).

2. Oregon Supreme Court

State v. Wagner, 309 Or 5, 786 P2d 93, cert. denied, 498 U.S. 879 (1990) (pre-1989 death penalty statute requires submission of additional question to sentencing jury, allowing jury to conclude mitigating evidence justifies life sentence; clarifies additional question to be asked pursuant to 1989 amendment).

State v. McDonnell, 313 Or 478, 837 P2d 941 (1992) (aggravating circumstances listed in § 163.095 presumptively constitute narrow class of conduct sufficient to justify death penalty).

State v. Guzek, 336 Or 424, 86 P3d 1106 (2004) (failure to give jury instructions on “true life sentencing option” in defendant’s third penalty phase proceeding was reversible error where defendant moved to have trial court instruct jury on that sentencing option and expressly waived all ex post factoguarantees), vacated on other grounds, 2006 WL 397856 (U.S. Feb. 22, 2006) (see above).

State v. Sparks, 336 Or 298, 83 P3d 304, cert. denied, 125 S Ct 219 (2004) (non-family members of a murder victim could present victim impact evidence during the penalty phase of the trial).

State v. Rogers, 330 Or 282, 4 P3d 1261 (2000) (vacating death sentence and holding that trial court erred in refusing to permit jury to consider option under ORS 163.150(5)(a)of sentencing defendant to life in prison without possibility of parole and to permit defendant to waive ex post factoobjection to jury’s consideration of that option).

State v. Cunningham, 320 Or 47, 880 P2d 431 (1994) (en banc), cert. denied, 514 U.S. 1005 (1995) (holding federal and state constitutions do not require district attorney to disclose statewide information about similarly situated defendants to analyze disparate treatment in plea bargaining or sentencing).

State v. Boots, 308 Or 371, 780 P2d 725 (1989) (jury must unanimously agree on manner in which aggravated murder was committed when alternative theories of aggravated murder are charged).

State v. Wright, 323 Or 8, 913 P2d 321 (1996) (stating that to be relevant to fourth statutory question, evidence must relate to some aspect of defendant’s character or background or to any circumstance of the crime).

B. Deadline for Filing the Opening Brief

The opening brief in the direct appeal of a capital case is due 180 days following record settlement. ORAP 12.10(6)(a).

Many have opined that six months is an insufficient period of time in which to complete the opening brief in a capital appeal. The Oregon Supreme Court, recognizing the importance and difficulty of the work involved, has shown some willingness to grant extensions of time to complete the brief. However, the six-month deadline represents a desire on the part of the court to prevent these cases from languishing in the state system. The attorney should not expect an extension as a matter of course.

The Supreme Court has indicated that it desires meaningful communication with counsel about the status of the case and the attorney’s work. Accordingly, the attorney should realistically estimate the time he or she will need to complete the brief and be prepared to explain why in the motion for extension. Should that new deadline also not be met, the attorney will again need to explain in some detail why more time is needed.

After the opening brief is filed, the state has 180 days to file its respondent’s brief. A capital defendant, unlike other criminal defendants, may file a reply brief without requesting permission. ORAP 12.10(6)(c). The reply brief is due 90 days after the state’s brief is filed.

C. Word Count Limitations

As with other types of appeals, the Oregon Supreme Court has transitioned from a page limitation to a word-count limitation. The current limitation is 28,000 words. ORAP 12.10(6)(d).

A party may ask the court for leave to file a brief that exceeds that limitation. SeeORAP 5.05(3)(a). Such motions are common in capital appeals. As with the motion for extension of the due date of the brief, the party will need to provide the court with a sufficient explanation regarding why a longer brief is necessary.

D. Reviewing the Record and Identifying Issues

The lead attorney, in reviewing the transcript and trial court record, should note all possible issues presented in the record. According to ABA Guideline 10.15.1(C), appellate counsel “should seek to litigate all issues, whether or not previously presented, that are arguably meritorious under the standards applicable to high quality capital defense representation, including challenges to overly restrictive procedural rules.” The commentary to the rule explains that “counsel should not let any possible ground for relief go unexplored or unexploited.”

After reviewing the record and noting and researching the issues, the lead attorney should inform the client by letter or memorandum of the potential issues for appeal. Ideally, the attorney should meet personally with the client to discuss the issues and strategies on appeal. In all cases, the client must be afforded an opportunity to discuss the issues presented by the case and to have input on which issues are raised during the appeal. If an issue is ultimately omitted from the brief, the lead attorney should inform the client of the reasons for not raising any previously identified issue.

E. Reply Brief

Unlike other criminal appeals, capital defendants are given the opportunity to file a reply brief without asking the court for leave. ORAP 12.10(6)(c). The reply brief is due 90 days after the state files its respondent’s brief.

F. Oral Argument

Once all the briefing is completed by both parties, the Oregon Supreme Court will set the case for oral argument.

Prior to the oral argument date, the court will contact the parties and ask them to identify the issues that they would like to address at oral argument. The court generally honors those elections, although it always reserves the right to question the attorneys about other issues raised in the briefs. The attorney should arrive at oral argument prepared to talk about any of the issues raised in the briefs, but focused on those issues identified by the parties prior to argument.

G. Petitions for Writ of Certiorari and Post-Conviction Relief

Once the Oregon Supreme Court issues an opinion in the case, the lead attorney should meet with the client personally as soon as practicable to discuss the opinion and the next procedural steps to be taken.

If the Oregon Supreme Court issues a favorable opinion, the attorney should contact trial counsel and provide assistance with the transition of the case to the trial court level. SeeABA Guideline 10.13(counsel has “a continuing duty to safeguard the interests of the client and should cooperate fully with successor counsel”).

If an unfavorable opinion issues from the Oregon Supreme Court, the client may be extremely despondent. It may be helpful to remind the client of the multiple stages of review (petition for reconsideration, writ of certiorari, post-conviction relief, post-conviction appeal, and federal habeas corpus review) and provide as much support as possible during this difficult and emotional stage of the appeal. If the outcome is unfavorable, one option is to file a petition for reconsideration in the Oregon Supreme Court. Appellate Division attorneys file a petition for reconsideration as a matter of course.

While the petition for reconsideration is pending in the Oregon Supreme Court, the attorney should obtain the client’s signature to proceed in forma pauperisin the United States Supreme Court.

If the Oregon Supreme Court denies the petition for reconsideration, the attorney should prepare and file a petition for writ of certiorariin the United States Supreme Court. The attorney should obtain the client’s signature to proceed in forma pauperisin the U.S. Supreme Court prior to the due date for filing the petition for cert.

While that writ is pending, the attorney may consider preparing a draft petition for post- conviction relief (PCR) for the client’s signature. Many attorneys disagree with that course of action, given the conflict of interest inherent in appellate counsel drafting a PCR petition alleging, among other things, ineffective assistance of appellate counsel.

However, the amount of time that passes between the U.S. Supreme Court’s denial of the petition for writ of certiorari(i.e., the end of the direct appeal) and the initiation of PCR petitions is critical— it counts against the client’s one-year statute of limitations for filing for federal habeas review. ORS

138.686(3)provides a timeline for filing a “notice of intent” to file a PCR petition in the PCR trial court. However, the better practice and Appellate Division policy is to file the PCR petition and a motion to stay the execution in the circuit court as soon as practicable after the U.S. Supreme Court denies the cert petition (or grants review and affirms the conviction and sentence). The time lost prior to filing the PCR petition deprives the federal habeas attorney of that much time to conduct an investigation and prepare the petition for federal habeas corpus relief.

In any event, if the U.S. Supreme Court denies the petition for writ of certiorari, the attorney should meet with the client as soon as possible to discuss the next steps in the process, including PCR and the applicable deadlines.

V. Appellate Case File

Appellate counsel has “a continuing duty to safeguard the interests of the client and should cooperate fully with successor counsel.” ABA Guideline 10.13.The lead attorney should provide physical and electronic file materials and information for complete case transfer to successor counsel and arrange a meeting with successor counsel to discuss the case in detail.
