Briefing

Alice Newlin-Cushing

The brief is by far the most important element of an appeal. The brief represents the best and most persuasive statement of your arguments and provides the court with the law it will need to decide the case. In many cases, the brief will be the onlyopportunity to present an argument to the court, and it must therefore stand on its own as a complete, clear, and comprehensive guide to the facts and law of the case.

Brief writing follows a strict structure; the court will expect the brief to adhere strictly to the rules of appellate procedure. But, within that structure, the writer should develop the argument persuasively and creatively. This chapter will describe the basic components of the appellant’s opening brief, explain how to construct the argument section, and give examples of some common claims on appeal. Additionally, it will address less common appellate briefing issues, such as reply briefs, cross-appeals, and cross-assignments of error.

I. Preliminary Components of the Appellant’s Opening Brief

ORAP 5.40sets out the requirements for the statement of the case, the first part of the appellant’s opening brief, comprising several sections. The rule provides a strict structure for these sections of the brief and requires each section to appear in the specified order and under a separate heading.

A. Nature of the Proceeding

ORAP 5.40(1) sets out the requirements for the first section of the appellant’s opening brief as follows:

A statement, without argument, of the nature of the action or proceeding, the relief sought and, in criminal cases, the indictment or information, including citation of the applicable statute.

The statement should be short and simple, generally one to two sentences. For example:

This is a criminal appeal in which defendant challenges her conviction for theft. Defendant was charged by information with one count of theft in violation of ORS ; a copy of the information is attached at ER- .

The charging instrument may be quoted in full, if appropriate, or attached in the excerpt of record at the end of the brief. This section of the brief is the first account of the case that the court will see, and after reading it, the court should have a basicunderstanding of what happened below.

B. Nature of the Judgment

ORAP 5.40(2)provides that this section of the brief should be:

A statement, without argument, of the nature of the judgment sought to be reviewed and, if trial was held, whether it was before the court or a jury.

This section provides the court with more specific information about the particulars of the judgment from which the appellant appeals. For example:

Defendant waived jury and tried his case to the court. The court convicted defendant of one court of theft and sentenced defendant to 12 months’ probation. A copy of the judgment is attached at ER- .

C. Jurisdiction

ORAP 5.40(3)requires a “statement of the statutory basis of appellate jurisdiction and, where novelty or possible doubt makes it appropriate, other supporting authority.” In criminal cases, appellate jurisdiction is generally under ORS 138.040 or 138.050. Other statutes, such as ORS 138.222, may limit the court’s scope of review of some criminal matters.

D. Notice of Appeal

ORAP 5.40(4)provides that this section must contain,

A statement of the date of entry of the judgment in the trial court register, the date that the notice of appeal was served and filed, and, if more than 30 days elapsed between those two dates, why the appeal nevertheless was timely field; and any other information relevant to appellate jurisdiction.

Be sure to note if the court gave leave to file a late notice or if the 30th day fell on a weekend or court holiday so that the notice was timely filed on the 31st or 32nd day.

E. Questions Presented

ORAP 5.40(6)describes the questions presented as “a brief statement, without argument and in general terms, of questions presented on appeal.” However, crafting the question presented can be more difficult than that simple instruction indicates.

The questions presented are the first opportunity to focus the reader’s attention on the crux of your case. The questions should be simple and narrowly focused and indicate to the reader the intersection of law and fact that gives rise to the issue the court must decide. A general question, such as “Did the trial court err by granting the state’s motion?” does not let the reader know what is at issue in the case or engage the reader’s attention.

When crafting the question or questions presented, there are many things to keep in mind. A good question serves three purposes. First, it identifies the legal issue and lets the court know what matters are vital to the case. Second, it informs the court more generally about the case, introducing the broader picture so that the arguments and facts can be more easily understood. Finally, though the questions are short and pithy and must be scrupulously accurate, they can serve as a persuasive tool. The questions frame the issue both for the structure of the brief and the reader’s understanding.

Here’s an example:

Did the trial court err in denying defendant’s motion to suppress the evidence on the ground that defendant was seized in violation of Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution when the officer asked defendant for his identification card without reasonable suspicion that defendant had committed a crime?

That question lets the reader know that the legal issue is suppression of evidence obtained from an unlawful seizure. It also gives the reader a sketch of the facts and arguments: the reader now knows that the defendant had an encounter with police during which he was asked for identification, and that the defendant is basing his arguments in Oregon constitutional law. Finally, the question frames the police encounter in terms of what police lacked—reasonable suspicion—rather than what knowledge police hadwhen they encountered defendant, which subtly frames the issue as one of insufficient justification rather than permissible police action.

F. Summary of Argument

ORAP 5.40(7)provides for a “concise summary of the arguments appearing in the body of the brief.” Accordingly, this section, though appearing in the first part of the brief, should probably be written last. It should capture the essence of the arguments appearing later in the brief so the reader knows exactly what to expect and is not left wondering about an issue that never fully materializes in the latter portions of the brief.

The court has said that the summary should be set forth in short, declarative sentences and short paragraphs. Generally, it should not exceed two pages, but the length depends somewhat on the number of issues and their complexity. The best summary in an appellant’s brief should leave the reader troubled, perhaps indignant, and wanting to know more.

The summary of the argument is a useful tool both to prepare the reader for the more detailed analysis of the issues and to serve as a quick reference for the reader of the critical points of your case when discussing or deciding your case later. Therefore, the summary should contain a short, concise and carefully selected sketch of the facts, a brief description of the rules of law and the best version of your thesis explaining how those rules apply to the facts to require the relief you seek.

G. Summary of Facts

ORAP 5.40(9)provides that this section should be as follows.

A concise summary, without argument, of all the facts of the case material to the determination of the appeal. The summary shall be in narrative form with references to the places in the transcript, narrative statement, audio record, record or excerpt where such facts appear.

Facts can be organized either chronologically or topically, but the critical framework—both for organization and for selecting which facts to include—is the standard of review that applies to your case. The standard of review may limit you to presenting the facts in the light most favorable to the state, or to including only the facts before the court at a particular point in the proceedings. Furthermore, the standard of review may lend itself more to a topical than chronological structure, or may require emphasis on procedural over historical facts.

The statement of facts mustflow from the standard of review so the reader has all the necessary information to review the case and onlyinformation that can factor into the analysis. The facts must be scrupulously accurate, but they need not be dry. The summary is an opportunity to tell the story of your case, capture the reader’s attention, and highlight the most important facts. Important facts include both those that support your argument and those that weaken it. Omitting facts that weaken your argument will affect your credibility with the court; include them and prepare to address them fully in your analysis.

H. Assignment of Error

After the statement of the case, ORAP 5.45sets out the guidelines for the assignment of error. Critically, it warns that:

No matter claimed as error will be considered on appeal unless the claim of error was preserved in the lower court and is assigned as error in the opening brief in accordance with this rule, provided that the appellate court may consider an error of law apparent on the face of the record.

Thus the import of that rule is that the assignment of error is the most critical choice the appellant can make. The court will consider only the matters the appellant assigns. The assignments narrow the case and identify what must be decided by the court; accordingly, they should be specific enough so that there is no question what action is being challenged, but not so detailed that multiple assignments are necessary to identify what is essentially a single error. However, similarerror cannot be joined simply because of the similarity. For example, each hearsay statement objected to may need its own assignment of error even if the arguments are identical; in that case, the arguments can be combined in a single section pursuant to ORAP 5.45(6).

Each assignment of error must also “identify precisely the legal, procedural, factual or other ruling that is being challenged.” ORAP 5.45. In effect, the appellant must identify what action the lower court took in error so that the reviewing court is able to correct it.

II. The Substance

A. Standard of Review

ORAP 5.45(5)provides as follows.

Under the subheading “Standard of Review,” each assignment of error shall identify the applicable standard or standards of review, supported by citation to the statute, case law, or other legal authority for each standard of review.

The standard of review governs not only how much deference the Court of Appeals will give to the lower court, but also the nature and degree of error the appellant must demonstrate to prevail. The standard of review can be found in case law; the court announces the standard(s) of review it is using to decide a particular issue in its written opinions, generally in the first paragraph of the opinion.

Also, the applicable standards of review in many criminal appeals are included in the “Common Claims” section of this chapter.

There are four main categories of issues on appeal: questions of law, questions of fact, discretionary rulings and mixed questions of law and fact. Findings of fact are reviewed to determine whether there is evidence to support them, with varying degrees of deference to the trial court’s findings. In this category, keep in mind that where the trial court did not make express findings, the appellate court will view the facts consistently with the trial court’s ultimate conclusion, so even in absence of clear statements on the record, the appellate court may defer to the trial court’s implied factual findings. Ball v. Gladden, 250 Or 485, 487, 443 P2d 621 (1968).

Pure questions of law are generally reviewed for legal error without any deference. However, where the issue is mixed law and fact, the review varies according to the nature of the issue and the procedural posture of the case, so the writer should take care when the answer to a purely legal question must be applied to the findings of historical fact, because that situation gives rise to mixed- question issues.

Finally, many rulings are within the trial court’s discretion, and so long as the court has chosen between a number of legally permissible outcomes, the appellate court will affirm. When assigning error to a court’s discretionary ruling, an advocate must, as ever, be aware of the standard of review because the review will be extremely deferential.

B. Preservation

ORAP 5.45requires that “each assignment of error, as appropriate, must specify the stage in the proceedings when the question or issue presented by the assignment of error was raised in the lower court, the method or manner of raising it, and the way in which it was resolved or passed on by the lower court.” Demonstrating preservation is key because the court’s “first door out,” or easiest way to decide any particular issue, may be not to decide it at all because it was not sufficiently raised below.

The simplest and probably best way to demonstrate preservation is to quote the portions of the record where the issue was raised and where the court ruled on the issue. Include argument at trial or relevant portions of motions or briefs. You may also wish to attach motions as part of the excerpt of record.

If an issue is unpreserved, the preservation section must still identify the challenged ruling, or, if there is none, show where in the record the error was made, and alert the court that you are requesting the court consider it as plain error. It is probably best to discuss the reasons for plain error review in the argument section of the brief, addressing the considerations laid out in State v. Brown, 310 Or 347, 355 (1990), and Ailes v. Portland Meadows, Inc., 312 Or 376, 382 (1991).

C. The Argument

The argument is the longest and most substantive portion of the brief and should be carefully constructed. It is meant to persuade the court that the relief requested is correct, or even mandatory, by demonstrating that the facts of the case come within the scope of the controlling law. Though this section must still be accurate and your factual and legal assertions well supported, the style of writing is not objective; it is as persuasive as you can make it.

Where to begin? Generally, the best argument begins with the strongest point, though the writer should be careful not to skip any necessary logical points that the court must consider before reaching that strongest piece. Open persuasively and powerfully to create a strong and favorable impression in the minds of the reader from the outset.

Then, break your argument down into easily digestible and discrete pieces or analytical steps. Each theory or ground for ruling in favor of your position should be a section of the argument under its own heading, broken into subheadings if necessary to draw the reader along the analytical path of your argument. Headings are more than just titles of sections; good headings can both organize the brief into manageable pieces for the reader andmake the argument when the reader skims the table of contents of the brief. Good headings and subheadings can be read together as a coherent summary of your argument’s development.

Headings should be declarative sentences containing a conclusion which follows from the principle of law being discussed and applied to the facts of the case. Both law and facts should be blended together in the heading, which must also be easily understood, interesting and informative. Headings introduce the argument and must bring the reader’s attention to each issue so that the reader is ready for the details and subtleties of your analysis. Headings and subheadings must also be brief because cumbersome headings break up the flow of the argument both visually and logically. You may lose your reader’s interest or attention if you bog down your brief with headings that are too long.

Finally, your argument should draw a logical line for the reader through your points as you carefully explain why you should win. Then, as appropriate, the argument section should use the same approach to argue why you shouldn’t lose; specifically, you should address unfavorable case law, weaknesses in your record, any potential harmlessness problems with the error you’ve assigned, and the strongest points of your opponent’s argument.

III. Common Claims

A. Demurrers

ORS 135.630provides that a defendant may demur to an accusatory instrument when, on the face of the instrument, the facts stated do not constitute an offense. Accordingly, review of a trial court’s ruling on a demurrer is for errors of law. However, like the trial court, the appellate court may not consider facts extrinsic to the allegations in the indictment, even ifthe parties have stipulated to those facts. State v. Cervantes, 232 Or App 567, 223 p3d 425 (2009).

B. Motions for Judgment of Acquittal

An appellate court reviews the denial of a motion for judgment of acquittal by examining the evidence in the light most favorable to the state to determine whether “a rational trier of fact, accepting reasonable inferences and reasonable credibility choices, could have found the essential element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt.” State v. Cunningham, 320 Or 47, 63, 880 P2d 431(1994). However, the appellate court reviews the entire record, including the evidence entered after the motion was denied, in determining whether the evidence was sufficient.

C. Motions to Suppress Evidence

There are many common grounds to suppress evidence. Most suppression issues are reviewed for errors of law. For example, the court reviews the trial court’s decision regarding the authority of a person to consent to police entry as a matter of law. In so doing, the appellate court accepts the historical facts found by the trial court, if the record contains evidence to support them. Similarly, it reviews the trial court’s ultimate conclusion that a defendant’s consent to a search was voluntary for errors of law. State v. Stevens, 311 Or 119, 135 (1991). Whether an officer’s conduct constituted a search or a seizure also presents a question of law. State v. Ehly, 317 Or 66, 75, 854 P2d 421 (1993). As does whether circumstances require an officer to provide a defendant with ''Miranda. State v. Roble-Baker, 340 Or 631, 640–41, 136 P3d 22 (2006).

D. Common Evidentiary Objections

Relevance and Prejudice: Relevance rulings are reviewed for errors of law. However, relevance is a very low bar for admissibility. Appellate review of a trial court’s ruling as to whether the probative value of otherwise relevant evidence was substantially outweighed by the prejudicial effect is for abuse of discretion.State v. Brumwell (2011)2011 WL 108647; ORS. 40.160. In determining whether probative value of evidence exceeds its prejudicial nature, trial courts are required to consider need for the evidence, certainty of the evidence, relative strength or weakness of the evidence, and its possible inflammatory effect on the jury. OEC 403; State v. Mason, 100 Or App 240, 785 P2d 378 (1990); State v. Hickam, 71 Or App 471, 692 P2d 672 (1984); ORS § 40.160.

Character Evidence:OEC 404(4)precludes OEC 403 balancing of the probative value of the other crimes, wrongs or acts of the defendant against, among other things, the danger of unfair prejudice. State v. Moore/Coen, 349 Or 371, 387–92, 245 P3d 101 (2010); State v. Phillips, 217 OrApp 93, 97–98, 174 P3d 1032 (2007).

Generally:With respect to rulings on evidence, an abuse of discretion occurs when a trial court exercises its authority outside the boundaries of a statute or rule that provides for the admission of evidence. State v. Rogers, 330 Or 282, 310-12 (2000). The appellate court reviews for abuse of discretion the court’s decision to admit evidence despite defendant’s objection that it was cumulative. State v. Skelton, 153 Or App 580, 584, rev den, 327 Or 448 (1998).

E. Jury Instructions

Review of a trial court’s refusal to give a requested instruction is for legal error. The jury instructions actually given are reviewed as a wholeto determine whether the instructions probably created an erroneous impression of the law in the minds of the jurors that affected the outcome of the case. Maasv. Willer, 203 Or App 124, 129, 125 P.3d 87 (2005), rev den, 340 Or 411, 134 P3d 169 (2006); see alsoState v. Oatney, 335 Or 276, 290, 66 P3d 475 (2003), cert. den.,540 U.S. 1151, 124 S Ct 1148, 157 L Ed 2d 1045 (2004) (“In determining whether it was error to give a particular instruction, the instructions are read as a whole to determine whether they accurately state the law.”).

However, review of the court’s choice among several legally correct instructions supplying the same information is for abuse of discretion. ORCP 59Hlimits the court’s review of jury instructions unless a defendant first objects to an offered instruction or offers an instruction that the court rejects, and then also takes exception after the jury instructions are given.

F. Motion for Mistrial

The appellate court’s review of a trial court’s decision to deny a motion for mistrial is limited to determining whether the court abused its discretion. State v. Terry, 333 Or 163, 175–76 (2001), cert den, 122 S Ct 2368 (2002); State v. Pratt, 316 Or 561, 583 (1993).

IV. Cross-Appeals and Cross-Assignments of Error

ORAP 5.57governs cross-assignments of error. A cross-assignment of error is an error that the respondent assigns in the response brief in order to raise the claim of error on appeal. The rule lays out the following circumstances when a cross-assignment of error is appropriate:

(a) If, by challenging the trial court ruling, the respondent does not seek to reverse or modify the judgment on appeal; and

(b) If the relief sought by the appellant were to be granted, respondent would desire reversal or modi- fication of an intermediate ruling of the trial court.

For example, a cross-assignment of error may be appropriate when the respondent prevailed overall below, despite having lost a motion during trial. The respondent would not otherwise wish to appeal, but might wish to make a cross-assignment of error to challenge the loss of the motion in the event that the relief sought by appellant is granted.

ORAP 5.57(2)(a–b)governs cross-appeals.

ORAP 5.65explains how a cross-appellant must construct the respondent’s answering brief including a section in the form of an appellant’s opening brief containing the cross-appellant’s assignments of error. Unlike cross-assignments of error, the assignments of error on cross-appeal do seek to reverse or modify the judgment, regardless of whether appellant’s requested relief is granted.

V. Reply and Supplemental Briefing

A. When to Reply

ORAP 5.70governs the reply brief. In criminal and many other categories of cases, an appellant may not submit a reply brief without leave from the court. ORAP 5.70(3)(a). Also, the reply brief is limited to matters raised in the respondent’s answering brief, and should not be used to merely restate the arguments in the opening brief. ORAP 5.70(1)(a). A reply brief is similar to a respondent’s answering brief in its form and structure.

Accordingly, a reply brief is most appropriate when the respondent’s brief raises an argument or cites authority not addressed in the opening brief and the appellant believes that the court requires more information to appropriately analyze those new concerns.

B. When to Supplement

Supplemental briefing is rarely necessary. However, new case law decided after briefing and before argument may change the analysis to such a degree that a memorandum of additional authorities would not suffice to treat the issues now presented in the appeal. Occasionally, the court will ask for supplemental briefing on an issue that comes up for the first time at argument, or the parties may ask for leave at argument to file supplemental briefs to better address such an issue.

C. Required Motions

ORAP 5.70(3)(b)provides that “a motion for leave to file a reply brief shall be submitted, without copies, within 14 days after the filing of the brief to which permission to reply is sought.” Again, a reply brief cannot be filed without leave from the court. It is critical to move for leave to file within fourteen days of the filing of the brief you wish to reply to.

VI. Petitions for Reconsideration

ORAP 6.25governs reconsideration by the Court of Appeals. The rule provides for a party to file a petition asking the court to reconsider decisions including opinions and orders ruling on a motion that disposes of an appeal. Petitions must be based on one of the following grounds: (1) a claim of factual error in the decision, (2) a claim of error in the procedural disposition of the appeal requiring correction or clarification to make the disposition consistent with the holding or the rationale of the decision or the posture of the case below, (3) a claim of error in the designation of the prevailing party or award of costs, (4) a claim that there has been a change in the statutes or applicable case law since the decision, or (5) a claim that the court erred in construing or applying the law.

Claims addressing issues already argued in the briefs are disfavored. ORAP 6.25(1)(e). In fact, the Court of Appeals discourages advocates from petitioning for reconsideration in general, but especially when the petition merely disagrees with the court’s decision. And, though a factual error in the decision may prompt the court to reconsider, such errors rarely result in any significant alteration of the analysis or outcome of the case.
