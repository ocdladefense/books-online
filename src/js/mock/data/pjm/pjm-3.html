Post Judgement Manual

2012 edition

Motions

Mary M. Reese

I. Introduction

This chapter concerns the filing of motions in criminal cases in the Oregon Court of Appeals. Generally speaking, the Court of Appeals has authority to decide all motions in an appeal unless the case is pending before the Oregon Supreme Court. See ORAP 9.30for further details.

This chapter is essentially a distillation of the materials in three main sources:

• The Oregon Rules of Appellate Procedure (ORAP).

• James W. Nass, Chapter 8, “Motion Practice and the Appellate Commissioner,” Appeal and Review: The Basics, pp 37–46, OSB Legal Publications (2010).

• Internal Practices Guidelines Oregon Court of Appeals(July 6, 2007). Refer to those sources for further information.

II. General Procedures

A. Filing the Motion

ORAP 7.05 to 7.55pertain to motions filed in the Court of Appeals (and Supreme Court). They also apply to a party’s response to any order of the court requiring a response, such as an order to show cause why the court should not strike a brief or to show cause why the court should not dismiss a case. Be sure to check the Oregon Judicial Department’swebsite for any amendments to the rules.

Appendix 7.10-1sets forth a list of commonly filed motions. That list is nota comprehensive list of the type of motions that may be filed. A motion is simply a request for the court to do or not do something. It may—but need not—be authorized by a particular statute or rule. If you want something, ask for it.

ORAP 7.05 and 7.10set forth the specifications for the preparation, filing, and service of motions. See, also, ORAP 1.35(regarding filing and service generally), ORAP 7.35(regarding motions seeking emergency relief ), and ORAP 16.03–16.60(regarding filing by electronic means).

Be sure to use the proper title for the motion. ORAP 7.10(1)(b), (c) and (d); Appendix 7.10-

1. If you are party to an expedited criminal appeal—for example, a state’s appeal of certain pretrial orders—state that fact in your title. ORAP 12.07(1)(a) or (2)(a). In the body of the motion, specify the relief requested at the beginning of the motion and, if the motion exceeds two pages, at the end of the motion. Do not submit a proposed order with the motion; the Court of Appeals prepares its own orders.

A party generally has 14 days to respond to a motion. ORAP 7.05(3); but see, ORAP 7.25(6)(noting that the Court of Appeals decides motions for extensions of time within a few days of filing). A party may request an extension of time in which to file a response. ORAP 7.25. Note: the Court of Appeals tends to give cursory review to unopposed motions and usually grants them. No ORAP provides for the filing of a reply to a response and the court discourages them. In practice, however, the court will consider a reply if it is filed before the court decides the motion.

B. Tolling Time

The filing of a motion “that must be ruled on before the next event in the appellate process” generally tolls the time “for the next event in the appellate process” until the court disposes of the motion. ORAP 7.30(1). The rule gives examples of such motions. The rule does not apply in four situations:

•First, filing a motion does not toll the time periods that apply in expedited criminal appeals.

ORAP 10.25(2)(b); ORAP 12.07(4)(b).

•Second, filing a motion does not toll any time period otherwise required by statute or rule in, for example, land-use cases, juvenile dependency cases, and adoption cases. ORAP 7.30(1); ORAP 4.72(land use cases); ORAP 10.15(8)(juvenile dependency and adoption cases).

•Third, filing a motion does not toll a time period when the court orders otherwise, such as when, for example, the court believes that the party is filing a motion for delay. ORAP 7.30(2).

•Fourth, filing a motion does not toll a time period when the court has already ordered that no further extension of time will be granted. ORAP 7.30(2).

C. Disposing of the Motion

No ORAP requires that the court decide a motion in a criminal appeal within any particular time. If, however, you are party to an expedited criminal appeal, state that fact in your title. ORAP 12.07(1)(a)or (2)(a).

A party may request emergency consideration when relief is required in less than 21 days. See ORAP 7.35. Do not file a motion for an extension of time as an emergency motion because the court decides such motions within a few days. ORAP 7.25(6).

There is no oral argument on a motion, unless the court or the appellate commissioner directs otherwise. ORAP 7.05(4). Oral argument is a rare event.

The Chief Judge may decide any motion or he may delegate that authority to another judge or the appellate commissioner. ORAP 7.15; ORAP 7.55; ORS 2.570(6). The court’s administrative staff also decides a limited category of motions. Motions that are filed after the final calendaring of a case for oral argument or submission on the brief and before the case is decided on its merits are submitted to the presiding judge of the three-judge panel hearing the case. ORAP 7.25(3).

1. Decisions by the Administrative Staff of the Records Section

Motions decided by the administrative staff are generally unopposed or stipulated motions such as a first motion for extensions of time to file a transcript or a brief, a motion to consolidate, most motions to substitute counsel, an appellant’s motion to dismiss, or a stipulated motion to dismiss. The orders are issued under the name of the Chief Judge.

2. Decisions by the Appellate Commissioner

The appellate commissioner has authority concurrent with the Chief Judge to decide motions. ORAP 7.55(1). In practice, the commissioner decides all motions not delegated to the administrative staff, except that he may not decide any motion that would dispose of the case on the merits. ORAP 7.55 (1) and (2). There are three exceptions to that prohibition:

•First, the commissioner may decide joint or stipulated motions for “disposition on the merits, where the relief granted is consistent with the relief sought in the motion.” ORAP

7.55(2)(a).

•Second, the commissioner may decide a motion to reverse and remand due to the loss or destruction of the trial court record. ORAP 7.55(2)(b).

•Third, and most significantly in criminal appeals, the commissioner may decide a motion for summary affirmance (see below). ORAP 7.55(2)(c).

The appellate commissioner may refer any motion to the Chief Judge or Motions Department. ORAP 7.55(3). The commissioner will refer a motion to the Motions Department when the perspective of the Motions Department would be beneficial or when the Motions Department may wish to publish an opinion on the issue raised in the motion.

3. Decisions by the Court of Appeals Motions Department

Once a month, the Motions Department decides motions that dispose of the case on the merits (with the exception of those described above that may be decided by the commissioner). The department is comprised of the Chief Judge and one presiding judge. ORS 2.570(2)(b). The judge may appoint a third judge to break a tie vote or if the department decides a motion by published opinion. Id. Staff attorneys attend department conferences. Occasionally, the Motions Department will refer a motion to the three-judge panel that will ultimately decide the appeal on the merits. That occurs when the motion is best considered with an issue raised or to be raised in the substantive briefs.

Except when a motion challenges the jurisdiction of the court, the denial of a motion by the Motions Department is usually final. ORAP 7.25(3). Occasionally, however, the Motions Department will specify that a party may re-raise the issue raised in the motion in a brief.

A ruling on a motion that challenges the jurisdiction of the court is not final; a party may challenge the jurisdiction of the court at any time, even after a contrary ruling by the Motions Department. ORAP 7.05.

D. Reconsideration of a Ruling on a Motion

1. Reconsideration of Ruling that Disposes of the Appeal

A party seeking reconsideration of an order disposing of an appeal—for example, an order granting a motion for summary affirmance or a motion to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction—shall file a petitionfor reconsideration. ORAP 6.25(1).

The petition must be filed 14 days after the decision in question. ORAP 6.25(2). ORAP

6.25(2) sets forth further specifications for the preparation and filing of the petition. If a response is filed, it must be filed within seven days. ORAP 6.25(4). If a party wishes to file a response, which is not required by rule, then the form of the response should comply with the specifications listed in ORAP 6.25(2).

The grounds for a petition for reconsideration are set forth in ORAP 6.25(1)(a–e). With one exception having to do with rulings made by the appellate commissioner, see below, one should not repeat an argument already made in the motion and addressed in the ruling. One must assume that the court “got” the argument, but rejected it. If, however, the court’s ruling depends upon the erroneous construction or application of law not addressed in the motion, then file a petition for reconsideration.

Once the court receives a petition for reconsideration, it need not await the filing of a response before ruling on the petition. ORAP 6.25(4) In other words, it may rule on the motion within seven days without considering any response. If, however, a response is filed before the court rules, the court will consider it. ORAP 6.25(4).

One need not file a petition for reconsideration as a prerequisite to filing a petition for review. ORAP 6.25(3). If one files a petition for reconsideration, then the time for filing a petition for review of an order disposing of the appeal does not begin to run until the Court of Appeals issues its written disposition of the petition. ORAP 6.25(2)(b)(i).

Exception:The one exception to the rule that a petitioner should not repeat an argument already made in the motion and addressed in the ruling is when the petitioner is seeking reconsideration of a decision by the appellate commissioner. ORAP 7.554(4)(a).

Example:Assume that the appellate commissioner affirmatively ruled on a motion for summary affirmance or a motion to reverse and remand due to the loss or destruction of the trial court record. A party may request reconsideration of a ruling of the appellate commissioner under ORAP 6.25. ORAP 7.55(4)(a). The appellate commissioner may reconsider his ruling and affirm, reverse, or modify it. Id.

If the appellate commissioner denies reconsideration or grants the petition and affirms the result, then he refers the petition to the Chief Judge or Motions Department for a ruling. ORAP 7.55(4)(a). When that happens, you will want the Chief Judge or Motions Department to review the arguments you made in the original motion as well as any additional arguments made in response to the appellate commissioner’s ruling. Accordingly, the petition should include the arguments made in the original motion as well as any additional arguments.

2. Reconsideration of Ruling that Does Not Dispose of the Appeal

A party seeking reconsideration of an order that does something other than dispose of the appeal—for example, an order denying a motion for an extended brief—may file a motionfor reconsideration. ORAP 6.25(5). Such a motion “is subject to ORAP 7.05regarding motions in general.” Id. Presumably, the other rules concerning general motions would also apply. See, e.g,ORAP 7.10. One may further presume that the grounds for such a motion are the same as those set forth in ORAP 6.25(1)(a–e).

There is no stated time limit for filing a motion for reconsideration; however, it is probably more efficacious to file such a motion within a short time while the matter is still “fresh” in the court’s mind. An adverse party has 14 days to respond. ORAP 7.05(3).

As noted in the preceding subsection, one should not repeat an argument already made in the original motion and addressed in the ruling. ORAP 7.55(4)(a). There is an exception to that rule: when one is seeking reconsideration of a decision made by the appellate commissioner.

Example:Assume that the appellate commissioner adversely ruled on some motion that does not dispose of the appeal. A party may move the commissioner to reconsider that ruling under ORAP 6.25(5). ORAP 7.55(4)(a). The appellate commissioner may reconsider his ruling and affirm, reverse, or modify it. Id.

If appellate commissioner denies reconsideration or grants the motion for reconsideration and affirms the result, then he refers the motion to the Chief Judge or Motions Department for a ruling. ORAP 7.55(4)(a). When that happens, you will want the Chief Judge or Motions Department to review the arguments you made in the original motion as well as any additional arguments made in response to the appellate commissioner’s ruling. Accordingly, the motion for reconsideration should include the arguments made in the original motion as well as any additional arguments.

No specific ORAP states that the time for filing a petition for review of an order that does something other than dispose of the appeal begins to run when the Court of Appeals issues its written disposition of the motion for reconsideration. ORAP 9.05(2)(b), footnote 2. But that is the accepted practice. See, generally, ORAP 9.05(2)(a).

E. Oregon Supreme Court Review of Rulings on Motions in General

A party may petition the Oregon Supreme Court to review a ruling on any motion by the Chief Judge or Motions Department. ORAP 9.05. Except in the case of a summary determination of appealability (see ORS 19.235; ORAP 2.35(4)), a petition for review is due to be filed within 35 days of the date that the Court of Appeals decides a motion. If one has filed a petition or motion for reconsideration, then the 35 days begins to run from the date that the Court of Appeals has decided the petition or motion for reconsideration. See the two preceding subsections.

One need not file a petition for reconsideration or a motion for reconsideration as a prerequisite to filing a petition for review of a ruling by the Chief Judge or Motions Departmenton any motion. ORAP

6.25(3). But, in the case of a ruling by the appellate commissioner, with one exception, one must file a petition for reconsideration or motion for reconsideration and obtain a ruling from the Chief Judge or Motions Department before filing a petition for review. ORAP 7.55(4)(b). See the two preceding subsections.

Exception:When the appellate commissioner makes a determination of appealability under ORS

19.235(3) and designates it a summary determination under ORAP 2.35(1), one may directly file a petition for review from that order. ORAP 7.55(4)(b).

III. Substantive Motions

A. Motion to Determine Appealability

A party may move the trial court to make a “summary determination” of the appealability of an order or judgment. ORS 19.235(1). The trial court may also make a “summary disposition” on its own motion. Id. If the trial court determines that the order or judgment is not appealable, then, in its discretion, it “may proceed through entry of judgment.” ORS 19.235(2). If it chooses to “proceed through entry of judgment,” it retains jurisdiction until “the appellate court has made an express determination that an appeal has been taken from an appealable order or judgment, all means for obtaining review of that determination under [ORS 19.235(3)] have been exhausted, and the State Court Administrator at the direction of the court has mailed copies of the final appellate court determination to the trial court and the parties.” ORS 19.235(4). Except for the entry of judgment, no action taken by the trial court before that time is void for lack of jurisdiction. Id.

Instead of choosing to “proceed through entry of judgment,” a trial court may refer the matter to the Court of Appeals. ORS 19.235(2). In addition, despite a trial court’s ruling of appealabilty (which is not itself appealable), a party may move the Court of Appeals to make a summary determination of appealability. ORS 19.235(2) and (3). The Court of Appeals may do the same on its own motion. Id.; ORAP 2.35(1). If the Court of Appeals undertakes the determination of the appealability of an order or judgment, then the trial court proceedings may be stayed at the discretion of the trial court or upon the order of the Court of Appeals, on its own motion or at the request of a party. ORS 19.235(2). The Court of Appeals may also stay any order or judgment entered by the trial court pending a final determination of appealability. Id.

ORS 19.235(3) and ORAP 2.35(4) provide for expedited review by the Oregon Supreme Court of a summarydetermination of appealability by the Court of Appeals. A petition for review must be filed within 14 days, unless ordered otherwise. Id. The petition must be entitled, “Expedited Summary Determination of Appealability Pursuant to ORAP 2.35(4).” ORAP 2.35(4).

Note:the Oregon Supreme Court will not review a determination of appealability unless it expressly states that “the determination is a summary determination under ORS 19.235(3).” ORAP 2.35(3)(a) and (b). The determination must also notify the parties that:

• The order or opinion is a summary determination of appealability under ORS 19.235(3).

• The determination is subject to review or reconsideration by the Oregon Supreme Court.

• The petition for review shall be filed within 14 days after the order or opinion or such short time as may be ordered.

• The Supreme Court will expedite its consideration of the petition.

ORAP 2.35(3)(a). If the Court of Appeals determination does not contain the required language, then the determination is not “summary” and does not qualify for expedited review. Presumably, however, one could petition for review in the normal course as provided by ORAP 9.05.

Note:If an appeal is pending and the Court of Appeals determines that the trial court intended to enter an appealable judgment but did not technically do so for one of three reasons, then the Court of Appeals may decide not to dismiss an appeal but to instead give the trial court leave to enter an appealable judgment. ORS 19.270(4). The three reasons are these:

•The judgment was defective in form.

•The judgment was entered at a time when the trial court did not have jurisdiction of the cause because of a premature notice of appeal.

•The trial court had not yet entered an appealable judgment.

Id. Note that ORS 19.270(4)applies on its face only to judgments and not to any other potentially appealable decision by the trial court.

B. Motion to Determine Jurisdiction or Motion to Dismiss for Lack of Jurisdiction

ORS 138.040, ORS 138.050, ORS 138.053, and ORS 138.060set forth the jurisdiction of the Court of Appeals in criminal cases. If you are the appellant, and “appellate jurisdiction is not free from doubt,” then include a statement asserting jurisdiction, with supporting citation, in the notice of appeal. ORAP 2.05(9). Doing so will potentially avoid dismissal of the appeal in response to a motion to determine jurisdiction or to dismiss for lack of jurisdiction or to the court’s own order. See, e.g., 7.55(5)(the Court of Appeals may, on its “own motion,” issue an order to show cause why an appeal should be dismissed for lack of jurisdiction).

A denial of a motion that challenges the jurisdiction of the court is not final; a party may challenge the jurisdiction of the court at any time, even after a contrary ruling by the appellate commissioner, Chief Judge, or Motions Department. ORAP 7.05(1)(c); ORAP 7.25(3).

C. Motion to Dismiss Upon Death of the Defendant

Any party who learns of the death of the defendant in a criminal case shall notify the court and all other parties within 28 days after learning of the defendant’s death. ORAP 8.05(2)(a). Any party may concurrently or subsequently move to dismiss the appeal. Id. Any party may file a memorandum addressing whether the court should dismiss the appeal or vacate the judgment or both within 28 days after the filing of the notice of death. Id. ORAP 8.05(2)(b) sets forth the presumptive dispositions upon the death of the defendant.

D. Motion to Dismiss Upon Abscond or Escape of the Defendant

If a defendant in a criminal case (and other similar types of cases) escapes or absconds from supervision, the state may move for a dismissal of the appeal. The court will dismiss the appeal if the appellant has not surrendered at the time the motion is decided by the court. And the court will presume the appellant has not surrendered if the court has not been advised to the contrary. ORAP 8.10(3).

If a such a motion is filed in your case, do two things. First, make every effort to contact the defendant. If you can do so, advise the defendant to surrender and warn him or her that the appeal will likely be dismissed if he or she does not do so. If the defendant surrenders, advise the court of that fact. Second, whether you can or cannot contact the defendant, review the motion to dismiss and any supporting affidavits and determine if it actually establishes a factual or legal basis for the appellate court to conclude that the defendant has escaped or absconded. If not, then file a response to the motion making those points.

E. Motion to Dismiss for Lack of Prosecution

ORAP 1.20provides that a party may move the court to dismiss an appeal for want of prosecution. The court may do the same on its own motion. Id. ORAP 10.25(1)provides that the state’s failure to file the opening brief within 35 days after the transcript settles in an expeditedcriminal appeal will result in automatic dismissal of the appeal.

F. Motion to Dismiss Because Appeal Is Moot

If a party who becomes aware of non-privileged facts that render the appeal moot, the party must notify the court and any party to the appeal of that fact. ORAP 8.45. The party—and presumably all parties to the appeal—may also file a motion to dismiss the appeal.

G. Stipulated or Uncontested Motions to Dismiss

If a motion to dismiss is the result of a negotiated settlement or compromise, then state that fact in the motion or in the response to such a motion. ORAP 7.45(1).

If a motion to dismiss is filed before the transcript has been filed, serve a copy of the motion on the transcript coordinator and, if known, the court reporters and transcribers who are responsible for preparing the transcript. ORAP 7.15(4).

The administrative staff of the records section ordinarily will allow an appellant’s motion to dismiss or a stipulated motion to dismiss. The appellate judgment will usually issue at the same time as the order of dismissal.

H. Motion for Summary Affirmance

In any case in which one of the parties is represented by court-appointed counsel, the respondent may move for summary affirmance after the appellant has filed the opening brief and before the respondent files the answering brief. ORAP 7.50; ORS 138.225. An appellant opposing such a motion must establish that the appeal “presents a substantial question of law.” Note that the appellant need only establish the existence of a real question of law, not that he or she would prevailon the question of law. If no substantial question of law is presented, the court may summarily affirm the judgment. ORAP 7.50; ORS 138.225.

The appellate commissioner may decide a motion for summary affirmance “to the same extent that the Chief Judge could decide the motion.” ORAP 7.55(2)(c). The Chief Judge—and therefore, the appellate commissioner—may grant an unopposed motion for summary affirmance. ORAP 7.50(1).

If the motion is opposed, the concurrence of two judges is necessary to pronounce judgment. ORS 2.570(4). The Motions Department usually decides such motions.

Summary affirmance constitutes a decision on the merits of the appeal. ORAP 7.50; ORS 138.225.

If the state has filed a motion for summary affirmance in response to a client’s Section B Balfour brief or in a case where the client has filed a pro sesupplemental brief, then forward a copy of the motion to the client. The client has 35 days from the date the motion was filedto answer the motion. ORAP 7.50(2).

I. Motion for Reversal of Judgment for Loss or Destruction of Record

ORS 19.420(formerly ORS 19.130) provides:

Whenever it appears that an appeal cannot be prosecuted, by reason of the loss or destruction, through no fault of the appellant, of the reporter’s notes or audio records, or of the exhibits or other matter necessary to the prosecution of the appeal, the judgment appealed from may be reversed and a new trial ordered as justice may require.

To be entitled to a new trial under ORS 19.420, the appellant must show two things:

•due diligence in attempting to find and supply a record for the purposes of appeal; and

•a prima facieshowing of error, or unfairness in the trial, or miscarriage of justice.

Smith v. Custom Micro, Inc., 311 Or 375, 379, 811 P2d 1371 (1991).

Regarding the first required showing, due diligence requires at least an attempt to prepare an agreed narrative statement. ORAP 3.45. An “agreed narrative statement” is “the parties’ stipulated account of proceedings in lieu of a transcript or audio record.” ORAP 1.15(b). The specifications for the preparation and filing of an agreed narrative statement are set forth at ORAP 3.45.

Regarding the second required showing, “an appellant must persuade the reviewing court that what is missing from the record would be a prima facieindication of an error or would demonstrate unfairness in the trial.” State v. Dam, 116 Or App 210, 213-14, 840 P2d 1317 (1992). The appellant must make that showing “despite the absence of a record to look at.” Id. To determine how that standard applies in the context of a particular case, one must consult the case law applying ORS

19.420and formerORS 19.130.

Example:A defendant might successfully obtain a reversal of the judgment under ORS 19.240 by establishing that he or she made a motion to suppress particular evidence before trial, that the parties on appeal tried but could not reconstruct the hearing on the motion to suppress or agree on a narrative statement, and that the trial court’s denial of the motion to suppress resulted in the admission of critical evidence at the defendant’s trial. Or a party might successfully obtain a reversal by establishing that he or she made a motion for judgment of acquittal at trial and that the parties cannot reconstruct the evidence supporting the contested elements of the state’s case.

J. Motion to Vacate the Judgment and Remand to the Trial Court

On occasion, the state may be convinced by the argument made in the defendant’s opening brief and, in response to a request from appellate counsel, agree to file a joint motion to vacate the judgment and remand the case to the trial court for “further proceedings” or a particular negotiated remedy, e.g, a new trial or resentencing. No statute or rule provides for such a motion; however, as noted above, the Court of Appeals does not require that a motion be authorized by a particular statute or rule before considering it. The only limitation on such a motion is that the remedy negotiated by the parties, if any, must be within the statutory authority of the Court of Appeals to grant. See, generally, ORS 138.240: “The appellate court may reverse, affirm or modify the judgment or order appealed from and shall, if necessary or proper, order a new trial.”

IV. Procedural Motions

A. Motion to Stay Enforcement of the Judgment

A defendant has no statutory or constitutional right to bail pending appeal and after conviction of a crime. Priest v. Pearce, 314 Or 411, 415, 419, 840 P2d 65 (1992).A party, however, may move the trial court, at its discretion, to stay the enforcement of the incarcerative termof the judgment, except in the case of murder or treason. ORS 135.285(2); ORS 138.135(1); State v. Wimber, 108 Or App 1,

4–5, 814 P2d 169 (1991).The Court of Appeals has no jurisdiction to review a trial court’s denial of a motion to stay. Wimber, 108 Or App at 5.

A party may move the trial court or the Court of Appeals to stay the enforcement of an order to pay fines or costs at its discretion and “upon such terms as it deems proper.” ORS 138.135(2).

The purpose of a stay is to preserve the statusquo or to protect appellate jurisdiction of a case.

Blair v. Blair, 199 Or 273, 279–87, 247 P2d 883 (1952).Accordingly, do not use a motion to stay to argue for affirmative relief.

B. Motion to Consolidate Appeals

When multiple notices of appeal have been filed in separate cases for the same defendant or in separate cases for different defendants, a party may move to consolidate the cases for “purposes of appeal,” or just for oral argument. ORAP 2.10(2)(b); ORAP 2.30. The administrative staff of the records will grant an unopposed motion. When it does so, the two cases are merged into a single file and the lowest appellate case number becomes the controlling case number. Only one “original” transcript is filed. ORAP 3.35(6). A party should file only one brief. ORAP 5.77(1).

The Court of Appeals may, on its own motion, consolidate the cases for “purposes of appeal,” or just for oral argument. ORAP 2.10(2)(a); ORAP 2.30.

A party opposing consolidation may file an objection within 14 days. ORAP 2.10(2); ORAP 2.30.

C. Motion to Amend the Designation of Record

ORS 19.250(1)(d) and (e)require that a notice of appeal contain “[a] designation of those portions of the proceedings and exhibits to be included in the record in addition to the trial court file” and “statement of points.” ORS 138.185(2) (making ORS 19.250 applicable to criminal appeals). One need not include a statement of points if one designates the entire trial court record, including all testimony, exhibits, and instructions.” ORAP 19.250(1)(e). A savvy appellant in a criminal case will designate the entire trial court record and avoid the necessity of including a statement of points.

An appellant may amend the designation of record at any time after the filing of the notice of appeal until 35 days after the transcript is filed. ORS 19.25(1)(d). To do so, one files and serves a notice of amended designation of record in the same manner as one files and serves a notice of appeal. Id. The notice shall “clearly indicate those portions of the proceedings and exhibits being added to or deleted from the original designation of record.” Id.

If 35 days have elapsed since the transcript was filed, then an appellant must move the court to amend the designation of record. ORS 19.250(1)(d). In the motion, one should explain why one needs the additional record and why one did not designate it before.

The opposing party may serve and file its own “designation of additional parts of the proceedings and exhibits to be included in the record” within 14 days of the filing of an appellant’s amended designation of the record. ORS 19.250(2). The designation is filed and served in the same manner as one files and serves a notice of appeal. Id.

D. Motion for Extension of Time (MOET) to File a Transcript

The court reporter or transcriber files a MOET to file a transcript in most cases. ORAP 3.30. That is because, in the usual criminal appeal, the court reporter or transcriber is responsible for preparing transcripts in a number of other cases and simply does not have the time to prepare the transcript in question. In that circumstance, the court reporter or transcriber is in the best position to explain the cause of delay to the court.

The motion is filed in the Court of Appeals, not the trial court. Id. ORAP 3.30sets forth the specifications for the preparation, filing, and service of motions. See also the sample motion at ORAP Appendix 3.30.

The party responsible for the filing of a transcript may also file a request for an extension of time. ORAP 3.30. A party should file a MOET to file the transcript when something other than time pressure on the court reporter or transcriber is the cause of the delay.

E. Motion to Correct or Supplement the Transcript Before the Transcript Settles

Within 15 days after the transcript is filed and served, any party may move the trial courtfor an order to correct any errors appearing in the transcript or, where the interests of justice require, to have additional parts of the proceedings included in the transcript. ORS 19.370(5); ORAP 3.40. Serve a copy of the motion on the Court of Appeals and the transcript coordinator. Id.

Note:a motion for extension of time to filea motion to correct or supplement the transcript is filed in the Court of Appeals, not the trial court. ORS 19.395.

The Court of Appeals will hold the appeal in abeyance pending the trial court’s decision on the motion to correct or supplement the transcript. ORAP 3.40(2). The appeal will remain in abeyance until the following events occur:

•If the trial court grants a motion to correctthe transcript, then the Court of Appeals will hold the appeal in abeyance until, upon a party’s motion, the trial court enters an order settling the corrected transcript. ORAP 3.40(4)(a); ORAP 3.40(5)(b) (the period for filing the appellant’s opening brief begins the day after the entry of the order settling the transcript).

•If the trial court grants a motion to supplementthe transcript, then the Court of Appeals will hold the appeal in abeyance until, assuming no motion to correct the supplemental transcript is filed, the transcript is deemed settled. A transcript is deemed settled 15

days after it is served. ORAP 3.40(4)(b); ORAP 3.40(5)(a) (the period for filing the appellant’s opening brief begins the day after the transcript is deemed settled).

•If the trial court denies the motion, then the appeal is reactivated. ORAP 3.40(4)(c); ORAP 3.40(5)(c) (the period for filing the appellant’s opening brief begins the day after the entry of the order settling the transcript).

If a party needs additional time to prepare and file a corrected or supplemental transcript, then the party may file a MOET with the Court of Appeals. ORS 19.395 (only the appellate courts may grant extensions of time for the performance of any act in connection with the preparation of the record); ORAP 3.30(1) (only the appellate court may grant an extension of time for the preparation of a transcript). One may, apparently, also file such a motion with the trial court. ORAP 3.40(3) (authorizing the trial court to grant an extension of time for making any corrections or additions to the transcript).

ORAP 8.40provides for the appellate review of a trial court’s decision whether to deny or grant a motion to correct or supplement the record.

F. Motion to Correct or Supplement the Transcript After the Transcript is Settled

After the record has settled, if it appears that “the record on appeal is erroneous or that the record does not contain material that should have been part of the trial court file, and the erroneous or incomplete record substantially affects the merits of the appeal,” then a party may move the court to correct or supplement the record “as may be just.” ORS 19.365(4).

In this singular instance, the Court of Appeals is not particular as to the form of the motion. If the transcriptionist prepared all of the portions of the record designated in the notice of appeal or an amended designation of record but additional transcripts are needed, then one could file an amended designation of record pursuant to ORS 19.250(1)(d). If the transcriptionist failed to prepare all of the portions of the record designated in the notice of appeal or an amended designation of record, then one could file a motion to supplement the record pursuant to ORS 19.370(5)and ORAP 3.40. Or, one could simply file a motion to supplement the record pursuant to ORS 19.365(4).

G. Motion for Withdrawal or Substitution or Association of Counsel

1. Rules for All Counsel

When an appeal is pending, counsel wishing to withdraw or substitute new counsel must obtain an order from the Court of Appeals. ORAP 8.10(1). The opposing party must be served. ORS

9.390; ORAP 8.10(1). Until that happens, the adverse party is bound to recognize the former attorney. ORS 9.390.

The motion to substitute counsel must be based on “good and sufficient cause.” ORS

9.380(2). The Court of Appeals will presume “good and sufficient cause” if both attorneys sign the motion for substitution of counsel. ORAP 8.10. The motion is deemed granted if is in proper form and signed by both attorneys. Id.

To associate an attorney from a different firm on a case, one need only file and serve a notice of association. ORAP 8.10(3). The association of out-of-state counsel (pro hac vice) is governed by ORS 9.241and UTCR 3.170. ORAP 6.10(4); 8.10(4).

2. Additional Rules for Court-Appointed Counsel and Legal Advisors

There are additional rules regarding motions to withdraw or substitute counsel filed by court- appointed counsel. ORAP 8.12. A court-appointed attorney has no obligation to withdraw or substitute counsel at the request of the defendant unless the attorney has a good-faith basis for the motion. ORAP 8.12(1)(b).

If there is a good-faith basis for a motion for substitution, the court-appointed attorney must consult with the Office of Public Defense Services (OPDS)“regarding the need for substitution and who should be substituted as new counsel.” ORAP 8.12(2)(a). If OPDS agrees to the need for substitution and to the qualifications of the attorney to be substituted, then the court- appointed attorney may file a motion for substitution pursuant to ORAP 8.10(1) and serve a copy on the state, OPDS, amicus curiae, if any, and the defendant. Id. Unless the court indicates otherwise, the motion is deemed granted seven days after the filing of the motion, if the three predicate conditions set forth in ORAP 8.12(2)(a)(i-iii) are met.

If OPDS does notagree to the need for substitution or to the qualifications of the attorney to be substituted, then court-appointed counsel may still file a motion for substitution of counsel. The motion must comply with ORAP 7.05, other rules generally related to motions (see section II. A.), and ORAP 8.10(1). Counsel must serve a copy on the state, OPDS, amicus curiae, if any, and the defendant. ORAP 8.12(2)(b). Motions filed without the approval of OPDS are notdeemed granted after seven days; the court must rule on them. ORS 138.500(2)(d)requires that the court’s decision be made pursuant to the policies, procedures, standards and guidelines of the Public Defense Services Commission, specifically, Public Defense Payment Policies and Procedure 1.7 (Substitution of Appointed Counsel).

These same rules apply to court-appointed legal advisors appointed under ORS 137.045(1)(d). ORAP 8.12(4).

3. Pro Se Motion for New Counsel

As noted above, court-appointed attorneys have no obligation to withdraw or substitute counsel at the request of the defendant unless the attorney has a good faith basis for the motion. ORAP 8.12(1)(b). If there is no good faith basis for the motion and the defendant insists upon new counsel, then advise the defendant to file a pro semotion for a new counsel in the appellate court and to serve the motion on counsel. ORAP 8.12(3)(a). Counsel must continue to act as the defendant’s legal representative until the appellate court issues an order relieving counsel of that duty.

H. Application to Appear as Amicus Curiae

ORAP 8.15governs the application of a person to appear as amicus curiae.

I. Motion for Extension of Time (MOET)

With one exception, ORAP 7.25sets forth the specifications for the preparation and filing of MOETs for “the performance of any actpertaining to an appeal” (emphasis added).

Exception:ORAP 3.30sets forth separate specifications for the preparation and filing of a MOET to file a transcript. ORAP 7.25(6). See section IV. D.

Be sure to use the proper title for the motion. ORAP 7.10(1)(b), (c) and (d); Appendix 7.10-1. In any MOET other than a first 28-day MOET to file a brief, include a statement as to whether opposing counsel objects to, concurs in, or has no position regarding the MOET. ORAP 7.25(3). See ORAP Appendix 7.10-3for examples of MOETs.

A party may not file or serve a MOET by telephonic facsimile communication device (fax), ORAP

7.35(3), but it may e-file a MOET. See generally ORAP 16.03-16.60(regarding filing by electronic means). ORAP 7.27provides for an oral MOET. This provision may only be used under restricted circumstances and, in practice, is rarely used in criminal cases.

If a party files a MOET past the due date of the filing in question, the party must also file a motion for relief from default and provide a good reason for the default.

Opposing counsel may file an objection to a MOET, but must “articulate specific grounds for the objection” and identify how an extension of time will cause prejudice. ORAP 7.27(4). A respondent to a MOET may file a response by fax. ORAP 7.25(5).

The administrative staff of the records department will allow an unopposed first MOET for 28 days or less. MOETs are usually decided within a few days. ORAP 7.25(6). Any objection filed after that time is treated as a motion for reconsideration. Id.

The Chief Judge or appellate commissioner decides other MOETs. In determining whether to allow a MOET, the court considers the amount of time requested, the total number of days since the notice of appeal was filed, the number and length of previous extensions granted, any time restraints imposed by statute or rule, and the nature of any objections.

Once the court has ordered no further extensions of time, a party may move for an extension of time only under extraordinary and compelling circumstances. ORAP 7.30(2).

J. Motion to Strike

ORAP 1.20provides that a party may move the court to strike—with or without leave to refile—any brief, excerpt of record, motion, or other thing that does not conform to applicable statutes or rules. Use this motion sparingly in relation to a motion or a brief and for good cause. The Court of Appeals does not appreciate petty sniping at your opponent.

K. Motion to File an Extended Brief, Excerpt of Record, and Appendix

ORAP 5.05(2)sets forth the word-count and page limits for appellate briefs, excerpts of record, and appendices. A party may move the court to file an extended brief, except of record, or appendix for a “specific reason.” ORAP 5.05(3). The party filing the motion “shall make every reasonable effort to file the motion not less than seven days before the brief is due.” Id. The court disapproves of the filing of a motion for an extended brief, excerpt, or appendix at the same time as the filing of the brief, excerpt, or appendix that exceeds the page limit.

Because the Court of Appeals is loathe to grant such a motion, the reason for the extended brief, excerpt, or appendix must be compelling. If true, counsel should consider arguing that filing a shorter brief does not permit appellate counsel to effectively represent his or her client in compliance with state and federal constitutional standards. Counsel might also consider attaching the particular argument he or she wishes to assert as part of the showing of need.

If the Court of Appeals denies the motion, and counsel is convinced that the court’s ruling will interfere with counsel’s duty toward his or her client, then counsel should consider petitioning the Oregon Supreme Court to review the denial of the motion. See, e.g, Pratt v. Armenakis, 335 Or 35, 56

P3d 920 (2002)(affirming page limit where petitioner had not made the requisite showing that the limit prohibited him from fully vindicating his constitutional rights in the appellate process).

Note:if counsel wishes to contest the state court’s limitations on brief length in a later habeas corpusproceeding in federal court, one mustpetition the Oregon Supreme Court to review the Court of Appeals ruling in order to exhaust state court remedies. 28 U.S.C. § 2254(b)(1)(A).

L. Motion to File a Reply Brief

An appellant in a criminal case may not file a reply brief except upon motion or the request of the court. ORAP 5.75(3). A motion to file a reply brief must “demonstrate the need for one.” Id. A party must file such a motion within 14 days after the filing of the respondent’s answering brief. ORAP 5.75(4).

M. Motion to File a Memorandum of Additional Authorities

A party may submit a memorandum of additional authorities withoutleave of the court at any time after the filing of the party’s brief but before oral argument. ORAP 5.85(1). Such a memorandum must include two things:

•citation of relevant case law; and

•the identification of the previously briefed issue to which newly cited case applies. ORAP 5.85(2). The memorandum may also contain a brief argument.

The memorandum shall not exceed two pages without leave of the court. ORAP 5.90(2)(b). Further specifications for the filing of the memorandum are set forth at ORAP 5.85(2)(c). An opposing party may respond with its own memorandum.

After oral argument, a party may not file a memorandum of additional authorities without leave of the court. ORAP 5.85(1). An opposing party has 14 days to respond, unless the court indicates otherwise. ORAP 5.85(3).

N. Motion for Relief from Default

If a party misses a filing date for something other than the notice of appeal, then a party may move the court for relief from default. Include a justification for the default in the motion. File such a motion along with a motion for an extension of time in which to file the document in question.

No statute or rule provides for a motion for relief from default; however, as noted above, the Court of Appeals does not require that a motion be authorized by a particular statute or rule before considering it.

O. Motion to Hold a Case in Abeyance

A party may move to hold a case in abeyance in the Court of Appeals pending that court’s disposition of another case in that court or in the Oregon Supreme Court. The Court of Appeals usually will not hold a case in abeyance pending the decision of a case in the Oregon Supreme Court.

A party may move to hold the filing of or the disposition of a petition for reconsideration in abeyance in the Court of Appeals pending that court’s disposition of another case in that court or in the Oregon Supreme Court. The Court of Appeals usually will not hold a case in abeyance pending the decision of a case in the Oregon Supreme Court.

A party may move to hold the filing of or the disposition of a petition for review in abeyance in Oregon Supreme Court pending that court’s disposition of another case.

No statute or rule provides for motions to hold a case in abeyance; however, as noted above, the appellate courts do not require that a motion be authorized by a particular statute or rule before considering it.

P. Motion to Disqualify an Appellate Judge

If an appellate judge “participated in the case in the proceedings being appealed or reviewed,” including the “underlying criminal proceedings,” the party should notify the court of that fact by letter “as soon as possible after discovering the judge’s participation.” ORAP 8.30 (1) and (2).

A party may also move to disqualify a judge for one or more grounds specified in ORS 14.210or because the judge’s participation would violate the Oregon Code of Judicial Conduct. ORAP 8.30 (3)(a). The judge in question will review the motion and may grant it. ORAP 8.30(2)(b). If he or she does not grant it, the Chief Judge—or, if he prefers, the full court—will consider the motion along with any response by the judge in question. Id. A copy of the response of the judge in question is provided to the parties. Id. If the Chief Judge is the judge in question then, the “next senior judge available”—or, if he or she prefers, the full court—rules on the motion. Id.

ORAP 8.30presumably applies to the appellate commissioner as well.

ORAP 8.30 appears to contemplate that another party to the appeal may wish to oppose the motion. In that case, as with any motion, a party has 14 days to respond. ORAP 7.05(3). The court, however, may decide the motion without awaiting a response. ORAP 8.30(2).
