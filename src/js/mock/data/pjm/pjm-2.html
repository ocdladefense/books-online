Initiating the Appeal

Dave Ferry

I. Step One: Determining the Entry of Judgment

Note that this chapter does not address the preliminary steps in the representation of any clients, like performing a conflicts check. Thus, Step One occurs only after you have performed your ethical and professional obligations connected with being retained to represent the appealing client.

Typically, the appellate process begins when the trial process ends: with the conviction and sentencing of the criminal defendant. At that point, trial attorneys have a duty to inform defendants of their right to appeal. Consequently, our typical clients make their decision about appealing at the close of their trial, or immediately upon pronouncement of sentence. However, for the appellate lawyer, the process cannot formally start until the entry of an appealable judgment.

A judgment is entered when it is posted in the trial court register, which is reflected in the OregonJudicial Information Network (OJIN) databaseby an entry date. Judgments are not often entered the same day as the sentence is announced. Accordingly, the first order of business upon being informed of a client’s desire to appeal is to determine the date of entry of the judgment. That date is critical, both to determine if you canfile your notice of appeal (NOAP), and for calculating the due date for that initiating document. If the judgment has not been entered, you cannot proceed with the appeal. In that event, you will need to carefully monitor the situation.

Often, the judgment is not yet entered at the time of your new client’s referral simply because they called too soon. Sometimes the call will come before the sentencing has been completed. In that event, only patience is required. Less frequently, however, the prospective judgment has slipped through a crack at the trial court. If the entry doesn’t appear after a reasonable period of time, polite nudging with calls to the trial court clerk may be warranted.

Thus, Step One in initiating the criminal appeal is to determine the date of entry of the judgment (or to get the judgment entered).

II. Step Two: Obtaining or Identifying an Appealable Judgment

Because appeals are creatures of statute, jurisdiction and reviewability are subject to statutory constraints. The types of orders from which one can appeal are provided in ORS 138.040, .050, .053 and .057.

Generally a judgment or order imposed after judgment is appealable if the court imposes a sentence on conviction, suspends imposition or execution of any part of a sentence, extends a period of probation, imposes or modifies a condition of probation or of sentence suspension, or imposes or executes a sentence upon revocation of probation or sentence suspension. See ORS 138.053(1).

The primary qualification for appealability of an order purporting to dispose of a criminal case is that it be a final judgment, i.e., one that disposes of all of the counts in the indictment or information. Note, however, that severing the counts in an indictment can create multiple cases, regardless of whether multiple case numbers are used, such that a judgment disposing only of the severed counts may be final. See State v. Smith, 100 Or App 284, 785 P2d 1081 (1990). If a judgment fails to dispose of all the counts of the charging instrument, one must go back to the trial court to obtain a judgment that indicates the disposition of all counts (the simplest method for doing so may be filing a motion to correct the judgment under ORS 138.083).

Thus, Step Two in initiating the criminal appeal is obtaining the judgment and verifying that it is

final.

The general step rules set out here are guidelines for good practice. They are not rigid. For example,it may occasionally be necessary to skip Step Two and file a NOAP to meet the time restrictions before obtaining a judgment when there is some difficulty getting one’s hands on that document. Of course, the judgment is required to be filed, and you will need to take corrective action to add it to the Court ofAppealsfile at a later date in order to avoid dismissal.

III. Step Three: Filing the Notice of Appeal (NOAP)

Once you have an appealable judgment, it’s time to prepare the Notice of Appeal or NOAP. Filing a NOAP is required for jurisdiction, and it must comply with statutory requirements. ORS 19.250(contents); ORS 19.255 (timing). ORS 138.071(1)provides that the NOAP must be filed within 30 days from entry of the judgment.

A Notice of Appeal from a guilty except for insanity verdict (GEI) is statutorily excepted from the 30-day rule. ORS 161.295(1)–161.327establishes a 90-day limit for such cases.

A. The Late NOAP

ORS 138.071(5) provides that a defendant may file a motion for leave to file a late NOAP, and the late NOAP itself, within 90 days of the entry of judgment. In addition to complying with the content rules of a standard NOAP, a late NOAP must also contain: (1) a statement making a “clear and convincing” showing that the failure to timely file was not attributable to the defendant personally (generally, that it was due to attorney office processing time, etc.), and (2) a colorable claim of error in the proceeding from which the appeal is taken. A colorable claim is, in essence, a predictionof a possible claim of error. Defendants are not, however, bound by inclusion or omission of issues as colorable claims when writing a brief—i.e., it is permissible to identify a particular issue in the colorable claim section of your NOAP, but notassign error to that issue when you write the appellate brief.

Late notice may be necessitated by the needs of special cases, discussed below, that have additional NOAP content requirements that may delay the process.

B. The General Contents of the NOAP

ORAP 2.05sets out the contents of the NOAP. The basic requirements for the content of the NOAP are listed in that rule and are not set out in full here.

The NOAP generally must attach the judgment from which one is appealing. ORAP 2.05(11). However, if there is a delay in obtaining the judgment, filing the NOAP without it will meet the filing deadline. The court will then issue a default notice providing an additional 14 days for correction (for filing the judgment). Extensions may be permitted.

The NOAP must also include a designation of record. ORAP 2.05(6). That is an identification of the proceedings from which transcripts need to be prepared (pretrial hearings, trial and sentencing dates). The designation of record may be expanded later by amending the designation of record if it is discovered that there were more material proceedings than were originally designated. ORAP 3.05(3).

The NOAP must also identify the statute or statutes that establish jurisdiction over the case (ORS

138.040, .050, .053, .057). Jurisdiction to appeal from a jury trial or bench trial that has resulted in conviction is conferred by ORS 138.040. 3

C. Special Content Requirements

The statutes set out additional requirements for NOAPs involving particular types of cases. In guilty pleacases, appeal is limited to the legality of the sentence imposed. Consequently, the NOAP from a guilty plea case must include a colorable showing either that the sentence “(a) Exceeds the maximum allowable by law; or (b) Is unconstitutionally cruel and unusual.” ORS 138.050(1). ORS 138.053(3) also requires inclusion of a colorable claim in NOAPs seeking to appeal in probation violationcases. Such colorable claims can assert that the finding of the violation in a contestedhearing was error or that the sentence imposed upon violation was error.

As noted above, a colorable claim and a reason for lateness are also required for any late NOAP, regardless of the underlying case type.

Thus, Step Three in initiating the criminal appeal is filing a NOAP.

IV. Step Four: Look Out for New Judgments and Amend the NOAP

Step Three is not necessarily the end of the initiating stage. On occasion, a trial court will issue an amended judgment. This often occurs when a judgment has been corrected pursuant to ORS 138.083, and sometimes as a result of a restitution hearing held after the initial judgment has been entered. When a trial court amends the judgment, an amended NOAP is required in order to safeguard the defendant’s right to appeal from the new, modified judgment. Filing an amended NOAP is done exactly like a standard NOAP except that you must put “amended” in the title, and you must cite to and provide a copy (preferably attached to the amended NOAP) of the amended judgment to the appellate court.

Failure to serve amended judgments on appellate counsel is unfortunately common. Consequently, it is good practice to regularly monitor OJINto learn of surprise amended judgments in a timely fashion. This is especially true after State v. Fowler, 350 Or 133, 252 P3d 302 (2011). In Fowler, the OregonSupreme Courtstrictly construed the 30-day rule to preclude appeal from a money term added to the original punishment through a supplemental judgment. The court noted that ORS 138.071(4)permits appeal from a supplemental judgment entered pursuant to ORS 138.083 (the motion to correct the judgment statute) within 30 days of noticeof the entry of that supplemental judgment. However, it

found that the hearing adding monetary terms to the judgment was notconducted under ORS 138.083. Moreover, Fowleralso suggested that notice to the defendant and his trial counsel that a supplemental judgment wouldbe entered—which was provided beforethe supplemental judgment was entered—would have triggered the clock under ORS 138.071(4). Thus, the amended NOAP was required within 30 days of entry in that case. Consequently, it is now critical to monitor appellate cases for ORS 138.083 hearings, restitution hearings, and other post-conviction hearings so that amended and supplemental judgments can be noticed and properly appealed from.

Thus, Step Four in initiating the criminal appeal is filing an Amended NOAP if and when necessary.
