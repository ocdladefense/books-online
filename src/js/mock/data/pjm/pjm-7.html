Oregon Supreme Court Review

Kristin Carveth

I. The Role of the Supreme Court

The Oregon Supreme Courtserves a different function than the Court of Appeals. The Court of Appeals is an “error-correcting court.” In other words, the Court of Appeals generally applies well-settled legal principles to a particular set of facts to determine whether the trial court made the correct ruling.

The Supreme Court, in contrast, is a “law-announcing court.” Its primary role is to announce the law that lower courts are to use when a particular issue comes before them. In Farmer v. Baldwin, 346Or 67, 91, 205 P3d 871 (2009),the court noted that its function “is to resolve significant issues of law, preferably issues of state law that will arise often, affect many people, and fit the other criteria listed under ORAP 9.07.”

II. Reviewable Decisions and Timelines

A. Decisions that Are Reviewable by the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court may review any decision of the Court of Appeals which includes an opinion, affirmance without opinion, order ruling on a motion, petition for attorney fees, or statement of costs and disbursements. ORAP 9.05(1).

Generally, you do not need to file a petition for reconsideration in the Court of Appeals as a prerequisite to filing a petition for review. ORAP 6.25(3).There is one exception to that rule, however: if you want to want to seek Supreme Court review of a ruling on a motion by the Appellate Commissioner, you must first petition for reconsideration in the Court of Appeals. ORAP 7.55(4)(b).

B. Timeline for Filing a Petition for Review

A petition for review must be filed within 35 days of the Court of Appeals decision. You may ask for an extension of time, but you must include the specific circumstances that caused you to need the extension. ORAP 9.05(2)(a).

If you file a petition for reconsideration in the Court of Appeals, the 35-day deadline for filing the petition for review in the Supreme Court does not begin to run until the Court of Appeals rules on the petition for reconsideration. If you have obtained an extension of time in which to file the petition for reconsideration in the Court of Appeals but do not file, the 35-day deadline for filing the petition for review in the Supreme Court begins to run on the expiration of the extension of time. ORAP 9.05(2)(b)(i).

If you miss the deadline to file a petition for review and the appellate judgment has issued, you may be able to reinstate the appeal for the purpose of seeking review, so long as it is done “within a reasonable time.” ORAP 9.05(2)(c).You should file a motion to recall the appellate judgment, together with a motion for relief from default and a motion for extension of time in which to file a petition for review. See, generally, ORAP Chapter 7 (Motions).

III. The Petition for Review – ORAP 9.05

Just as the Supreme Court serves a different function than the Court of Appeals, so does the petition for review serve a different function than the Court of Appeals brief. Aside from a few exceptions, the Supreme Court’s criminal docket consists of those few cases the court chooses to accept as an exercise of discretionary review. The Supreme Court does not accept review of a case simply because the Court of Appeals applied the law to a set of facts incorrectly. Rather, the Supreme Court will exercise its discretion to review a case when it affects a large number of people and provides an appropriate vehicle to announce the law. For that reason, an effective petition for review should be an advertisement that attracts the Supreme Court to a significant legal issue. It should not be a restatement of the Court of Appeals brief (and, in fact, the Supreme Court will already have a copy of the Court of Appeals brief ).

A. Contents of the Petition for Review

The petition for review should generally be in the form of a brief prepared in conformity with ORAP 5.05, but must not exceed 5,000 words. ORAP 9.05(3)(a).It should contain the following sections. They are listed in the order in which they should appear in the petition.

1. Historical and Procedural Facts

A short statement of the relevant historical and procedural facts, “but facts correctly stated in the decision of the Court of Appeals should not be restated.” ORAP 9.05(4)(a).

2. Question(s) Presented and Proposed Rule(s) of Law

The question presented should concisely identify the legal issue, and the proposed rule of law should identify the legal principle that resolves the question. They should not be overly specific; rather, to the extent possible, you should strive to provide questions presented and proposed rules of law that are general enough to have wide-ranging applicability.

3. Reasons for Allowing Review

This is perhaps the most important section of a petition for review. The Supreme Court considers the following list relevant in determining whether to grant discretionary review, and an effective petition for review will reference any and all that are applicable:

• Whether the case presents a significant issue of law, such as the interpretation of a constitutional provision or statute. ORAP 9.07(1).

• Whether the issue or a similar issue arises often. ORAP 9.07(2).

• Whether many people are affected by the decision. ORAP 9.07(3).

• Whether the issue is one of state law. ORAP 9.07(4).

• Whether the issue has ever been addressed by the Supreme Court. ORAP 9.07(5).

• Whether the same or a related issue is currently pending before the Supreme Court. ORAP

9.07(6).

• Whether the legal issue is properly preserved and free from factual disputes or procedural obstacles that may prevent the court from reaching the issue. ORAP 9.07(7).

• Whether the record presents the desired issue. ORAP 9.07(8).

• Whether the present case law is inconsistent. ORAP 9.07(9).

• Whether it appears that trial courts or administrative agencies are inconsistent or confused in ruling on the issue. ORAP 9.07(10).

• Whether the Court of Appeals published a written decision. ORAP 9.07(11).

• Whether the Court of Appeals was divided. ORAP 9.07(12).

• Whether the Court of Appeals decided the case en banc. ORAP 9.07(13).

• Whether the Court of Appeals decision appears to be wrong. ORAP 9.07(14).

• Whether the issues are well presented in the briefs. ORAP 9.07(15).

• Whether an amicus curiaehas appeared or is available. ORAP 9.07(16).

4. Argument

You may include an argument section in support of your petition for review, but only if space permits and you want to include one – it is not required. ORAP 9.05(4)(d).

5. Decision of the Court of Appeals

Attach a copy of the decision of the Court of Appeals, including any concurring or dissenting opinions. ORAP 9.05(4)(e).

B. Response to a State’s Petition for Review

If the state files a petition for review, you may file a response to the petition within 14 days. ORAP 9.10(2).

The conventional wisdom is to refrain from filing a response to a state’s petition. However, if the state’s petition has considerable facial merit and you can readily identify a fatal procedural defect or fundamental flaw that renders the case less attractive to the Supreme Court, a response to the petition is warranted.

Further, if the Court of Appeals resolved any issues against you, you may file a response to the petition in which you include a contingent request to review those issues should the Supreme Court allow review. ORAP 9.10(1).

IV. Brief on the Merits

The Supreme Court will allow review if one less than a majority of the judges eligible to vote on the petition votes to allow it. ORAP 9.20(1).The Supreme Court may limit the questions on review. If the court does not specifically limit the questions, all questions properly before the Court of Appeals included in the petition or response are before the Supreme Court. ORAP 9.20(2).

Supreme Court cases typically have far-reaching precedential importance and therefore require the investment of significant resources. If you have a case in the Supreme Court, enjoy the excitement but understand that the preparation of the brief on the merits and the preparation for oral argument will dominate a major part of your life. Do not underestimate the importance of the case to Oregon law and the professional challenges it poses.

The scope of this section does not include the mechanics of writing a brief on the merits, but the following observations may be helpful.

First, identify the source of law underlying the issue. Does the issue arise from and involve an ordinance, administrative rule, statute, common law, state constitutional provision, or federal constitutional provision?

Second, an irony of Supreme Court practice is that the cases tend to turn on elemental questions of law. Always determine how the foundational legal model works before attempting to address the specific issue on review.

Lastly, before you begin writing your brief, look at copies of briefs on the merits filed in past Supreme Court cases. They are available both at the Supreme Court Law Library and on the court’swebsite.

The brief on the merits should generally conform to the requirements of ORAP 5.05. The length of a brief on the merits may not exceed 14,000 words. ORAP 5.05(2)(b)(i).It should contain the following sections.

1. Question(s) Presented and Proposed Rule(s) of Law

Begin with a concise statement of the question presented and proposed rule of law. It should not be argumentative or repetitive, and the phrasing need not be identical to that used in the petition for review. However, the brief may not raise additional questions or change the substance of the questions presented in the petition for review. ORAP 9.17(2)(b)(i).

2. Historical and Procedural Facts

Include the nature of the proceeding, the relief sought, and the nature of the judgment rendered by the trial court and Court of Appeals. ORAP 9.17(2)(b)(ii)(A).

Additionally, include all relevant historical facts, in narrative form, with citations to the appropriate place in the record. ORAP 9.17(2)(b)(ii)(B).

3. Summary of the Argument

A concise summary of your argument similar to the requirement for Court of Appeals briefs. See Chapter 5, “Briefing,” section I F, “Summary of Argument.”

4. Argument

The argument section should support your proposed rules of law. It should be modeled on the argument section in a Court of Appeals brief, but will likely be lengthier and more detailed. SeeChapter 5, “Briefing,” section II C, “The Argument.”

V. Oral Argument in the Supreme Court

Oral argument in the Supreme Court is not vastly different than oral argument in the Court of Appeals, although each side has 30 minutes as opposed to 15 minutes. See Chapter 6, “Oral Advocacy.”

Due to the nature of a Supreme Court case and the length of argument, the court typically asks detailed questions, and preparation in the form of moot courts is always advisable. You may also consider watching the oral argument in other cases, either in person or on your computer—the court now streams oral arguments live and on demand from its website.

VI. Federal Habeas Corpus: Exhaustion of State Remedies

Any federal issue that you seek to preserve for federal habeas corpus review must be raised in the petition for review before the Oregon Supreme Court. O’Sullivan v. Boerckel, 526 US 838 (1999). For a more detailed discussion of the exhaustion of state remedies doctrine, see Section II, “State Habeas Corpus,” in this manual.
