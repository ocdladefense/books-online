Appraising Appellate Issues

Laura Frikert

In every criminal appeal, there is a presumption that the judgment in the case is correct. Therefore, the appellant has the burden to convince the appellate court that the trial contained reversible errors.

An appellate attorney appraises appellate issues using the following criteria:

1) Was the issue preserved?

2) What is the standard of review?

3) Is the substantive issue meritorious?

4) Was the ruling harmful?

The above considerations bear directly on the strength of a particular issue and must be considered when deciding whether to urge an issue on appeal.

I. Preservation

As a general rule, an appellate court will only review an error if the error was preserved by the timely identification of the legal issue in the trial court.

Appellate courts may review an unpreserved error under the “plain error” doctrine.

ORAP 5.45(1)provides that “No matter claimed as error will be considered on appeal unless the claim of error was preserved in the lower court . . ., provided that the appellate court may consider an error of law apparent on the face of the record.”

A. The Purposes of Preservation

The purpose of the preservation requirement is to ensure that the trial judge gets the first opportunity to make a given ruling, and to get it right if possible.

The preservation requirement is also meant to promote fairness to the parties by ensuring that parties are not taken by surprise, misled, or denied opportunities to meet an argument.

B. Preservation Tests

What is required to preserve an issue can vary depending on the issue. Very generally, the issue of preservation often turns on whether “the policies underlying the rule have been sufficiently served.” State v. Parkins, 346 Or 333, 340-41, 211 P3d 262 (2009).

Most preservation issues are resolved under the analytic framework set out in State v. Wyatt, 331Or 335, 343, 15 P3d 22 (2000)or the less favored framework set out in State v. Hitz, 307 Or 183,

188, 766 P2d 373 (1988).

An appellate practitioner faced with a possible preservation problem should make an argument under Wyattand rely on Hitzonly to the extent necessary.

1. The WyattTest

Did the party alleging error “provide the trial court with an explanation of his or her objection that is specific enough to ensure that the court can identify its alleged error with enough clarity to permit it to consider and correct the error immediately, if correction is warranted”? 331 Or at

343.

The Wyatttest is a more rigorous preservation test than the Hitztest. Under Wyatt, the Court of Appeals generally requires that the particular argumentbe preserved rather than just the more general issue. For example, in State v. Jones, 246 Or App 412, 266 P 3d 151 (2011),the prosecutor offered misconduct evidence under State v. Johns, 301 Or 535, 725 P2d 312 (1986),

as evidence of the defendant’s intent. In the trial court, the defendant argued that the misconduct evidence did not satisfy the Johnstest. On appeal, defendant advanced a different argument—that the Johnstest was inapplicable because intent was not at issue in the case. The Court of Appeals declined to address that argument, concluding that it was unpreserved under Wyatt.

2. The HitzTest

Did the party alleging error raise an issue at trial? “There is a distinction between raising an issueat trial, identifying a source for a claimed position, and making a particular argument.. . . The first ordinarily is essential, the second less so, the third least.” 307 Or at 188 (emphasis in original).

Practice Tip

Arguments that are advanced in writing are sufficient to satisfy the preservation requirements, even when the trial court does not expressly rule on the argument. SeeState v. Walker, 350 Or 540, 550, 258P3d 1228 (2011)(“This court has never required that each and every argument that has been asserted in writing must be repeated orally in court in order for the argument to be preserved.”); State v. Nelson, 246Or App 91, 265 P 3d 8 (2011) (accord).

Practice Tip

If there is any question about whether a particular argument is preserved under Wyatt,an appellate practitioner should also make an argument that the error warrants plain error review.

C. Unpreserved Error

Unpreserved claims of error are disfavored. An appellate court has discretion as to whether to correct a plain error or not. The Oregon Supreme Court has stated that a “court’s decision to recognize unpreserved or unraised error in this manner should be made with utmost caution. Such an action is contrary to the strong policies requiring preservation and raising of error.” Ailes v. Portland Meadows,

312 Or 376, 382, 823 P2d 956 (1991).

Practice Tip

An appellate court will not reach an unpreserved claim of error unless the appellant satisfies the two- part test set forth in Ailes.

1. Step One of the AilesTest: The first step incorporates the following three requirements:

• The claimed error is an error of law.

• The claimed error is obvious and not reasonably in dispute.

• The claimed error appears on the face of the record.

If all the requirements of the first step are satisfied, then the court proceeds to the second step.

2. Step Two of the AilesTest: The second step requires the court to decide whether to exercise its discretion to consider the error.

Even if an appellate court concludes that an error is plain, it must exercise its discretion, explaining its reasons for doing so, to consider the claim of error. The court may consider the following factors in determining whether to exercise its discretion to consider the error:

• The competing interests of the parties.

• The nature of the case.

• The gravity of the error.

• The ends of justice in the particular case.

• How the error came to the court’s attention.

• Whether the policies behind the general rule requiring preservation of error have been served in the case in another way, i.e., whether the trial court was, in some manner, presented with both sides of the issue and given an opportunity to correct any error.

Generally, appellate counsel should raise unpreserved errors that are crucial to the integrity of the process, such as:

• A jury instruction containing a material error or a material omission. See State v. Brown,

310 Or 347, 355–56, 800 P2d 259 (1990)(trial judge’s failure to instruct the jury on cau- sation reviewed and reversed as plain error).

• A trial court’s imposition of a particular sentence that exceeds its statutory authority. State

v. Mitchell, 236 Or App 248, 235 P3d 725 (2010)(trial court committed plain error in im- posing indeterminate post-prison supervision sentence); State v. Donner, 230 Or App 465,

215 P3d 928 (2009)(the trial court plainly erred in imposing a sentence that exceeded the statutory maximum).

• Issues that have become viable claims as a result of a significant change in case law. State v.Jury, 185 Or App 132, 136, 57 P3d 970 (2002), rev den, 335 Or 504 (2003)(error appar- ent is determined according to “the law existing at the time the appeal is decided”). See,

e.g. State v. Inloes, 239 Or App 49, 243 P 3d 862 (2010)(reviewing plain error sufficiency claim where the controlling legal standard was materially altered between trial and appeal as a result of an Oregon Supreme Court decision); State v. Bahmatov, 244 Or App 50, 260 P

3d 592 (2011)(reviewing admission of a medical diagnosis of sexual abuse as plain error in light of the decision in State v. Southard, 347 Or 127, 218 P3d 104 (2009)).

• Errors that infect the integrity of the process or undermine core constitutional rights. See,e.g. State v. Benson, 246 Or App 262, 265 P 3d 58 (2011)(concluding that the trial court plainly erred when it caused the defendant to invoke his right to remain silent in the pres- ence of the jury).

In addition, where a party is denied a reasonable opportunity to make an objection, the rules of preservation are not a bar to appeal. “A party cannot be required to raise an objection contemporaneously with a trial court’s ruling or other action when the party was not on notice of the trial court’s intended action and had no opportunity to be present when the trial court acted.” State v. DeCamp, 158 Or App 238, 240, 973 P 2d 922 (1999).

A defendant is not required affirmatively to assert the right to be present at sentencing to preserve it; rather, the state must demonstrate that defendant waived his right to be present. Statev. Jacobs, 200 Or App 665, 117 P3d 290 (2005).

The claim, that a trial court conducted a crucial stage of a criminal case while the defendant was without counsel and without first obtaining an informed or intelligent waiver of the right to counsel, is preserved, even without an objection by the unrepresented party that his or her waiver of counsel was not an informed waiver. “A defendant whose waiver of counsel is accepted without first being apprised of the risks of self-representation cannot be expected to object to acceptance of that waiver on the ground that he or she was not apprised of those risks.” State v. Cole, 323 Or 30,

912 P2d 907 (1996).

II. Standard of Review

ORAP 5.45(5)requires an appellant to identify the standard of review for each assignment of error. A standard of review is the level of deference given by the appellate court to the decision or issue under review.

The standard of review differs depending on whether the issue involves:

•A decision on a question of fact.

•A decision on an issue of law.

•A decision on a discretionary ruling.

Different standards of review apply to each of those three different kinds of decisions.

Practice Tip

Effective appellate advocacy requires more than just identifying the applicable standard of review. The standard of review should be used as a framework for presenting legal arguments.

For example, it is persuasive to be able to inform the appellate court that it should decide an issue without any regard to the trial court’s legal conclusions. SeeSection B, Issues of Law. Conversely, when

an issue is reviewed for abuse of discretion, there are very limited and specific circumstances that support reversal of a trial court’s ruling. SeeSection C, Discretionary Rulings.

A. Questions of Fact

An appellate court reviews questions of fact to determine whether there is any evidence in the record to support them. This is a significantly deferential standard. An appellate court does not reweigh the evidence or speculate whether the evidence might have supported other factual findings than those made by the trial court. It simply determines whether any evidence does support the findings of the trial court. See State v. Cunningham, 337 Or 528, 545, 99 P3d 271 (2004)(applying that standard of review and concluding the trial court’s factual finding was supported by evidence in the record).

Where the trial court made express findings on contested facts, the trial court’s findings are accepted as binding on appeal as long as they are supported by evidence in the record. State v.Edwards-Peecher, 218 Or App 311, 313, 179 P3d 746 (2008).

Where the trial court did not make express findings on contested facts, the appellate court will view the facts in a manner consistent with the trial court’s ultimate conclusion. Ball v. Gladden,

250 Or 485, 487, 443 P2d 621 (1968). Ballalso allows an appellate court to infer a finding of fact that the trial court did not expressly make when the court can deduce that the trial court’s chain of reasoning must necessarily have included that fact as one of its links. State v. Lunacolorado, 238 OrApp 91, 243 P3d 125 (2010).

In practical terms, the only way to prevail under this standard of review is to show that no evidence presented could directly or impliedly support the trial court’s factual finding.

B. Issues of Law

An appellate court reviews issues of law without deference to the trial court. The appellate court asks does the law dictate the result the trial judge reached? Under this standard, an appellate court may substitute its own judgment for that of the trial court. This is the most favorable standard of review in criminal cases. See State v. Cunningham, 337 Or 528, 545, 99 P3d 271 (2004)(a trial court’s legal conclusion is reviewed without deference to determine whether the trial court has made an error of law).

C. Discretionary Rulings

The exercise of judicial discretion is difficult to attack on appeal, because the decision, by law, was left to the judge in the first place. However, an abuse of discretion may be found under the following circumstances:

•When a trial court’s ruling is not within the permissible range of decisions.

•When the trial court used the incorrect legal criteria to make the decision.

•When the trial court had insufficient information to make the decision.

State v. Rogers, 330 Or 282, 312, 4 P3d 1261 (2000).

Examples of rulings reviewed for abuse of discretion include the trial court’s denial of a motion for continuance and a trial court’s denial of a motion for mistrial. State v. Barone, 328 Or 68, 83,

969 P2d 1013 (1998), cert den, 528 US 1135 (2000)(denial of motion for mistrial); State v. Hickey,

79 Or App 200, 204, 717 P2d 1287 (1986)(abuse of discretion to deny the defendant’s motion for a continuance when his counsel’s briefcase was stolen the day before trial); State v. Worth, 231 OrApp 69, 218 P3d 166, rev den, 347 Or 718, 226 P3d 709 (2010)(denial of defendant’s motion for

mistrial was an abuse of discretion because the decision “did not fall within the range of legally correct choices and did not produce a permissible, legally correct outcome”); State v. Romero, 236 Or App

640, 237 P 3d 894 (2010)(trial court’s decision to grant the state’s motion to postpone was based on an erroneous legal premise and did not fall within the permissible range of choices).

III. Merits

To identify possible issues on appeal, the attorney should review the “record” for trial court “error.” The “record” is composed of transcripts of what happened in court, exhibits, and trial court file. The only matters that may be appealed are those contained within the record. The appellate court will not consider any matter on appeal that is not within the record.

Trial court “error” generally means that at some point during the pretrial, trial or sentencing proceedings, a judge made a ruling that is contrary to the law. Determining whether a trial court committed some error begins by conducting a full analysis of the record on review and the relevant law.

A. Common Examples of Appellate Issues

1) The magistrate issued a search warrant without probable cause.

2) The trial court denied defendant’s pretrial motion to suppress evidence.

3) The trial court denied defendant’s pretrial motion to dismiss for lack of a speedy trial.

4) The trial court denied defendant’s demurrer.

5) Over defendant’s objection (typically a pretrial motion in limine), the court allowed the state to introduce evidence during trial that the state or federal constitutions or the Oregon Rules of Evidence prohibit.

6) Over defendant’s objection, the court excluded evidence during trial that the state or federal constitutions or the Oregon Rules of Evidence allow.

7) The state introduced insufficient evidence of guilt to proceed to a verdict, and thus, the trial court erred by not granting defendant’s motion for judgment of acquittal. In a trial before the court, a defendant’s closing argument challenging the sufficiency of the evidence is treated as a motion for judgment of acquittal. See, e.g., State v. Forrester, 203 Or App 151, 155, 125 P3d 47 (2005),rev den, 341 Or 141 (2006)(closing argument during bench trial that “[t]his is not enough” was sufficient to preserve argument challenging the legal sufficiency of the state’s evidence).

8) The trial court erroneously instructed the jury on the applicable law, or the trial court failed to instruct the jury on a requested instruction.

9) The actual sentence violates the law by exceeding the statutory maximum, or defendant received a sentencing enhancement, or the evidence did not support a sentence enhancement factor.

Practice Tip

The issues that may arise on direct appeal are expansive. The only clear limitation is that claims of ineffective assistance of counsel may not be raised on direct appeal. Bryant v. Thompson, 324 Or 141,

149, 922 P 2d 1219 (1996).Direct appeal issues may arise under both state and federal law.

In addition, the line between frivolous and potentially meritorious issues is not always easy to perceive.

Crawford v. Washington, 541 U S 36, 124 S Ct 1354, 158 L Ed 2d 177 (2004)upset years of settled precedent regarding the meaning of the federal confrontation clause.

Blakely v. Washington, 542 U S 296, 124 S Ct 2531, 159 L Ed 2d 403 (2004)effectively rendered numerous federal and state sentencing laws unconstitutional.

State v. Southard, 347 Or 127, 218 P3d 104 (2009)upset established precedent and resulted in the later reversal of numerous cases where a diagnosis of sexual abuse was admitted in the absence of physical evidence of abuse.

Practice Tip

The law is dynamic. Appellate advocates must be creative with the law. Established precedent may not determine whether an issue is frivolous. The inquiry for the savvy appellate lawyer is whether a legal principle is arguable.

B. Issues Requested by the Client

An attorney is not required to raise an issue requested by the client. The United States Supreme Court has held that an attorney is to exercise professional judgment in determining which issues to present on appeal. Jones v. Barnes, 463 US 745, 751 (1983)(an indigent defendant does not have “a constitutional right to compel appointed counsel to press nonfrivolous points requested by the client, if counsel, as a matter of professional judgment, decides not to present those points”).

The attorney should explain to the client why counsel is electing not to raise the issue and the wisdom of focusing on strong issues.

If a client is insistent on having the issue presented in the brief, the attorney should consider strength of the “good” issues in the case. If the issues are potentially winnable, the attorney should not do anything to jeopardize the chances of success. However, if the “best” issue is non-frivolous, but unlikely to be successful, the attorney should be flexible and consider including the client’s preferred issues.

If the client’s preferred issue is frivolous and should not be raised, the attorney should provide a written explanation and take notes for the file explaining why the issue requested by the client is not contained in the brief.

IV. Harmless Error

Even if a defendant can establish that the trial court committed legal error, the appellate courts will not reverse a conviction unless the error was prejudicial. Article VII (Amended), section 3, provides, in part:

If the supreme court shall be of opinion, after consideration of all the matters thus submitted, that the judgment of the court appealed from was such as should have been rendered in the case, such judgment shall be affirmed, notwithstanding any error com- mitted during the trial[.]

Oregon’s constitutional test for affirmance despite error is whether there is little likelihood that the particular error affected the verdict. The correct focus of the inquiry is on the possible influence of the error on the verdict rendered, not whether the appellate court, sitting as a fact-finder, would regard the evidence of guilt as substantial and compelling. State v. Davis, 336 Or 19, 32, 77 P3d 1111 (2003).

A federal constitutional error is harmless error when the reviewing court, in examining the record as a whole, can say, beyond a reasonable doubt, that the error did not contribute to the determination of guilt. Delaware v. Van Arsdall, 475 US 673, 681 (1986). In making that assessment, and taking into account all properly admitted evidence, the court considers the “importance” of the improperly excluded evidence to defendant’s case, the “presence or absence of evidence corroborating or contradicting” the evidence on “material points,” and “the overall strength of the prosecution’s case.” Id.at 684.

When analyzing harm and advocating that the error was prejudicial, the appellate practitioner may consider the following arguments:

1) Whether credibility was an issue in the case. State v. Ferguson, 247 Or App 747, 271 P3d 150 (2012 ) (error in admitting impermissible vouching testimony of victim’s father in rape trial was not harmless error; victim’s credibility was central issue in case);

2) In cases in which evidence was improperly admitted, the extent the prosecutor relied on the evidence, i.e., did he or she comment on it during opening or closing arguments. State v. Moore, 243 Or App 433, 258 P 3d 1279 (2011)(admission of prior bad act evidence was not harmless when the prosecutor invited the jury during closing arguments to use the misconduct evidence to draw the prohibited propensity inference).

3) The nature of the evidence and its persuasive effect. State v. Southard, 347 Or 127, 218 P3d 104 (2009)(discussing prejudicial impact of expert testimony).

4) Whether the evidence is cumulative to other evidence. State v. Prange, 247 Or App 254, 268P 3d 749 (2011)(the exclusion of bias evidence is harmless only if the jury otherwise had an adequate opportunity to assess the witness’s credibility or if the witness’s credibility was not at issue in the case).

5) Whether the evidence went to a contested issue in the case. State v. Moller, 217 Or App 49, 174 P 3d 1063 (2007)(admission of evidence concerning a central factual issue that was relied on heavily by the prosecution during closing arguments was not harmless).

6) Additional arguments to consider include the egregiousness of the error and whether the jury was unanimous.

V. Conclusion

Evaluating a case on appeal is a multifaceted inquiry. The substantive merit of an issue is of principle importance to consider. Is the argument well grounded in settled law, or will it require that current precedent be extended or overruled? Does the case present an issue of first impression? However, issues of preservation and harmlessness can not be overlooked. Is the issue preserved? Was the error likely to have affected the verdict?

Those considerations loosely reflect the respondent’s three main lines of defense on appeal: (1) the appellant is wrong on the merits of the argument, (2) the issue is unpreserved, and (3) the error is harmless and does not warrant reversal of the judgment.

Deciding which issues to raise on appeal requires the exercise of thoughtful, creative, and considered legal judgment. The practice of appellate law is an art not a science.
