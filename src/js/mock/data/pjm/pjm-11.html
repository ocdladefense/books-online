Setting the Stage for a Successful Appeal

Stephanie Hortsch

This section discusses basic preservation strategies that can be used to set the stage for a successful appeal. Given the vast variety of issues that may arise at trial, it is impossible to cover every possible topic. However, the chapter provides basic preservation information and should serve as a helpful resource.

I. Preservation

ORAP 5.45(1), the basic rule on appellate preservation, provides:

No matter claimed as error will be considered on appeal unless the claimed error was preserved in the lower court and is assigned as error in the opening brief in accordance with this rule, provided that the appellate court may consider an error of law apparent on the face of the record.

It is very rare that an appellate court will consider an issue as “plain error.” Accordingly, issues must be “preserved” so that, in the face of an adverse ruling, the appellate courts can review and address the issue.

In State v. Hitz, 307 Or 183, 188, 766 P2d 373 (1988), the court instructed that there are “distinctions between raising an issueat trial, identifying a sourcefor a claimed position, and making a particular argument.” (Emphasis in original). That test should not be administered mechanically but, rather, pragmatically. State v. Rumler, 199 Or App 32, 40, 110 P3d 115 (2005); State v. Taylor, 198 OrApp 460, 469, 108 P3d 682, rev den, 339 Or 66 (2005). In considering whether an issue presented at trial is the same as the one advanced on appeal, “an appellate court must view the facts in light of the purposes of fairness and efficiency that underlie the requirement.” State v. Stevens, 328 Or 116, 122, 970 P2d 215(1998).

In State v. Wyatt, 331 Or 335, 15 P3d 22 (2000), the court surveyed its cases addressing preservation and concluded that “a party must provide the trial court with an explanation of his or her objection that is specific enough to ensure that the court can identify its alleged error with enough clarity to permit it to consider and correct the error immediately, if correction is warranted.” Id. at 343. Accordingly, rules of preservation require that no party is taken by surprise, misled, or denied an opportunity to respond to an argument. State v. Chambers, 226 Or App 363, 368-69, 203 P3d 337 (2009).

A. Pretrial Motions

1. Demurrer

ORS 135.630sets out six grounds for demurring to an indictment. That statute provides:

The defendant may demur to the accusatory instrument when it appears upon the face thereof.

(1) If the accusatory instrument is an indictment, that the grand jury by which it was found had no legal authority to inquire into the crime charged because the same is not triable within the county; (2) If the accusatory instrument is an indictment, that it does not substantially conform to the requirements of certain enumerated statutes regarding content of charging instruments];

(3) That the accusatory instrument charges more than one offense not separately stated; (4) That the facts stated do not constitute an offense;

(5) That the accusatory instrument contains mater which, if true, would constitute a legal justification or excuse of the offense charged or other legal bar to the action; or

(6) That the accusatory instrument is not definite and certain.

A demurrer should be filed, in writing, by the time of arraignment. ORS 135.610. Additionally, trial courts have discretion to permit a defendant to file a demurrer at some later time. State v. Hankins, 342 Or 258, 264, 151 P3d 149 (2007); State v. Wimber, 315 Or 103, 112,

843 P2d 424 (1992); ORS 135.610(1). However, it is an open question as to whether a defendant may file a demurrer after the trial has commenced. SeeState ex rel Schrunk v. Bonebrake, 318 Or

312, 320–21, 865 P2d 1289 (1994)(suggesting that a trial court’s discretion to permit a mid- trial demurrer is limited); Wimber, 315 Or at 112 n 18(“We do not wish to be understood as approving of the practice of raising demurrers at trial.”); State v. Montez, 309 Or 564, 597, 789P2d 1352 (1990)(“The proper time for defendant’s objection to the indictment was before trial. A pretrial demurrer would have given both the court and the state a timely opportunity to review the indictment and to make any necessary corrections before trial.”). Accordingly, the best practice is to file a demurrer by the time of arraignment.

ORS 135.640provides that four of the grounds in ORS 135.630may be raised only by demurrer.1 {Note: 1 ORS 135.640provides: “When the objections mentioned in ORS 135.630appear upon the face of the accusatory instru- ment, they can only be taken by demurrer, except that the objection to the jurisdiction of the court over the subject of the accusatory instrument, or that the facts stated do not constitute an offense, may be taken at the trial, under the plea of not guilty and in arrest of judgment.”}

Accordingly, the other two grounds—objection to the jurisdiction of the court and a claim that the facts stated do not constitute an offense—may be raised by some other means than a demurrer, such as a motion in arrest of judgment.2 {Note: 2 See section I(H) for motions in arrest of judgment.}

Hankins, 342 Or at 264–65. When a defendant contends that the facts stated in the accusatory instrument do not constitute an offense and does so by means of a motion for judgment of acquittal, the trial court can properly deny the motion with leave to renew the motion after the verdict. State v. McKenzie, 307 Or 554, 561, 771

P2d 264 (1989). If the jury finds the defendant guilty, the defendant should then file a motion in arrest of judgment and argue that the facts stated in the indictment do not constitute an offense.

Hankins, 342 Or at 265.

2. Motion to Suppress

The appellate courts will not consider an issue if it is raised in the written motion to suppress, but not pursued at the suppression hearing. State v. Turley, 202 Or App 40, 48, 120 P3d 1229 (2005),rev den, 340 Or 157 (2006).

[T]he statement in the motion to suppress put the trial court on notice that defendant was raising a Mirandaclaim. However, defendant made no effort to pursue that contention after her motion was filed. Under those circumstances, the trial court was entitled to infer that, although defendant had initially raised the issue in her motion, she was no longer asserting it.

Id.But see State v. Walker, 350 Or 540, 550, 258 P3d 1228 (2011)(“This court has never

required that each and every argument that has been asserted in writing must be repeated orally in court in order for the argument to be preserved.”). To ensure that the issue is preserved, alert the trial court at the completion of the evidence that a ruling is sought on the specific issue raised. Id.

“To preserve a claim that a trial court improperly denied a motion to suppress evidence as a result of an unlawful seizure, a defendant must present to the trial court an argument as to why a specific action of an officer constituted a seizure.” State v. Amaya, 336 Or 616, 630, 89 P3d 1163 (2004). If the issue involves the legality of a stop, it is appropriate to identify progressive points throughout the encounter, each of which may arguably be considered a stop (if applicable). The officer’s reasonable suspicion likewise should be analyzed at each point in time when the defendant was arguably stopped.

All exhibits that are admitted during a motion to suppress hearing must be labeled and made part of the record.

3. Motion in Limine

Often a trial court is asked to make an evidentiary ruling prior to the commencement of trial. The appellate courts will not review a court’s ruling, however, if the ruling was not final. Following are examples of situations when the appellate courts would not review the issue, due to the lack of a final ruling:

• The trial court entered an order excluding evidence but left the door open to further consideration depending on later developments at trial. Steele v. Mayoral, 231 Or App 603, 610, 615, 220 P3d 761 (2009)(“[T]he trial court’s ruling on the motion in liminewas not its final word on the admissibility of the evidence. Without a final ruling, we have nothing to review.”).

• The trial court merely made subjective statements of its anticipated rulings, which it expressly made subject to further consideration at trial. State v. Tyler, 213 Or App 109, 113–15, 159 P3d 1218, rev den, 343 Or 467 (2007)(the following trial court statement was not a final ruling: “Like I say, at this point I don’t think it’s relevant either. We’ll have to cross the bridge when we get there. It depends on what the evidence is, and I don’t know that.”).

• The trial court’s preliminary ruling “assumed” that certain witnesses would testify as represented by the prosecutor and, based on that “representation,” indicated that it “would overrule the objection.” State v. Adams, 296 Or 185, 188, 674 P2d 593 (1983). The defendant’s “motion in limine” was actually a request for an advisory ruling. Id. at 189. The trial court merely made a subjunctive statement of its anticipated ruling if certain events transpired at trial. Id. “The trial court was never given the opportunity to hear the specifics of the proposed evidence[.]” Id. at 190.

•The trial court “denied the [pretrial] motion, explaining that, because it had not heard any of the case, it did not know whether the evidence was relevant and thus could not weigh relevancy against possible prejudice.” State v. Lockner, 63 Or App 354, 357, 663 P2d 792(1983). The trial court’s statement was not a ruling that the evidence was admissible. Id. at

358. Rather, the court in effect stated that “the motion was premature.” Id. Accordingly, it was incumbent on the defendant to object to the evidence at trial to allow the trial court an opportunity to rule on the objection. Id.

At times it is clear that the trial court’s ruling is not final. In those circumstances object during the trial when the evidence is about to be introduced. Additionally, because it may be difficult to ascertain whether the court’s ruling was final, and because a trial court may have a change of heart once the issue is placed in better perspective following the introduction of evidence, renew the objection during trial to preserve the issue for appellate review. Doing so also serves the function of alerting not only the trial court, but the appellate courts, to the specific evidence in question.

Finally, if the trial court is fully apprised of the nature of the defendant’s argument against admission of evidence and has clearly ruled on the matter, the defendant may ask the court to make a “standing objection” to avoid the need for repeated objections throughout trial. ''See Statev. Metz, 162 Or App 448, 455–56, 986 P2d 714 (1999), rev den, 330 Or 331 (2000)(issue preserved for review when the trial court was fully aware of the issue and specifically allowed defense counsel to make a standing objection to the use of the evidence).

However, it is important to identify the specific testimony that is objectionable. An objection to evidence as a whole is insufficient when any part of the evidence objected to is admissible. Statev. Madison, 290 Or 573, 580, 624 P2d 599 (1981). Accordingly, when making an objection, segregate inadmissible portions of testimony from the admissible ones:

It is well established that when evidence is offered as a whole and an objection is made to the evidence as a whole and is overruled, the trial court will ordinarily not be reversed on appeal if any portion of the offered evidence was properly admissible, despite the fact that other portions would not have been admissible had proper objections been made to such portions of the offered evidence.

State v. Brown, 310 Or 347, 358–59, 800 P2d 259 (1990)(quoting Sproul v. Fossi, 274 Or 749,

755, 548 P2d 970 (1976)).

B. Evidentiary Objections

A trial attorney must clearly state the grounds for the objection. In Shields v. Campbell, 277 Or

71, 77, 559 P2d 1275 (1977), the Oregon Supreme Court said, “A party owes the trial court the obligation of a sound, clear and articulate motion, objection or exception so as to permit the trial judge a chance to consider the legal contention or to correct an error already made.”

The appellate courts will not consider objections to testimony not urged before the trial court. State v.Engweiler, 118 Or App 132, 135, 846 P2d 1163, rev den, 317 Or 486 (1993).

C. Offer of Proof

To preserve an issue that the trial court erred in excluding evidence, trial counsel must make an offer of proof. State v. Haugen, 349 Or 174, 191, 243 P3d 31 (2010). The offer of proof must identify what the excluded evidence would have shown. State v. Busby, 315 Or 292, 298, 844 P2d 897 (1993). “The purpose of [the] rule is to assure that appellate courts are able to determine whether it was error to exclude the evidence and whether any error was likely to have affected the result of the case. State v.Affeld, 307 Or 125, 128, 764 P2d 220 (1988).

OEC 103provides, in part:

(1) Evidential error is not presumed to be prejudicial. Error may not be predicated upon a ruling which admits or excludes evidence unless a substantial right of the party is affected, and:

(a) In case the ruling is one admitting evidence, a timely objection or motion to strike appears of record, stating the specific ground of objection…; or

(b) In case the ruling is one excluding evidence, the substance of the evidence was made known to the court by offer….

(2) The court may add any other or further statement which shows the character of the evidence, the form in which it was offered, the objection made and the ruling thereon. It may direct the making of an offer in question and answer form.

(3) In jury cases, proceedings shall be conducted, to the extent practicable, so as to prevent inadmissi- ble evidence from being suggested to the jury by any means, such as making statements or offers of proof or asking questions in the hearing of the jury.

To make an offer of proof, the defendant should identify the witness he would have called and the testimony that would have been given. Haugen, 349 Or at 192. Additionally, the witness can be called to the stand outside the presence of the jury to offer the testimony. Affeld, 307 Or at 128. Sometimes a witness is on the stand, but is not allowed to answer a particular question due to a ruling by the trial court. In that situation, the witness should be asked the question and allowed to answer outside the presence of the jury. Do not assume that the substance of the proffered testimony is apparent from the context of the question. SeeState v. Bowen, 340 Or 487, 500–01, 135 P3d 272 (2006)(the defendant failed to make an adequate record for review when he did not make offer of proof regarding the witnesses answer and made no attempt to inform the trial court of the purported relevance of the testimony).

If the evidence in question is an exhibit, the item should be marked as an exhibit and entered into the record so that the item can be reviewed on appeal.

If the issue involves the appropriateness of a redaction (to a document, audio recording, or video recording), the defendant’s version should be marked and entered as an exhibit so that the appellate courts may compare the version admitted at trial with the version urged by the defendant.

“The only situations in which an offer of proof is not required are those situations in which an offer of proof is impossible because of a trial court’s refusal to allow the offer of proof to be made.” Affeld, 307 Or at 129.

It may be necessary to break the offer of proof into segments if any portion of the offer contains both admissible and inadmissible evidence. If a single offer of proof contains both admissible and inadmissible evidence, the trial court does not err if it rejects the entire offer. State v. Tiner, 340 Or

551, 557, 135 P3d 305 (2006); Pumpelly v. Reeves, 273 Or 808, 812–13, 543 P2d 682 (1975).

D. Motion for Judgment of Acquittal

ORS 136.445 sets out the standard for granting a motion for judgment of acquittal: “In any criminal action the defendant may, after close of the state’s evidence or of all the evidence, move the court for a judgment of acquittal. The court shall grant the motion if the evidence introduced theretofore is such as would not support a verdict against the defendant. The acquittal shall be a bar to another prosecution for the same offense.”

The state must prove each and every element of the offense by proof beyond a reasonable doubt. State v. Allison, 325 Or 585, 588, 941 P2d 1017 (1997); State ex rel Mikkelsen v. Hill, 315 Or 452, 458, 847 P2d 402 (1993); ORS 136.415. The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution 3 {Note: 3 The Fourteenth Amendment provides, in part: “No State shall … deprive any person of life, liberty, or property, without due process of law[.]”}

mandates “that no person shall be made to suffer the onus of a criminal conviction except upon sufficient proof – defined as evidence necessary to convince a trier of fact beyond a reasonable doubt of the existence of every element of the offense.”

In accessing the merits of a motion for judgment of acquittal, the court must view the evidence and reasonable inferences in the light most favorable to the state to determine whether any rational trier of fact could find the essential elements of the crime proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Statev. King, 307 Or 332, 339, 768 P2d 391 (1989); State v. Begay, 233 Or App 201, 203, 225 P3d 108 (2010). Impeachment of a witness’ testimony does not render that testimony insufficient to withstand a motion for judgment of acquittal. State v. Guy, 229 Or App 611, 216, 212 P3d 1265, rev den, 347Or 269 (2009).

It is well established in Oregon that the determination of a witness’s credibility is the sole prov- ince of the trier of fact. This court previously has rejected arguments that, because there are inconsistencies in a primary witness’s recounting of events or inconsistencies in evidence, there is insufficient evidence to support a conviction. When a defendant moves for a judgment of acquit- tal, it is not proper for the court to hold that there is reasonable doubt simply because there are conflicts in the evidence. Rather, all such conflicts must be resolved in favor of submitting the matter to the jury. Id. at 616–17 (internal citations omitted).

The motion is made at the close of the state’s case, and the motion can be renewed upon closing of the evidentiary portion of the trial. ORS 136.445. On review, the appellate court will “consider the whole record to determine whether there is sufficient corroborative evidence” no matter when the motion was made at trial. State v. Koenig, 238 Or App 297, 301, 242 P3d 649 (2010), rev den, 349Or 601 (2011).

A generic motion for judgment of acquittal is insufficient to preserve the “issue” for appellate review. State v. Matheson, 220 Or App 397, 406, 186 P3d 309 (2008)(“A general motion for judgment of acquittal without specifying any theory on which the state’s proof was legally insufficient preserves no ground for challenge on appeal.”) (internal brackets and ellipses omitted). Accordingly, the motion must state the specific theory on which the state’s proof was insufficient. State v. Paragon, 195 Or App 265, 268, 97 P3d 691 (2004).

If the case is being tried to the court, the best way to challenge the legal sufficiency of the evidence is by motion for judgment of acquittal. However, if the defendant clearly raises a sufficiency issue in closing argument, the appellate courts can reach the issue. State v. Forrester, 203 Or App 151, 155, 125 P3d 47 (2005), rev den, 341 Or 141 (2006). See alsoState v. Gonzalez, 188 Or App 430, 431, 71P3d 573 (2003)(in trial to the court, the defendant’s closing argument that the state had not adduced legally sufficient evidence of a voluntary act, which is required to establish criminal culpability, “is the equivalent of a motion for judgment of acquittal”); State v. Hamilton, 186 Or App 729, 731 n 2, 64P3d 1215 (2003)(closing argument in bench trial was sufficient to preserve issue for appeal despite the fact that the defendant did not formally move for a judgment of acquittal); State v. Andrews, 174Or App 354, 359, 27 P3d 137 (2001)(reaching the question of what elements the state must prove under a statute on appeal, based on the defendant’s closing argument in a bench trial).

E. Motion for Mistrial

To preserve error, a motion for a mistrial must be timely. A motion is timely if it is made when the objectionable statement was made. State v. Williams, 322 Or 620, 631, 912 P2d 364 (1996). A trial court must be given an opportunity to “grant the motion, … cure the effect of inappropriate conduct or testimony by giving a proper instruction instead, or do nothing at all.” State v. Veatch, 223 Or App 444, 453, 196 P3d 45 (2008). The requirement of a timely mistrial motion allows the court to take prompt curative action, if the court believes it is warranted, to avert the need for a mistrial. Id. at 453–54.

The following circumstances are illustrative of untimely motions:

•Although trial counsel timely alerted the court that it “had a matter for the court,” counsel waited until after the parties had delivered their closing arguments and the jury had retired to deliberate before making the mistrial motion. [http://scholar.google.com/scholar_case?q=melton%2B112&hl=en&as_sdt=4%2C38&case=15480253433854472630&scilh=0 State v. Melton, 112 Or App 648, 649, 829 P2d 1053, rev den, 314 Or 176 (1992).

•Motion for mistrial was untimely when made after the jury entered deliberations with objectionable exhibits because “the court had no opportunity to give admonishing instructions to the jury to disregard defendant’s statement [regarding the objectionable material displayed on the exhibit] or to excise the matter from the plaintiff ’s exhibits.” Moodyv. Rasmussen, 274 Or 605, 611–12, 547 P2d 623 (1976).

•The trial court sustained the defendant’s objection to the prosecutor’s question, but the defendant waited until two unrelated witnesses testified before moving for a mistrial. State v.Walton, 311 Or 223, 809 P2d 81 (1991).

•The defendant moved for a mistrial on the grounds that a statement had been improperly admitted. However, the defendant waited until the following week, when the trial resumed, to make the motion. State v. Clark, 217 Or App 475, 480-81, 175 P3d 1006, rev den, 344Or 670 (2008).

•The defendant timely objected to a witness’s improper testimony, and the trial court stated, “I’ll strike that.” State v. Kolbe, 115 Or App 268, 273, 838 P2d 612 (1992), rev den, 315Or 644 (1993). The witness continued his testimony (which translated into 40 pages of testimony), and the court recessed. Id. During the recess, the defendant moved for a mistrial. Id. The court found that the motion came “after the incident was allowed to pass. That was too late.” Id.

The appellate courts review the trial court’s decision to deny a motion for a mistrial for an abuse of discretion, which is a very deferential standard. State v. Larson, 325 Or 15, 22, 933 P2d 958 (1997). Further, the appellate courts do not ordinarily “reverse a case for the denial of a motion for mistrial in which the trial court has sustained an objection to evidence and has admonished the jury to disregard it.” State v. Jones, 279 Or 55, 62, 566 P2d 867 (1977). When a trial court gives a purportedly curative instruction, the question on appeal is whether the instruction “was sufficient to unring the bell.” Statev. Bowen, 240 Or 487, 511, 135 P3d 272 (2006), cert den, 549 US 1214, 127 S Ct 1258, 167 L Ed 2d 89 (2007).

F. Jury Instructions

1. Take Exception AfterInstructions Are Given

ORCP 59 H(1) 4{Note: 4 ORCP 59 H(1)applies to criminal trials. ORS 136.330(2).}

provides, in pertinent part:

A party may not obtain review on appeal of an asserted error by a trial court in … giving or refusing to give an instruction to a jury unless the party who seeks to appeal identified the asserted error to the trial court and made a notation of exception immediately after the court instructed the jury.

(Emphasis added). The timing of the exception is critical. A defendant does not preserve a jury instruction issue when the defendant makes the exception beforethe jury was instructed. State v.Guardipee, 239 Or App 44, 47, 243 P3d 149 (2010)(The language in ORCP 59 H(1) “is explicit and unambiguous. Because defense counsel failed to make ‘a notation of exception immediately after the court instructed the jury,’ the matter was not preserved for our review.”).

Accordingly, it is necessary that the defendant take exception to the trial court’s refusal to give a requested instruction after the trial court instructed the jury. State v. Pena, 239 Or App 356, 243 P3d 1218 (2010)(claimed error unreviewable because the defendant did not take an exception after the instructions were given). Likewise, it is necessary to take exception to the instructions when the trial court gives an instruction over the defendant’s objection. Finally, if a trial court simply forgets to give an instruction that the defendant requested, it is necessary to except to the instructions, despite the fact that the trial court had previously indicated its willingness to give the instruction. State v. Derrick, 198 Or App 358, 363, 108 P3d 608, revden, 339 Or 475 (2005)(because it is unclear whether the trial court made an adverse ruling or simply forgot to give the instruction, the defendant was obligated to bring the issue to the court’s attention in order to clarify).

2. Concurrence Objection (Boots)

Article I, section 11, of the Oregon Constitution 5 {Note: 5 Article I, section 11, provides that, “[i]n all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall have the right to public trial by an impartial jury…in the circuit court ten members of the jury may render a verdict of guilty or not guilty[.]”}

and ORS 136.450(1),6 {Note: 6 ORS 136.450provides: “(1) Except as otherwise provided in subsection (2) of this section, the verdict of a trial jury in a criminal action shall be by concurrence of at least 10 of 12 jurors. (2) Except when the state requests a unanimous verdict, a verdict of guilty for murder or aggravated murder shall be by concurrence of at least 11 of 12 jurors.”}

require jury concurrence. Accordingly, a requisite number of jurors must agree on the factual occurrences that constitute a crime. State v. Boots, 308 Or 371, 378–79, 780 P2d 725 (1989), cert den, 510 US

1013, 114 S Ct 606, 126 L Ed 2d 571 (1993). A court’s failure to give a jury instruction requiring “agreement on all material elements of a charge in order to convict” is error. State v. Lotches, 331Or 455, 472, 17 P3d 1045 (2000), cert den, 534 US 833, 122 S Ct 82, 151 L Ed 2d 45 (2001).

When a concurrence issue arises, the defendant should first move the trial court to require the state to make an election. State v. Dubois, 221 Or App 644, 655, 191 P3d 670 (2008)(a motion to require the state to elect can often obviate the need for a jury concurrence instruction).

The timing of any election is within the discretion of the trial court. State v. Magana, 212 OrApp 553, 564, 159 P3d 1163, rev den, 343 Or 363 (2007)(“[T]he rule governing election is flexible so that the state will not be forced to make a choice when it cannot intelligently do so, but which will afford the defendant sufficient time, after the choice has been made, to defend himself properly.”).

If no election is made, the defendant should ask for a special jury instruction instructing the jury that at least ten of its members (in a case involving charges other than murder) must agree on the same factual occurrence that constitutes the offense. The defendant should also provide the court with a verdict form that asks an additional question to ensure a valid verdict. For example, the question on the verdict form following the jury’s verdict could ask, “Did ten of the jurors agree that the basis for the guilty verdict was [identify specific conduct]?”

3. Requested Instruction Must Be Correct Statement of Law

The appellate court will review the jury instruction at issue to determine whether it was a correct statement of the law. State v. Schwab, 234 Or App 43, 45, 227 P3d 1182, rev den, 349 Or 52(2010). “It is a long-standing principle of Oregon law that a trial court’s failure to give a requested instruction is not error unless the requested instruction is clear and correct in form and substance and completely free from error.” State v. Nebel, 237 Or App 30, 35, 238 P3d 423, rev den, 349 Or 370 (2010).

Accordingly, if the trial court refuses to give a requested instruction, it is necessary for the defendant to make sure that the requested jury instruction is in the record. This can be accomplished either by submitting a written copy of the instruction (and seeing that it is properly filed in the trial court file), or by reading the instruction at issue – verbatim – into the record.

4. Miscellaneous Jury Issues

If the jury submits a question to the court during deliberations, the question should be marked as a court exhibit and made part of the record. Additionally, the parties’ discussion regarding the answer should be on the record, and the trial court’s oral or written response should be made part the record for appeal.

It is a good practice to always ask the trial court to poll the jury. If the court polls the jury by asking for a show of hands, make sure that the court states the results orally on the record.

G. Constitutional Objections and Federalization

On appeal, the defendant will likely raise an issue under both the Oregon and federal constitutions. This is particularly true if the defendant is serving a lengthy sentence and will ultimately challenge his conviction in a federal habeas corpus proceeding. Accordingly, it is necessary to preserve the federal issues at the trial level to ensure that defendant’s issues are properly preserved for his future habeas proceeding.

To obtain appellate review of constitutional error, trial counsel must articulate the constitutional basis for the objection. Without such specificity, the constitutional issue will likely be waived. ''See Statev. Moore, 324 Or 396, 418, 927 P2d 1073 (1996). In addition, to preserve an objection or issue for federal habeas review, trial counsel should state the federal constitutional basis for the objection.

If the issue is the sufficiency of the evidence, cite the Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution as well as Jackson v. Virginia, 443 US 307, 99 S Ct 2781, 61 L Ed 2d 560 (1979).

For sentencing issues, it may be appropriate to argue that trial court’s disregard of state sentencing law and the imposition of an unauthorized sentence violates the defendant’s right to liberty and his due process rights under the United States Constitution. See, e.g., Hicks v. Oklahoma, 447 US 343, 346, 100 S Ct 2227, 65 L Ed 2d 175 (1980); Whalen v. United States, 445 US 684, 689 n 4, 100 SCt 1432, 63 L Ed 2d 715 (1980).

H. Motion for New Trial & Motion in Arrest of Judgment

ORS 136.535(1)makes ORCP 64A and B and D to G, involving a motion for a new trial, applicable to criminal trials.

The appellate courts will review the denial of a motion for a new trial if the motion was based on one of the following:

(1) juror misconduct that did not come to light until after the verdict was rendered, or

(2) newly discovered evidence that was not known at the time of the trial.

State v. Sullens, 314 Or 436, 440-43, 839 P2d 708 (1992); State v. Alvarez-Vega, 240 Or App 616, 619, 251 P3d 199, rev den, 350 Or 297 (2011); State v. Grey, 175 Or App 235, 245, 28 P3d 1195(2001), rev den, 333 Or 463 (2002); State v. Mayer, 146 Or App 86, 88, 932 P2d 570 (1997).

A motion in arrest of judgment may be founded on one or both of the following:

(1) a claim that the grand jury had no legal authority to inquire into the crime charged in the indictment because the offense is not triable in the county, or

(2) a claim that the facts stated do not constituted an offense.

ORS 135.630(1), (4); ORS 136.500. The timing of a motion for a new trial and a motion in arrest of judgment is governed by the provisions of ORCP 64F. ORS 136.500; ORS 136.535. The motion must be filed no later than 10 days after the judgment was entered. ORCP 64F(1). A motion filed before the judgment is entered is considered timely if there is no objection. State v. Starr, 210 Or App 409, 412-13, 150 P3d 1072 (2007); State v. Howard, 205 Or App 408, 415–16, 134 P3d 1042, rev den, 341 Or 198 (2006); Wayv. Prosch, 163 Or App 437, 442, 988 P2d 422 (1999). The trial court may grant an extension of time withinthe 10-day time period. ORCP 64F(1); State v. Provonsha, 107 Or App 571, 573, 813 P2d 563, rev den, 312 Or 235 (1991).

The motion must be heard and determined by the court within 55 days of the judgment entrydate. ORCP 64F(1). If the court does not enter an order within 55 days, the motion will be deemeddenied. ORCP 64F(1). Accordingly, if the trial court orally rules in the defendant’s favor, follow up to make sure that the order granting the motion is timely filed in the case register.

I. Miscellaneous

Below are some miscellaneous preservation factors to keep in mind when trying a case.

•If the defendant is being tried with a codefendant, and you want to rely on the same arguments/ motions/objections/offer of proof/etc. as made by the codefendant, explicitly state so on the record. This can be accomplished either by informing the trial court orally on the record, prior to trial, that the defendant wishes to adopt all of the codefendant’s arguments and objections, or it can be done on a case-by-case basis. When in doubt, alert the court that the ruling it was asked to make also applies to your client.

•Often an audio or video recording is submitted as an exhibit and played for the jury in the courtroom. When this happens, make sure that a microphone is placed near the recording, so the record will include the exact portions of the recording played at trial.

•Often parties discuss issues in chambers prior to trial. In the case of an adverse ruling, make sure to make a complete record of the issue, arguments, and ruling for the record. This also applies to those rare circumstances when the parties approach the bench to discuss an issue. At the first opportunity outside of the jury’s presence, counsel should state on the record the content of the discussion and any adverse rulings.

•If the use of challenges during voir direis at issue (such as the trial court’s refusal to excuse a juror for cause), the record should reflect exactly how many challenges, both for cause and preemptory, were made, how they were used, and what jurors were affected by the court’s ruling.

•When the trial court rules against the defendant it is appropriate to request the trial court to make findings so that the appellate courts will know the basis for the court’s ruling. In the case of factual findings, if a trial court does not make an explicit finding on the record, on appeal it will be presumed that the facts were decided in a manner consistent with the trial court’s ultimate conclusion if there is evidence from which the facts could be so found. Ball v. Gladden, 250 Or 485, 487, 443 P2d 621 (1968). Accordingly, if you believe that any of the findings are in your favor, despite an adverse ruling, it is desirable to have the court place those findings on the record.

•There are times when counsel submits additional cases to the trial court during an argument (i.e., those that are not otherwise cited in a memorandum or motion). In those circumstances, read the case name and citation into the record so that the appellate courts (and counsel) will know what case law the trial court relied on in making its ruling.

•If hearsay is admitted over the defendant’s objection, ask the trial court to state on the record the reason for the ruling, such as: (1) whether the testimony fell under an exception to the hearsay rule and, if so, which exception, or (2) whether the testimony was allowed for some other purpose, such as for impeachment purposes.

•The record of trial proceedings is oral. As a result, all nonverbal gestures, conduct, etc., are not captured for the record. Likewise, any visual materials that are used (such as a photograph the prosecutor may display throughout a closing argument) are not reflected in the record. Accordingly, it is necessary that trial counsel orally describe any pertinent circumstances for the record.

II. Invited Error Doctrine

A party cannot seek a reversal due to a legal error if the party was actively instrumental in bringing about the error (that is, invited the error). State v. Cervantes, 232 Or App 567, 577, 223 P3d 425 (2009). “The doctrine is generally applicable when a party has invited the trial court to rule in a particular way under circumstances that suggest that the party will be bound by the ruling or will not later seek a reversal on the basis of that ruling.” State v. Kammeyer, 226 Or App 210, 214, 203 P3d 274, rev den, 346 Or 590 (2009). “The goal of the rule is to ensure that parties who make intentional or strategic trial choices do not later ‘blame the court’ if those choices prove to be unwise.” Id.

III. Conditional Pleas

The defendant may decide to enter a conditional plea if he or she loses a dispositive pretrial motion. ORS 135.335(3)provides: “With the consent of the court and the state, a defendant may enter a conditional plea of guilty or no contest reserving, in writing, the right, on appeal from the judgment of any specified pretrial motion. A defendant who finally prevails on appeal may withdraw the plea.”

As the statute provides, the conditional nature of the plea must be in writing. The plea petition should (1) indicate that the plea is conditional, and (2) set forth the exact ruling(s) of the trial court that the defendant is reserving for appeal. City of Lake Oswego v. Albright, 222 Or App 117, 193 P3d 988 (2008), illustrates the precision necessary to preserve an issue for appeal in this context. In Albright, the court found that the defendant did not properly reserve his right to appeal the trial court’s adverse pretrial ruling. There, defendant filed a motion to suppress “all evidence relating to his arrest” for DUII. Id. at 119. Following the trial court’s denial of the motion, the defendant ostensibly entered a conditional guilty plea. Id. During the plea colloquy, the trial court and the parties contemplated that the defendant was reserving his right to appeal the denial of his motion to suppress. Id. However, the plea petition included:

only a handwritten notation that state[d], ‘Stip facts with reservation of appeal rights,’ and there [was]

no other writing authored by [the] defendant or his counsel expressing a reservation of his right to seek review of the denial of the motion to suppress. Id. On appeal, the court found that the “key word” in the conditional plea statute was “specified” in the phrase “any specified pretrial motion.” Id. at 119–20. The court found that the defendant’s handwritten notation on the plea petition did not “specify in writing the pretrial motion as the subject of the contemplated appeal” as required by statute and, accordingly, the appellate court was unable to review the trial court’s denial of the defendant’s motion to suppress. Id. at 120.

IV. Common Sentencing Issues

A. Merger

Merger of convictions is governed by ORS 161.067. That statute provides, in part:

(1) When the same conduct or criminal episode violates two or more statutory provisions and each provision requires proof of an element that the others do not, there are as many separately punishable of- fenses as there are separate statutory violations.

(2) When the same conduct or criminal episode, though violating only one statutory provision in- volves two or more victims, there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are victims.…

(3) When the same conduct or criminal episode violates only one statutory provision and involves only one victim, but nevertheless involves repeated violations of the same statutory provision against the same victim, there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are violations, except that each vio- lation, to be separately punishable under this subsection, must be separated from other such violations by a sufficient pause in the defendant’s criminal conduct to afford the defendant an opportunity to renounce the criminal intent.…

1. ORS 161.067(1)

In State v. Barrett, 331 Or 27, 10 P3d 901 (2000), the Supreme Court held that ORS 161.067“requires boththat a defendant’s acts violate ‘two or more statutory provisions’ and that each ‘statutory provision’ require ‘proof of an element that the others do not.’” Barrett, 331 Or at 32(emphasis in original). Thus, the first question under ORS 161.067(1)is whether defendant’s conduct violated two or more statutory provisions.

To determine whether a single act violated two or more statutory provisions, it must be determined whether the legislature intended to create a single crime or two or more crimes. Statev. White, 346 Or 275, 280, 211 P3d 248 (2009). To do so, one must examine “the statute as a whole, looking to the text, context, and, when appropriate, legislative history of the statute.” Id. at 283. The analysis includes a consideration of whether offenses address one “unified legislative objective.” Id.at 284. See also, State v. Parkins, 346 Or 333, 349, 211 P3d 262 (2009)(same).

If the defendant’s conduct does violate two or more statutory provisions, “only thendoes the court consider whether each statutory provision requires proof of an element that the others do not.” Barrett,331 Or at 32 (emphasis added).

If both questions can be answered in the affirmative, “there are as many separately punishable offenses as there are separate statutory violations.” ORS 161.067.

2. ORS 161.067(2)

ORS 161.067prohibits merger if the same conduct violates only one statutory provision, but involves two or more victims.

3. ORS 161.067(3)

ORS 161.067 prohibits merger when the same conduct involves repeated violations of the same statutory provision against the same victim and there was sufficient pause to afford the defendant an opportunity to renounce the criminal intent.

The Court of Appeals has found an insufficient pause when there was no evidence that one statutory violation “ended” before another began. State v. Sanders, 185 Or App 125, 57 P 3d 963(2002), rev den, 336 Or 657 (2004). Additionally, the mere passage of time between individual acts does not necessarily satisfy the sufficient pause requirement. Id.at 130 (“The mere passage of time, by itself, does not establish that one assault ended before the other begin.”); but cf. State v.Gilbertson, 110 Or App 152, 822 P2d 716 (1991), rev den, 313 Or 211 (1992)(The defendant entered his wife’s house and held a knife to her hand. She broke free and retreated to the laundry room where she remained until the defendant broke the door down. The time between the wife’s flight and the defendant’s entry into the laundry room afforded the defendant the opportunity to renounce his criminal intent.).

4. Judgment Following Merger of Offenses

If the trial court orders the merger of two or more offenses, review the judgment to ensure that it properly reflects the merger. Seesection V(A)(1), below.

B. Departure Sentences

A sentencing judge must impose a presumptive sentence within the range established by the appropriate grid block unless there are “substantial and compelling” reasons—either in aggravation or in mitigation—to depart from the presumptive range. State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 487, 847 P2d 834(1993); OAR 213-008-0001. A sentence that is either greater or lesser in length than that allowed by the presumptive range is called a “durational departure.” Davis, 315 Or at 488. If the trial court departs from the presumptive sentence, it must state the substantial and compelling reasons for doing so on the record.

See below for limitations on the imposition of a departure sentence (such as the 400-percent rule).

C. Consecutive Sentences

A court may impose a consecutive sentence only if done in accordance with the provisions of ORS 137.123. Additionally, there are rules that establish the limits for consecutive sentences under the guidelines. The 200-percent rule, 400-percent rule, and shift-to-I rule are discussed below.

D. Guideline Rules

For a good explanation of the basics of the Oregon felony sentencing guidelines and how they operate, review the Supreme Court opinion in State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 486–88, 847 P2d 834(1993). Also refer to OCDLA’s Felony Sentencing in Oregonmanual by Jesse Wm. Barton.

1. Reconstitution of Criminal History

OAR 213-004-0006(2)provides, in part: “An offender’s criminal history is based upon the number of adult felony and Class A misdemeanor convictions and juvenile adjudications in the offender’s criminal history at the time the current crime or crimes of conviction are sentenced. For crimes committed on or after Nov[ember] 1, 1989[,] a conviction is considered to have occurred upon the pronouncement of sentence in open court.”

Thus, a conviction becomes part of a defendant’s criminal history “upon the pronouncement of sentence in open court.”

Complications arise, however, when a defendant is sentenced for two or more convictions in a single sentencing hearing. Once the trial court sentences a defendant on the first conviction, it becomes part of the defendant’s criminal history and must be used to calculate the defendant’s criminal history for the second conviction. State v. Bucholz, 317 Or 309, 321, 855 P2d 1100(1993). Those two convictions must then be used to calculate the defendant’s criminal history for the third conviction, and so forth. This process is referred to as “reconstituting” criminal history. State v. Langevin, 203 Or App 395, 124 P3d 1281 (2005).

However, as this court explained in State v. Allen, 151 Or App 281, 289, 948 P2d 745(1997), a conviction may not be used to reconstitute the criminal history of convictions that arose from the same criminal episode: “[C]onvictions may be considered part of a defendant’s ‘criminal history’ within the meaning of OAR [213-004-0006(2)] only if they arose out of a separate criminal incidentfrom the one that gave rise to the ‘current crime or crimes’ for which the defendant is being sentenced.” (Emphasis added). Charges are part of the same criminal episode if they are “so closely linked in time, place and circumstance that a complete account of one charge cannot be related without relating details of the other charge.” State v. Fitzgerald, 267 Or 266, 273, 516P2d 1280 (1973). See alsoState v. Boyd, 271 Or 558, 565–66, 533 P2d 795 (1975)(holding “same criminal episode” barring multiple prosecutions and “same transaction” in mandatory joinder statute synonymous); ORS 131.505(4)(defining “criminal episode” as “continuous and uninterrupted conduct that establishes at least one offense and is so joined in time, place and circumstances that such conduct is directed to the accomplishment of a single criminal objective”).

2. Shift-to-I Rule

OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a)is the so-called “shift-to-I rule.”7 {Note: 7 OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a)provides: “Subject to the provisions of subsection (b) [the 200% rule] of this section, the pre- sumptive incarceration term of the consecutive sentences is the sum of: (A) the presumptive incarceration term or the prison term defined in OAR 213-008-0005(1)imposed pursuant to a dispositional departure for the primary offense, as defined in OAR 213-003-0001(17); and (B) up to the maximum incarceration term indicated in the Criminal History I Column for each additional offense imposed consecutively.”}

The rule applies when a defendant is sentenced for multiple felonies in the same proceeding and the crime involves one criminal episode and one victim. State v. Mayes, 234 Or App 707, 709 n 1, 229 P3d 628, rev den, 348Or 669 (2010). In that circumstance, the defendant’s true criminal history score is used in assessing the gridblock classification on the primary offense as well as any other offenses for which concurrent sentences are imposed. Id. However, if the trial court imposes any consecutive sentences, the court is required to use the criminal history score “I.” Id.

3. 200-Percent Rule

OAR 213-012-0020(2)(b)is the so-called “200-percent rule.”8 {Note: 8 OAR 213-012-0020(2)(b) provides, in pertinent part: “The total incarceration term of consecutive sentences, including the incarceration term for the primary offense, shall not exceed twice the maximum presumptive incarceration term or the prison term definedin OAR 213-008-0005(1) imposed pursuant to a dispositional departure of the primary sentence except by departure as provided by OAR 213-008-0007.”}

State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 492,

847 P2d 834 (1993). It establishes the limit when no departure sentence is imposed. Id. at 491. The rule limits a trial court’s authority to impose consecutive sentences when the defendant’s convictions arise out of the same criminal episode and there is only one victim. State v. Miller,

317 Or 297, 302-05, 855 P2d 1093 (1993). “Criminal episode” is defined in ORS 131.505(4) as “continuous and uninterrupted conduct that establishes at least one offense and is so joined in time, place and circumstances that such conduct is directed to the accomplishment of a single criminal episode.”

Under [the 200-percent] rule, no matter how many convictions arise out of a single case, if any of the sentences are imposed to run consecutively, the total incarceration term for all of the convictions combined may not exceed twice the maximum presumptive incarceration term for the primary offense, except by departure.

Davis, 315 Or at 492. OAR 213-003-0001(17)defines the “primary offense” as “the offense of conviction with the highest crime seriousness ranking.” If more than one offense is classified in the same crime category, the sentencing judge shall designate which offense is the primary offense.

Id.

4. 400-Percent Rule

OAR 213-008-0007(3), when read in conjunction with OAR 213-012-0020(2)(the 200-percent rule) is the so-called “400 percent rule.”9 {Note: 9 OAR 213-008-0007(3)provides: “When a departure sentence is imposed for any individual offense sentenced consecu- tively, the incarceration term of that departure sentence shall not exceed twice the maximum incarceration term that may be imposed fro that offense as provided in OAR 213-012-0020(2)(a) [the 200% rule]. This limit on the duration of a departure sentence does not apply to any indeterminate sentence imposed on a dangerous offender under ORS 161.725and 161.737not to consecutive sentences imposed for crimes that have different victims.”}

State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 492, 847 P2d 834 (1993). The 400-percent rule provides a limit on incarceration terms when multiple sentences are imposed consecutively and include one or more departure sentences. State v. Young, 183 Or App 400, 405, 52 P3d 1102 (2002).

The rule operates as follows. First, the sentencing judge must calculate the presumptive incarceration term for each conviction without departure, applying the 200-percent rule. Davis, 315 Or at 493(the presumptive incarceration terms cannot exceed 200 percent of the presumptive incarceration term for the primary offense). See alsoState v. Langdon, 330 Or 72, 82n 7, 999 P2d 1127 (2000)(the 400-percent rule operates in reference to presumptive sentences that the court has already adjusted under the 200-percent rule); State v. Longenecker, 175 Or App 33, 39 n 3, 27 P3d 509, rev den, 332 Or 656 (2001)(either the 200-percent rule is applied alone or boththe 200- and the 400-percent rules are applied). After calculating the presumptive terms, the judge may impose a departure sentence on any or all of the individual convictions. Id. However, under the 400-percent rule, the incarceration term of each departure sentence may not exceed twice the presumptive incarceration term already determined for that offense under the 200-percent rule. Id.

Because these presumptive incarceration terms will have been limited already by operation of the 200-percent rule, the maximum incarceration term that may be imposed for all the consecutive sen- tences together by departure cannot exceed four times the maximum presumptive incarceration term of the primary offense.

Id. Note that although 400 percent is the absolute maximum in any case, in some circumstances the maximum incarceration term may be lessthan that amount. See Davis, 315 Or at 494 n 16, for an example of how an incarceration term may be less than four times the presumptive sentence of the primary offense, but nonetheless is still the maximum total incarceration term that may be imposed under the guidelines. See also Longenecker, 175 Or App at 39 n 4.

For purposes of review, it is important that the defendant ask the trial court to clearly establish how it arrived at the sentence. For instance, the record should reflect how that trial court applied the 200-percent rule, as the initial step, and then how the court departed from that sentence. As noted above, the 400-percent rule does not always equate to 400 percent of the primary offense. Rather, there are times when the maximum total incarceration time is less than 400 percent of the primary offense. However, if the record does not reflect how the trial court arrived at the sentence, the appellate court will necessarily rely on the overall practical effect of the 400-percent rule. Longenecker, 175 Or App 39 n 4 (“Here, as in many cases, we do not have the information needed to determine the effect of the 400-percent rule on the individual incarceration term for each offense, because we do not know how the trial court would have or did adjust those individual sentences under the 200-percent rule. Thus, in determining whether the trial court erred in imposing the sentence that it did, we necessarily rely on the overall practical effect of the 400-percent rule on [the] defendant’s total sentence, rather than on its actual effect on this sentence for each offense.”).

When the defendant is convicted of offenses that carry a mandatory minimum sentence, conflicts may emerge with the guideline rules. Those cases should be examined on a case-by-case basis, but it may be helpful to review the following cases:

•State v. Langdon, 330 Or 72, 84, 999 P2d 1127 (2000)(Measure 11 and 400-percent rule are in conflict, cannot be harmonized, and Measure 11 prevails).

•State v. Longenecker, 175 Or App 33, 27 P3d 509, rev den, 332 Or 656 (2011).

E. Restitution

ORS 137.106sets forth the requirements for imposition of restitution.10 {Note: 10 ORS 137.106provides, in part: (1) When a person is convicted of a crime, or a violation as described in ORS 153.008, that has resulted in economic damages, the district attorney shall investigate and present to the court, prior to the time of sentencing, evidence of the nature and amount of the damages. If the court finds from the evidence presented that a victim suffered economic damages, in addition to any other sanction it may impose, the court shall include one of the following in the judgment: (a) A requirement that the defendant pay the victim restitution in a specific amount that equals the full amount of the victim’s economic damages as determined by the court. (b) A requirement that the defendant pay the victim restitution, and that the specific amount of restitution will be established by a supplemental judgment based upon a determi- nation made by the court within 90 days of entry of the judgment. In the supplemental judgment, the court shall establish a specific amount of restitution that equals the full amount of the victim’s economic damages as determined by the court. The court may extend the time within which the determination and supplemental judgment may be completed for good cause.”}

There are three prerequisites to an order of restitution: (1) criminal activities, (2) economic damages, and (3) a causal relationship between the two. State v. Edson, 329 Or 127, 132, 985 P2d 1253 (1999).

“Criminal activities” means “any offense with respect to which the defendant is convicted or any other criminal conduct admitted by the defendant.” ORS 137.103(1). There are times when the state seeks imposition of restitution on unconvicted conduct, e.g., as part of a plea agreement. In those circumstances, the trial court’s authority to impose restitution “hinges on the defendant’s admission of criminal conduct.” State v. Thorpe, 217 Or App 301, 306, 175 P3d 993 (2007). A statement in a plea petition that the defendant agrees to pay restitution to victims of crimes he was not convicted of committing is not sufficient to establish the “criminal activities” prerequisite to restitution. State v.Armstrong, 44 Or App 219, 223, 605 P2d 736 (1980)(trial court lacked statutory authority to order the defendant to pay restitution to the victim of the theft offense that was dismissed). In contrast, a defendant’s admission of complicity in charges that were dismissed is sufficient to constitute an admission of criminal conduct with respect to the dismissed charges. State v. Boswell, 52 Or App 535, 538, 628 P2d 763, rev den, 291 Or 419 (1981)(the defendant admitted complicity in all counts, including those that were ultimately dismissed, therefore restitution was authorized on all counts).

ORS 137.106(1)provides three steps for the court to order restitution:

First, the district attorney must investigate and present to the court, prior to the time of sentencing, evi- dence of the nature and amount of damages. Second, the court must find from the evidence presented that the victim suffered economic damages. Finally, assuming the first two requirements are satisfied, the court can either impose restitution at the sentencing hearing under ORS 137.106(1)(a) or order the defendant to pay restitution in an amount to be established at a restitution hearing within 90 days of the entry of judgment under ORS 137.106(1)(b).

State v. McLaughlin, 243 Or App 214, 219, 258 P3d 1241, adh’d to on recons, 247 Or App 334, 269 P3d 104 (2011) (emphasis added).

The Court of Appeals has found that the restitution statute unambiguously mandates that “evidence of the nature and amount of the damages” be presented beforethe time of sentencing. Id. In McLaughlin, the court found that the state arguably presented evidence of the nature of damages in the guilt-phase portion of the trial, but that it failed to timely present sufficient evidence of the amount of damages. Id. Accordingly, if the state does not present boththe nature and amount of the damages prior to sentencing, the defendant should object; deferral of the determination of restitution for up to 90 days (under ORS 137.106(1)(b)) is not authorized in those circumstances.

When the trial court orders the defendant to pay restitution, but defers making a determination of the specific amount of restitution for up to 90 days pursuant to ORS 137.106(1)(b),11 {Note: 11 As noted, the state’s failure to provide sufficient evidence of the nature and amount of damages does not trigger the provi- sions of ORS 137.106(1)(b). Accordingly, the circumstances in which the specific amount of restitution may be established in a supplemental judgment likely arises when the state submits evidence of the nature and amount of damages and the de- fendant objects to the specific amount of the restitution. In those circumstances, a restitution hearing is necessary to litigate the amount of damages.}

the 90-day deadline is mandatory. State v. Murrell, 242 Or App 178, 183, 255 P3d 574 (2011). That time period may be extended only for “good cause.” Id.; ORS 137.106(1)(b). “Good cause” does not include prosecutorial inadvertence or neglect. Murrell, 242 Or App at 183-84, State v. Biscotti, 219 Or App 296, 301-02, 182 P3d 269 (2008).

If the trial court enters a restitution judgment after the notice of appeal has been filed, immediately contact appellate counsel so that he or she can file a timely amended notice of appeal.

F. Compensatory Fine

Before the trial court may impose a compensatory fine pursuant to ORS 137.101(1),12 {Note: 12 ORS 137.101(1)provides: “Whenever the court imposes a fine as penalty for the commission of a crime resulting in injury for which the person injured by the act constituting the crime has a remedy by civil action … the court may order that the defendant pay any portion of the fine separately to the clerk of the court as compensatory fines in the case. The clerk shall pay over to the injured victim or victims, as directed in the court’s order, moneys paid to the court as compensatory fines under this subsection. This section shall be liberally construed in favor of victims.”}

it must first impose a fine as a penalty under ORS 161.625(1).13 {Note: 13 ORS 161.625(1)provides the limits on fines for felonies:

(1) A sentence to pay a fine for a felony shall be a sentence to pay an amount, fixed by the court, not exceeding: (a) $500,000 for murder or aggravated murder. (b) $375,000 for a Class A felony. (c) $250,000 for a Class B felony. (d) 125,000 for a Class C felony.

SeeState v. Barkley, 108 Or App 756, 761, 817P2d 1328 (1991), aff ’d in part and rev’d in part on other grounds,315 Or 420, 846 P2d 390 (1993)(“A court must first impose a fine ‘as a penalty’ before it may order a defendant to pay any portion of that fine to the clerk of the court in the form of a ‘compensatory fine.’”).

“[T]he compensatory fine statute comes into play only if the injured person has a remedy by civil action for the injuries he or she suffered as a result of the crime.” State v. Haines, 238 Or App 431,

435, 242 P3d 705 (2010).

If the injured person has a remedy by civil action, the next question is whether there is a “victim” eligible to receive the “compensatory fine monies.” Id. The definition of “victim” for purposes of the compensatory fine statute is found in ORS 137.103(4). That statute provides:

(4) ‘Victim’ means:

(a) The person against whom the defendant committed the criminal offense, if the court determines that the person has suffered economic damages as a result of the offense.

(b) Any person not described in paragraph (a) of this subsection whom the court determines has suf- fered economic damages as a result of the defendant’s criminal activities.

Thus, to be a victim under ORS 137.101, the person must have suffered “economic damages.”

Haines,238 Or App at 436. “Economic damages,” in turn, is defined as

[O]bjectively verifiable monetary losses including but not limited to reasonable charges necessarily in- curred for medical, hospital, nursing and rehabilitative services and other health care services, burial and memorial expenses, loss of income and past … impairment of earning capacity, reasonable and necessary expenses incurred for substitute domestic services, recurring loss to an estate, damage to reputation that

is economically verifiable, reasonable and necessarily incurred costs due to loss of use of property and reasonable costs incurred for repair or for replacement of damaged property, whichever is less.

ORS 31.710(2)(a)(referenced in ORS 137.104(2), which provides that, with the exception of future impairment of earning capacity, economic damages has the meaning given in ORS 31.710).

A compensatory fine may therefore be awarded only if the trial court finds that the recipient suffered an economic loss as a result of the defendant’s criminal activities and the economic loss is recoverable by civil action. State v. Barkley, 315 Or 420, 438, 846 P2d 390, cert den, 510 US 387(1993); Haines,238 Or App at 435–36; State v. Donahue, 165 Or App 143, 145, 995 P2d 1202(2000).

G. Miscellaneous Sentencing Issues

If the pre-sentence investigation (PSI) report contains any errors, the corrections should be made on the report and the corrected version should be made part of the trial court record. The PSI in the trial court file is the document that will be relied on in future proceedings, and any corrections made only orally in court will not be reflected.

V. Review of Judgment

It is important to review the final written judgment for accuracy. Many errors can be easily corrected by filing a motion to correct the judgment (seesection V(B)(1) below). Below are some common mistakes that appear on a final judgment.

A. Common Errors

1. Merger Language

When the trial court merges offenses, the resulting judgment should reflect only one offense. Language such as, “the counts shall merge for sentencing purposes” does not achieve a merger. State v. Lopez-Lorenzo, 226 Or App 269, 270, 203 P3d 299, rev den, 346 Or 364 (2009). Rather, that language conveys that there are separate convictions, but that the sentences are ordered to run concurrently. Id. A merged offense should not reflect any indication of a conviction, such as a gridblock classification.

Suggested language includes:

•“The finding of guilt in Count 2 merges with the conviction in Count 1.”

•“The offense in Count 2 shall merge with Count 1.”

2. Indeterminate Sentence

Often the length of imprisonment, when added to the term of post-prison supervision (PPS), exceeds the statutory indeterminate sentence provided in ORS 161.605(particularly when the offense is a class C felony). When this occurs, the trial court must reduce the PPS term to the extent necessary to conform the total sentence length to the statutory maximum. OAR 213-

005-0002(4). However, when the court attempts to do so, it is not uncommon for the resulting judgment to reflect a sentence that impermissibly imposes an indeterminate length of PPS.

OAR 213-005-0005states that “[e]ach judgment of conviction … shall state the length of incarceration and the length of post-prison supervision.” The Court of Appeals has interpreted that provision “to require that a judgment of conviction state a definitesentence.” State v. Stalder,

205 Or App 126, 132, 133 P3d 920 (2006)(emphasis added).

A judgment that attempts to prospectively reduce the length of PPS in reference to an as- yet-undetermined actual length of imprisonment does not comply with OAR 213-005-0005. Language in a judgment such as, “If the length of incarceration plus the length of PPS exceeds the statutory maximum indeterminate sentence, then the length of PPS is reduced to the extent

necessary to conform the total sentence length to the statutory maximum,” impermissibly imposes an indeterminate term of PPS. State v. Mitchell, 236 Or App 248, 252–53, 235 P3d 725 (2010). This is so because the resulting length of PPS may vary depending on how long the defendant ultimately serves in custody. Id.

As an example, in the case of a class C felony, the length of prison, when added to the length of PPS, must equal no more than 60 months. If the presumptive gridblock sentence is a 50-month term of incarceration and a 36-month term of PPS, the resulting judgment must impose 50 months’ imprisonment followed by 10 months’ PPS. SeeMitchell, 236 Or App at 255(“In this case…once [the trial court] selected a 58-month incarceration term – [it] was required to impose only a two-month term of PPS.”).

3. Contempt

Because contempt is not a crime, a finding of contempt cannot be entered in the record as a “conviction.” State v. Lewis, 240 Or App 430, 431, 247 P3d 328 (2011). Likewise, the finding should be memorialized in a “general judgment of contempt,” not in the form of a criminal judgment that states that the defendant was “convicted” and “sentenced.” State v. Reynolds, 239Or App 313, 315–16, 243 P3d 496 (2010). Finally, the defendant is “found in contempt” rather than found guilty of a “crime.” State v. Litscher, 207 Or App 565, 567–68, 142 P3d 549 (2006)(reviewing ORS 161.505and ORS 161.515and concluding that violating a restraining order— punishable as contempt—was not a crime).

One important reason for verifying that the judgment is correct is to avoid future problems. For instance, if the judgment states that contempt is an “unclassified misdemeanor,” which often happens, the contempt may be subsequently and erroneously used in criminal history calculations.

A unitary assessment may only be imposed following conviction for a misdemeanor or a felony, or for a violation. ORS 137.290(unitary assessment shall be imposed upon commission of a crime or a violation). Although a person found in contempt may be sanctioned to jail for up to six months, the Court of Appeals has held that contempt is not a crime. Litscher, 207 Or App at 567–68; State v. Lam, 176 Or App 149, 158, 29 P3d 1206 (2001)(“[C]ontempt is neither civil or criminal. Instead, it is a unique and inherent power of a court to ensure compliance with its orders.”) (internal citations omitted). Accordingly, the trial court is not authorized to impose a unitary assessment upon a finding of contempt.

4. Credit for Time Served

When a judgment includes commitment to the county jail or to Department of Corrections (DOC), the defendant will receive credit for time that he was confined following the arrest for the crime for which sentence was imposed. ORS 137.320; 137.370(2)(a). DOC will compute the time. ORS 137.320(3). Some things to be aware of that might affect the credit calculation are:

•Although the trial court does not have authority to deny credit for time served,14 if the judgment mistakenly states “no credit for time served” (or “NCTS”), DOC will honor the court’s order despite the court’s lack of authority to deny credit. In such a circumstance, a motion to correct the judgment can be filed pursuant to ORS 138.083.

•The defendant may not receive credit if the original indictment is dismissed and the defendant is ultimately convicted, following re-indictment, of an offense that was not listed in the original indictment (although he may receive credit if DOC determines that the conviction is for a lesser-included offense of an original charge). ORS 137.370(2)(a); OAR 291-100-0080(3)(a).

•The defendant will only receive credit if he was held solely for the charges that resulted in the DOC sentence. ORS 137.370(2)(a).

•The defendant will not receive credit for any time spent on house arrest, electronic monitoring, at treatment centers or work release centers. OAR 291-100-0080(3)(g).

5. Judgment Must Dispose of All Counts in Indictment

A notice of appeal cannot be filed from a judgment that does not dispose of all counts in the indictment. A count can be disposed of in a judgment by a finding of guilt, an acquittal, or an order of dismissal.

In some circumstances, counts will need to be severed. For instance, if a jury returns a verdict on some counts, but remains hung on other counts, the trial court can sever the counts, enter a judgment of conviction on the guilty counts, and sever the remaining counts for a new trial (in some circumstances, the state may decided to move to dismiss the hung counts rather than re-try the defendant).

Regardless of how it is accomplished, the judgment must dispose of all counts in the indictment, or the counts must be severed, before the defendant will be able to file a notice of appeal. ORS 137.071(2)(f ).

1. Motion to Correct the Judgment

If you discover an error in the written judgment, you can file a motion to correct the judgment pursuant to ORS 138.083. ORS 138.083(1) provides, in part: “The sentencing court shall retain authority irrespective of any notice of appeal after entry of judgment of conviction to modify

its judgment and sentence to correct any arithmetic or clerical errors or to delete or modify any erroneous term in the judgment.”

If a corrected judgment is entered, notify appellate counsel (if applicable) so that he or she can timely file an amended notice of appeal.

2. Modification of Judgment to Comply with ORS 137.750 and 137.752

ORS 137.754provides that the trial court retains authority to modify a judgment if the judgment failed to comply with the provisions of ORS 137.750and ORS 137.752(seebelow).

ORS 137.750(1) provides that the trial court shallorder in open court as part of the sentence that defendant may be considered for any form of temporary leave from custody, reduction in sentence, or work release, unlessthe trial court finds on the record “substantial and compelling reasons” to order that defendant not be considered for such programs. The correction agency will consider a defendant eligible for programming “only upon order of the sentencing court appearing in the judgment.” ORS 137.750(2).

ORS 137.752(1) provides that the trial court shallorder in open court as part of the sentence that defendant may be considered of any form of alternative sanctions (AIP), unlessthe court finds on the record “substantial and compelling reasons” to order that the defendant not be considered for AIP. The correction agency will consider a defendant eligible for AIP “only upon order of the sentencing court appearing in the judgment.” ORS 137.752(2).

Accordingly, it is imperative that the written judgment reflect the court’s order allowing 936 credits and/or AIP. If the court’s order is erroneously omitted from the judgment, the attorney should file a motion to modify the judgment pursuant to ORS 137.754. If a modified judgment is entered, notify appellate counsel (if applicable) so that he or she can timely file an amended notice of appeal.

VI. Referral of Case to Appellate Counsel

If your client wishes to appeal his conviction and/or sentence and qualifies for court-appointed counsel, contact the Office of Public Defense Services (OPDS). An on-line referral form can be located at www.oregon.gov/OPDS. OPDS can also be contacted by calling 503-378-3349.

A. Timing of Appeal

1. Timely Notice

A notice of appeal must be filed within 30 days of the judgment entry date. ORS 138.071(1).

2. Late Notice

In some circumstances, a notice of appeal may be filed up to 90 days following the judgment entry date. ORS 138.071(5). However, in those circumstances, the defendant must comply with additional statutory requirements.

First, the defendant will have to prove by clear and convincing evidence that he was not personally responsible for the late filing. ORS 138.071(5)(a)(A). A defendant would not be considered personally responsible for a late filing if he asked his trial attorney to file a notice of appeal, or contact OPDS, and the attorney failed to do so. If the defendant did not decide whether he wanted to appeal until the 30-day deadline had passed, however, defendant would be responsible for the late filing.

Second, the defendant must identify a colorable claim of error. ORS 138.071(5)(a)(B). This can be accomplished by identifying any adverse ruling during the trial, such as the denial of a motion for judgment of acquittal or motion to suppress.

B. Appeal from a Guilty or No Contest Plea

A defendant may not file a notice of appeal following entry of a judgment reflecting a plea of guilty or no contest unless the defendant can make a colorable showing that the disposition (1) exceeds the maximum allowable by law, or (2) is unconstitutionally cruel and unusual. ORS 138.050(1).

If a defendant loses a pretrial motion and subsequently enters a guilty or no contest plea, the defendant waives his right to appeal the adverse pretrial ruling (unless the defendant enters a conditional plea, seebelow).

==C. Appeal Following Motion in Arrest of Judgment or Motion for New Trial=

If the defendant filed a timely motion in arrest of judgment or timely motion for a new trial, the motion must be heard and determined within 55 days of the judgment entry date. ORCP 64F(1). The notice of appeal must be filed no later than 30 days following the order entry date. ORS 138.071(2)(a). If the court does not enter an order within 55 days, the motion will be deemeddenied. ORCP 64F(1). In that circumstance, the notice of appeal must be filed no later than 30 days from the date the motion was deemed denied. ORS 138.071(2)(b).

For additional information, see section I(H).

D. Conditional Plea

As discussed in section III, the defendant must specify in writing the pretrial ruling that defendant wishes to reserve for appeal. City of Lake Oswego v. Albright, 222 Or App 117, 120, 193 988 (2008); ORS 135.335(5).

Please refer to section III for additional information.

E. Entry of Subsequent Judgments

The appellate attorney will need to file an amended notice of appeal from each and every amended or supplemental judgment. Accordingly, if a judgment is entered after entry of the original judgment, immediately notify appellate counsel so that he or she may timely file an amended notice of appeal.

F. Designation of Record

Appellate counsel will move to obtain a transcribed copy of the trial and relevant pretrial hearings.

Voir direis not typically transcribed for appeal. If the defendant’s case involves a voir direissue, please indicate to appellate counsel that he or she will need to “designate” voir direat the initiation of the appeal.
