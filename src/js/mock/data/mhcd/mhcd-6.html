Mental Health and Criminal Defense

2010 edition

Dangerousness

I. Introduction

Most topics in this book lend themselves to a familiar legal format: statutes, case law, discussion and practice tips. Dangerousness is a more cumbersome topic because there is neither a discrete body of law nor a singular point of usage. Instead, there are at least four separate bodies of law—one for each statutory scheme that requires a finding of dangerousness. The ways in which the concept can be used are limited only by the creativity of attorneys and judges.

For example, in Multnomah County it is standard practice on any felony sex case for the defendant to undergo a psychosexual evaluation as a prerequisite to plea bargaining. The psychosexual evaluation is not required, suggested or regulated by any criminal statute or case. Moreover, it is not usually a psychological evaluation so much as an attempt to assess the likelihood of recidivism. In other words, it is an assessment of dangerousness.

Every day, throughout the nation, judges and lawyers attempt to peer into the future to divine how the fate of a defendant will affect the future safety of the community. If a judge could know that the defendant will commit a horrific crime in the near future if released from custody, then the decision would be easy. If the judge could know that the defendant would successfully complete treatment or the incident was a one-time event, then the decision would also be easy.

The problem is that such soothsaying is an unattainable pipe dream. What is attainable is something much more limited and much more complicated. It involves false negatives and base rates and actuarial assessments of risk of the sort that insurance companies use. It is critical that defense attorneys understand the more limited and complicated nature of risk assessment because, more often than not, political realities and concern for community safety will cause judges and district attorneys to latch onto any scientific-sounding statement that seems to point toward danger. It is incumbent on the defense attorney to dial back the magnetic draw of statements that sound like predictions and, rather, elucidate the reality of risk assessment: that evaluations are neither as reliable nor as individualized as the courts usually assume them to be.

Addressing the difficulty of predicting future behavior, the U.S. Supreme Court said:

The fact that such a determination is difficult, however, does not mean that it cannot be made. Indeed, prediction of future criminal conduct is an essential element in many of the decisions rendered throughout our criminal justice system. The decision whether to admit a defendant to bail, for instance, must often turn on a judge’s prediction of the defendant’s future conduct. And any sentencing authority must predict a convicted person’s probable future conduct when it engages in the process of determining what punishment to impose. For those sentenced to prison, these same predictions must be made by parole authorities. The task that a Texas jury must perform in answering the statutory question in issue is thus basically no different from the task performed countless times each day throughout the American system of criminal justice.

Jurek v. Texas, 428 US 262 at 274 (1976). Courts will continue to demand and make predictions of future behavior and assessments of risk at almost all stages of a criminal case. Defense attorneys must understand how risks should be assessed so they can make informed arguments to the court. Attorneys should be wary of an expert evaluation that does not carefully state how a conclusion is made, cautioning the reader of a report, for example, about the weaknesses of prediction. And attorneys should be ready to directly confront a pronouncement of dangerousness, whether judicial or clinical, which is based solely on experience and judgment.

Thus, this chapter will begin with a discussion of dangerousness, risk assessment and some of the essential concepts involved. The overview section will at times sound philosophical and at times like a science article. The overview is first because it is essential to everything after it. Following risk assessments will be a more familiar series of sections regarding the statutory schemes involving assessments of dangerousness and what little case law accompanies them.

II. The Current State of Future Dangerousness

A. The Recent Past

The mental health profession has a long and often controversial history of assisting courts in predicting future events. As recently as 1983, the American Psychiatric Association acknowledged in an amicus curiae brief to the U.S. Supreme Court that psychiatrists were typically incorrect in their predictions of future dangerousness in 2 out of every 3 cases. That is, they would have performed better by flipping a coin. Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 US 880 (1983). The defendant in Barefoot had been convicted of murdering a police officer and sentenced to the death penalty. The admissibility of psychiatric testimony during the [death] penalty phase that the defendant would be likely to present a continuing threat to society was raised. The reliability of a psychiatric prediction of future dangerousness was at issue, and the American Psychiatric Association filed a brief for the defendant stating unequivocally that such predictions were not reliable. In a case that still reverberates in the criminal justice system, the Supreme Court rejected the APA’s view:

The suggestion that no psychiatrist’s testimony may be presented with respect to a defendant’s future dangerousness is somewhat like asking us to disinvent the wheel. In the first place, it is contrary to our cases. If the likelihood of a defendant’s committing further crimes is a constitutionally acceptable criterion for imposing the death penalty, which it is, Jurek v Texas, 428 US 262 (1976), and if it is not impossible for even a lay person to sensibly arrive at that conclusion, it makes little sense, if any, to submit that psychiatrists, out of the entire universe of persons who might have an opinion on the issue, would know so little about the subject that they should not be permitted to testify.… The petitioner’s view mirrors the position expressed in the amicus brief of the American Psychiatric Association.… We are not persuaded that such testimony is almost entirely unreliable, and that the factfinder and the adversary system will not be competent to uncover, recognize and take due account of its shortcomings.

Barefootat 897–899. Thus, the Supreme Court found that predictions of future behavior are not so unreliable as to be unconstitutional. The fight over just how unreliable such predictions are continues to this day.

Over the last two decades, there has been a pronounced shift in the field of forensic mental health, from bare clinical judgment to a reliance on actuarial measures still in their infancy. Despite progress in developing protocols to standardize aspects of the assessment process, the science of forensic psychology is still quite limited in accurately predicting which defendants will re-offend. However, with the movement toward the use of more reliable (though depersonalized) instruments has come a paradigmatic change in the way the psychological and psychiatric fields view their task where the court wants to peer into the future. Most forensic experts will now say that what they are doing should in no way be confused with predicting the future. What they are doing is assessing risk. It makes a difference, primarily because the more mundane sounding “risk assessment” is rife with the sort of caution that accompanies any scientifically rigorous statement.

The psychiatrist in Barefoot predicted with one hundred percent certainty, based solely on his experience and clinical judgment, that the defendant would be a continuing threat through violent acts. No current, respected expert would be one hundred percent certain about events that have not yet occurred. No current, respected expert would rely solely on clinical judgment assessment tools, actuarial instruments or even a structured evaluation.

One of the biggest problems with using clinical judgment alone is that the research has shown experts to be just as susceptible to the kinds of assumptions and cultural biases that nonexperts are. Thus, some variables which have little predictive value are given great weight because it seems like they should be predictive. Other variables which have high predictive value are given little weight because it seems like they are irrelevant. Such assumptions are so entrenched that most experts, in the absence of structured evaluations or empirical testing, will rely on them under the rubric of instinct or experience even if they are aware of the dearth of supporting research. For example, “victim empathy” or “denial” of the event intuitively seems like powerful evidence but clinical research has not demonstrated strong predictive power.

Cultural differences can also lead to errors in prediction. Even highly trained clinicians are more likely to find risk and danger when they are feeling less understanding and empathetic toward the defendant. Levinson and Ramsey, cited in Psychological Evaluations for the Courts, Melton, et al. (1996) at 279. Despite their best efforts, forensic experts are not immune to the natural tendency to understand and empathize more with people who have the same cultural assumptions. For example, American culture is strongly biased toward individual identity and personal property ownership. In most Asian cultures, however, personal identity is very much wrapped up in the identity of the family and the community. Thus, shame and honor are critical components of most Asian cultures but not American culture. It is easy to envision how an American psychologist might be tone deaf to a statement or gesture which is meant only to deflect the appearance of dishonor or shame.

B. A Definition of Terms

One of the biggest problems in the area of risk assessment is that far too often there are no definitions of the most basic terms. If forensic experts are expected to assist in labeling a person as “dangerous,” as is required by the dangerous offender statute, for example, and courts are going to rely on such assistance, then all parties should be on the same page as to the meaning of dangerousness. Is the defendant dangerous if she is likely to commit a serious crime of violence in the near future? What if the defendant may have fleeting thoughts of committing a crime of violence in the next ten years? And what do we mean by “likely to,” “may have,” “serious crime,” “near future,” “committing” and “violence”?

Part of the difficulty is that the word “dangerousness” is used as if it were a disorder or a psychiatric term of art. It appears to imply that the defendant has a psychological trait or tendency toward violence. Yet there is no diagnosis of “dangerousness” in the most recent version of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV-TR) or any other widely used diagnostic manual. Nor is “dangerousness” a concept which follows from or attaches to any disorder in the DSM-IV-TR. Thus, if a diagnostic label is placed on the defendant, it would be done outside the boundaries of the DSM-IV-TR. What this means is that there is no accepted set of definitions or terminology that would allow everyone to be assured that any word or phrase means what they think it means.

The inconsistency problem is widespread in both the psychological and legal arenas. For example, the term “violence” is often used in psychological testing in a much broader sense than one would expect in the legal system. The SVR-20 (see Chapter 8, “Clinical Assessment and Testing”), one of the most prominent sex offender risk assessment tools, considers an obscene letter to be “sexual violence.” Fundamentals of Forensic Practice, Rogers and Shuman (2005). The instrument defines sexual violence as “actual, attempted or threatened sexual contact with a person who is non-consenting.” Might attempted sexual contact even include a rebuffed kiss on a first date? Most assessment tools view violence as a broader and more elastic concept than the courts, which would presumably lead to a basic tension if the legal system didn’t have it’s own definitional issues.

In the legal system the problem is both vagueness and inconsistency. For example, the cases citing the dangerous offender statute simply repeat the statutory mantra that the judge must find that the “defendant is dangerous” and “that because of the dangerousness of the defendant an extended period of confinement is required for the protection of the public.” See, for example, State v. Huntley, 302 Or 418 (1986), and State v. Odoms, 117 Or App 1 (1992). Nowhere is the judge or forensic expert given any guidance as to how one might come to these determinations. The U.S. Supreme Court has indicated that something as minor as an attempted shoplifting may be considered dangerous. Jones v. United States, 463 US 354 (1983). Oregon courts imply that more is required, but both the legislature and courts have largely been silent in defining dangerousness outside the unique realm of civil commitments.

The actual definition of any term is much less important than the idea that everyone needs to be using the same definition in any particular case. If the judge, the attorneys and the clinician all understand that the term “violence” includes obscene letters and shopliftings, then everyone can adjust their arguments and decisions accordingly. But if the judge and the attorneys assume “violence” means an incident which causes serious physical injury but the expert who is assessing the risk of future violence is using the broadest possible definition, then the case is ripe for injustice and confusion.

C. Assessment of Risk

Most forensic experts will be very uncomfortable with the perception that they are predicting future events. At best, an expert—assuming a certain set of background variables and a consistent environment—can state with a certain percent accuracy that there is a certain percent risk the defendant will commit a certain sort of crime, within a certain period of time. Obviously, this is significantly less satisfying for a court than the old-school expert who would say with certainty that the defendant is going to commit another similar crime within 18 months if released. Unfortunately for those trying to peer into the future, the choice is between “satisfying but wildly unreliable” or “less satisfying but significantly more accurate.” In any case, it is important for the defense attorney to understand what the expert is saying about the following components of risk assessment:

Imminence of the risked event (within 6 weeks or 60 years)
Magnitude of the risked event (obscene letter or stranger rape)
Likelihood that the event will come about (5 percent likely or 90 percent likely)
Confidence with which the assessment of risk is made (51 percent sure or 99 percent sure)
Frequency of the risked event (1 time or many).

Discussion of the examiner’s decision-making should be explored and compared with the current state of clinical research. In arriving at clinical hypotheses, diagnostic impressions, recidivism risks and treatment recommendations, the role of the evaluator goes beyond merely comparing defendants on the criteria of applied tests and diagnostic tools. If risk prediction were this simple, a doctoral-level psychologist or medical doctor would not be required to instruct the court on such matters.

Clinical prediction of future dangerousness is plagued by several factors, first and foremost of which is that the prediction of future behavior is always, at best, a reasonable guess.

Error and Base Rates

Actuarial measures such as the Static-99 and VRAG (see “Clinical Assessment and Testing,” Chapter 8) provide descriptive labels such as high, medium and low risk. However, such measures may also include percentages of individuals who did or did not re-offend. Inclusion of such statistics permits legal counsel to address error rates. In other words, the attorney can inform the court that while certain scores on measures may correlate to a certain outcome, in a majority of cases they are unlikely to predict future behavior. In addition, it is important to convey the reliability and validity of the assessment which led to the prediction.

In any clinical or medical prediction or diagnosis there are four possibilities. It is easiest to conceptualize with a medical diagnosis. For example, if a doctor is trying to diagnose whether a patient is having a heart attack, there are two options for the doctor (having and not having a heart attack), and in each of the two options the doctor could be correct or incorrect. This leaves us with four possibilities:

1. A true positivein which the doctor makes a correct diagnosis that the person is having a heart attack.

2. A false positivein which the doctor believes the person to be having a heart attack but it turns out to be indigestion.

3. A false negativein which the doctor believes that the person has indigestion but the person is actually having a heart attack.

4. A true negativein which the doctor makes a correct diagnosis that the person is not having a heart attack.

This is important because lay people tend to focus on the true positives, entirely ignoring the other boxes. In this case, when we ask how often the doctor is correct, we want to know how often he correctly identified a heart attack but also how often he identified a heart attack which was actually something else entirely. A doctor who diagnosed every single patient with a heart attack would be correct every time the patient was actually having a heart attack but he would have a ton of false positives.

Base Rates

For the preceding information to be valuable we need to know one more piece of information: how many people were in each group. If it turns out that there were far more people in the false identifications, then it matters little how often the doctor was accurate. This is because the less common an event the more difficult it is to predict: If I predict a plane crash every day, I will correctly identify every day with a plane crash but I will be wrong the vast majority of the time. If I predict no plane crashes every day, I will be correct the vast majority of the time but I will never correctly predict a plane crash.

Imagine instead of heart attacks and plane crashes the district attorney is using a forensic expert with a particular violence prediction tool to predict that the defendant will commit another violent crime. Assume, for the purposes of this hypothetical, a highly accurate assessment which is able to correctly identify 80 percent of the people who will reoffend.

According to the assessment, your client has a profile of a “very violent offender”—a person who will commit another violent crime. As mentioned earlier, one should not assume that “very violent” is referring to the magnitude of the crime or that “violence” means that someone will be hurt. But assuming the definitions have been appropriately limited and the question of whether the defendant actually fits the profile has been exhausted, it seems pretty bad on first glance. It seems like there is an 80 percent likelihood that your client will commit another violent crime. However, while it may or may not be bad news, there is most certainly not an 80 percent likelihood that your client will commit another violent crime.

In order to know anything, you need more information. What you need is the base rate—the actual recidivism rate for people who have committed a violent crime.

Assume that out of a population of 1,000,000 violent offenders the recidivism rate is 9 percent. That is, 90,000 violent offenders out of 1,000,000 would be expected to commit another violent crime. Our absurdly valid instrument was able to identify 80 percent of the people who will recidivate. That means that 80 percent (or 72,000) of the 90,000 will be identified. But it also means the instrument will give us an incorrect prediction of future violence 20 percent of the time (20 percent of the time a person who will not reoffend is identified as a person who willreoffend).

The tricky part is that the false negatives are taken from the group of people who won’t reoffend—the 91 percent, not the 9 percent. The amount of people who will not reoffend is 91 percent of 1 million or 910,000 people. Twenty percent of 910,000 is 182,000 people who will be incorrectly identified as a person who will recidivate.

What should be immediately obvious is that a lot more people are wrongly predicted to commit future violence than are people correctly predicted to commit future violence. Stated another way, your positively identified client is more likely to be falsely accused than correctly identified.

To get the probability of a correct prediction in a particular case we divide the true positives (72) by the total number of positive hits (72,000 + 182,000 = 254,000). Then, 72 divided by 254 equals a probability of .2835 or 28 percent—28 percent, not 80 percent. Intuitively, it seems like a test which is correct 80 percent of the time would yield a predictive probability of 80 percent in a particular case. But it depends entirely on the frequency of the predicted event. In this case, a frequency of 9 percent means that our client is very likely to be wrongly identified.

The sensitivityof a particular test is how good the test is at picking out defendants who will recidivate. It is simply the percentage of true positives. Specificityis the ability of the test to pick out defendants who do not go on to commit future acts of violence.

Although traditional clinical opinion is still widely used for conducting sex offender risk assessments, it is easily the least accurate method. Studies going back to the 1950s show that, left to their own devices, forensic examiners fearing false negatives (i.e., failure to identify a serious re-offender) tend to overestimate risk. They tend toward predicting a plane crash every day because they do not want to miss the plane crash when it happens. The power of prediction lies in systematic comparison.

III. Dangerous Offender

A. The Statute

ORS 161.725 allows a judge to sentence a person to thirty years in prison if:

1) the court finds that because of the dangerousness of the defendant an extended period of custody is required for the protection of the public

2) the conviction is either (a) an A felony or (b) a B or C felony that endangered the life or safety of another and there is at least one past conviction that endangered the life or safety of another

3) the defendant is suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another.

The dangerous offender statute is infuriatingly vague, eluding attempts to pin it down at every turn. What does “dangerousness” mean? Is it a label, a diagnosis, a prediction or a superfluous cipher given form by other parts of the statute like “required for the protection of the public,” “endangered the life or safety of another” and “severe personality disorder”? What does it mean to endanger the safety of another? By driving recklessly or by actually inflicting serious violence or something in between? What is a severe personality disorder?

Personality disorders are identified disorders in the mental health field but there is no modifier for severity. Nor is there an identifiable subset of personality disorders that “indicate a propensity” toward crimes of any sort. The statute is an odd mixture of clinical, legal and novel terms. Unfortunately, the courts have not helped to clarify either the individual terms or the spirit of the compilation. Instead, the courts have left each trial court free to interpret for themselves what the terms might mean.

This section will attempt to tease out the absurdities and encourage defense attorneys to challenge the court. Unfortunately, this section cannot be a beacon of guidance because there is little light to be found in this area of the law.

B. “Dangerousness”

The dangerous offender statute firstrequires the sentencing court to “find that because of the ‘dangerousness of the defendant’ an extended period of incarceration is required.” State v. Huntley, 302 Or 418 at 428 (1986) (quoting ORS 161.725(1)). The statute does not provide clear criteria for “dangerousness.” Id. In effect, this determination seems to be largely bound up with the determination of a “severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another,” discussed below. As such, courts look to see if the defendant has committed violent acts and looks at his or her criminal history—including nonviolent criminal history.

Huntley

The Huntleycase law is instructive. First, “at the presentence hearing, in order to make the first finding of dangerousness, the judge must find the defendant has engaged in dangerous behavior that involves a crime of violence against a person.” Id. at 429. Thus, the statute is applicable only to defendants whose mental or emotional disorder creates a propensity to commit violent, as opposed to nonviolent but habitual, criminal acts. State v. Moen, 309 Or 45, 74 (1990).

Second, the Huntleycourt held, “the legislature in utilizing the statutory language referring to dangerousness […] meant to include persons with antisocial personality disorders who have committed violent acts against other persons.” Huntley at 432.

Third, a person’s criminal history, whether nonviolent or remote in time, is relevant in assessing future dangerousness. Moen at 74 (interpreting Huntley).

For example, in Huntley the reviewing court noted acts by the defendant that “certainly qualify as dangerous behavior.” Huntley at 429. In particular, the court found that the “[d]efendant previously had been convicted of sodomy in the first degree committed against a 10-year-old female victim who had been riding her bicycle. He forced that victim down a bank, pushed her to the ground and subjected her to forceful anal intercourse.... These acts certainly qualify as dangerous behavior.”

The crime at issue in Huntley was “sodomy in the first degree committed against an 11-year-old female victim, defendant’s niece. Defendant enticed the child to go to a secluded area. When defendant grabbed the victim from behind and she yelled for help, he placed his hand over her nose and mouth and threatened to cause her to pass out. He then forced her to the ground, removed her pants and underpants, and repeatedly subjected her to forceful anal intercourse.” Id. at 429.

Unfortunately, the crimes in Huntley are so beyond the pale that they hardly give guidance to trial courts in determining what “violence” or “endangering the safety of another” means.

In State v. Smith, 66 Or App 374 (1984), the court sentenced the defendant as a dangerous offender after he was found guilty of attempted murder, first-degree assault and first-degree robbery. The Court of Appeals affirmed the sentence after finding that, despite admitting a potentially inadmissible expert opinion (as being barred by Miranda and Estelle v. Smith, 451 US 454 (1981)), the enhanced sentence was “overwhelmingly supported by the record, even without [the] report.” Smith at 385. The court found:

Defendant perpetrated a violent attack on the victim. After spraying Ms. Hutt with mace, he threatened her with a gun. He forced her to lie face-down on the floor and tied her hands behind her back. Although the record is unclear as to whether defendant used a gun or a hammer, he beat her severely. She was in critical condition when she arrived at the hospital. The jury concluded that, in fact, defendant had attempted to murder her. In addition, he has at least six felony convictions, including another violent assault and robbery on a woman and an assault with intent to kill a police officer.

The trial court concluded at the sentencing proceeding that, even if Oregon statutes do not require a psychiatric report, “this case would stand clearly as sufficiently being loaded with evidentiary matters from which the court could make its own conclusion that Mr. Smith is a dangerous offender.” Given the violent circumstances surrounding the instant offenses for which defendant was convicted and his lengthy history of criminal activity, the court had ample evidence before it from which to conclude that defendant is a dangerous offender entirely aside from the psychiatric evidence.

Id. It appears that the “dangerousness” determination boils down to almost a gut-level decision by the sentencing judge. The standard could be termed a “reasonable judge” standard. The sentencing judge looks at the defendant’s criminal acts, both in the present case and in past cases, and looks to see if those acts are violent or otherwise “dangerous.” Neither Huntley nor Smithare particularly sympathetic cases, but the appellate court does not limit the scope of the dangerous offender statute to such cases. The door would seem to be wide open for a zealous prosecutor to significantly lower the bar.

C. “Required for the Protection of the Public"

“The second finding the judge must make is that because of the dangerousness of the defendant, an extended period of confined correction treatment or custody is required for the protection of the public. This finding implies a prediction by the court that the defendant is likely to engage in dangerous behavior and constitutes a future threat of injury or death to other persons. This prediction of future behavior and potential or propensity for inflicting serious bodily harm on another overlaps to some extent the fourth finding the judge must make, that the defendant is suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity.” Huntley at 429.

As with the element above, the case law provides little guidance for determining when extended custody is “required.” Again, there is a sort of gut-level, reasonable-judge standard with the various elements bound up together. However, the Huntley court does seem to indicate that the court must be able to identify a “potential or propensity for inflicting serious bodily harm on another.” Serious bodily harm is a threshold not found in the statute or indicated even by the other part of the same sentence. But defense attorneys should argue that Huntley stands, in part, for the proposition that custody is only “required” where the State has established that the defendant has a propensity toward inflicting serious bodily harm.

In State v. Odoms, 117 Or App 1 (1992), the defendant sought review in the Oregon Supreme Court of a trial court’s decision that sentenced him as a dangerous offender after a finding of guilt for first-degree kidnapping, first-degree rape, first-degree sodomy and third-degree robbery. The defendant challenged, inter alia, his dangerous offender sentence as not supported by the record or by the clinician’s evaluation. The Oregon Supreme Court affirmed the sentence on the basis that the sentencing court had the authority to make its own determinations. This was true even though the sentencing court’s determinations were made without any obvious criteria beyond general findings. Id. at 7–8. The Odomscourt quoted the sentencing court’s satisfactory findings:

In my opinion, you’re an extremely dangerous offender, you’re a vicious predator, you exploit women, you don’t have any remorse for the behavior, you don’t have any sense of guilt, you don’t have any concern about the dignity of other human beings, you’re a danger to any susceptible woman in this community, and you clearly are a dangerous offender. You suffer from—in the Court’s opinion, I’ll find beyond a reasonable doubt that you suffer a personality disorder that indicates a likelihood and propensity toward future criminal activity of a dangerous nature. And there’s no way that you should be released from custody.

Id. at 7 (sentencing statement by trial court). And in its judgment, the sentencing court found:

[T]he defendant is a Dangerous Offender suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity, and that because of the dangerousness of the defendant an extended period of incarceration is required for the protection of the public.

Id. at 7–8. “Those findings, taken together, are sufficient to support the conclusion that defendant is a dangerous offender.” Id.at 8.

In Huntley, the [sentencing] court addressed the issue whether defendant needed to be isolated for the protection of society. [The judge] stated:

[T]his case is simply beyond any effort to deal with […] outside of a prison setting. […] [R]ehabilitation can happen […] inside and that’s up to you. Frankly though my concern here is protection of society, specifically protection of minor females.

The history that I am dealing with speaks with a loud voice. There are two prior convictions. One for Sodomy in the First Degree, one for Rape in the First Degree. Both involve female victims. One was 10, one was 16. The circumstances of those cases although perhaps more aggravated in some respects, are basically the same sorts of things that occurred here.

There are a number of things that dictate to my mind that a maximum sentence is appropriate and to my mind the question is, what maximum sentence the law allows. Things that to my mind mandate that kind of sentence as a protection of society are the degree of the abuse or threatened use of force, the age of the victim, the past history that I referred to, and […] the fact that you were on temporary leave status at the time that this occurred.

Huntley at 434–435. Thus, the Huntleysentencing court’s determination of the need for extended incarceration was largely caught up in the judge’s reaction to the overall dangerousness and reprehensibility of the crime.

While a sentencing court must find that “because of the dangerousness of the defendant, an extended period of confined correction treatment or custody is required for the protection of the public,” Id. at 429, it can do so without meeting any criteria beyond its own reasonable determination, inclinations and view of the crime.

D. “A Felony that Seriously Endangered the Life or Safety of Another” (or a Class A Felony)

The court must determine “the defendant is being sentenced for a Class A felony or that the defendant is being sentenced for a felony that seriously endangered the life or safety of another and has been previously convicted of a felony not related to the instant crime as a single criminal episode.” Huntley at 429–430.

If the sentencing court has just found the defendant to be guilty of a Class A felony, this element is clearly satisfied. The element is satisfied by a Class A felony even where the defendant was found guilty as an accomplice and “without regard to what he actually did during commission of the class A felony.” State v. Mastne, 91 Or App 31 at 35 (1988).

Less clear is what it means that a felony “seriously endangered the life or safety of another.” The Oregon Supreme Court has seemed to indicate that the element is intertwined with the initial determination of dangerousness as well as the propensity determination. The court noted that “the statute is a dangerous offender statute, not an habitual offender statute, and it would make no sense for a court to find that someone has engaged in dangerous conduct but has a propensity in the future to continue only non-dangerous criminal activity such as committing forgery.” Id.at 430. Thus a court must find that “the defendant has engaged in dangerous behavior that involves a crime of violence against a person.” Id. at 429. As such, the statute is applicable only to defendants who commit violent, as opposed to nonviolent but habitual, criminal acts. State v. Moen, 309 Or 45, 74 (1990).

E. “Severe Personality Disorder”

A court must find that the defendant suffers from “a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another.” ORS 161.725(1). When making this determination, a court must consider “a presentence investigation and an examination by a psychiatrist or psychologist.” ORS 161.735. However, the conclusions of that examination are not dispositive. Huntley at 435; State v. Trice, 146 Or App 15 (1997).

Instead, this determination is left ultimately to the judge who can make it in the face of evaluations indicating that the defendant does not suffer from a personality disorder. Odoms at 7 (“Although the sentencing court must consider the report, it is not bound by the expert’s evaluation”). As such, “if all the report discloses is that the defendant was uncooperative and that a psychiatric analysis could not be made, that does not render the report insufficient for purposes of ORS 161.735.” Id. (citing State v. Pryor, 96 Or App 181, 772 P2d 431, rev den 308 Or 158 (1989). Thus, a concurring clinician’s evaluation is not required to support the sentencing judge’s findings.

In Huntley, the court’s evaluator found that the defendant was not suffering from a personality disorder. Despite this evaluation, the sentencing court stated that “although [the judge] respected Dr. Holland’s opinion, he simply disagreed with it and felt that, as the trial judge, he was free to make his own determination after reviewing the psychiatric evidence, presentence report and other evidence available”; the reviewing court agreed. Huntley at 435.

Whether clinically supported or not, the court must find that the defendant suffers from three things: 1) a personality disorder, 2) of a severe nature, 3) indicating a propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another. ORS 161.725(1(a)–(c).

1. “Personality Disorder”

First, “the statute refers to a mentally abnormal person and not to a dangerous normal person.” Huntley at 430. The sentencing court mustfind that the defendant has a “personality disorder.” ORS 161.725(a),(b) or (c); see also Huntley at 423.

“[T]he phrase ‘personality disorder’ is a term of art as to which the [Diagnostic and Statistic Manual of Mental Disorders] [is] the definitive source [;] this court has referred to the DSM for guidance in cases involving individuals with mental diseases or defects.” Mueller v. Psychiatric Sec. Review Bd., 325 Or 332, 339–340 (1997); Huntley at 431–36. However, as mentioned above, a sentencing court “is not bound by the conclusions of any [expert] but is required by statute to make his or her own findings on that issue.” Huntley at 435.

Thus, a personality disorder should be found using the DSM but may be found solely on the sentencing judge’s layperson application of the DSM. Huntley at 432–434 (discussing and setting forth DSM-III criteria for antisocial personality disorder); and id. at 435 (applying DSM criteria to Huntleyfacts).

However, the current version of the DSM (DSM-IV-TR) specifically warns against such use:

DSM-IV is a classification of mental disorders that was developed for use in clinical, educational, and research settings. The diagnostic categories, criteria, and textual descriptions are meant to be employed by individuals with appropriate clinical training and experience in diagnosis. It is important that DSM-IV not be applied mechanically by untrained individuals. The specific diagnostic criteria included in DSM-IV are meant to serve as guidelines to be informed by clinical judgment and are not meant to be used in a cookbook fashion.

Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders,Fourth Edition Text Revision, p xxxii, American Psychiatric Association (2000). Counsel should continue to challenge the proposition that a judge can make lay findings that a personality disorder exists. If “personality disorder” is a term of art, then it must be used in a manner consistent with the DSM, the definitive source. Because the DSM-IV-TR specifically discourages both lay use and cookbook findings, there is no method by which the courts could use the DSM diagnostic criteria without the assistance of a trained professional.

Unfortunately, the appellate law is inconsistent as to the necessity of finding an actual personality disorder at all. In Nickell, which was argued and handed down on the same day as Huntley, the defendant was found guilty of attempted murder and sentenced as a dangerous offender. State v. Nickell, 302 Or 439 (1986). The sentencing judge found that the defendant had a “personality disorder” on evidence that he had psychosis and did not discuss or find facts specifically relating to a personality disorder as set forth in the DSM. See Nickell at 444–445.

In State v. Odoms, the Oregon Court of Appeals, in reviewing a dangerous offender sentence of a kidnapping, rape, sodomy, compelling and promoting prostitution, robbery, and vehicular offense case, found that the sentencing judge was justified in imposing a dangerous offender sentence after finding, in part, that the defendant had a “personality disorder” based on a decade-old evaluation and the defendant’s criminal history. Odoms at 7. At no point did the judge allude to the existence of any of the DSM diagnostic criteria, nor did the court’s “expert” actually examine the defendant. Such lax practices should be challenged pursuant to the Huntley court’s determination that the dangerous offender statute avoids Due Process Clause vagueness problems because it requires, at a minimum, findings in accordance with the DSM criteria for a personality disorder.

The general diagnostic criteria for a personality disorder are as follows:

A.An enduring pattern of inner experience and behavior that deviates markedly from the expectations of the individual’s culture. This pattern is manifested in two (or more) of the following areas:

  1. Cognition (i.e., ways of perceiving and interpreting self, other people, and events)
  2. Affectivity (i.e., the range, intensity, lability, and appropriateness of emotional response)
  3. Interpersonal functioning
  4. Impulse control

B. The enduring pattern is inflexible and pervasive across a broad range of personal and social situations.

C. The enduring pattern leads to clinically significant distress or impairment in social, occupational, or other important areas of functioning.

D. The pattern is stable and of long duration, and its onset can be traced back at least to adolescence or early adulthood.

E. The enduring pattern is not better accounted for as a manifestation or consequence of another mental disorder.

F. The enduring pattern is not due to the direct physiological effects of a substance (e.g., a drug of abuse, a medication) or a general medical condition (e.g., head trauma)

DMS-IV-TR, p. 689. In addition to the general criteria, further findings are required for a specific disorder. For example, the diagnostic criteria for borderline personality disorder are as follows:

A. A pervasive pattern of instability of interpersonal relationships, self image, and affects, and marked impulsivity beginning by early adulthood and present in a variety of contexts, as indicated by five (or more) of the following:

(1) Frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment. Note: Do not include suicidal or self-mutilating behavior covered in Criterion 5.

(2) A pattern of unstable and intense interpersonal relationships characterized by alternating between extremes of idealization and devaluation.

(3) Identity disturbance: markedly and persistently unstable self-image or sense of self.

(4) Impulsivity in at least two areas that are potentially self-damaging (e.g., spending, sex, substance abuse, reckless driving, binge eating). Note: Do not include suicidal or self-mutilating behavior.

(5) Recurrent suicidal behavior, gestures, or threats or self-mutilating behavior.

(6) Affective instability due to a marked reactivity of mood (e.g., intense episodic dysphoria, irritability, or anxiety usually lasting a few hours and only rarely more than a few days).

(7) Chronic feelings of emptiness.

(8) Inappropriate, intense anger or difficulty controlling anger (e.g., frequent displays of temper, constant anger, recurrent physical fights).

(9) Transient, stress related paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms.

DSM-IV-TR, p. 711.

2. “Severe Personality Disorder”

The sentencing court must find that the defendant’s personality disorder is a “severe” one. ORS 161.725(1). Presumably, a merely nonsevere personality disorder would not be sufficient. Unfortunately, neither the statute nor the DSM provide criteria which differentiate severe and nonsevere personality disorders. In fact, one criticism of the DSM has been that it does not view diagnoses on a continuum of severity. As such, in State v. Smith the defendant argued, “[T]he statute is unconstitutionally vague in that it does not define [ ] a ‘severe personality disorder.’” State v. Smith,66 Or App 374, 383–384 (1984). The Smith court resolved this claim with a fairly summary discussion:

When a statute is attacked as vague, it is our obligation to determine whether the statute can be saved by a judicial interpretation that gives it the required definiteness. State v. Robertson, 293 Or 402, 411, 649 P2d 569 (1982). The meaning of the statute here is easily discernible by reading its plain language. “Severe” is generally defined as: “Of a greater degree or an undesirable or harmful extent: marked, serious.”

Id. So it is not vague because “severe” means greater than not severe. Not to put too fine a point on it, gradations of personality disorder neither exist nor do they make sense within the structure of the DSM-IV-TR.

The Huntleycourt held that “the legislature in utilizing the statutory language referring to dangerousness and persons with severe personality disorders meant to include persons with antisocial personality disorders who have committed violent acts against other persons.” Huntley at 432 (emphasis added). Additionally, in dicta, the Huntley court found that the DSM “also describes other personality disorders that may be relevant for application in other cases. The manual presently lists the following personality disorders: Schizoid, Avoidant, Antisocial, Passive-Aggressive and Borderline Personality Disorders.” Id. at 434.

Thus, Huntleyseems to say that a defendant determined to suffer from Antisocial Personality Disorder and who has committed violent acts against other persons is per se suffering from a severe personality disorder. This per se severity rule could be expanded to include defendants found to have the above listed personality disorders as well. Of course, such a rule would simply mean that the modifier “severe” is superfluous.

3. “Indicating a Propensity Toward Crimes that Seriously Endanger the Life or Safety of Another”

The sentencing court must find that the defendant’s severe personality disorder indicates “propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another.” ORS 161.725(1). As above, the “propensity” element is not defined or given defining criteria by statute. As such, in State v. Smith the defendant argued “the statute is unconstitutionally vague in that it does not define “a propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another.” Smith at 384.

Much like the above severity discussion, the Smith court resolved this issue in a summary manner:

When a statute is attacked as vague, it is our obligation to determine whether the statute can be saved by a judicial interpretation that gives it the required definiteness. State v. Robertson, 293 Or 402, 411, 649 P2d 569 (1982). The meaning of the statute here is easily discernible by reading its plain language. […] ‘Propensity’ is ‘a natural inclination.’ Webster’s Third New International Dictionary (1976).”

Smithat 383–384. This construction is not particularly helpful; however, the case law is a bit more instructive.

First, even though it is difficult—if not impossible—to accurately predict future dangerousness, the dangerous offender statute’s “propensity” element does not offend federal due process guarantees. “The fact that a penal statute is based on a prediction of future dangerousness does not render the statute violative of the Due Process Clause.” State v. Smith, 66 Or App at 383 (citing Jurek v. Texas, 428 US 262 (US 1976).

In Jurek, a Texas statute that provided for the death penalty if a jury determined, inter alia, that a defendant would again commit criminal acts of violence and thus constitute a continuing threat to society, was facing a due process challenge alleging that the statute was so vague as to be meaningless. The Jurek court held:

It is, of course, not easy to predict future behavior. The fact that such a determination is difficult, however, does not mean that it cannot be made. Indeed, prediction of future criminal conduct is an essential element in many of the decisions rendered throughout our criminal justice system. The decision whether to admit a defendant to bail, for instance, must often turn on a judge’s prediction of the defendant’s future conduct. And any sentencing authority must predict a convicted person’s probable future conduct when it engages in the process of determining what punishment to impose. For those sentenced to prison, these same predictions must be made by parole authorities. The task that a Texas jury must perform in answering the statutory question in issue is thus basically no different from the task performed countless times each day throughout the American system of criminal justice.

Jurek at 274–276. Thus the “propensity” element is not in violation of due process in that it requires a prediction of the future (which is usually considered impossible).

Second, a defendant’s criminal history—whether violent or nonviolent, recent or distant—is relevant in assessing future dangerousness. State v. Moen, 309 Or 45, 74 (1990) (citing Huntley as “specifically reject[ed] the suggestion that a person’s criminal history, whether nonviolent or remote in time, is irrelevant in assessing future dangerousness”); also State v. Smith, 310 Or 1, 29 (Or 1990).

Third, a finding that the defendant suffered from “antisocial personality disorder”—as defined by elements given in the DSM and, thus, is supported by a fairly substantial history of past violent and/or criminal acts—satisfies the “propensity” element of the statute. Huntley at 435–436. In Huntley, the sentencing court recited the past criminal or unlawful history of the defendant:

This defendant before age 15 had a history of four of the criteria. The manual states that there must be “at least three.” This defendant had been (1) expelled from Coquille High School for misbehavior, (2) arrested, referred to juvenile court because of delinquent behavior and committed to McLaren School for Boys, not once but twice, (3) stole an automobile and committed a burglary, and (4) chronically violated rules at his home in that he was taken from the home and failed to adjust in foster care. As to his adult behavior, he (1) failed to accept social norms with respect to lawful behavior in that he was arrested on multiple occasions resulting in at least two felony convictions; (2) demonstrated irritability and aggressiveness, physically assaulting various victims; he was convicted of menacing and assault in the third degree; (3) demonstrated a failure to plan ahead and acted impulsively in that he absconded from supervision; (4) demonstrated recklessness by driving while intoxicated and in another incident attempted to elude a police officer; and (5) engaged in a pattern of continuous antisocial behavior in which he violated the rights of others, particularly the rights of young girls: he forced a 10-year-old to suffer the indignity of anal sodomy at knifepoint and raped a 16-year-old after using a weapon with threats of death. Dr. Holland failed to set forth or comment on these facts and their application to a diagnosis under DSM-III. The trial judge was fully justified to reject the psychiatrist’s conclusion and to properly sum up defendant as a dangerous offender requiring an “extended period of confinement for the protection of society who had been convicted of Class A felonies and who is suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity” as those terms have been interpreted in this opinion.

Huntley at 435–436. However, it is unclear if other “personality disorders” considered to be “severe,” which are not defined in the DSM via substantial past history of criminal or violent acts, would also automatically satisfy this “propensity” element.

Lastly, the Oregon Supreme Court recently found that the following jury instruction is sufficient to instruct the jury on the law of this element:

The second question asked by the law is, is there a probability, meaning is it more likely than not, that the Defendant would commit criminal acts of violence that would constitute a continuing threat to society. “Criminal acts of violence” refers to acts characterized by the application of force which has the potential for inflicting bodily injury on another person or the overt threat of such force.

State v. Reyes-Camarena, 330 Or 431, 441–442 (2000). Thus, the “propensity” for future dangerousness appears to largely be a reasonable common-sense decision.

F. Application of Apprendi/Blakely

The state need not allege dangerous offender sentencing facts in its charging instrument. State v. Heilman, 339 Or 661, 671 (2005); State v. Cox, 337 Or 477, 499, 98 P3d 1103 (2004); and cf. State v. Terry, 333 Or 163, 185, 37 P3d 157 (2001).

However, dangerous offender factual elements should be found by a jury. The Oregon Supreme Court has held that:

[T]he imposition of a 30-year dangerous offender sentence on a defendant based on a finding that the defendant suffered from a “severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward crimes that seriously endanger the life or safety of another,” ORS 161.725, violated the rule of law announced in Apprendi and Blakelythat “other than the fact of a prior conviction, any fact that increases the penalty for a crime beyond the prescribed statutory maximum must be submitted to a jury, and proved beyond a reasonable doubt.

See also Apprendi, 530 US at 490; State v. Williams, 197 Or App 21, 23 (2005); State v. Warren, 195 Or App 656, 670 (2004).

This holds true even if the defendant has waived his or her right to a jury trial in the case in chief. “We cannot assume that defendant, by waiving a jury trial on the burglary charge, intended to waive the right to have a jury determine the facts required for imposition of an enhanced dangerous offender sentence. […] At the time that defendant opted for a court trial, he not only was unaware that he had a right to a jury determination of facts used to impose a dangerous offender sentence, but he also did not know of the possibility of such a sentence. We therefore reject the state’s argument that, by waiving his right to a jury trial on the burglary charge, defendant implicitly waived his right to a jury determination of facts to support imposition of a dangerous offender sentence.” Id. at 25.

However, this appellate court holding has been thrown into doubt by a recent Oregon Supreme Court case holding that if the defense lawyer fails to specifically object to the state not submitting dangerous offender elements to a jury when the defendant pleads out, the issue may not be appealed. State v. Heilman, 339 Or 661 (2005).

Thus, it remains to be seen if a waiver of jury trial via the common plea form (such as that used in Multnomah County) would also be considered a waiver of jury trial on the dangerous offender elements, because some plea forms do give notice of such a possibility. Cf. Johnson v. Zenon, 105 Or App 621, 805 P2d 750 (1991), review denied 311 Or 266, 810 P2d 854 (1991) (defendants who plead guilty and are sentenced under the dangerous offender statute must be advised of that statute before entering their pleas).

G. General Considerations and Relevant Legislative History

State v. Huntley contains an extensive discussion and analysis of the legislative history of the dangerous offender statute, ORS 161.725 et seq. Huntley concerned a case in which the Oregon Supreme Court reviewed the enhanced dangerous offender sentence of George M. Huntley, who entered a guilty plea to first-degree sodomy. After his plea, the Douglas County Circuit Court found the defendant to be a dangerous offender. Discussing the statute’s legislative history, the Oregon Supreme Court noted:

The current Oregon Criminal Code, of which ORS 161.725 and 161.735 are part, is the product of three years of concerted effort by the Oregon Criminal Law Revision Commission. That commission was created by the 1967 legislature and submitted its final draft of the code in July 1970. The code (Senate Bill 40) was enacted by the 56th Legislative Assembly, Or Laws 1971, ch 743, and became effective January 1, 1972, as amended.

ORS 161.725 and 161.735 were sections 85 and 86, respectively, of the 1970 final draft, and were approved by the 1971 legislature without amendment.

A review of the legislative history of the dangerous offender statutes is beneficial in explaining our decision on this matter. The official commentary to section 86 of the Proposed Oregon Criminal Code 83 (1970), now ORS 161.735, states:

‘Section 86 establishes the procedures for determining whether a defendant comes within the dangerous offender provision of § 85. The purposes of the section are to provide procedural safeguards for the defendant, while at the same time furnishing the court with as much information as can be obtained on which to base an intelligent decision.’

In his testimony before the Senate Criminal Law and Procedure Committee on February 17, 1971, Mr. Donald L. Paillette, the project director for the preparation of the 1970 final draft and report of the Proposed Oregon Criminal Code, was questioned on the intent of section 86. Mr. Paillette stated that the language and intent of section 86 was based on former ORS 137.111 to 137.115 (repealed by Or Laws 1971, ch 743, § 432) and the Model Sentencing Act, Council of Judges of the National Council on Crime and Delinquency, New York (1963). At that time, ORS 137.111 stated:

‘After the presentence hearing and upon the consideration of the psychiatric report required by ORS 137.112 to 137.115, the court may, in its discretion, in lieu of any other sentence authorized by law for such crime, sentence any person convicted under ORS 163.210, 163.220, 163.270, 167.035, 167.040 or 167.045 to an indeterminate term not exceeding the natural life of such person if:

‘(1) The offense involved a child under the age of 16 years; and

‘(2) The court finds that such person has a mental or emotional disturbance, deficiency or condition predisposing him to the commission of any crime punishable under ORS 163.210, 163.220, 163.270, 167.035, 167.040 or 167.045 to a degree rendering the person a menace to the health or safety of others.’

The legislature adopted the wording of the 1963 Model Sentencing Act regarding dangerous offender sentencing, which read:

‘The defendant shall not be sentenced under subdivision (a) or (b) of section 5 unless he is remanded by the judge before sentence to [diagnostic facility] for study and report as to whether he is suffering from a severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity; and the judge, after considering the presentence investigation, the report of the diagnostic facility, and the evidence in the case or on the hearing on the sentence, finds that the defendant comes within the purview of subdivision (a) or (b) of section 5.…’

1963 Model Sentencing Act at § 6 (emphasis added). When asked how the determination of a severe personality disorder would be made, Mr. Paillette acknowledged that the statute provided no specific criteria. The determination of the types of tests, the type of examination, and the diagnosis of any mental disorder would be made by the psychiatrist. Nothing in Mr. Paillette’s testimony suggested that he felt a psychiatric diagnosis within the terms of the statute was a prerequisite or essential to the application of the statute if the evidence could be obtained from other sources. This would be consistent with the stated objective of section 86 of placing as much information before the court as possible from which the court could make an intelligent decision.

ORS 161.735(6) states: “If, after considering the presentence report, the psychiatric report and the evidence in the case or on the presentence hearing, the court finds that the defendant comes within ORS 161.725, the court may sentence the defendant as a dangerous offender.”

The commentary to the 1963 Model Sentencing Act did not discuss in any detail how the determination of a “severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity” would be made. In 1972 that wording of the Model Sentencing Act pertaining to dangerous offenders was changed to require the court to find that the defendant “is suffering from a severe mental or emotional disorder indicating a propensity toward continuing dangerous criminal activity.” Model Sentencing Act, supra at 7 (2d ed 1972). The comment to this wording specifically refers to the Oregon dangerous offender statutes. It reads:

‘The requirement of diagnosis of severe mental or emotional disorder indicating a propensity toward continuing dangerous criminal activity is admittedly difficult to accept, and we do not dismiss its uncertainties in theory and practice. The behavioral sciences do not now have sufficient expertise to carry out this assignment adequately. How, then, can the procedure not only be proposed but also (as in Oregon, where the act has been passed) be made operable?

‘First, contrary to the impression that some people receive or give, the effect of the diagnosis provision will be in general to shorten terms, not to lengthen them. In almost all jurisdictions today, in the absence of usable criteria and wholly at the discretion of the sentencing judge, sentences of thirty years and more are not rare.

‘In contrast a term of such length may, under the Model Act, not be imposed without meeting certain criteria.

‘A diagnostic clinic has no difficulty determining whether a man is suffering from a severe mental or emotional disorder. Relating that kind of diagnosis to a likelihood that he will commit further crimes does present a problem. But even here the Model Act says that the defendant’s likelihood of committing further crimes must be supported by his record. A judge may find, for example, that a man who committed the type of crime cited in the section did so under unusual circumstances or that he was, shall we say, over twenty-five at the time and had never previously committed such a crime. He is justified in finding that this defendant is not likely to commit that crime again.

‘In the light of today’s limited knowledge of prediction of criminal behavior (or any behavior), we make it clear that the judge, representing society, makes a common-sense decision based on as much relevant information as possible. Sentencing of any offender is a judicial task, not a decision for doctors or behavioral scientists.

‘But the expertise of psychologists and psychiatrists can be helpful to the judge.

‘Our ‘Guides to Sentencing the Dangerous Offender’ was produced in response to the question that these comments seek to answer.…’

Id. at 10–11. As to the change of wording, the commentary reads:

‘In subdivision 1(c) the language used is “severe mental or emotional disorder,” contrasting with “severe personality disorder” in the first edition. The change was adopted because a number of psychiatrists had pointed out that the term “personality disorder” might be mistakenly construed as the classification of a specific type of disorder. Our intent, now clarified, was to refer to defendants who, in the terms of laymen, were suffering from some mental or emotional disorder and not to limit the disorder to any one specific diagnosis.’

Id.What is the relevance of the discussion of the Model Sentencing Act’s comments to sentencing under its amended language when the Oregon legislature left the statute in its original form calling only for a finding of a “severe personality disorder indicating a propensity toward criminal activity”?

It can be relevant only if it reflects the view of the drafters of the 1963 Model Sentencing Act that was the basis for Oregon’s law as well as their view of the 1972 text. Of course, the 1972 modification of the Model Sentencing Act that the court must find the defendant ‘is suffering from a severe mental or emotional disorder indicating a propensity toward continuing dangerous criminal activity’ does not change the meaning of the Oregon statutes. However, we do not think the change in language is significant for interpretation purposes. The authors of the Model Sentencing Act assert that the language was changed only to emphasize that the diagnosis would be that of a lay person and not of a behavioral scientist. Both the Oregon statutes and the amended Model Sentencing Act are aimed at dealing with dangerous offenders who are apt to repeat dangerous activity and not offenders who merely repeat non-dangerous criminal activity. The comments to the 1972 Model Sentencing Act therefore may be of some help because the essential meanings of the language of the Model Sentencing Act and the Oregon dangerous offender statutes remain the same.

Huntley at 423–427. As well, the Huntley court noted:

According to the Guides for Sentencing, Dangerous Offenders, promulgated by the Council of Judges of the National Council on Crime and Delinquency (2d ed 1974), the essence of the dangerous offender classification is not one specific diagnosis, but any significant mental or emotional disorder or disturbance—a lay concept—and that the finding should be based on the judge’s evaluation of all the information gathered, not exclusively on the clinical diagnosis.

These national guides, promulgated two years after the passage of the Oregon dangerous offender statutes, are inadequate in assisting a judge to make one of the most serious sentencing decisions. Neither the Model Act nor the guidelines gives any legal or lay definition of a severe personality disorder. Further, the comment to the Model Sentencing Act that the judge, representing society, makes a common sense decision based on as much relevant information as possible is much too vague to use as a basis for incarcerating a person for up to 30 years.

Huntley at 430–431. In addition, the legislative history suggests that the dangerous offender statute should be narrowly applied. “The drafters of the Model Sentencing Act, the Act upon which ORS 161.725 and 161.735 were based, contemplated that very few defendants would be found to be dangerous offenders.” Huntley at 437.

In conclusion, the statute’s legislative history suggests three things: 1) the statute does not require final determinations of dangerousness and personality disorder be made by a clinician, and even allows the judge to do so contrary to a clinician’s conclusions, 2) neither the statute’s plain language nor its legislative history provide any clear standards for dangerous offender sentencing, and 3) but in any case the statute is intended to apply to “very few” defendants.

IV. GEI Sentencing

Following a finding of guilty except for insanity, the court must discharge the defendant unless all the following ORS 161.327 findings are made:

1) the criminal charge was either a felony or a misdemeanor which involved physical injury or the risk of physical injury (ORS 161.327(1)(a))

2) a person affected by a mental disease or defect in a state of remission is considered to have a mental disease or defect requiring supervision when the disease may, with reasonable medical probability, occasionally become active and, when active, render the person a danger to others” ORS 161.327(3) (emphasis added)

3) the person “presents a substantial danger to othersrequiring commitment” to the state hospital (or an intensive inpatient facility if the person is under 18) ORS 161.327(1)(a)(A)(B) (emphasis added).

If the court makes these findings, it must then further make a decision regarding conditional release: “If the court finds that the person presents a substantial danger to others but that the person can be adequately controlled with supervision and treatment if conditionally released and that necessary supervision and treatment are available, the court may order the person conditionally released,” subject to any requirements the court wishes to set. ORS 161.327(2)(b) (emphasis added).

In making the decision whether to authorize conditional release, “the court shall have as its primary concern the protection of society.” ORS 161.327(5) (emphasis added).

The phrase “substantial danger to others” is neither defined in the GEI statutes nor is it defined in case law. However, several things are worth noting.

First, the phrase “danger to others” is modified upward by the word “substantial.” One would presume it means something like “of ample or considerable amount.” Random House Unabridged Dictionary, 2006. Of course, even if we assume it means something like “considerable,” we still don’t know if it refers to the frequency, the magnitude or the likelihood of the potential danger.

Second, the phrase “substantial danger to others” is modified by the phrase “requiring commitment.” That is, this is no ordinary substantial danger but one where commitment to the supervision of the PSRB is absolutely necessary. Again, the clause “requiring commitment” must mean something or the legislature would not have made it part of the same phrase as “substantial danger to others.” The legislature could have said, “If the court finds that the person is a substantial danger to others then it is required to commit…” Instead, “requiring commitment” appears to be an additional finding the court must make; a personal feeling on the judge’s part that this person truly needs commitment.

Third, none of the GEI-specific phrases are defined, but there is a danger-related definition in the definitions section of ORS chapter 161. ORS 161.015(1) defines “dangerous weapon” as “any weapon, device, instrument, material or substance which under the circumstances in which it is used, attempted to be used or threatened to be used, is readily capable of causing death or serious physical injury.” It would be odd if the level of potential harm inherent in the core word “danger” was death or serious physical injury in the definitional section of chapter 161 but a few sections later plummeted to minor physical injury, for example.

Fourth, note that there is no temporal or immediacy aspect to the danger calculation. If we expect that in three years the person will become violent, is that good enough? The near future? Anytime in the defendant’s life? Note also that there is no threshold for the quantity of risk required. If there is a ten-percent chance that the person will become violent within three years, is he a “danger to others”? What about thirty percent? Sixty percent?

Fifth, the court must have the protection of society as its primary concern only when making the conditional release decision. The dangerousness decision precedes the conditional release decision and must be made on its own merits.

The phrase “danger to others” implies a current state of being or a current trait in which it is likely that the person will cause or attempt to cause death or serious physical injury in the near future. If we know the person will not be violent for three years, then the person is not dangerous right now. If the person has only a ten-percent likelihood of being violent, then it is very likely that the person is not dangerous at all.

As an absolute floor, the definitions included in the case law for civil commitment should be used. The U.S. Supreme Court set forth a minimum standard required in order to take away a person’s liberty for the protection of society (rather than as punishment). O’Connor v. Donaldson, 422 US 563 (1975). The Court in Donaldson ruled that there is no constitutional basis for confining persons involuntarily if they are dangerous to no one and can live safely in freedom. In Oregon, that standard has been developed over the last thirty years in the civil commitment case law. (See Chapter 5 on Civil Commitment for complete discussion.)

VI. Sexually Violent Dangerous Offenders

ORS 137.765:

(1) As used in this section:

(a) “History of sexual assault” means that a person has engaged in unlawful sexual conduct that:

(A) Was not committed as part of the same criminal episode as the crime for which the person is currently being sentenced; and

(B) Seriously endangered the life or safety of another person or involved a victim under 12 years of age.

(b) “Sexually violent dangerous offender” means a person who has psychopathic personality features, sexually deviant arousal patterns or interests and a history of sexual assault and presents a substantial probability of committing a crime listed in subsection (3) of this section.

(2) Notwithstanding ORS 161.605, when a person is convicted of a crime listed in subsection (3) of this section, in addition to any sentence of imprisonment required by law, a court shall impose a period of post-prison supervision that extends for the life of the person if:

(a) The person was 18 years of age or older at the time the person committed the crime; and

(b) The person is a sexually violent dangerous offender.

(3) The crimes to which subsection (2) of this section applies are:

(a) Rape in the first degree and sodomy in the first degree if the victim was:

(A) Subjected to forcible compulsion by the person;

(B) Under 12 years of age; or

(C) Incapable of consent by reason of mental defect, mental incapacitation or physical helplessness;

(b) Unlawful sexual penetration in the first degree; and

(c) An attempt to commit a crime listed in paragraph (a) or (b) of this subsection.

The sexually violent dangerous offender statute, in direct opposition to the dangerous offender statute, is carefully written to capture the truly dangerous offenders without being overbroad. It is also much more in keeping with the clinical requirements of risk assessment. One is not left wondering what dangerous means. A person is dangerous if he or she has psychopathic personality features, sexually deviant arousal patterns or interests, a history of sexual assault and a substantial probability of committing one of the listed crimes. These are all factors that are testable or verifiable to some extent.

If there is a finding that the person is a sexually violent dangerous offender then the court must impose a lifetime period of post-prison supervision.

VII. Civil Commitment

The law allows involuntary commitment if a person has a mental disorder which is causing him or her to be “dangerous to self or others.” Like the dangerous offender statute, the civil commitment statute is silent as to the parameters of dangerousness. Unlike the dangerous offender statute, the case law has delineated reasonably specific guidelines. (See Chapter 5.)

For the purposes of civil commitment, dangerousness is worked into the very definition of “mentally ill person” and “mentally retarded person.” ORS chapter 426 only allows for a person to be civilly committed to the Department of Mental Health if the person meets the definition of a “mentally ill person” in ORS 426.005, which includes, among other things “a person who, because of a mental disorder is … (A) Dangerous to self or others.”

The general standard is whether there is a reasonable medical probability that the allegedly mentally ill person is presently a danger to others. Evidence of either actual violence or threats of violence may be sufficient if they are predictive of harm in the near future. State v. Allmendinger, 36 Or App 381 (1978).

If the basis of the commitment is a threat of violence, the court should consider the following:

• whether there was an actual threat of violence

• whether the allegedly mentally ill person is capable of carrying out such a threat

• whether the threat is directed at specific and identifiable person

• whether there was some overt physical act indicating propensity.

Id.Past acts are relevant but insufficient in and of themselves. There must be evidence that explains how the mental disorder causes the allegedly mentally ill person to be a danger to anyone. State v. Miller,198 Or App 153 (2005):

In determining whether a person poses a danger to others, it is appropriate for a court to consider the testimony of mental health experts, a person’s past acts, and the person’s apparent condition at the time of the hearing. State v. Furnish, 86 Or App 194, 197 (1987). Past threats coupled with violent acts may constitute clear and convincing evidence that a person is dangerous to others if “there is no indication that such violence is an isolated occurrence.” State v. Allmendinger, 36 Or App 381, 384 (1978). “But a mere recitation of past acts, in the absence of a showing that such clearly forms the foundation for a prediction of future dangerousness, cannot serve as the basis for a finding that one is a “mentally ill person” pursuant to ORS 426.005. State v. Lucas, 31 Or App 947, 950 (1977).

VIII. Capital Punishment

Death penalty work is a subspecialty beyond the scope of this book. However, a risk assessment is required of a capital jury so it must be mentioned here. In addition to other issues, the following must be considered by the jury:

“…whether there is a probability that the defendant would commit criminal acts of violence that would constitute a continuing threat to society.” ORS 163.150(1)(b)(B). The strange aspect of this particular assessment of risk is that the alternative to the death penalty is life in prison without parole. So, presumably the society in question is the one that would be present in a maximum security prison. But how might the jury assess such a risk?

If the defendant has previously been violent in custody, then a decision can be based on something specific. Otherwise, it would seem that the state would have to tie the normal risk factors for out-of-custody violence together with prison setting. The base rates would have to be based on prison populations. Justice Breyer, in Ring v. Arizona, addressed recidivism rates for persons who are post-sentencing in a death penalty case:

As to incapacitation, few offenders sentenced to life without parole (as an alternative to death) commit further crimes. See, e.g., Sorensen and Pilgrim, “An Actuarial Risk Assessment of Violence Posed by Capital Murder Defendants,” 90 J. Crim. L. & C. 1251, 1256 (2000) (studies find average repeat murder rate of .002 percent among murderers whose death sentences were commuted); Marquart and Sorensen, “A National Study of the Furman-Commuted Inmates: Assessing the Threat to Society from Capital Offenders,” 23 Loyola (LA) L. Rev. 5, 26 (1989) (98 percent did not kill again either in prison or in free society).

Ring v. Arizona, 536 US 584 (2000).
