Mental Health and Criminal Defense

2010 edition

A Systematic Approach

We must not become resigned to mental health treatment in jails and prisons. The field is open for us to seize the fight and take the upper hand. Be creative. Be inspired. Be resolute in your opposition to your client being punished a second time due to a broken mental health system that denied the client treatment and now wants to deny the client freedom.

Discussion

I. Shift from Mental Health System to Criminal Justice System

Over the course of the last thirty years there has been a slow but steady criminalization of mental illness. Nationwide, there are more than three times as many seriously mentally ill persons in prisons and jails than in hospitals. In most places one can safely assume that the county jail is the largest local mental health facility and the state prison is the largest statewide inpatient provider. Not since the 1840s, when Dorothea Dix led a reform movement, have we imprisoned so many of our mentally ill citizens. (More Mentally Ill Persons Are in Jails and Prisons than Hospitals, E. Fuller Torrey, et al., 2010).

The reasons for this shift are relatively simple. First, there has been a drastic reduction in the number of psychiatric beds over the last fifty years from one bed per three hundred people in 1955 to one bed per three thousand people in 2005. Id. This trend is often referred to as “deinstitutionalization.” A convergence of human rights, civil rights and fiscal reasons drove this trend. Civil rights lawsuits put pressure on state governments. State legislatures were happy to save the costs associated with institutionalization. At the time, everyone said that funds would follow people into the community. We could be more humane and fiscally sound by shifting stabilized folks out of the asylums and into the community. In 1964, John F. Kennedy signed the Community Mental Health Centers Act, saying, “Reliance on the cold mercy of custodial isolation will be supplanted by the open warmth of community concern and capability.” There was, essentially, bipartisan agreement.

The pace of deinstitutionalization accelerated in the late ’80s and early ’90s. This was partly due to the increased number of lawsuits and deficit concerns. But this was also when psychiatric medications started becoming truly effective and commonplace. It was during this acceleration that a concurrent trend became clear: many of those benefiting from deinstitutionalization were ending up not in new homes in the community, but in the jails and prisons. Thus, for many people, it wasn’t a release from institutionalization so much as a shift between institutions. Unfortunately, the new institution was neither designed nor funded for treatment. (See, for example, “From Hospitals to Jails,” American Journal of Orthopsychiatry, Whitmer, 1980, and “The Mentally Ill in an Urban County Jail,” Archives of General Psychiatry, Lamb and Grant, 1982.)

Second, it turned out that only a small portion of hospital funding followed people into the community when the hospitals were shut down. And that already inadequate funding for community mental health has continuously been whittled away over the years. The most basic resources for treatment in the community such as therapy, case management, skills training and medication management are increasingly rare and unavailable. In Oregon, a distressing number of people with mental illness must enter a lottery to win their medications. Predictably, a certain portion of folks who are unable to acquire medications for their illness become acutely mentally ill. When the Oregon Health Plan cut a number of people from its rolls seven to eight years ago, the difference was visible on the streets of Portland within a few weeks. There were significantly more people wandering the streets talking to themselves and showing signs of florid psychosis.

Third, we have not only cut the inpatient hospital beds and community mental health resources down to the bare minimum (or less), we have also cut almost all of the respite beds throughout the state. If a person with mental illness or their caseworker notices increasing symptoms, there used to be places to go for a few days to get back on track with medications and treatment. There are very, very few such places left. We simply let most of those folks become so acutely symptomatic that they need hospitalization. If a police officer comes in contact with a person that they know needs hospitalization, there is nowhere the officer can take that person except a hospital or jail. Taking the person to the hospital means spending several hours of their shift in the emergency department waiting room. It also means that the person will likely be released and back in the same situation in a matter of hours. Taking the person to the jail, rather, is efficient and guarantees that the person will be safe and get some minimal treatment. We have left officers with an obvious choice: find a crime to charge and take the person to jail. There’s almost always something to charge the person with: disorderly conduct, interfering with public transportation, criminal trespass, public indecency, resisting arrest, etc., etc. We have become so used to acutely mentally ill people flooding the criminal justice system that the trend is rarely questioned anymore.

A fundamental premise of this book is that, except in serious, violent cases, it is inappropriate and immoral to use the criminal justice system as a backstop for the mental health system. A person with acute illness should be treated, not punished and marginalized. The stigma of having a mental illness is great. The stigma of having a criminal record is also great. But the stigma of being one of the “criminally insane” is a fate that should not be wished on one’s worst enemy. Additionally, neither jails nor prisons are appropriate treatment facilities. They aren’t funded for the task and they aren’t designed for the task. Inmates with mental illness are often segregated, left to deal with voices and hallucinations in a tiny cement cell. The Multnomah County jail system has only a single part-time psychiatrist for all of its inmates.

Nobody is saying that using the criminal justice system rather than the mental health system to treat our citizens is a good thing. But a sort of dour resignation has become de rigueur. Portland City Council member Amanda Fritz, when talking about the interaction of police and people with mental illness, said flatly, “We don’t have a [mental health] system.” (Mental Health Portland, August, 2010). Multnomah County District Attorney Chuck French, speaking on the record about the 2009 Corrections Grand Jury, said, “One of the problems with the mental health system is that there has been no support for community mental health.” (“Jail Inc.,” Portland Mercury, February 2010.) This is a very common sentiment I hear at all levels. Jails are the only option because community mental health has been defunded. At a public meeting, in response to a question I asked, former Portland Police Chief Rosie Sizer admitted that it is not uncommon for her officers to take people with mental illness to jail because there’s no other option. The clear implication was that these were not people who would get charged with crimes if there was any other alternative. Yet such folks are getting charged with crimes in lieu of genuine treatment. And once a person has been charged with a crime, arguments that a defendant’s mental illness should get them back out of the criminal justice system will generally fall on deaf ears.

Which leads me to the second fundamental premise of this book: it is up to the criminal defense attorney to fight back. We must not ourselves become resigned to mental health treatment in jails and prisons. We must see every mental health case as an opportunity to decry the inhumanity of criminalizing mental illness. We must not let our clients be convicted of crimes for which they would not have been charged but for their illness. We must stand in the way of the system and say, “Stop. This is a person with hopes and fears and memories like you and me. Do not turn him into a criminal just because our community mental health system is broken. That’s not a solution to a problem. It’s doubly cruel. First, we refuse to treat this person, knowing the repercussions. And then we punish him for being untreated. This is our fault. Not his.”

This book is an attempt to say “Stop” in a systematic way. My hope is that defense attorneys will start thinking about these cases as filled with opportunity rather than difficulty. These cases contain all the usual defenses plus some additional ones, as well as the moral high ground. The district attorney will often attempt to ignore completely the issue of mental illness as it rarely helps their case. The field is open for us to seize the fight and take the upper hand. Be creative. Be inspired. Be resolute in your opposition to your client being punished a second time due to a broken mental health system that denied the client treatment and now wants to deny the client freedom.

II. A Systematic Approach to Assessing the Case

A. Spotting the Issue

With some clients, it’s difficult to miss the symptoms of mental illness or severe cognitive disability. With others, it’s extremely difficult to spot. The most important thing to be aware of is that many folks with a mental or cognitive issue have developed an ability to get by without being noticed. Just because a client is looking at you like he understands you and telling you he understands when you ask him, it does not mean he actually understands. It is important to follow up with questions of your own to make sure the client has absorbed the basics of what you’ve told him. So, instead of something like, “Do you understand?” or “Do you have any questions?” ask, “What did you understand from what we just talked about?” or, “I want to make sure that I was clear—can you give me a quick summary of what I just said?” With answers to open-ended questions like these, you check your client’s under-standing but also can peripherally do a quick assessment of attention, thought-process and awareness.

B. Honing the Issue

Once you have determined that your client struggles with a mental illness or cognitive disability, use the Interview Sheet (see appendices) to get a fuller understanding of their perspective. Included in those sheets are some basic mental health interview questions and tools that will help you get a handle on the extent and variety of the client’s symptoms. The point isn’t for the attorney to make a diagnosis. Diagnosis, prognosis and treatment should be left to mental health professionals. However, it is important that the defense attorney develop a basic understanding of the client’s symptoms right up front in order to investigate appropriately, develop the right defenses and hire the right sort of mental health expert.

After developing an understanding of the client’s symptoms, the attorney should consider what that means in terms of the allegations. What are the delusions, hallucinations, or misunderstandings that can be connected with the allegations? What are the elements of the crime’s charged? Do the charges still make sense in the context of a person with this client’s symptoms? Do the police mention the symptoms in the reports?

Finally, think about the particular symptoms in terms of the defenses it might provide to the allegations. Will the state have difficulty proving what was in your client’s mind at the time of the incident? Was the client unable to control his behavior at the time of the incident? Is the client so out of it that he either lacked the capacity to understand the criminality of what he was doing or lacks the capacity now to be a defendant?

C. Investigating

It is important to determine whether the allegations against your client can be attributed to his or her mental illness. Acquire collateral evidence—such as hospital records, commitment files, medical records, jail records, outpatient records, prior psychological evaluations, and prior cases—to build your case.

Consider recording the client as early in the case as possible, before competence is regained. This solves the problem that sometimes arises of how to get a jury to understand how symptomatic the client was at the time of the crime when the jury is seeing the client after many months of being treated at the hospital.

If there are family members, friends, caseworkers or other treatment providers, track them down and talk to them. Start building the story of the client’s life—how his illness or disability affected his life and the arc it took over the long term. Try to figure out what it’s like to be the client, to think like the client. What are the obvious symptoms and what are some of the more subtle symptoms that you only really understand after years of knowing the client? Ultimately, it may be crucial to tell the client’s story to a jury and get them to really understand the defendant’s mind, so it’s important to start finding the details that make the story come alive.

D. Consider Aid and Assist Issues

1.Rule of thumb: Can the client freely decide whether to plead guilty or go to trial, whether to request a jury or have a bench trial, or whether to testify or remain silent? It is unethical for the attorney to decide any of these questions for the client. Thus, if the client can’t decide, the case must come to a screeching halt. The more common questions of whether the defendant understands the roles of the parties and what happens at a trial are all subsumed into this larger “rule of thumb” issue. After all, if the defendant doesn’t understand the role of the defense attorney or what a jury is, then she can hardly decide whether to plead guilty or go to trial. However, decisional competence also wraps in a number of more abstract abilities related to executive functioning, abilities that get lost if the defendant is simply asked to repeat phrases about the court or the process. For example, I have personally witnessed more than one profoundly ill, incompetent client who was able to say that the judge is “the boss of the courtroom” just as they were taught in the hospital class.

2. ORS 161.360 + Dusky v. U.S., 362 US 402 (1960)

A defendant must have sufficient present ability to:

• Factually understand the nature of the proceedings. • Rationally understand the nature of the proceedings. • Assist and cooperate with counsel with a reasonable degree of rational understanding. • Participate in the defense of the legal case.

3. It is almost always worth taking the time to hire an expert. It allows you to bypass the 30-day evaluation in 161.365(2) and confirm your concerns in a manner that you can control.

There are several reasons why you don’t want to use the state hospital to perform your evaluations. First, if given the choice between allowing your client’s statements to be confidential or open to the public, the default choice should always be to opt for confidentiality. The same default choice should be fairly obvious if forced to choose between your own expert and the state’s expert. No decent defense attorney would think of handing their client off to the state’s polygrapher without first having the client take a private, confidential lie detector test. A mental health evaluation isn’t much different in its potential for negative consequences. Moreover, it is important to get the defense expert involved as early as possible. In a battle of experts, the expert that gets to see the client closer to the incident date or in a worse state will have significant advantage. The second expert will, by necessity, have to base a significant portion of their report and their opinion on the report of the first expert, making it appear as though great deference is being given.

If there are severe and immediate issues, in rare cases you may want to schedule a hearing and present testimony by employees of the jail, preferably with a report from the jail psychiatric staff. In some cases, you may want to consider a civil commitment. Depending on the situation and the gravity of the case, civil commitment may put more pressure on the district attorney to dismiss the case, and it may allow the court and the district attorney to feel more comfortable because Mental Health has it handled.

E. Hire an Expert

There are four steps in hiring an expert.

1. Choose the expert.

When choosing an expert to work on your case, a number of factors should be considered. First, and most important, you need to decide whether this is the sort of case that a normal forensic psychologist can handle or, instead, if there are more specialized issues. For example, where there are difficult brain issues like head injuries or cognitive disabilities affecting executive functioning, you may want to consider hiring a neuropsychologist. If there are medical or medication issues that appear to be causing the symptoms, you may want to consider hiring a psychiatrist. If there are psychopathy issues, you may want to consider hiring a psychologist who has particular experience assessing or treating psychopaths and doing risk assessments. If there are drug or alcohol issues, you may want to consider a psychologist with experience assessing or treating dual diagnosis patients.

Second, ask yourself which you care about the most:

• The thoroughness and look of the report (for the DA or judge’s benefit). • The quality of the analysis (for your benefit). • The quality of the potential testimony (for the trier of fact). Different psychologists have different skills. Generally speaking, the ones who are the best in court are not the ones who write the best reports. Court testimony requires a high degree of confidence and willingness to dump scientific jargon and caution in order to simplify and cut to the chase. Really good reports build slowly, in a thorough scientific way, the psychological or psychiatric perspective. Ask around to determine which psychologists have the best reputations for what you need.

Third, for what purpose are you requesting an evaluation? If it’s an issue of negotiation and sentencing, you may want a treatment-oriented report that will focus exactly on what could be set up to moderate the client’s symptoms and reduce the risk of recidivism. If it’s an evaluation for the purpose of working up trial defenses, then the request to the expert should make that clearly known, laying out the defense theory, including the law. (As a brief aside, some defense attorneys hesitate to be specific about their legal theory or the symptoms they have seen, fearing they will be seen as manipulating the psychologist. Defense attorneys should do their best to ignore those concerns. The expert will want as much information as possible, no matter the source. Moreover, a huge problem for evaluations is an insufficiently clear referral question with which to begin the process. Too often attorneys simply refer the client to the psychologist with a general request to help figure out possible defenses. In terms of manipulation, the fear of psychologists is that the attorney will pressure them to come up with a particular result. Defense attorneys should bend over backwards to make it clear that they are expecting an objective report and have no desire to put any pressure on the expert to say anything in particular.)

Finally, issues of timeliness should also be considered. How quickly does the work need to be done? How busy is the expert? Does the expert have an ability and reputation for completing work in a timely fashion without sacrificing quality?

2. Call the expert.

After determining who will be the best fit for your case, confirm that your expert is able and willing to do an evaluation. Discuss how long it will take to conduct and report on the evaluation as well as the expert’s fees, and use the conversation as an opportunity to frame the issues of the case with a brief set of facts.

3. Apply for money, if you’ve been appointed or the defendant is indigent. State the symptoms you’ve observed, the theoryof the case and why the expert is necessary.See Appendix B.

4 .Write a letter to the expert.

In your letter, confirm your oral conversation and specific requests. Again, use the opportunity to frame the issues by stating the symptoms you’ve observed, the charges against your client, the theory of the case, and the basic facts as they fit into the theory. Include a brief review of the relevant legal standard(s) and provide basic legal information such as court dates, Jail ID#, current jail, and maximum and likely jail sentences. Include any of the aforementioned collateral documents such as police reports, jail records and psychological evaluations, indicate other documents still on the way and important informational sources such as family members or caseworkers. See Appendix C.

F. Deal With Aid and Assist Issues With Advice from Your Expert

1. In most cases, you’ve already had your own expert evaluate your client. Thus, if there are fitness issues, you can simply schedule a court hearing, submit your evaluator’s report and leave it up to the court to order the defendant to be treated until fit. In the vast majority of counties in Oregon, the DA will take no position on the matter since the defendant will go down to the hospital in custody. If the DA objects to a treat until fit order under ORS 161.370 and requests an evaluation at OSH under ORS 161.365, you should make the argument that such an evaluation is not required, whether the DA requests it or not. Moreover, it would be an unnecessary and inappropriate use of resources: for an evaluation under .365 (aka a 30-day evaluation), the defendant will sit in jail for most of the 30 days and then be taken down to Salem by the sheriff for a few hours. That 30 days would be used for treatment if a treat until fit order is made and if, for some reason, they didn’t think the defendant was unfit, they can do an evaluation and send him back early. There is nothing to be gained by doing a .365 evaluation when there’s already a report declaring the person to be unfit to proceed.

2. Note that the judge may order that a person be treated until fit in the community if it “would better serve the defendant and the community.” ORS 161.370(2). Community treatment is most appropriate where either it is unlikely that a person will improve with medications (as with retardation or a cognitive disability) or there is a local mental health program or current, good placement. In Multnomah County, the Forensic Diversion Program, run by County Mental Health, is attempting to divert appropriate defendants away from the hospital to be treated until fit in the community. They will even put the person through a class that’s similar to the one at OSH.

3. Technically, there is no statutory mechanism for a judge to dismiss a case up front on the basis that the client will not be fit to proceed in the foreseeable future. However, where there has recently been an order on a different case that the defendant was unlikely to become fit in the foreseeable future, defense attorneys should make the argument anyway. Similarly, where the defendant is clearly unfit as a result of retardation or cognitive disability that isn’t amenable to medication treatment, defense attorneys should request an up front dismissal.

4. Don’t forget that some parts of the case may proceed even though the defendant is committed for treatment. Specifically, demurrers and statute of limitations arguments can be made in the defendant’s absence. 161.370(12).

5. Note that the maximum period of commitment is the lesser of three years or the maximum sentence the defendant could serve. 161.370(6).

6. The client entitled to a 60-day evaluation and a 90-day hearing to decide whether it can still be said that the defendant is both presently unfit and unlikely to become fit in the foreseeable future. The longer a person is treated, the less likely it becomes that he or she will become fit in the foreseeable future. After all, if it’s so likely, shouldn’t it have already happened? Sometimes there’s a good answer to that question. And sometimes the doctors and evaluators at the hospital are thinking of “likely to become fit” more as “Is it time, medically, to give up?”

7. Aid and Assist–based Dismissal of the Case

a.ORS 161.370(2): “so much time has elapsed…that it would be unjust to resume the criminal proceeding,” e.g., served way more than likely sentence; e.g., lost witnesses.

b.ORS 161.370(6): Client has served maximum time: three years/maximum sentence.

c.ORS 161.370(9): “no substantial probability that the defendant, in the foreseeable future, will gain the capacity to stand trial.”

d.Schedule the aid and assist hearing so that the expert can testify. Ask the expert to have a brief chat with the client to gauge potential improvement.

e.Note that OSH evaluations generally lack the sort of rigorous formal testing done by the best private psychologists, though the evaluators at OSH have access to the full range of tests and some evaluators use them liberally. Note also that the .370 class at OSH primarily deals with factual understanding and is in parts, legally wrong. For example, in the class materials as of September 2010, “Diminished Capacity” is listed as one of the possible pleas. It is described as “I did the crime, but, I had no criminal intent, due to my mental condition.” Of course, there is no such plea. There is a GEI defense where the defendant can argue that “I did the crime, but, I lacked the capacity to understand it was a crime.” And, there is a complete defense that the defendant did not have the required mens rea or mental state for the crime: “I didn’t do the crime because I had no criminal intent.” In neither case should this result in a sentence of prison where the defense is successful, as is stated in the OSH materials. I have been in evaluations where the defendant gave a correct legal answer but was stopped and “corrected” by the evaluator in order to “teach” a legally erroneous proposition.

f.Do not make the mistake of thinking of the state hospital as a monolithic unit that speaks as an entity. There are some really good evaluators that handle evaluations for the state hospital. And, independent of quality, every evaluator, at OSH or not, has their own biases, philosophy, tendencies and style. Moreover, the Forensic Evaluation Service is completely independent of the treatment teams and the wards.

G. Consider a Disordered Mental State Strategy

1. ORS 161.300: “Evidence that the actor suffered from a mental disease or defect is admissible whenever it is relevant to the issue of whether the actor did or did not have the intent which is an element of the crime.”

2. ORS 161.300 is nothing more and nothing less than a special evidentiary rule which requires the admissibility of psychological evidence so long as it is relevant to mental state. Taken at face value, the statute would, for example, seem to trump the hearsay rules.

3. A disordered mental state trial strategy is not a diminished capacity defense.

a.It is not a defense in the sense that the defendant has a burden to prove something.

b.It is not about capacity. The defendant may or may not have had a diminished ability to form intent at the time of the crime, but such inability is beside the point. The point is that the defendant’s mind was and continues to be disordered, chaotic, abnormal.

c.The explicit argument is that the DA uses inferences to prove mental state and all such inferences assume a rational, healthy, intentional mind rather than a disordered mind. It is the difference between picking a needle up from a clean table and picking a needle out of a haystack. The subtext of the argument is that criminality is about criminal intent and whatever it means to be a criminal, our mentally ill/brain injured/retarded client isn’t it.

For more on this subject, see Chapter 3.

4. 161.300 applies to intent, knowledge and recklessness. State v. Smith, 154 Or App 37 (1998), specifically includes knowledge and implies the inclusion of recklessness. Recklessness is a higher level mental state than knowledge or intent. For example, one could argue that animals can intend to eat or know where their food is but nobody would argue that even the most intelligent animal can consciously disregard a substantial risk that a future event will occur.

5. 161.300 applies to almost any mental disorder. 161.295 excludes abnormalities “manifested only by repeated criminal or otherwise antisocial conduct” or “constituting solely a personality disorder.”

6. A disordered mental state trial strategy does not require an expert. Sometimes the testimony of a client’s mother is better than an expert at making the jury feel what it’s like to have the client’s disorder.

7. Experts need not have a confident opinion re: mental state at the time of the crime. In fact, one of the most helpful conclusions that can be drawn by an expert is that it is almost impossible to conclusively state what was in the defendant’s mind at the time of the crime. After all, if a psychologist with far more training, experience, time and access to documents than the jury cannot make a confident conclusion then what hope can there be for 6 or 12 laypeople.

8. Notice is required only if an expert will be used. Notice can be as simple as: “Defendant, by and through his attorney, gives notice of intent to rely upon evidence of mental disease or defect, including expert testimony, as provided for in ORS 161.300.”

9. A jury instruction is unnecessary and potentially harmful. There is a danger that the jury could interpret an instruction as imposing a burden on the defense.

10. Keep in mind the huge shift of mentally ill folks to the criminal justice system. Community resources are being slashed and the state is shoving off their responsibility to care for our most fragile citizens onto prisons and police.

For more on his subject and topics, see Chapter 3.

H. Consider GEI

1. The Standard: “...as a result of a mental disease or defect at the time of engaging in conduct, the person lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality of the conduct or to conform the conduct to the requirements of the law.”

2. “…as a result of a mental disease or defect”: A mental disease or defect is any mental abnormality (including mental retardation) so long as it is not:

• Solely a personality disorder. 161.295(2). • Manifested only by antisocial conduct. 161.295(2). • Substance or alcohol dependency. Tharp v. PSRB, 338 Or 413 (2005). • A combination of disorder and intoxication where the disorder alone would be insufficient. State v. Peverieri, 192 Or App 229 (2004). • A sexual conduct disorder. Beiswenger v. PSRB, 192 Or App 38 (2004) unless it requires more than a manifestation of criminal or antisocial conduct. Rios v. PSRB, 135 Or App 265 (1995).

3. “…at the time of engaging in conduct”: The insanity defense is retrospective. Thus, it is important to document observations of the client and hire an expert as early as possible.

4. “…appreciate the criminality of the conduct”: Did the defendant understand what he was doing at the time of the crime? For example, the defendant may have been motivated by delusions without a logical sense of consequence.

5. “…conform the conduct to the requirements of the law”: Often called “the control test,” this prong of the insanity defense requires proof that despite defendant’s understanding, he was unable to control his behavior. The question is one of volitional conduct. For example, persons experiencing strong command hallucinations often have the feeling that the conscious self has faded into the background and, as a result, feel powerless to make the body resist the demands of the voices.

6. Notice: Filing a notice raises the defense. This should be done as soon as counsel is confident that a GEI defense will be raised. Though the statute requires that notice be filed at the time of the not guilty plea unless just cause is established, counsel should file the notice and argue just cause at a later date. The DA will rarely argue a lack of sufficient notice unless he or she has truly been caught off guard by an eleventh hour filing.

7. Affirmative defense: GEI is an affirmative defense that must be proven by a preponderance of the evidence.

8. State evaluation: Once defense notice has been filed, the state has the right to have the defendant examined. The state must file written notice of its request for an evaluation. The defendant may object to the evaluator and, assuming good cause, the state must choose a different one. The court can, in its discretion, commit the defendant for up to 30 days for observation and examination.

9. Disposition: The court must discharge the defendant after GEI unless all of the following findings are made:

a.The criminal charge was either:

a felony or
a misdemeanor with physical injury or risk of injury.

b.The person has a mental disease or defect.

c.The person presents a substantial danger to others.

10. Conditional release: Even if the court makes the findings for commitment, it may release the defendant to the Psychiatric Security Review Board (PSRB) under a conditional release. The court must find that “the person can be adequately controlled with supervision and treatment” and “necessary supervision and treatment are available.”

11. Maximum sentence: The maximum term of a GEI commitment is the statutory maximum sentence the defendant could have received had he or she been convicted and sentenced to jail. If the defendant pleads or is found GEI on multiple crimes or counts that could run consecutively under the statute, the maximum GEI term is calculated as if the defendant had been sentenced to consecutive, as opposed to concurrent, time. The sentencing guidelines are irrelevant for determining the maximum term of a GEI commitment.

12. The PSRB: a probationlike monitoring program that supervises the defendant after a GEI commitment. If the defendant is initially sent to the hospital, she will spend a considerable amount of time working her way through the multistage PSRB program. The statutes dictate that the PSRB’s main concern must be the “safety of the community.” On a Class A misdemeanor, the defendant will almost surely spend 10–12 months in the hospital. The average stay on a Class C felony is 46–48 months. After release from the hospital, the defendant will likely be placed in a locked group home, continued under PSRB supervision and returned to the hospital if there is a failure to comply with all conditions.

13. A GEI adjudication is not a conviction: It does not trigger mandatory fines and it does not count as a prior conviction for the sentencing guidelines. State v. Saunders, 195 Or App 357 (2004); OAR 253-09-002. However, a GEI adjudication will trigger sex offender registration because ORS 181.594(5)(b) specifically includes within the definition of “sex offender” a person who “has been found guilty except for insanity of a sex crime.”

For more on GEI, see Chapter 4.

III. Some Common Ethical Issues

A. The Suicidal Client

The issue:Suppose that your client tells you she’s planning to commit suicide. Is it unethical to do something about it?

1. Confidentiality

a.Is it confidential? Oregon Rule of Professional Conduct (ORPC) 1.6 states, “A lawyer shall not reveal information relating to the representation of a client unless. . . [an exception applies].” So the question is not whether the information is confidential. The question for this rule is whether the information “relates to the representation” of your client. It could be argued that a statement regarding a future act does not relate to the representation on the client’s criminal case. However, this term should be interpreted very broadly. If the information is gained in the course of an attorney-client interview on the criminal case, then it is related to the representation. First, this broad reading is supported by the fundamental principle that confidentiality and trust are at the core of the attorney-client relationship. If a client can’t trust that his words will be kept close by the attorney, this will have a chilling effect on the whole relationship. Clients will be afraid to open up and say anything for fear that it could be passed on to the authorities. Second, the very first exception to the confidentiality rule is the authority to disclose a client’s future intent to commit a crime. If the rules intended that such information not be included within the scope of confidentiality then there would be no reason to have an exception.

b.Can it be disclosed under Rule 1.6(b)(1), which states, “A lawyer may reveal information relating to the representation to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes necessary: (1) to disclose the intention of the lawyer’s client to commit a crime”? Here, the answer would clearly seem to be “no.” First, suicide is no longer a crime in this or any other state. Second, there is a more appropriate section dealing with potential harm: Rule 1.6(b)(2).

c.Can it be disclosed under Rule 1.6(b)(2), which states, “A lawyer may reveal information relating to the representation to the extent the lawyer reasonably believes necessary: (2) to prevent reasonably certain death or substantial bodily harm.”? Note that in order to disclose information, the lawyer must (a) reasonably believe it to be necessary to prevent substantial bodily harm, and (b) believe that the likelihood of the harm is “reasonably certain.” Presumably this is to hold back a squeamish attorney who believes it would be necessary to disclose an uncertain potential for harm. Note also that reasonably necessary limits the amount of information that may be released: i.e., the minimum possible to accomplish the goal. So, is a threat of suicide reasonably certain to lead to substantial bodily harm? First, the attorney must assess whether the client is reasonably certain to follow through on the threat. This is a real problem. Suicide assessment is tricky and should be left to a clinician.

So, the first step should probably be to contact a psychologist or therapist you know and ask them to help you do an assessment. That said, some objective factors that a person is likely to follow through on a threat of suicide include past suicide attempts, an actual plan, access to the means to carry out the plan, hopelessness, an independent psychiatric diagnosis, and a lack of support like family or friends. It would have to be a really clear-cut case to rise to the level of “reasonable certainty” that the person would follow through. Second, even if the person follows through on their threat, it doesn’t mean that an attempt will actually result in death or substantial bodily harm. The plain language of the rule would seem to want attorneys to be reasonably certain about the degree of harm. The more appropriate and moral reading, however, would be that if you’re reasonably certain that there will be an attempt to cause substantial bodily harm then you can break confidentiality to the extent necessary to stop it, even if you have no way of knowing whether the attempt is likely to be successful.

d.If you are reasonably certain that great bodily harm will occur if you don’t divulge information, then are you required to do something? The clear answer here is “no.” Rule 1.6 is a permissive rule. It sets out the situations in which an attorney may break confidentiality. It says nothing about when an attorney must act. It seems to say that even if you are sure that your client will murder someone, if you don’t act, you still don’t have to do anything.

2. Current Client Conflict

Rule 1.8(b) states, “A lawyer may not use information relating to representation of a client to the disadvantage of the client” without informed consent. Presumably, if you actually save the client’s life, then no result would be a disadvantage compared to death. But what if the client wasn’t going to attempt suicide? What if your actions cause the client to get locked up in a hospital and have his freedom taken from him? A loss of freedom would certainly be a disadvantage. As would the stigma associated with an implication of mental illness or instability. Since there are no exceptions to this rule, it’s not entirely clear how one gets through this ethical puzzle.

B.The Incompetent Client

1. Why is competency an ethics issue?

a.There are certain decisions that the client must make that the attorney is not ethically allowed to make on behalf of the client. If the client is unable to make those decisions due to a grossly diminished decisional capacity, then the case is at a standstill until the defendant becomes fit. ORPC Rule 1.2(a) states, “A lawyer shall abide by a client’s decisions concerning the objectives of representation and, as required by Rule 1.4, shall consult with the client as to the means by which they are to be pursued. . . In a criminal case, the lawyer shall abide by the client’s decision, after consultation with the lawyer, as to a plea to be entered, whether to waive jury trial and whether the client will testify.” Rule 1.4(b) requires the lawyer to “explain a matter to the extent reasonably necessary to permit the client to make informed decisions regarding the representation.” The upshot of these rules is that the client gets to make the decision to exercise or waive his constitutional rights to trial, to a jury and to remain silent. The attorney may not waive constitutional rights and make those decisions for the client. In fact, the attorney is ethically bound to explain a matter to whatever extent is necessary to allow a client to make the requisite decisions.

b.Defendants have a basic constitutional due process right to be competent in a criminal case. This right is so strong that the Ninth circuit recently reversed a case where a judge did not, sua sponte, revisit a competency decision he had already made when the client’s behavior should have triggered concerns. Maxwell v. Roe, __ F3d __, 2010 WL 1997700 (9th Cir. May 20, 2010). Knowingly violating a client’s due process by proceeding with the case while the client lacks the capacity to be a defendant would clearly be a current client conflict under rule 1.8(b). That having been said, there are close calls. If the client seems able to make a decision and understands the ramifications of the decision, then the fact that the client is also extremely ill is relevant but not conclusive. For example, a client is in jail on a misdemeanor, wants to get out and there’s a time-served offer that doesn’t involve probation. Assuming the client already has a criminal record, there isn’t much in the way of downstream dangers in taking the plea. So the level of required competency would not be as high as in a more serious or complex case in which there would be serious risks of either going to trial or of pleading guilty.

c.Does an attorney’s duty of candor toward the tribunal (Rule 3.3) require the attorney to divulge to a judge an attorney’s competency related concerns? Perhaps. While it is undisputed that a trial court judge has the affirmative duty to hold a competency hearing whenever he or she should reasonably doubt a defendant’s competency (see Maxwell, supra), the defense lawyer’s duty is less clear. Rule 3.3 certainly does not specifically address the question of a duty to raise the issue of competency. Some courts have held that the attorney, like the judge, indeed has such a duty whenever competency is reasonably in question. See, e.g., In re Fleming, 16 P 3d 610, 617 (Wash Sup Ct 2001) (“When defense counsel knows or has reason to know of a defendant’s incompetency, tactics cannot excuse failure to raise competency at any time so long as such incapacity continues”); United States v. Jackson, 2006 US App LEXIS 12387 at *32 (6th Cir 2006) (lawyer was “discharging his professional duty” in failing to raise the issue when he had a good-faith doubt as to his client’s competency).

However, a 2008 law review article by Professor John D. King argues that the defense lawyer’s unique duty of zealous representation should trump the duty of candor toward the tribunal. King takes the position that an attorney is not required to raise doubts about client competency to the court, arguing “to the contrary, that the dignity and moral authority of the criminal justice system is best served by a defense lawyer being free to operate solely in the interest of her client.” John D. King, “Candor, Zeal, and the Substitution of Judgment: Ethics and the Mentally Ill Criminal Defendant,” 58 Am. U. L. Rev. 207, 214 (2008). King acknowledges that “most commentators place upon the defense attorney an obligation to raise the issue of competency of the defendant any time that the attorney believes that his client may not be competent.” Id. at 235. But he argues that “such an approach is not justified by history, necessity, or logic and undermines the integrity of the attorney-client relationship and, therefore, the integrity of the criminal justice system.” Id. King’s ultimate advice to defense attorneys is: “In the absence of a rule specifically requiring disclosure of a lawyer’s doubts about her client’s competency to proceed, the appropriate conclusion for a lawyer or a court to draw is that the lawyer’s duties of confidentiality and zeal prohibit her from revealing such doubts if they are adverse to her client’s interests.” Id. at 262.

2. Isn’t it a violation of confidentiality to tell a judge that the client is incompetent?

Rule 1.14 deals specifically with this issue:

(a) When a client’s capacity to make adequately considered decisions in connection with a representation is diminished, whether because of minority, mental impairment or for some other reason, the lawyer shall, as far as reasonably possible, maintain a normal client lawyer relationship with the client.

(b) When the lawyer reasonably believes that the client has diminished capacity, is at risk of substantial physical, financial or other harm unless action is taken and cannot adequately act in the client’s own interest, the lawyer may take reasonably necessary protective action, including consulting with individuals or entities that have the ability to take action to protect the client and, in appropriate cases, seeking the appointment of a guardian ad litem, conservator or guardian.

(c) Information relating to the representation of a client with diminished capacity is protected by Rule 1.6. When taking protective action pursuant to paragraph (b), the lawyer is impliedly authorized under Rule 1.6(a) to reveal information about the client, but only to the extent reasonably necessary to protect the client’s interests.

Rule 1.14 states that a lawyer may break confidentiality to the limited extent necessary to protect a client with diminished capacity who is at risk of substantial harm and cannot act in his own interest. A serious violation of the client’s due process rights would be a substantial harm; as are the potential consequences of a criminal case. So if the client cannot act in his own interest (i.e., he’s incompetent), then an attorney may break confidentiality to tell the judge and start the process of regaining the client’s competency. This assumes, of course, that there are no other options. If it is possible to regain the client’s competency in the community without telling the judge, then it is not “necessary” to break confidentiality in that way.

C. The Marginally Competent Client

1. The Issue

Can the attorney bring up the issue of mental illness at trial even in the face of his client’s explicit wishes not to bring it up? What often happens when a client is treated until fit is that the most acute symptoms will disappear, like disorganization and hallucinations, but the client will still lack self-awareness regarding the illness. This lack of “insight” will often guide the client’s decision-making. A similar issue arises when all of the symptoms resolve except a fixed delusion. Such clients understand the issues and are decisionally competent in that they can weigh options, integrate legal advice and decide between the options. But where the client’s overriding concern is to get the factfinder to agree with the delusion or the absence of a clearly documented illness, the ethics rules fall short. Criminal defendants have certain decisions that are theirs and theirs alone to make. If a marginally competent client wants to take a terrible plea for absurd reasons, it’s the attorney’s duty to assist the client in making the plea. It is the client’s right to make disastrous choices. However, the attorney has a duty to make the final decisions regarding the means by which such decisions and objectives are carried out. Rule 1.2. The attorney must consult with the client and keep the client informed but, ultimately, the attorney is in charge of the strategy. Rules 1.2, 1.4. The attorney may take such action as is impliedly authorized to carry out the representation. Rule 1.2. And the attorney may disclose such information as is impliedly authorized by representation. Rule 1.6. Finally, the attorney has a duty to competently represent the client. Rule 1.1.

2. Argument against the attorney being able to introduce evidence of mental illness against the client’s wishes.

The most compelling argument in favor of this position is that neither an action nor a disclosure can be impliedly authorized if it’s explicitly barred. Thus, for example, Rule 1.6 says that all information relating to the representation is confidential information. That information can be released if it’s impliedly authorized in order to carry out the representation. One could argue that release of the best evidence in favor of innocence is impliedly authorized by the client’s decision to go to trial and objective to win at trial. However, it’s hard to see how the client could have impliedly authorized the release of information when she has explicitly asked that it not be released.

3. Argument for the attorney being able to introduce evidence of mental illness against the client’s wishes.

The lawyer is in charge of strategy and means. When the client decides to go to trial and sets the objective of winning, the client has impliedly authorized all strategies and means to get the job done. Obviously, the client should be consulted and, in most cases, the attorney will defer to the client’s wishes regarding strategy. But, ultimately, it’s the attorney’s call. This can be seen when one considers that while a client’s disastrous choice regarding settlement is the client’s to make, a client’s disastrous choice regarding strategy will fall on the attorney’s shoulders. Perhaps more importantly, if the client were able to decide exactly what confidential information could be released at trial, the strategy would be almost entirely in the hands of the client. Confidential information includes all information relating to the representation. By controlling the release of confidential information, the client could decide which questions could be asked, which witnesses could be called, which motions could be made, etc. There would be little point in dividing between strategy and objectives because virtually everything would be in the hands of the client.

4. My Resolution

I believe, as a matter of personal ethics, that it is important to have a client-centered practice where the client is given free will to make as many decisions as possible and is involved in all of the strategy. If a client has particular concerns or wishes regarding the case, I will do my best to follow those concerns and wishes. However, I also believe that I’m ultimately responsible for all the strategic decisions. If I defer to the client’s wishes and he’s convicted as a result, that happened because of my decision, not the client’s. It follows that a client’s decision to go to trial is an implied authorization to employ whatever strategies and use whatever information I believe will best succeed in getting that job done. Thus, I will have a frank discussion with a client prior to trial about the idea that deciding to go to trial puts the responsibility to choose strategy and means on my shoulders. I also say that I will do my best to follow whatever his wishes are in that regard. However, if issues come up we will need to meet them head on. We can’t let the DA get away with implying one thing when we know the opposite is true. Trials are all about attack and counterattack and it’s impossible to anticipate what information will need to be used until we get to trial.

D. Candor to the Tribunal

1. Issue: Can you put a delusional client on the stand to present testimony that you know to be false?

2. It’s not suborning perjury because the client is telling the truth from the client’s perspective. A material element of perjury is that the person making the statement must know it to be false.

3. It could be an issue of “candor to the tribunal.” Rule 3.3 states, “(a) A lawyer shall not knowingly…(3) offer evidence that the lawyer knows to be false.” Rule 3.3(a)(3) also states, “A lawyer may refuse to offer evidence, other than the testimony of a defendant in a criminal matter, that the lawyer reasonably believes is false.” To summarize, a criminal defense attorney may not and must offer the testimony of a defendant which is known to be false. These contradicting statements follow from the dueling issues of candor and due process, but they are utterly unhelpful in our situation. It seems to me that the solution to this dilemma is to be up front with the judge prior to the testimony: “Judge, pursuant to Rule 3.3, I want to inform you that I expect my client to testify, consistent with his delusion, to information that he believes to be true and I believe to be false.” Rule 3.3(3) specifically authorizes remedial measures, including disclosure to the tribunal.

IV. Laying a Foundation on Direct

There are two goals when laying a foundation on direct examination. The first is to make sure the evidence is admitted and that the expert is acknowledged as an expert. The second is to establish a convincing aura of scientific reliability.

On direct examination, it is very important for both attorneys and clinicians to take a slow, baby step–by–baby step approach that carries the judge and jury along and doesn’t leave anyone behind. To that end, it’s vital that the expert answer the question asked, not the question they believe will be asked next. Most experts, particularly those with a PhD, are able to think two or three steps ahead. Experts should be advised to resist the temptation to jump directly to the greater point of the question. The main problem is that the greater point only makes sense if the expert builds up to it. The steps in between the question and the ultimate point are crucial for basic understanding. Thus, the answer will either seem disjointed, pompous or, on cross, even hostile. Of course, experts should give a complete answer to open-ended questions.

The defense attorney should go through at least the following four steps with every expert prior to testimony:

1) Discuss with the expert (even an experienced one) how important it is to slowly teach the judge and jury about the subject.

2) Practice. Even 15 minutes of practicing direct with a particular expert will give the expert an idea of what the attorney wants and it will give the attorney a good idea of what the expert needs to maximize his or her teaching skill.

3) Demonstrate the pace of the questions on direct examination so that the expert is trained to give short, simple responses.

4) Review the points the attorney wants to get out of direct. The reason this is so important is that some experts will speak in a direct, effective manner during a conversation about the case before trial. But when the formality of questioning begins, the expert will respond in a way that feels more formal and scientific for the expert. Unfortunately, what sounds formal and scientific to someone with a PhD will often sound vague, hedging and weak to the court system.

1. Introduce the expert to the judge and jury by establishing the expert’s “qualifications and stature.”

“Dr., please introduce yourself to the jury.

—What is it that you do for work?

—And is that why you’re here today?

—Are you being paid to be here today?

—Are you being paid more than your normal rate or less?

—Did I ask you to talk to Mr. Defendant?

—Did you talk to him?

—Did you perform any tests?

—Would you consider yourself to be an expert in administering and scoring tests like the MMPI?

—How much school did you have to become a psychologist?

—What kind of training have you had?

—What about your work experience?

—Are you licensed or certified by anyone?

—Do you have any education or training specific to the MMPI?

—How experienced are you at administering the MMPI?”

2. Introduce the evidence to the judge and jury and establish that the evidence is not “novel.”

“What does MMPI stand for?

[Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory]

—Is “inventory” another word for test?

—And this is a personality inventory?

—Does the word “personality” have a specific meaning in psychology?

—So what is a personality inventory?

—And what does it mean that this is a multiphasic inventory?

—Why is that important?

—And was this developed in Minnesota?

—How long has this test been around?

—Is there a more widely used personality inventory?

—How long has it been the premiere personality assessment?

—How does the test work?

—And what do you do during and after the test?

[Administration, Interpretation, Scoring]

—Can I find this test on the Internet?

[“No, in fact it’s only available to those who have gone through the special training and… Even in talking about the test today I won’t be able to reveal…”]

—Why do psychologists use this test?

—Why did you use the test in this case?

—Did you find the answers you were looking for?”

3. General acceptance in the field.

[1) Establish the appropriate field, 2) establish general agreement, and 3) hit on the use which has been made of the technique.]

“Are there minimum credentials for being trained to administer the MMPI?

—And then you have to go through a training?

—What does that training include?

—And are there continuing seminars?

—So, can you describe the field of people who actually administer this test?

—In that group of people, would you say that the MMPI is a controversial test?

—In that group of people is there general agreement that the MMPI is useful for some purposes?

—What are those purposes?

—And again, for what purpose did you use this test?

—What about in the broader world of psychologists generally, is this a controversial test?

—Are there a lot of psychologists who have never heard of the MMPI?

—Is there general agreement that the MMPI is useful for the purpose you used it?”

4. The existence of specialized literature.

“In addition to psychologists who administer the MMPI, are there also psychologists who study and research the MMPI?

Do those studies get published?

—Can you approximate the number of studies published on the MMPI?

—What is a meta-study?

—Are there meta-studies on the MMPI?

—What is peer review?

—Is there peer review for the MMPI?

—Is there literature regarding the administration of the MMPI?

—Is there literature regarding interpretation and how to improve your technique?

—Have you kept up-to-date with the literature?

—Is that a requirement for being licensed to work in your field?”

5. The potential rate of error and the extent to which the technique relies on the subjective interpretation of the expert.

“What does “margin of error” mean?

—Does the margin of error protect against a person administering the test badly?

—What is a bad administration error called?

—Are there safeguards in the administration of the test to protect against such errors?

—Are there safeguards in the interpretation and scoring of the test to protect against such errors?

—Who developed the standards for administering and scoring the test?

—What is reliability?

—Is this a reliable test?

—What is validity?

—Is this reliable test also a valid test?

—Can you tell me in a little more detail how the test is interpreted and scored?

—How much subjective interpretation is involved in interpreting and scoring?”

6. Bring it home.

“You gave this reliable test to Mr. Defendant?

—What did this reliable test tell you about Mr. Defendant?”

V. Working With Clients Who Have Mental Disorders

Defense attorneys working with mentally ill defendants are faced with diagnostic and therapeutic issues not likely covered in law school. The new attorney is often exposed to clients with pathology comparable to inpatient facilities. This section is designed to help provide a brief primer on dealing with clients who present with major mental illness. It should be recalled that many clients arrive at their attorney’s office with years of emotional turmoil and few skills to deal with the stress. On top of that, various studies have shown that a criminal case is one of the most stressful experiences a person can have. It is more stressful than divorce or the death of a loved one (statistically speaking). It should be remembered that in conjunction with criminal charges, the client may also face additional life stressors such as the loss of a spouse or family, unemployment, or mandated sobriety.

If the attorney suspects that her client has a mental illness, she might consider asking during the initial interview if the client takes any medications or has ever been to the hospital. That will provide clues as to other professional opinions of mental illness. But do not presume anything about a particular illness or diagnosis. Read the symptoms and react accordingly. A diagnosis may only lead you to presume symptoms that may not be present.

A question such as “How do you believe that I may be of help?” or “How have you spent your day so far?” may provide a valuable opportunity for rapport-building and gauging basic reality testing.

Clients with symptoms of a mental disorder: What follows is information about mental illness and how it might manifest itself in the attorney-client relationship. This is not meant to be an exhaustive treatise on the subject, but a place to begin. Through research and experience you can learn to communicate well with clients who are emotionally, psychologically, cognitively, and socially impaired.

A. Clients With Symptoms of Schizophrenia or Other Thought Disorders

The current theory of schizophrenia is that there is a whole spectrum of different manifestations of the illness, some of which never arise to the official diagnosis of schizophrenia. For example, Schizotypal Personality Disorder is a pervasive pattern of acute discomfort with close relationships as well as distortions of perception and eccentric behavior. The person may have social anxiety, strange or restricted emotions, odd behavior, unusual experiences, bizarre beliefs or peculiar speech and thinking. Schizoid Personality Disorder is a pattern of detachment from social relationships and a restricted range of emotions. Such a person usually chooses solitary activities, has little interest in sex or relationships, takes pleasure in few activities, appears indifferent to praise or criticism and seems cold and detached.

Where a schizophrenia spectrum disorder is suspected, you should balance questions between open-ended ones to get information and closed-ended questions to structure the conversation. One should avoid emotionally charged subject matter. Counsel should be mindful of not reinforcing bizarre ideas or inappropriate behaviors. Keep the topics simple, concise and clear, using signposts at the beginning of every topic change.

1. The Client Experiencing Delusions

Delusions are false beliefs which are firmly held in spite of incontrovertible and obvious evidence to the contrary. For example, a client may confide that they have been chosen by God. Or perhaps they’re a CIA agent. Treatment professionals working with patients with delusions are encouraged to utilize a frank and supportive attitude (First and Tasman, 2010). Open challenges or confrontation of delusional subject matter may create distrust between attorney and client. Delusional and psychotic disorders are often treated with medication. Inquiry into current and past medication is very important. To ascertain the nature and quality of the delusional content, collateral interviews with treatment providers and trusted family members can be quite informative.

2. The Disorganized Client

Another common characteristic in thought disorders such as schizophrenia is disorganized speech, behavior, and/or flat or inappropriate affect. A common symptom of schizophrenia is a tendency to rapidly and loosely shift between emotions (lability) or conversational topics (derailment). In the extreme, a client’s speech can sound like “word salad”: incomprehensible speech or thinking in which apparently random words are joined together in what seem like clauses and sentences. One should first assess whether the client is oriented to person, place or time. Simply ask them their name and if they know where they are and what the date is. If there’s no coherent response then there’s probably little point to extending the interview. A professional evaluation is necessary. If the client is somewhat oriented and able to track the conversation, then chop the topics into clear, short chunks. One shouldn’t be afraid to politely redirect the client back to the discrete topic at hand. For example, it’s not uncommon for a disorganized client to be able to answer your question directly and concisely but then without taking a breath seamlessly segue into a superficially related topic and then another. Interrupt the client by saying “Excuse me,” and politely tell the client that the information sounds important (it is if they think it is) and you’d like to hear it later but you need to talk about topic X for right now. First and Tasman emphasize the importance of consistency, acceptance, and appropriate levels of warmth. Given such impediments, the changing of attorneys should be discouraged if possible.

3. The Client Experiencing Hallucinations

The most common auditory hallucinations are of voices speaking to the person or maintaining a running commentary on the person. Eye contact may be threatening. But this is a person who is likely able to interact quite well. One should be cognizant that it’s very distracting to hear other people talking to you or about you while you’re trying to think or have a conversation. In such cases, it may be preferable to reschedule this type of client when he is better able to attend and track the conversation.

d. The Paranoid Client

Paranoid personality disorder is a pattern of distrust and suspiciousness such that others’ motives are interpreted as malevolent. This is a person who sees the world through victim-colored glasses. Regardless of the thought content, it should be remembered that this individual likely believes in its authenticity. There is nothing that you will be able to do to change the client’s perspective. Attempts to directly challenge the belief system may unduly impair the attorney-client relationship. When confronted by extraneous and overly suspicious subject matter, counsel is encouraged to say, “This may be true, but we should return to the topic at hand.” The paranoid client may read demeaning or threatening meanings into benign statements. She may be quick to react angrily or counterattack where there was no attack or criticism intended. Counsel should avoid being reactive and challenging of the client’s paranoid presentation. Legal information can be shared in a step-by-step manner. It may be advisable to speak to family members or an individual close to the client to ascertain how much or how little of the paranoid content is based on reality. In many cases, the paranoid client may be able to hold onto aspects of his beliefs while being able to focus on separate matters such as his legal case. Offering to put everything in writing may help provide a sense of security and relative trust in this type of client. Legal counsel may further bond with the paranoid client by making promises that can easily be kept. Then when possible follow through on them sooner and better than promised.

B. Clients With Symptoms of Mood Disorders Such as Major Depression and Bipolar Disorder

1. The Manic Client

First and foremost, clients experiencing a manic phase of their Bipolar condition should contract with a mental health professional for safety. A client presenting violent ideation should be taken at his word and efforts should be made to ensure client and community safety. An extremely elevated or grandiose client is not likely to thoughtfully engage in legal consultation. Manic episodes may range in length from 4 to 13 months. Thus, rescheduling a meeting due to manic presentation is not likely to change the results. In evaluation of GEI and mental state, an attorney should attempt to arrange a mental health evaluation as soon as possible to ensure continuity of the manic phase. This type of client may exhibit rapid, pressured speech and grandiose thinking. For example, she may believe that she possesses exceptional intelligence, power, wealth or connections. Here is an exception to the open-ended question rule. The client will move too fast and too tangentially unless you ask discrete, closed-ended questions. Keep the pace of the interview brisk, using themes that the client has raised. Remain firm and nonjudgmental, redirecting the client where needed. If the client is agitated, don’t take it personally.

It’s very important to distinguish the basis of the manic presentation. Individuals suffering from substance abuse disorders or anxiety may also exhibit similar symptoms. If counsel has concerns about the possibility of mania or hypomania, a nonthreatening observation such as “You seem excited about what we are discussing, maybe we should take a brief break and collect our thoughts.” If the client returns to the meeting comparably elevated, the chances of the client suffering from Bipolar Disorder (as opposed to, say, anxiety) is significantly greater. Given the nature of Bipolar Disorder, attorneys should be mindful to not escalate clients with provocative subject matter. It may be necessary to establish ground rules regarding communication at the beginning of the session. For example, counsel should relay early on that due to time limitation it may be necessary to interrupt and refocus the discussion. Client meetings should be very brief and task-focused. At the beginning of a session, the main goals of the meeting should be established and written down for the client. During instances when he becomes distracted or tangential, the written goals of the meeting can be easily referenced by counsel. In cases where the examinee may be agitated or very elevated, the attorney may consider configuring the meeting room so that the client has the seat closest to the door. This allows the individual to exit without having to feel restrained by anyone or anything. In such cases, the door may be left open or slightly ajar.

Some individuals with Bipolar Disorder may experience hypersexuality as part of their manic process. If hypersexuality is a concern, the attorney may consider having an assistant of the opposite gender present during client interactions. In general, it is not advisable to offer coffee or other caffeinated beverages. Brief meetings (20 minutes) may be favorable in order to avoid sensory overload. The attorney may wish to politely interrupt the tangential client, if one waits for a natural pause in the examinee’s soliloquy, he may be waiting for a very long time. In cases where mania is suspected but not formally diagnosed, evaluation and treatment should be strongly considered by counsel.

2. The Client Exhibiting Symptoms of Severe Depression

A severely depressed person may appear distraught and tearful or flat, unemotional and lethargic. Symptoms of depression may include tearfulness, emptiness, diminished pleasure or interest in life, significant weight loss or weight gain, insomnia or excessive sleeping, slowed physical reflexes, fatigue, loss of energy, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, and inability to think, concentrate or make decisions. The presentation of symptoms of major depression raises concerns around suicide potential. Suicide risk may be assessed by nonclinicians through inquiry about suicidal ideation, extreme feelings of hopelessness, and past suicide attempts. Identified by the 1993 Depression Guideline Patterns, the following factors are associated with suicide (quoted in First and Tasman): 1) history of prior episodes of depression, 2) family history of depressive disorder especially in first-degree relatives, 3) history of suicide attempts, 4) female gender, 5) client’s age is under 40, 6) postpartum period, 7) comorbid medical illness, 8) absence of social support, 9) negative, stressful life events (i.e., prospect of long-term incarceration), and 10) active alcohol or substance abuse.

Regardless of suicidality, it is not advisable to present overly pessimistic or worst-case scenarios. The client may hear you saying something that is a level or two worse than what you are actually saying (also known as “catastrophizing”). When possible, the attorney is encouraged to reassure the client as best as the situation allows. There is also likely to be some slowed information processing. So you should slow your own pace and keep the questions simple. Individuals suffering from major depression may experience deficits in short-term memory processing. Thus, it’s advisable to take notes for the client and/or inform an individual from her support system with information that can be shared later.

C. Personality Disorders

Personality disorders such as Narcissistic, Borderline, and Antisocial are likely to be encountered in criminal practices. Due to similarities and comorbidity inherent in these diagnoses, many of the approaches which apply to one may be relevant and helpful in the other two.

1. The client Experiencing Symptoms of Antisocial Personality Disorder

In the mental health world, a diagnosis of ASPD is one of the most negative labels that can be affixed to a client. In some cases, ASPD may be a misdiagnosis provided to an individual for excessive involvement in the legal system. For other individuals, ASPD may be a guise for the more extreme psychopathy or sociopathy. As many experienced criminal defense attorneys will tell you, a large percentage of criminal defendants fit all or the majority of the DSM-IV-TR criteria for ASPD.1 Repeated criminal activity, bad planning and irresponsibility describes about 90 percent of our clients. For the purpose of this manual, we’re talking about a relatively rare person: the cold, callous, entitled conman interested only in what he can get. He will attempt to manipulate you or pull you into a power struggle if he thinks it can gain him some advantage. Rules which apply to working with real or possible ASPD may just as easily be applied to all clients. For example, for the ASPD client, rigidity to rules and practices is especially important.

For individuals who have been diagnosed with ASPD, efforts should be made to clearly define your role as legal counsel, the rules of engagement, and the consequences for noncompliance. It may be helpful to verbally explain office policies and informed consent even if the individual has provided her signature. Issues around missed appointments or late payment should be dealt with immediately. Any consequence discussed during the initial meeting should be enforced. Send the client a letter documenting important client decisions or attorney advice. This tactic may help provide data if the client attempts to manipulate verbal information. In the case of true ASPD or sociopathy, it may be important for counsel to distinguish himself as the authority. Common methods of rapport-building such as sharing personal information or human weaknesses are generally contraindicated. Such information may be used internally or externally to usurp power and control in the professional relationship.

2. Borderline Personality Disorder

Along with ASPD, Borderline Personality Disorder carries with it many conceptions and misconceptions about anticipated behavior. Comparable to ASPD, boundaries are of the utmost importance. Early in the legal relationship, counsel should consider discussing the unique nature of her role as well as office policies and procedures. Attorneys are encouraged to remain steadfast in their goals of providing criminal defense. A client with diagnosed BPD may attempt to develop a personal or sexual relationship with counsel. Interactions between client and counsel should be especially brief and goal-directed. The sharing of personal information may serve as the means to blurring boundaries and altering the balance of power within this professional relationship. An attorney should anticipate the possibility of being placed on a pedestal as well as being vilified. Attorneys vulnerable to sexual innuendo or invitation may be challenged by this type of personality disorder.

Attorneys should be cognizant of suicidality related to BPD. In certain cases, it may be advisable to establish a hard boundary and report suicidal ideation to proper authorities. Given related issues of sexuality associated with BPD, use of an assistant of the opposite gender could be considered. Sexual jokes, overly intimate conversation, or physical displays of affection (hugs) should be strictly forbidden. Written documentation of meetings and directives should be thoroughly documented. Impeccable record keeping may serve to enhance boundaries, reinforce the legal mission, and provide defense to counter possible allegations of ethical violations.

3. Narcissistic Personality Disorder

Individuals diagnosed with NPD often believe that they can only be understood by persons of comparable intelligence and talent. During the initial meeting, legal counsel may explain their educational and experiential qualifications. It may also help to configure one’s office so that the client faces the wall with the diplomas and commendations. Attorneys should avoid becoming embroiled in intellectual conflicts. Boundaries around legally germane subject matter should be strictly enforced. In the case of NPD as well as BPD, an important thing to remember is to not take any of their statements or actions personally. Understand that they see the world, including you and your actions, through a lens which distorts reality toward their own rejection.

D. The Anxious, Panicked or Traumatized Client

This can happen when the client has a panic or anxiety disorder or it can happen when a person is withdrawing from a powerfully addictive drug like heroin. Both types of client may present as desperate for and fixated on some immediate outcome. In both cases, the only thing to do is be patiently firm in telling them that you will do your best to accomplish the outcome but things take time and don’t always work. The anxious client may require extra time at the beginning of the legal relationship for reassurance. Legal counsel is encouraged to ask the client what is making him anxious and then address it honestly and directly. Because anxiety often takes root in the fear of the unknown, counsel is encouraged to ask the client if there is anything that can be done or clarified to help allay fears. In such cases, the attorney may wish to avail themselves to telephone calls.

Clients who appear anxious or who have been exposed to recent trauma may benefit from efforts by the attorney to allow them to control the flow of information and to take breaks when necessary. In particular, the individual should be assured that they may excuse themselves from your office at anytime. Attorney-client consultations may evoke and rekindle past trauma. It may be advisable to arrange for the client to be around their social support following an intense meeting. In some cases, the traumatized or anxious client may be comforted by having a loved one present when discussing provocative aspects of their case. If it appears to counsel that the client has become panicked, she should help encourage this individual to engage in deep diaphragmatic breathing. For individuals with recent trauma, it may be advisable to invite an assistant of the opposite sex to sit in at the meeting. This gesture should be at the complete discretion of the client.

VI. Framing Mental Health Issues in the Criminal Arena

=

A frame is a larger metaphorical story into which your trial story or argument fits so that it is immediately and instinctively understood. For example, if I tell you a “rags to riches” story, you will know where I’m going and fill in the gaps before I get there. In fact, this idea of “pulling yourself up by your bootstraps” is so powerful a story in American life that politicians, whose life stories are their currency, will generally go to any length to frame their lives as self-made, rather than a product of birth or luck. For example, Jeb Bush, when running for Governor of Florida, consistently referred to himself as a self-made man despite the fact that he was the son of a President, the brother of the President and the grandson of an incredibly wealthy Wall Street tycoon. Such is the nature of this particular metaphor that it instinctively made sense to Florida voters despite the factual absurdity of it. Framing is really just a matter of making your words feel instinctively right because at a deeper level what you’re saying fits with what people already know and believe about the world.

A. How One Frames a Story

How one frames a story is often more important to the story than the story itself. That is, an identical story framed two different ways will trigger two completely separate (and sometimes diametrically opposed) responses in the listener. For example, when doctors were asked about the level of risk they would take if they were implementing a national flu program, it made all the difference whether the question took the perspective of the number of people that lived or the number of people that died. When doctors were asked the following question, 72 percent of them chose the safe strategy:

The U.S. is preparing for the outbreak of an unusual Asian disease, which is expected to kill 600 people. Two alternative programs to combat the disease have been proposed. Assume that the exact scientific estimates of the consequences of the programs are as follows: If program A is adopted, 200 people will be saved. If program B is adopted, there is a one-third probability that 600 people will be saved and a two-thirds probability that no people will be saved. Which of the two programs would you favor?

When doctors were asked the following question 78 percent of them chose the risky strategy:

The U.S. is preparing for the outbreak of an unusual Asian disease, which is expected to kill 600 people. Two alternative programs to combat the disease have been proposed. Assume that the exact scientific estimates of the consequences of the programs are as follows: If program C is adopted, 400 people will die. If program D is adopted, there is a one-third probability that nobody will die and a two-thirds probability that 600 people will die. Which of the two programs would you favor?

The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice, Kahneman and Tversky, Science, 1981. Note that in both stories, the number of people that will live and die are exactly the same. Yet you can switch the decision of the majority of people by simply switching the frame. Is this a story about people dying or is this a story about people being saved? Neither story is more or less honest than the other. But it’s a basic trait of human nature that we have an aversion to loss such that we are willing to take significantly more risks to avoid loss than to gain anything, even lives.

Now take this idea into the adversarial world and think about how important framing is to your objective. If you were trying to sell someone on the risky strategy and you were using the frame of saving people you would probably be sabotaging your cause, no matter how impassioned, articulate and knowledgeable you were on the subject. It just wouldn’t feel right. You don’t gamble with people’s lives. On the other hand, if you were trying to sell someone on the safe strategy using the frame of people dying, it also wouldn’t feel right. People are dying; we have to do what it takes.

Even worse, if you didn’t use any frame at all, you would sound muddled and confused, mixing together living and dying and saving and safe and risky and blah, blah, blah. If the frame is unclear, it is exceedingly difficult to understand the essential point because our instincts aren’t leading us anywhere except to distrust of the speaker.

In every jury trial, there are essential framing decisions about whether this is a story about the victim or the defendant; a story about the actions of the defendant or the defendant himself; a story in which the defendant is “one of us” or “one of them”; a story in which the defendant let us down or in which we, as a community, let the defendant down, and many, many others. In each of these cases, the frame will tell the story and guide the result as much as the underlying facts of the story itself.

B. Stickiness

Framing is also important to make the defense stick in people’s memory. Whether an idea, argument, story or a label is “sticky” has everything to do with how hard people have to work to understand the concept. If the argument is consistent with what the listener already believes, it will be easy to understand. If a story resonates as having an essential truth, it will be easy to understand. If a label is consistent with a prevailing generalization about a particular group of people, it will be easier to understand. Again, we’re not talking about being dishonest in any way. We’re only talking about shifting the frame of understanding. For example, is the defendant’s mental illness an essential aspect of the defendant as a person, like an allergy or a propensity for nasty hangovers? Or is the mental illness an attacker that does nothing to define who the defendant is as a person, like cancer or Alzheimer’s Disease? Neither is more or less true, medically speaking. And the position never needs to be specifically stated. It will get built into the language of the story and the way in which it’s told. The frame, in this case, will determine the “truth” of responsibility and blame because, where the illness is an external agent, there is now something else to blame—the mental illness, and secondarily whoever allowed it grow stronger and more dangerous—the officer, a mental health provider, or even us as a society.

If you question why stickiness is important to a jury trial, consider this recent research: Imagine you’ve signed up to be a subject in a study. You’re given a multidigit number to memorize and told that you need to hold that number in your mind while you walk out of the room down a hallway and into another room where you will write it down. So down the hall you proceed, recounting the number in your head, until you’re stopped by one of the researchers holding a plate of cake and fruit. She tells you that as a thank you gift for participating in the study you may take either a piece of chocolate cake or some fruit. 

 

Do you know which one you would choose? Cake or fruit? It turns out that if you are like the great majority of subjects, the answer is that it depends on the number you’re memorizing. If you are memorizing a seven-digit number then you will most likely choose the cake. If you’re memorizing a two-digit number then you will probably choose the fruit.

To understand why a seven-digit number will cause you to act differently than a two-digit number requires knowing something about the human brain. The brain can loosely be divided into an unconscious, automatic system that has developed over millions of years and a conscious, rational system that developed fairly recently. The unconscious system does the vast majority of our perceiving and thinking. The conscious system is feeble and easily overwhelmed. In fact, seven digits are about what it takes to completely consume the capacity of the conscious mind. That’s why the variable part of phone numbers is seven digits. If you’re stopped and offered cake or fruit while your conscious, rational brain is tied up, you will choose with your unconscious system built on thousands of millennia of experience. That is, you will choose the gooey, sweet choice that the body craves. You will choose the cake even though your rational mind, were it not taken up with seven digits, would come up with a dozen reasons why a bunch of empty calories and a huge sugar rush in the middle of the day is a terrible idea. “Heart and Mind in Conflict: Interplay of Affect and Cognition in Consumer Decision Making,” Baba Shiv, Journal of Consumer Research (1999). See also, How We Decide, Jonah Lehrer (2009).

Now imagine that you’re brought to an unfamiliar, very formal setting and told that you’re going to have to make a very important choice deeply affecting the lives of others. You’re given hundreds of pieces of information but it’s doled out in a disorganized, incomplete and conflicting manner. You’re given dozens of rules by which your decision is governed but you’re given no training and little guidance as to how to use the rules. You’re flooded with arcane and unfamiliar terms and jargon that everyone except you seems to understand. You are a juror. 

A juror is given far more than seven digits worth of information. That means that, to the extent the rational system is working at all, it is coming up with reasons to support a decision which has been made by the unconscious, automatic system. On the one hand, this is a dismal state of affairs. The criminal justice system is premised on the idea that jurors are fully capable of sorting through all the information and rules and coming up with the right decision. On the other hand, it presents an enormous opportunity for trial attorneys to persuade the unconscious, automatic brain directly. If you don’t attempt to structure your arguments so they’re persuasive to the unconscious brain then you are probably missing the basis of the jury’s decision entirely. 

C .Context

It is not so difficult to put facts and arguments in a context so the automatic mind of the juror will shade them the way you want them to be shaded. Take the fundamental attribution error. It is a social, psychological concept that is beyond dispute and at the heart of many criminal cases. Despite the fact that ignorance of the concept will lead you to forsake winning arguments and may even unwittingly cause you to persuade the jury of your client’s guilt, there are very few attorneys aware of the concept.

The fundamental attribution error is that we simultaneously believe that other people’s actions are indicative of their character while we also know that our own actions are situational. When somebody cuts you off while driving, you think to yourself,“What a jerk—that guy is a bad driver.” You don’t think, “That guy is probably distracted—maybe he’s on his way to the hospital because something happened to his wife.” You see the action and you attribute a character flaw. One time I was telling a friend about a manslaughter case in our office. The client had been distracted, ran a red light and killed someone. He was in no way impaired, he had a perfect driving record and was cooperative and appropriately remorseful with the police. There was no evidence of negligence, much less recklessness. My friend said, “I don’t know. It’s just really, really difficult for me to believe that the guy wasn’t a terrible driver all the time.” That’s the fundamental attribution error. The guy did this thing. Therefore, he’s like that all the time. Therefore, he’s a reckless, dangerous person who needs to be stopped. This thinking happens automatically.

Once one understands the underlying dynamic, it is possible to counter the effect by 1) offering as much evidence as possible that the client is normally a responsible, safe driver, 2) by offering as much evidence as possible that the client is a responsible, safe person, and 3) least intuitively, by introducing the jury to the client as a person. After all, we don’t make the fundamental attribution error about people we know. We only do it in a vacuum of knowledge about a person.

The point here is that the fundamental attribution error is a useful shortcut that allows us to perceive the world correctly a large portion of the time. We simultaneously believe that actions are and are not indicative of character, depending on the frame, just like we overwhelmingly prefer the safe strategy and the risky strategy depending on the frame. Consider three additional examples:

1. Availability deals with predictions of likelihood. It says that when an event is familiar or recent it seems more likely. So, for example, people assume that homicides are far more likely than they are in comparison to suicides because we often hear about homicides but we rarely hear about suicides. Same thing goes for deaths in plane crashes versus auto accidents. And right after 9/11 it seemed like terrorists were everywhere. Likelihood of an event is important because it determines how afraid a person is of an event and how much risk a person is willing to take in relation to the risk. The more risk, the more a juror is likely to shade their unconscious decision making away from the risk as a precaution. The most obvious application of the Availability error is with the risk of false conviction. It is possible through voir dire, opening and closing to make the chance of false conviction seem familiar, salient and, therefore likely. Just get people talking about it in voir dire in relation to news stories people have read. Talk about it yourself in opening and closing in the context of why the burden of proof is so high. The jurors will instinctively make decisions to avoid that risk. This also works with mental illness or the deficits in community mental health or horrifying stories about police interacting with people exhibiting symptoms of mental illness or distress. Get people talking about it to bring the stories to the forefront and then, with your language, frame your story to mirror the common theme.

2. Status Quo Biasmeans that people select the default option the great majority of the time. Lots of television executive resources are spent on what show should follow a popular show because so few people are willing to take the step of pushing the remote control button to effectuate change. Given the choice to pull the trigger or do nothing, we will choose nothing. The presumption of innocence is ripe for a status quo framing. After all, the presumption of innocence says that the default option is not guilty. Only if the State proves the case beyond a reasonable doubt should the jury pull the lever for guilt. Otherwise, they should do nothing. Unfortunately, if we don’t frame it this way then the jury instructions will make it seem like the jury has to make a choice between option A and option B, either of which is pulling the lever. That makes it much easier for the jury to choose guilt.

3. Hindsight Bias:“Hindsight bias refers to people’s tendency to view an event as more inevitable and foreseeable after the event has occurred (Fischhoff, 1975, 1977; Hawkins & Hastie, 1990; Leary, 1982). Knowing the outcome of an uncertain event makes that outcome seem to have been more likely in the first place (i.e., in hindsight), compared to likelihood estimates made before knowing the outcome (i.e., in foresight). This retrospective distortion presumably results from an automatic cognitive assimilation of new and old information (Fischhoff, 1977).” Bryant and Brockway, 1997.

Thus, for example in the APSO/blame the cop case, the jury will see the sequence of events and presume they were inevitable and foreseeable. If the officer should have foreseen that his actions would create an incident then the officer is far more blameworthy than the defendant, who was largely being controlled by the external force that is his mental illness. It’s all about responsibility and the officer is responsible for making choices that led to the foreseeable result. 

4. Additional Framing

There are many more thinking errors and shortcuts that are relevant to criminal trials, each of which lends itself to framing in a particular direction. Some major categories include schemas(the idea that a word like “doctor,” “store” or “cop” comes with a mental picture and lots of details that will be assumed unless specifically contradicted), picture completion (the fact that the human brain fills in the gaps whenever it has incomplete information, allowing us to imply what’s in the gaps and let the jury run with it), loss aversion (losing something hurts about twice as much as gaining feels good), the Lake Wobegon problem (that 90 percent of people rate themselves above average in most domains), and representativeness (aka stereotyping).

In addition to storytelling and stickiness, there’s a more subtle scientific reason why it’s so important to frame a story or argument in a way that’s consistent (rather than inconsistent) with what the listener already “knows” about the world. The primary method of learning for both primates and humans is through positive and negative prediction errors. When we’re correct we get a rush of dopamine that makes us feel good. When we’re incorrect we get a negative prediction error signal, a drop in dopamine, that feels bad.

D. Methods of Framing

There are three general methods of framing: broadening the frame, narrowing the frame, and shifting the frame.

1. Broadening the Frame

The first possibility is to broaden the frame from portrait to landscape. The state usually wants to fight the battle of stories on the ground of the specific incident,—what happened during a two-hour period, for example, that constitutes the charged crime. The defense attorney should do whatever possible to pan back to a larger narrative that explains how this person came to be in this situation. This also means shifting from a focus on the individual to a more complex understanding of the development of the defendant as a person, the family systems in which he became who he is and the community and governmental systems that may or may not hold some immediate responsibility for “what happened.”

2. Narrowing the Frame

The second possibility, not mutually exclusive from the first, is to narrow the frame of what happened from a cold, distant objectivity to a close-up of understanding, empathetic subjectivity. In cases involving mental illness, it is essential that the jury understand what it is like to be the defendant. In the absence of such understanding, they will substitute in their healthy minds and ask what they would have been doing if they had done what the defendant did.

3. Shifting the Frame

To start off with, the attorney must consciously shift or choose a single frame when there are two mutually exclusive possibilities. Like saving people versus letting people die, there are lots of situations where we believe both things, just not at the same time. The biggest one for the purposes of this book is to shift the frame of the illness as something internal to something external, like we all would if a person was dying of cancer. The first step is to rid your language of any terms that equate the person with the disease. For example, saying a person is schizophrenic is like saying a person is cancerous. The person in that situation is nothing more than the disease. Instead, you can say that the person has been diagnosed with cancer or schizophrenia.

Then, use active verbs to refer to the disease as if it were an autonomous agent. For example, the schizophrenia “attacked” the person or “wanted” the person to do something.

Finally, shift the blame and “personal responsibility” to the illness. The key to this third step is to make the mental illness a character in your client’s story of innocence. The defendant is a hollow vessel through which the outside force of the mental illness acted. The mental illness must appear to be independently responsible for the bad act. You may want to refer to the mental illness directly, anthropomorphizing and externalizing it. For example, “It grabbed his mind and wouldn’t let go, squeezing harder and harder on his brain until there was nothing there except what It demanded.” Or, “It created a fog and draped it over the defendant’s mind so that she couldn’t think straight.” The key to successful externalizing is the use of active verbs like “grabbed,” “squeezing” and “created” which imply that the defendant was unable to resist the force of the mental illness. In cases where the crime is more serious, counsel may even want to consider implying that the mental illness had some sort of criminal intent toward the defendant by use of phrases such as “hijacking” or “kidnapping” the defendant’s mind, or “deviously manipulating” the defendant’s perception.

VIII. Conclusion

It is my fervent hope that this book will provide the inspiration, fodder or knowledge for attorneys to seize back the power in cases where the client has symptoms of mental illness or cognitive issues. Early in this chapter I set forth a series of steps for systematically approaching such cases. But the preceding section sets forth an equally important step that is bigger than any one legal case: understand the broader context of the shift of folks from the mental health system to the criminal justice system. Unless your case is very serious, it is unlikely to have been charged as a crime just a couple decades ago. Understand that the state’s case likely depends on an underlying assumption or inference that the defendant has a healthy mind. Understand that the mental health system has collapsed and that necessary supports and medication were probably not available to your client; that acute mental health crises are built into our current mental health model. Understand and be outraged. Preserve evidence of your client’s symptoms at the very beginning of the case. Track down collateral evidence and witnesses that will help make the defendant’s story come alive. Be creative with defenses and evidence. Push back against the state’s attempts to punish a person for having a mental illness.

The following chapters in this manual will expand upon the subjects of fitness to proceed/ competency, a disordered mental state, guilt except for insanity, dangerousness, pursuing a civil commitment, clinical assessments, and cross-examining the mental health expert. By studying these concepts in greater depth, you will be better able to represent your clients and defend them in the face of a broken mental health system which has abdicated responsibility.
