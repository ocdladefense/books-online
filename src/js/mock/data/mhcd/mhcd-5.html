Civil Commitment

Definition

Civil commitment is the process by which a county (in the name of the State of Oregon) attempts to involuntarily hospitalize a person with a mental disorder or mental retardation for the safety of the person or the community. By statute, the person who the state is trying to commit is called an “allegedly mentally ill person” (or AMIP). “Mentally ill person” is a legal conclusion which means that a person has a mental disorder (like schizophrenia or bipolar disorder) or mental retardation that is causing an imminent risk of harm to the person or the community. ORS 426.005(1)(e); ORS 427.215. “Involuntary commitment or recommitment to a mental institution involves a massive curtailment of liberty and, consequently, invokes due process protections.” State v. Johansen, 125 Or App 365 (1993). “The mere desirability of institutionalization for a person’s condition is insufficient for commitment.” State v. Johnson, 8 Or App 263 (1972).

Twelve Elements You Need To Know About Civil Commitments

1. The Timeline

The statutory timelines are short for everyone.If, as is true in most cases, the allegedly mentally ill person (AMIP will be used throughout this chapter as shorthand) is involuntarily held in the hospital then there must be a hearing within five judicial days after the hold begins. ORS 426.095(2)(a)(b). After the hold (aka the Notice of Mental Illness) is written on day zero, the investigator has only three additional days to recommend a diversion, recommend a hearing or drop the hold. Most cases do not go to hearing. In Multnomah County, for example, only about 10 percent of holds end in a hearing. If a case is recommended for hearing, the defense attorney is appointed and notified on the morning of day four, at least 24 hours before the hearing is scheduled. Unless the AMIP consents to a setover, the hearing must be held on day five. If the hearing is to be held on day six or (or later), the court must dismiss the case because it essentially has lost jurisdiction after day five. State v. Bradsteen, 196 Or App 634 (2004).

2. Ultimate Issues

A. By Clear and Convincing Evidence

  • Does the allegedly mentally ill person (AMIP) have a mental disorder?
  • Is the AMIP likely not to survive in the near future as a result of being unable to acquire basic needs?
  • Is the AMIP dangerous to self?
  • Is the AMIP dangerous to other people?
  • Is the mental disorder causing the dangerousness or inability to survive or might there be an independent reason?
  • Does the AMIP meet the statutory requirements for chronic mental illness under ORS 426.005(1)(e)?
  • Older than 18.
  • Diagnosed with chronic mood or psychotic disorder.
  • Twice hospitalized in last three years.
  • Substantially similar behavior or symptoms each time.
  • Unless treated, the person will continue, to a reasonable medical probability, to deteriorate to the point of being dangerous or unable to survive.

B. By a Preponderance of the Evidence

  • Is the AMIP willing and able to participate in voluntary treatment?
  • Will the AMIP probably participate in voluntary treatment?
  • In State v. T.M., 229 Or App 325, 211 P3d 359 (2009), the Court of Appeals settled the question of who bears the burden to show that the AMIP is able to participate in and would benefit from voluntary outpatient treatment. The burden rests on the AMIP to show by a preponderance of the evidence that he or she can access and will participate in voluntary treatment.

C. Without a Burden of Proof

  • Is there a conditional release that is available and acceptable?
  • Has the county mental health program made an outpatient commitment available?
  • Should the mentally ill person be committed for up to 180 days or should the maximum term of commitment be set at a lower amount?

3. Mental Illness

“Mentally ill” is a statutory term meaning that the allegedly mentally ill person is committable. ORS 426.005(1)(e) states that mentally ill person” means a person who, because of a mental disorder, is one or more of the following:

(A) Dangerous to self or others

(B) Unable to provide for basic personal needs and not receiving such care as is necessary for health or safety

(C) A person who:

(i) Is chronically mentally ill, as defined in ORS 426.495

(ii) Within the previous three years, has twice been placed in a hospital or approved inpatient facility by the division under ORS 426.060

(iii) Is exhibiting symptoms or behavior substantially similar to those that preceded and led to one or more of the hospitalizations or inpatient placements referred to in sub-subparagraph (ii) of this subparagraph; and

(iv) Unless treated, will continue, to a reasonable medical probability, to physically or mentally deteriorate so that the person will become a person described under either or both subparagraph (A) or (B) of this paragraph.

4. Clear and Convincing Evidence

The burden of proof is clear and convincing evidence. “ORS 426.130(1) provides that a court may subject a person to involuntary commitment if the state shows, by clear and convincing evidence, that the person is mentally ill.” State v. Shorett, 194 Or App 587 (2004). “Clear and convincing evidence” is evidence of “extraordinary persuasiveness,” such that the “truth of the facts asserted is highly probable.” State v. RH, 212 Or App 479 (2007).

5. Mental Disorder—One Element of Mental Illness

“Mental disorder” is a diagnosis. There is no definition of the term “mental disorder” in statute or case law. However, the statutory context of the term makes it clear that “mental disorder” is supposed to be a medically diagnosable condition. The authoritative clinical text on mental disorders is the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (the DSM, its most current edition from 1994 known as the DSM-IV, or the “text revision” of 2000 as the DSM-IV-TR). The DSM-IV lays out very specific definitions and criteria that must be met for particular mental disorders to be diagnosed. One interesting aspect of civil commitment hearings is that rarely enough evidence is found to make a proper diagnosis pursuant to the DSM-IV. For example, in order to diagnose schizophrenia, the clinician (in this case the court examiner) must find, among other things, that certain symptoms have been present for a significant portion of a one-month period and continuous signs of the disturbance for a six-month period. Often, the examiners have access to no more information than the week prior to the hearing but presume the presence of symptoms for the necessary longer time periods. It is incumbent on the defense attorney to hold the examiners to their clinical duty to make a proper diagnosis and to their statutory duty to find the presence of a mental disorder to be “highly probable.” There are certainly hearings in which the presence of a mental disorder is so obvious that to focus on the lack of medical evidence would be a strategic mistake. But there are also many hearings in which examiners use diagnostic shortcuts to reach conclusions they would not make in their own practice with the same amount of information. Defense counsel should expose these shortcuts and remind the court that we should be more careful, not less, when a person’s liberty is at stake.

6. Evidence of Danger to Others Is Relevant Only to the Extent It Is Predictive of Future Behavior

Evidence of dangerousness must be predictive of actual harm in the near future. Evidence of actual violence, actual harm or threats of violence may be sufficient to establish to a reasonable medical probability that the AMIP is presently a danger to self or others if they are predictive of harm in the near future. See, e.g., State v. Miller, 198 Or App 153 (2005):

In determining whether a person poses a danger to others, it is appropriate for a court to consider the testimony of mental health experts, a person’s past acts, and the person’s apparent condition at the time of the hearing. State v. Furnish, 86 Or App 194, 197 (1987). Past threats coupled with violent acts may constitute clear and convincing evidence that a person is dangerous to others if “there is no indication that such violence is an isolated occurrence.” State v. Allmendinger, 36 Or App 381, 384 (1978). “But a mere recitation of past acts, in the absence of a showing that such clearly forms the foundation for a prediction of dangerousness, cannot serve as the basis for a finding that one is a ‘mentally ill person’.” State v. Lucas, 31 Or App 947, 950 (1977).

[This case is distinguished in State v. D.L., 122 P3d 97, but not in a way that matters for this point.] For example, in State v. Siebold, 100 Or App 365 (1990), the court reversed a commitment where the AMIP had recently crashed his car while distracted by the voices in his head. There was no evidence that the crash was anything more than an isolated incident, therefore there was insufficient evidence that the AMIP would do something similarly dangerous in the near future.

7. Evidence of Danger to Self Is Relevant Only to the Extent It Is Predictive of Future Behavior

If past actions which seem risky are not proven harmful then they are weak evidence of dangerousness. For example, if an AMIP has a propensity for licking the floor, one’s visceral instinct should be that it’s a really bad idea, or at least that it’s revolting. But what exactly is the harm? Can a layperson confidently say that licking the floor is likely to be harmful? Even if one is confident that, say, floor bacteria is likely to cause significant damage in the near future, bacteria and its transmissive and harm-causing properties are scientific evidence requiring an expert in such things. In the absence of an expert the most that can be said is that the AMIP is likely to engage in future behavior which may or may not be harmful. Stated another way, do not presume that apparently risky conduct was likely to result in harm. “A person can be deemed dangerous to self if he or she has established a pattern in the past of taking certain actions that lead to self-destructive conduct, and then he or she begins to follow the pattern again.… However, the state must present clear and convincing evidence that a person’s mental disorder has resulted in harm or created situations likely to result in harm.” State v. Judd,206 Or App 146(2006). The following apparently risky actions were not evidence of dangerousness because there was no evidence that they were likely to result in harm:

  • Hitting one’s head hard against a Plexiglas window for several minutes. State v. Powell, 178 Or App 89 (2001).
  • Riding one’s bike nude in an unsafe area. State v. Webb, 186 Or App 404 (2003).
  • Swimming nude in 40-degree weather. State v. Hambleton, 202 Or App 526 (2005).
  • Picking at open wounds. State v. Turel, 182 Or App 235 (2002).

However, the Court of Appeals has recently been focusing on an AMIP’s pattern of placing his or herself in “harm’s way.” A number of risky behaviors that are likely to result in harm can be used to establish that an AMIP is dangerous to self because he or she cannot keep out of harm’s way. See State v. Judd, 206 Or App 146, 135 P3d 397 (2006). These “harm’s way” commitments should be challenged because they are often based on speculation that an AMIP will place him or herself in danger.

8. Basic Needs

“Basic needs” commitments are about whether the person will survive if released. The “basic needs” criterion for commitment has two prongs under ORS 426.005(1)(e) First, the state must prove that the person is “unable to provide for basic personal needs.” Second, the state must prove that the person “is not receiving such care as is necessary for health and safety.” The standard “focuses on the capacity of the individual to survive, either through his own resources or with the help of family and friends. The state must establish by clear and convincing evidence that the individual, due to a mental disorder, is unable to obtain some commodity (e.g., food and water) or service (e.g., life-saving medical care) without which he cannot sustain life.” State v. Jayne, 174 Or App 74 (2001). The question, more succinctly, is whether the person is likely not to survive in the near future. State v. Kasper, 203 Or App 797 (2006).

9. Plans

A plan is really just a demonstration by the AMIP that he or she has thought through the issues and has some idea of how to prevent another hospitalization (or worse) in the near future. The word “plan” comes up in almost every civil commitment context. The two most likely scenarios are the safety plan for AMIPs with suicidal thoughts and a basic needs plan for providing for essential, life-sustaining needs.

An AMIP who was hospitalized for suicidality will have to answer the question: what will be different if you feel like killing yourself in the future? The plan simply needs to reflect something different about the situation: a different person that the AMIP is comfortable calling or a different living situation or a trigger which has subsided.

A basic needs plan should always be presented even if it seems, on its face, to be grossly insufficient.The appellate court has set a very low bar for AMIPs to jump: “When an individual can articulate a reasonable plan for obtaining the necessities of life and has a demonstrable ability to support himself or herself, a civil commitment is inappropriate because there is no clear and convincing evidence that the individual cannot provide for his or her basic needs.… A mentally ill person has a sufficient plan where she simply ‘recognizes the necessity of some activity on [her] part to provide for [her] safe survival. . . Furthermore, we have also held that ‘although the lack of certain shelter is not a good plan, we cannot say that homelessness by itself is sufficient grounds for commitment.’” State v. Pierce, 205 Or App 52 (2006). Thus, establishing a basic needs plan is important but it may mean little more than making sure the client states on the record his or her recognition of the importance of eating and drinking liquids and sleeping in a place that is not overtly dangerous. If the client can articulate some places where he or she might acquire food, water or reasonably safe shelter then the case is even better.

10. Mental Status on the Day of the Hearing Should Be the Focus of the Hearing

The court must find that the person is “mentally ill” at the time of the hearing. Evidence that a person was dangerous or suicidal or unable to care for basic needs one or two weeks before the hearing is only relevant to the extent that it shows the person is still dangerous or unsafe at the time of the hearing. “Whether a person falls within the definition of mentally ill for the purposes of involuntary commitment is determined by his condition at the time of the commitment hearing as understood in the context of his history.” State v. King, 177 Or App 373 (2001).

11. Voluntary Treatment = Release

A judge must release a mentally ill person who is able and willing to voluntarily treat. The AMIP has burden of proof under ORS 426.130(1)(b) to show that he or she is able and willing to voluntarily treat. If the AMIP satisfies that burden then involuntary commitment is not “necessary” and the person must be discharged. State v. M.C.M.,212 Or App 158 (2007).

12. Appellate Review Is De Novo

Cases are reviewed de novoby the Court of Appeals. This means that cases are much more likely to be reversed if arguments are properly preserved. It also means that the appellate courts are both elucidating the law and demonstrating the process by which the decisions of the trial judge should be made.

Discussion

I. The Bigger Picture

A. A Brief History of Civil Commitment

Since its advent in the 16th century, civil commitment has always been a legislative creation with authority to act derived from the government’s police and parens patriaepowers. Parens patriae, or the parental authority of the state, is used to protect people from themselves, whereas the police power is used to protect the community from dangerous people. The states are vested with the historic parens patriaepower, including the duty to protect “persons under legal disabilities to act for themselves.” O’Connor v. Donaldson, 422 US 563 (1975). Civil commitments involve police power when the state is involuntarily confining a person who is dangerous to others. But the state uses its parental power when involuntarily confining a person who is dangerous to themselves or whose life is in danger because of an inability to acquire basic needs like food or water. In courtroom practice, there is little legal differentiation between the two separate sources of power when depriving people their freedom, but a different energy and emotion is attached to the different hearings. Where police power is being used, a certain ineffable similarity occurs to criminal cases. For example, accusations of specific dangerous behavior must be contested and potential (or actual) victims may be present. There also seems to be a lower tolerance for risk when others are in potential danger.

In 1862, just three years after obtaining statehood, Oregon established its first civil commitment statute. The first mental hospital was opened the same year. By 1880, the hospital had grown to more than 200 patients and was bursting at its seams. In order to accommodate the growing need for hospital beds, the Oregon State Insane Asylum opened in 1883. This legacy lives on, currently known as the Oregon State Hospital. The first civil commitment statute required only that a court make a finding that a person “by reason of insanity or idiocy, as the case may be, is suffering from neglect, exposure or otherwise, or is unsafe to be at large, or is suffering under mental derangement.” Even then, however, the assistance of friends and family was a relevant consideration, as no person could be committed that had “friends that can, or desire to provide for their safekeeping and medical treatment.”

From the late 1800s until well after World War II, the civil commitment process was designed to facilitate the hospitalization of the mentally ill. This sentiment was apparent in the limited due process considerations in the statutory scheme, as well as the language from appellate opinions stressing the benefits of hospitalization.

In the 1960s and early ’70s, forces converged to shift the focus to personal rights. First came the civil rights movement, which reflected a nationwide trend toward acknowledging the rights of all, not just the majority. Earl Warren, Chief Justice of the Supreme Court from 1953 to 1969, blazed a trail toward personal rights at the expense of property rights. The vision of the Warren Court was equality for all people, ranging from voting rights to the rights of the accused in criminal proceedings; from a newfound constitutional right to privacy to abolishing racial discrimination. The legal system began to acknowledge that involuntary confinement for mental illness was a serious deprivation of basic human liberties and should only be used when treatment in the community was truly impractical.

Simultaneously, awareness dawned that many state hospitals were inhumane. Ken Kesey’s novel One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest,was published in 1962. It was inspired by Kesey’s experiences as an orderly at a mental health facility in California and vividly describes the absurdities and inhumanities of mental health treatment at that time. The movie, released in 1975, was filmed at the Oregon State Hospital.

The medicalization of mental illness was also coming into its own during this period. Past versions of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, or DSM, the diagnostic bible for the mental health profession, were based on a psychodynamic theory of treatment, which ascribed patients’ motivations to unconscious drives. These archaic notions were replaced with the more modern third version of the DSM, published in 1980 but developed throughout the 1970s. The DSM-III was based on the biomedical model, focusing on the pathology, biochemistry and physiology of mental illness. The new text placed little importance on the role of psychological or environmental factors, thereby treating a mental disorder as a disease.

The DSM-III created a multi-axial system, separating mental disorders (such as schizophrenia and depression), personality disorders (such as antisocial and borderline personality disorders), and environmental and social factors (such as homelessness or domestic abuse). Specific diagnostic criteria with mutually exclusive diagnoses were developed and a medical nomenclature was provided to clearly distinguish “normal” from “abnormal.” These theoretical developments among clinical psychologists and psychiatrists were contemporaneous with (and, at least in part, caused by) a quickly growing group of psychiatric medications which altered mental processes by affecting the balance of chemicals in the brain. The first antipsychotic medications were developed and tested in the 1960s. By 1970 there was a growing belief that even the most troublesome of mental disorders, such as schizophrenia, could be effectively treated, if not partially cured, with medication. This meant that most people could be treated in the community, rather than in a locked hospital setting.

State legislatures found themselves in a situation in which legal, moral and financial pressures all seemed to push in the direction of releasing people from state hospitals and phasing out a large portion of the beds.

Society as a whole began to view commitment as a significant deprivation of an individual’s liberty, not always to the benefit of the individual or the community. Doctors and psychologists began using a model of decision making which favored the least restrictive setting and method for treatment.

In sum, it was a perfect storm. Between 1960 and 1980 almost 90 percent of the psychiatric hospital beds in the U.S. were cut. The legal system was altered in order to grant a wider array of civil rights. For example, one significant change during this time was the decoupling of civil commitments and civil competency. In contrast to those who have been found incompetent, civilly committed patients retain the rights to vote, exercise control over property and make treatment decisions.

In 1973, the Oregon legislature made sweeping changes to the commitment scheme, bringing it in line with the aforementioned nationwide trends toward community treatment over institutional treatment except where necessary. They recognized the individual’s right to be represented by an attorney and narrowed the definition of mental illness such that only those who were dangerous, or in danger, could be committed. This new scheme emphasized personal and civil rights. According to the exhibit submitted by the Mental Health Division task force that created the legislation:

This entire bill is designed to encourage voluntary treatment within the community in preference to state hospitalization after commitment. Commitment is considered to be appropriate only for individuals who, because of mental illness, are dangerous to themselves or others or are unable to provide for their basic personal needs and are not receiving such care as is necessary for their health and safety.... Mental illness is not defined in existing statutes. Therefore, the proposed definition is restrictive and relates to involuntary commitments only…. Persons may legitimately seek and need voluntary admission to a hospital but not be a “mentally ill person” under this definition…. At all steps in the process, the person’s civil rights are carefully protected. Representation by legal counsel becomes mandatory, except in unusual circumstances, to be paid for by the county if the person cannot pay.Consistent with recent court decisions, an expanded list of personal and civil rights is defined in this bill, and this statement of rights would be posted in a prominent place in all facilities housing committed patients. The rights to communicate freely, wear one’s own clothes, have privacy and religious freedom and be paid for work performed are designed to enhance personal dignity and sense of individuality and to develop skills necessary for return to the community. Persons would not lose their civil rights simply because of commitment to a mental hospital unless adjudicated incompetent as a separate court action.

Exhibit A in the Senate Hearing on SB 510, a memorandum from J.D. Bray of the Mental Health Division to the Senate Committee on Judiciary (April 4, 1973).

In enacting these requirements, the legislature stated repeatedly that civil commitment was to be a legal result of last resort. Community treatment and placement were to be used if at all possible.

Two years after the passage of Oregon’s new civil commitment scheme, the U.S. Supreme Court made Oregon’s dangerousness standard the law of the land. O’Connor v. Donaldson, 422 US 563 (1975). Mr. Donaldson’s plight was a fine example of the unconscionable result of many a state’s commitment schemes. He had been locked up for decades with scant evidence of benefit or need for secure treatment.

Many credit (or blame) the Supreme Court generally and the Donaldsondecision specifically for spurring nationwide deinstitutionalization of hundreds of thousands of people from state mental hospitals. See, e.g., Rael J. Issac & Virginia C. Armat, Madness in the Streets: How Psychiatry and the Law Abandoned the Mentally Ill (1990) (“The chief impact of the Donaldson case was . . . in spurring deinstitutionalization”). The fact that Oregon’s statutory scheme already reflected the Donaldson requirements is strong evidence that the Supreme Court was not the only force behind the reforms.

Sadly, three trends have been growing nationwide and specifically in Oregon over the last forty years: more drugs, more illness, and fewer hospital beds. The amount of money we spend on psychiatric drugs in the United States is more than the gross national product of two-thirds of the world’s nations. In 2004, the top four antidepressants netted more than ten billion dollars for GlaxoSmithKline, Pfizer and Wyeth Pharmaceuticals. The most popular antipsychotic drug, Zyprexa, made almost four and a half billion dollars for Eli Lilly in 2004 (MedAd News, “Top 500 Prescription Drugs,” May 2005).

At the same time, the percentage of mentally ill and chronically mentally ill citizens has continued to grow. According to an exhaustive National Institute of Mental Health study completed in 2005, almost one in five Americans (17 percent) were treated for mental illness in 2004, while one in four met the standard diagnostic criteria for mental illness. The United States now has the highest percentage of mentally ill people of any nation in the world (“U.S. Leads in Mental Illness, Lags in Treatment,” Washington Post, June 7, 2005).

To complicate the problem, as noted above, more than 90 percent of state psychiatric hospital beds have been eliminated since 1960 (E Fuller Torrey, Wall Street Journal, August 4, 1998). Only a small portion of the resources that had been dedicated to hospitalization were shifted to community placement. And, when resources are scarce, the money for community treatment is often the first to be cut. When community placement is not available, jails, shelters, nursing homes and general hospitals frequently fill the gap.

The Oregon commitment scheme, like that of most other states, finds itself awkwardly situated in a mental health system that has neither sufficient community resources nor sufficient hospital beds to compensate for the lack of resources. For example, there are very few sub-acute or respite beds where a person can go when decompensating but short of acute crisis. At this point, most such folks deteriorate into acute crisis and are then hospitalized or arrested. The criminal justice system has seen an explosion of defendants with mental illness and the jails and prisons have become de facto psychiatric facilities without the funding, the staffing or the regulations. As a result, people in the community often see civil commitment as a sort of golden ticket to acquire resources that could not be found through less coercive means. But the commitment scheme is not a separate system. It is merely one part of the broader mental health system; a mechanism of last resort for coercive treatment in the face of imminent danger. It was not designed to be a path to otherwise unavailable resources because a separate path creates an almost overwhelming pressure to sacrifice a person’s liberty and reputation in the name of “best interests.” Yet such is the position of Oregon’s civil commitment scheme.

B. Civil Commitment Is the Path of Last Resort

Regardless of the lack of community resources in the mental health system, the courts have been exceedingly clear that commitment may only be used in a very narrow set of circumstances. It may not be used merely because commitment would be medically or psychiatrically best for the allegedly mentally ill person. It may not be used to acquire additional resources for the AMIP. The liberty interests and potential for stigma are too high to allow for commitment to be anything but a coercive path of last resort in the face of imminent danger. The following cases (and many others) emphasize different aspects of the one basic principle that civil commitment may only be used when absolutely necessary because the stakes for the AMIP are too high to do otherwise.

Involuntary commitment or recommitment to a mental institution involves a massive curtailment of liberty and, consequently, invokes due process protections. State v. Johansen, 125 Or App 365 (1993).

Committing a person to the hospital against his or her will is a governmental intrusion into a person’s freedom warranted only in the narrow set of circumstances carved out by the legislature and the courts—that is, where there is a real danger in the near future. The Oregon courts have consistently emphasized that “the mere desirability of institutionalization for a person’s condition is insufficient for commitment.” State v. Johnson, 8 Or App 263 (1972).

Involuntary commitment for a mental disorder constitutes a serious deprivation of personal liberty and the Constitution does not allow for it to be imposed solely to achieve desirable social goals such as the provision of custodial care until a person has mastered the skills to live on his or her own. In re Pike, 197 Or App 508 (2005).

“It is not the state’s prerogative under the civil commitment statutes to interfere with a free, knowing and rational choice. Unless a mental disorder has impaired autonomous choice, civil commitment cannot be a vehicle for ‘saving people from themselves.’” State v. Gjerde, 147 Or App 187 (1997).

The U.S. Supreme Court has stated repeatedly that there is no constitutional basis for involuntarily confining a person with a mental illness who is dangerous to no one and can live safely in freedom. See, e.g., O’Connor v. Donaldson, 422 US 563 at 575, 95 S.Ct. at 2493, 45 L.Ed.2d at 407 (1975); Cooper v. Oklahoma, 517 US 348, 116 S.Ct. 1373, 134 L.Ed.2d 498 (due process requires at a minimum a showing that the person is mentally ill and either poses a danger to himself or others or is incapable of “surviving safely in freedom”). “The loss of liberty produced by an involuntary commitment is more than a loss of freedom from confinement. It is indisputable that commitment to a mental hospital ‘can engender adverse social consequences to the individual’” and “[w]hether we label this phenomena ‘stigma’ or choose to call it something else. . .we recognize that it can occur and that it does have a significant impact on the individual.” Vitek v. Jones, 445 US 480, 63 L.Ed.2d 552 (1980).

Civil commitment may not be used except in the face of imminent danger. Anything else is overstepping and the courts have repeatedly rejected such commitments. Even the best interests of the person are irrelevant if serious harm cannot be predicted for the near future. Civil commitment, the most restrictive option, must be an option of last resort.

C. Ramifications

Aside from the obvious loss of physical liberty and the somewhat less obvious loss of treatment rights, there are several other legal rights that are affected by commitment.

First, a person who has been committed loses his or her right to possess firearms, and the possession of a firearm is a criminal offense under ORS 166.250. A person who was committed twenty years ago on a finding that she was unable to meet her basic needs may be convicted of a class A misdemeanor for having a hunting rifle in her house, regardless of whether a firearm prohibition was specifically ordered.

Additionally, in order to implement the gun prohibition, the fact of commitment will be entered into the law enforcement data system (LEDS). Thereafter, any time the individual has an encounter with police, whether as victim, witness or suspect, the police officers involved will be aware that the person was so mentally ill at one time her liberty had to be taken away. As a result, she may be seen as having less credibility, less capable of rational thought and with a greater tendency toward unpredictable, dangerous behavior.

Second, other criminal statutes regulate the committed as well. For example, it is a class A misdemeanor to depart from a state hospital without authority while confined by court order. ORS 162.175. (Though there is an argument that a commitment does not confine a person in the state hospital. Rather, it gives power to the state mental health division who may or may not authorize the person to be confined in the state hospital. Since there’s no court order of confinement the statute would not apply on its face.)

Third, a commitment makes it easier for a person to be committed in the future under the expanded criteria of chronic mental illness. See “expanded criteria,” II. G., below.

Fourth, recommitment becomes a distinct possibility. A hearing is not required to recommit a person if the state hospital believes that person is still dangerous or unable to care for basic needs at the end of the 180-day commitment. A committed person must protest recommitment in order to have a right to either a hearing or an attorney. At the time at which the physician initiates the extension of the commitment process, however, the hospital has complete control over the person’s communication with the court. If the committed person does not demand an attorney then one is not provided, and if the committed person does not demand a hearing through protest then the court must sign the extension of commitment without inquiry.

Fifth, the state may charge the costs of hospitalization to the AMIP or his family. This consequence of commitment is often the most burdensome. It is almost impossible for a mentally ill person to recover financially after a civil commitment because the costs of inpatient hospitalization are so great.

II. Mental Illness

A. Mental Illness Is the Ultimate Conclusion

When it created the new commitment scheme, the Oregon legislature made the unfortunate decision to use two remarkably similar terms to mean two very different things. “Mental illness” is the final legal conclusion that is the point of a commitment hearing. A “mental disorder” is a mental health diagnosis.

A “mentally ill person” is one who is unsafe or dangerous as a direct result of a mental disorder. Thus, mental illness encompasses three separate legal findings: dangerousness or a lack of safety, the mental disorder, and a nexus between the two. ORS 426.005(1)(e) states that “mentally ill person” means a person who, because of a mental disorder, is one or more of the following:

(A) Dangerous to self or others

(B) Unable to provide for basic personal needs and not receiving such care as is necessary for health or safety

(C) A person who:

(i) Is chronically mentally ill, as defined in ORS 426.495

(ii) Within the previous three years, has twice been placed in a hospital or approved inpatient facility by the division under ORS 426.060

(iii) Is exhibiting symptoms or behavior substantially similar to those that preceded and led to one or more of the hospitalizations or inpatient placements referred to in sub-subparagraph (ii) of this subparagraph; and

(iv) Unless treated, will continue, to a reasonable medical probability, to physically or mentally deteriorate so that the person will become a person described under either or both subparagraph (A) or (B) of this paragraph.

B. Mental Disorder—One Element of Mental Illness

While the term “mental disorder” is neither defined in statute nor case law, it is reasonably clear from the statutory context and the practical requirements of the commitment scheme that a mental disorder is a psychiatric diagnosis. As a practical matter, the DSM-IV’s categorization of psychiatric disorders is the only functional way for courts to determine the presence of a mental disorder. The DSM-IV is used by the full spectrum of mental health professionals; there is no other commonly used set of diagnostic definitions in the country. If the DSM-IV were not the reference point, the only other choices would be to either invent new definitions for terms like “depression” or “schizophrenia” in lieu of the agreed upon definitions used in a normal professional context, or to use an alternative diagnostic manual like the ICD-10, the primary European manual. Neither option makes sense for a mental health professional in the United States.

Some have suggested that a statutory definition of mental disorder can be found within the definition of mental illness, hidden in what is commonly referred to as the “expanded criteria.” The chronic mental illness subsection of ORS 426.005 limits the definition of mental disorders to psychotic disorders and major mood disorders. ORS 426.005(1)(e)(C). However, the structure of the statute suggests that the point of the limited mental disorder definition was to limit the subsection to certain types of chronic mental disorders. The idea was that there are certain disorders which, in certain people at certain times, are predictable in their progression of symptoms such that we don’t need to wait until people are actually dangerous to confine them. But there is no indication that one can extrapolate from the special definition to a general one for the rest of the statute. In other words, the chronic mental disorder statute is of little use in helping determine the precise outline of a regular mental disorder in the commitment scheme.

The appellate court has provided little guidance, preferring to leave the issue to the professionals. The existence of a mental disorder, we are told, is a determination that must be made by the examiners and the courts on a case-by-case basis. State v. Furnish, 86 Or App 194 (1987).

The presence of a mental disorder must be more than merely probable. State v. Alexander, 26 Or App 943 (1976); State v. Jepson, 48 Or App 411 (1980). Evidence of a mental disorder must be clear and convincing, the definition of which is evidence which makes it highly probable. Thus, while a “probable” mental disorder is no legal disorder at all, a “highly probable” mental disorder is good enough to pass statutory muster. State v. Sickler, 133 Or App 50 (1995).

Not only must the examiners state their firm opinion that a mental disorder exists, they must specify their reasoning. “The reasons for that judgment must be supplied. Further, the actual and possible effects of the mental illness should be catalogued, with specific reference to the individual at hand.” State v. Alexander, 26 Or App 943 (1976). To state that an individual is “mentally ill” because he suffers from “psychosis,” without further support or explanation, is simply not sufficient evidence upon which to base an order of involuntary commitment. State v. Jepson, 48 Or App 411 (1980).

A list of symptoms and behavior is also insufficient. In State v. Billingsley, 85 Or App 387 (1987), the AMIP had frenetic speech patterns during the hearing, an apparent delusion about an FBI agent, and stated that he was “mentally unbalanced” to hospital staff in order to “get some rest and relaxation” at the hospital. In reversing the commitment, the court found it highly significant that the examiners made no statement as to the nature of the mental disorder. In order for an AMIP to be committed involuntarily, there must be a specific mental disorder, not simply a general idea that, for example, “there must be a mental disorder if the behavior is this strange.” Id.

1. Chronic Alcoholism—A Special Situation

“Chronic alcoholism” is defined as a “pathologic condition, affecting chiefly the nervous and gastroenteric systems, caused by the habitual use of alcoholic beverages in poisonous amount.” State v. Sickler, 133 Or App 50 (1995). Alcoholism can also be considered a mental disorder if the person, due to his alcoholism, is dangerous to himself or others or unable to provide for basic needs. The fact that a person’s alcoholism is causing his or her brain and body to deteriorate is insufficient by itself because deterioration, by definition, is ongoing and not likely to result in near-future harm. State v. Smith, 71 Or App 205 (1984). In State v. Sickler, there was evidence of alcohol and cocaine abuse in the past but no evidence of recent use, and the court held that this could not suffice for a finding of chronic alcohol or drug use. A court will generally focus on the nexus between the alleged alcoholism and the recent dangerous behavior. It is unlikely that a court would make a diagnosis of chronic alcoholism without evidence of recent use. Sickler, 133 Or App 50 (1995).

2. Paraphilia Can Be a Basis for Commitment Where the Person Lacks Control

To be used in a civil commitment, a mental disorder must be of the sort which causes the person to have serious difficulty controlling his or her behavior. The state may not civilly commit people who can control their behavior because to do so would permit civil commitment to be a mechanism for retribution or general deterrence—functions properly those of criminal law, not civil commitments. State v. Gibson, 187 Or App 207 (2004). In Gibson, the court found that paraphilia (a condition in which sexual arousal is dependent on atypical and extreme behaviors) could be a mental disorder for the purposes of the civil commitment statutes where it directly caused dangerous behavior. Id. at 217. In that case, the expert’s sex offender evaluation clearly linked Gibson’s “entrenched” and “reinforced” “thought processes” involving “forcing women to have sex with him and hurting them if they resist” to his clear “potential for acting out aggressively on females.” Id. at 217. But the court went out of its way to emphasize the narrow path it was delineating:

[a]ppellant is not a typical criminal recidivist for whom the civil commitment process is being used in place of criminal sanctions. He is, instead, someone with a severe mental disorder that causes him serious difficulty in controlling his behavior. The nature of his mental disorder in combination with his impaired ability to control his impulses causes him to fall within the narrow overlap between the criminal law and civil commitment systems.

Id. at 221. In other words, where a person has control over the link between impulse and behavior, paraphilia is not a disorder which may be used for civil commitment. This is apparently true despite the fact that regardless of such lack of control, paraphilia would clearly qualify as a mental disorder in the DSM-IV. (See, for example, Pedophilia, DSM-IV-TR, code 302.2.) Thus, paraphilia is a rare situation in which the DSM-IV definition of a mental disorder is not transferable to the commitment process.

C. Dangerous to Self

1. The General Standard

In order to establish that a person is a danger to herself, the state must show that her mental disorder has “resulted in harm...or created situations likely to result in harm.” State v. Christofferson, 47 Or App 1087 (1980). The likelihood of harm need not be immediate but “must exist in the near future.” State v. Jacobson, 142 Or App 371, 377 (1996). Proof cannot be speculative or conjectural. See State v. Ayala,164 Or App 399 (1999) (“Apprehensions, speculations and conjecture are not sufficient to prove a need for mental commitment”). Stated another way, “whether the state’s evidence at the hearing was sufficient to support commitment on the ground that appellant was a danger to her self turns on whether her mental disorder would cause her to engage in behavior that is likely to result in physical harm to herself in the near future.” State v. N.A.P., 216 Or App 432 (2007).

In determining danger to self, the court should consider whether the AMIP is actually harming herself, engaging in genuinely dangerous behavior, or demonstrating an inclination or propensity for dangerous behavior. State v. Sea, 137 Or App 333 (1995). However, such considerations are only sufficient for commitment if they form a basis for concluding it is likely that a person will engage in genuinely dangerous behavior in the near future.

2. The Danger Must Be Immediate, Not Speculative

There must be some immediacy of danger. In order to meet this standard, the threat must exist in the near future, but there does not have to be an immediately dangerous situation. State v. Jacobson, 142 Or App 371 (1996). In Jacobson, an alcoholism case, the Oregon Court of Appeals found there to be an imminent situation where 1) the drinking had caused serious physical problems such as liver dysfunction and seizures, 2) continued drinking put Mr. Jacobson at serious risk in the near future, and 3) Mr. Jacobson would continue to drink because he was compelled to do so.

Contrast immediate danger to speculative danger. There are several types of speculative danger. In the first, the allegedly mentally ill person has some genuinely concerning propensities, but they don’t rise to the level of severity or dangerousness needed to commit. In State v. Fletcher, 60 Or App 623 (1982), the court reversed a commitment where the AMIP frequently drove too fast, drank too much, slept irregularly, refused to take prescribed antidepressant medications, was ill-tempered and often unable to make decisions but had never demonstrated any genuinely self-destructive tendencies.

The second type of speculative danger occurs when the AMIP has done something genuinely dangerous in the recent past but it is not possible to predict with any confidence that it will happen again in the near future. In State v. Siebold, 100 Or App 561 (1994), the court reversed a commitment where the AMIP crashed his car because he was distracted by voices in his head, but no evidence indicated the incident was anything but isolated. That is, there was insufficient evidence that Mr. Siebold would crash his car again in the near future. In State v. North, 189 Or App 518 (2003), the court reversed a commitment where the state established that the AMIP had been assaulted in the past and had difficulty interacting with people, holding that such evidence did not establish that Mr. North was likely to be assaulted again, much less that he was in any sort of life-threatening danger in the near future.

A third example of speculative danger is where the AMIP has done something that seems like it should be dangerous but no evidence of harm actually occurred or was likely to occur. “A person can be deemed dangerous to self if he or she has established a pattern in the past of taking certain actions that lead to self-destructive conduct, and then he or she begins to follow the pattern again…. However, the state must present clear and convincing evidence that a person’s mental disorder has resulted in harm or created situations likely to result in harm.” State v. Judd,206 Or App 146(2006). The appellate court has interpreted this standard strictly. Thus, the following actions, for example, were not evidence of dangerousness because there was no evidence that harm occurred as a result:

  • Hitting one’s head hard against a Plexiglas window for minutes. Powell, 178 Or App 89 (2001).
  • Riding one’s bike nude in an unsafe area. Webb, 186 Or App 404 (2003).
  • Swimming nude in 40-degree weather. Hambleton, 202 Or App 526 (2005).
  • Having and picking at open wounds. Turel, 182 Or App 235 (2002).

Finally, the most pernicious type of speculative danger is “harm’s way,” the hypothesis that the allegedly mentally ill person is so detached from reality that if released he will unintentionally put himself in a position where he will likely be harmed, such as walking into traffic or venturing into a dangerous part of town and falling victim to some type of violent crime. The appellate court has specifically prohibited this sort of paternalistic hypothesizing. State v. Woolridge 101 Or App 390 (1990). In Woolridge, the court disdainfully pointed out that there was “little to indicate that he is a danger to himself, other than the trial court’s conclusion that appellant would find himself in ‘harm’s way’ if his conduct continued.” In State v. Christofferson, 47 Or App 1087 (1980), the words “harm’s way” weren’t used, but the court was clear that a commitment may not be based on speculation that the AMIP could agitate someone to violence if he or she were released. Simply put, if there were specific evidence supporting a finding of dangerousness to self, then the words “harm’s way” would not need to be used. For example, if the AMIP had previously walked in traffic, the “harm’s way” designation would be unnecessary because the examiners and the court could point to previous behavior as a consistent manifestation of mental illness and predictive of similar behavior in the near future. “Harm’s way” is generally used where there is scant evidence of dangerous behavior in the past but the examiners or the court are apprehensive about the future. This is not a legal basis for commitment.

3. The Danger Must Be at the Time of the Hearing

The determination as to whether a person is dangerous must focus on his or her condition at the time of the hearing. Although the actions and statements of the AMIP prior to the hearing hint at his present mental condition, they are not sufficient for a finding of mental illness unless they clearly form the foundation for a prediction of future dangerousness. State v. Lucas, 31 Or App 947 (1977).

Whether a person is mentally ill for the purposes of involuntary commitment is determined by his condition at the time of the commitment hearing as understood in the context of his history. State v. King, 177 Or App 373 (2001).

4. Alcoholism

Alcoholism is sometimes enough to satisfy this prong of the inquiry if a mental disorder is causing the AMIP to drink and the AMIP, when drinking, is a danger to himself. The deterioration of the brain caused by chronic alcoholism is, however, not sufficient. State v. Smith, 71 Or App 205 (1984). To illustrate, in State v. LeHuquet, 54 Or App 895 (1981), a psychiatrist testified that alcohol-induced brain damage (evidenced by impaired thinking, grand delusions, and false beliefs) made the AMIP dangerous. The court found that although the alcoholism was causing long-term deterioration of the brain, the brain damage was not sufficient to be considered a mental disorder for the purposes of the civil commitment statutes.

In stark contrast, in State v. Jacobson, 142 Or App 371 (1996), a chronic alcoholic who suffered seizures and liver dysfunction from alcohol abuse, and showed no insight into the effect drinking had on his physical condition, was found to have a mental disorder. This was largely because his lack of insight increased his risk of hip fracture, seizures and liver failure. The court found that the mental disorder caused a serious threat to his health and, therefore, rendered him a danger to himself, rejecting the argument that danger to self requires a threat of violence or an immediately dangerous situation if the threat exists in the near future.

5. Failure to Take Medications

Refusal or failure to take psychiatric medications can be sufficient to show dangerousness to self if it truly and immediately threatens the AMIP’s life. In State v. Desassive, 118 Or App 383 (1993), the court stated, “[d]efendant’s mental disability causes him to refuse his medication, which, in turn, causes his lack of awareness of the effects of alcohol. It is clear that this lack of awareness poses a real danger to him.… Standing alone, failure to take medication is not sufficient if it only causes a speculative threat. In this case, however, AMIP’s failure to take his medication almost caused his death.” Contrast the more typical case, State v. Brungard, 101 Or App 67 (1990), where the court found that although the AMIP was unlikely to take his prescribed medication, there was no convincing evidence that this would cause harm in the near future. The fact that the AMIP had attempted suicide in the past while unmedicated was relevant but insufficient because it was impossible to say that another attempt in the near future was highly probable, whether or not the AMIP took his medications.

6. Danger to Self Cases Reversed (with an opinion)

She then became combative with the police when they took her into custody. The trial court found that she was a danger to self: “‘The court can take absolute judicial notice today that fighting with the police, with unknown police officers in Multnomah County, is certainly something that puts mentally ill people at risk of death or serious physical injury.’ The court commented on the then-recent death of James Chasse, stating that Chasse, an unarmed mentally ill person, ‘was confronted by the police, and he was beaten to death.’” Id. At 401. The appellate court rejected such speculation: “The abstract possibility that a person’s bizarre and agitated behavior might theoretically draw a violent response from someone cannot satisfy the requirement that the threat of harm be real, rather than speculative, and ‘exist in the near future.’” Id.

Here, there was insufficient evidence to indicate that the AMIP would actually inflict harm on herself in the near future if released.

State v. Both examiners recommended commitment:

I think, given the whole picture, the depression is still there. It is in just a slightly different phase, but she was extremely angry during the hospitalization. So if she has a medication available to her, she has just been handed another tool which she really doesn’t need. She lives alone. She has admitted to problems with coping with things. And just because people are intelligent, that doesn’t mean they are born knowing how to handle their problems. She is not involved with anybody at this time. She has a distrust of organized medication. She has self-discontinued her meds in the past. I think she is at extremely high risk.

Id.at 399. The appellate court reversed, saying, “We find little in this record to support the state’s assertion that it satisfied its burden of proof. Even if it is inferred that appellant’s past actions were intended to take her life, the state was required to show by clear and convincing evidence that her depression at the time of the hearing was so severe that she was a danger to herself. In the context of appellant’s rational presentation at the hearing and her personal plan to deal with her depression, the evidence simply does not demonstrate that it was highly probable at the time of the hearing that appellant would repeat her suicide attempt.”

D. Dangerous to Others

1. The General Standard

The general standard for determining dangerousness to others is whether there is a reasonable medical probability that the allegedly mentally ill person is presently a danger to others. Evidence of either actual violence or threats of violence may be sufficient if they are predictive of harm in the near future. State v. Allmendinger, 36 Or App 381 (1978).

State v. Bodell, 120 Or App 548 (1993), is a close case in which the AMIP was committed. Mr. Bodell was a paranoid schizophrenic who believed he was a demi-god chosen to rule the world. He had expressed repeatedly his desire to “sacrifice” his one-year-old child. He also told his pregnant wife that she and the baby would have to die because they were evil. A psychiatrist testified at the hearing that there was a genuine danger that the AMIP would act on his delusional beliefs. Bodell is a close case because, although there were threats against specific people, there was no evidence of a violent tendency or, for that matter, any specific instance in which Mr. Bodell had ever been violent. Nor was there any evidence that Mr. Bodell was capable of carrying out his delusional beliefs. The court ultimately decided that where the delusion is so entrenched and the threats so specific, actual acts of violence are not required.

Past acts are relevant to the determination of present dangerousness but are insufficient in and of themselves. There must be evidence that explains how the mental disorder presently causes the AMIP to be a danger to others. In State v. Miller, 198 Or App 153 (2005), the court stated:

In determining whether a person poses a danger to others, it is appropriate for a court to consider the testimony of mental health experts, a person’s past acts, and the person’s apparent condition at the time of the hearing. State v. Furnish, 86 Or App 194, 197 (1987). Past threats coupled with violent acts may constitute clear and convincing evidence that a person is dangerous to others if “there is no indication that such violence is an isolated occurrence.” State v. Allmendinger, 36 Or App 381, 384 (1978). “But a mere recitation of past acts, in the absence of a showing that such clearly forms the foundation for a prediction of future dangerousness, cannot serve as the basis for a finding that one is a “mentally ill person” pursuant to ORS 426.005. State v. Lucas, 31 Or App 947, 950 (1977).

2. Threatening Statements

If the basis of the commitment is a threat of violence, the court should consider all of the following:

  • Whether there was an actual threat of violence.
  • Whether the AMIP is capable of carrying out such a threat.
  • Whether the threat is directed at a specific and identifiable person.
  • Whether there was some overt physical act indicating propensity.

State v. Allmendinger, 36 Or App 381, 384 (1978). In order for threats to be sufficient for commitment, they must either be (1) accompanied by an overt act to follow through on the threat, or (2) made under circumstances that make future violence highly likely. It is not enough that specific and violent threats are made to specific people.

For example, in State v. R.H.,212 Or App 479(2007), the AMIP had made what seemed like very specific threats to kill or “get” people based on perceived wrongs. However, the appellate court found that this did not rise to the required level of evidence:

We have previously held that verbal threats of violence do not satisfy the future dangerousness requirement if they are not accompanied by any overt act to follow through with the threat or if they are not made under circumstances that make actual future violence highly likely. State v. Beil,196 Or App 501, 506 (2004). There is no evidence of any acts by appellant suggesting that he would follow through with his threats. Cf. State v. Woolridge, 101 Or App 390, 395, 790 P2d 1192, adh’d to as modified on recons, 102 Or App 559 (1990) (an isolated threat, unaccompanied by any overt act to follow up the threat, is insufficient to establish future dangerousness). Nor does any evidence of his general demeanor or other circumstances under which he made the threats suggest that he was highly likely to become violent. Cf. State v. Pieretti, 110 Or App 379, 383–84 (1991), rev. den, 313 Or 354 (1992) (graphic threats to kill, coupled with violent reactions to frustration, were sufficient to establish future dangerousness even in the absence of past violent acts toward others). Nothing in the record indicates that appellant actually intended to harm Freeman or Dewayne in any way. None of the actions that appellant took either with them or with his mother suggest that he intended to follow through with his threats.

State v. R.H.,212 Or App 479 (2007). In State v. Woolridge, the AMIP had made threats against the mayor. The appellate court rejected the state’s argument that involuntarily committing appellant was proper where “[t]he trial court could reasonably conclude that appellant might carry out the threat to blow up the [mayor’s] house and office in light of his violence in the police car and his demeanor in the courtroom.” [emphasis supplied] 101 Or App 390 (1990). Such speculative findings fail to meet the clear and convincing evidence standard.

State v. Pieretti,110 Or App 379 (1991),is a threats case in which the evidence was sufficient. In State v. Pieretti, the AMIP had recently made graphic threats to kill specific people. He had also reacted violently to everyday frustrations several times in the previous month. Close friends and relatives testified at the hearing that they were afraid for their safety because they believed the threats were real and that Mr. Pieretti was capable of carrying them out. Mr. Pieretti also displayed vehement anger and an inability to control himself during the hearing. In this case, each item from the above checklist was present. The apparent propensity for violence had not relented in the time prior to the hearing. Thus, the AMIP was committed.

3. Dangerousness Must Be Present on the Day of the Hearing

The standard here is identical to the standard for determining dangerousness to self. Whether a person falls within the definition of mentally ill for the purposes of involuntary commitment depends upon his condition at the time of the commitment hearing as understood in the context of his history. State v. King, 177 Or App 373 (2001).

4. Danger to Others Cases Reversed (with an opinion)

State v. K.L., 220 Or App 647 (2008): AMIP made a series of scary, delusional statements to her neighbors. She said she would like to see the children’s heads on her fence; that it was late and the children should come into her house and go to bed; that she would kill one of the children if the family dog came near her yard again; and that the neighbors’ daughter was her granddaughter who she would “get back” so they should “watch out.” When the officers came to her door she thought they were vampires and barricaded herself in her bathroom with weapons for protection. The appellate court found that there were no overt acts or circumstances indicating that it was highly likely that AMIP would follow up on the threats. The confrontation with the police was not one that was highly likely to repeat itself in the near future.

State v. P.B., 216 Or App 184 (2007): AMIP had a single manic episode in which he was sure he was destined to stop an imminent Armageddon and threatened to kill his wife if she stood in his way. The appellate court believed it to be a close call: “Appellant’s threats were, as noted, credible. They were also repeated. Further, although appellant achieved considerable insight into his condition by the time of his hearing, he was unwilling or unable to reject the possibility that he was, indeed, chosen by God to prevent the end of the world. His improved condition at his hearing likely resulted from his course of medication, yet he acknowledged that he needed help in monitoring that treatment. His actions when denied a shower indicate a potential for violence. One mental health examiner believed appellant’s thinking to be logical only on the surface; another had no doubt that he was dangerous. Significantly, the trial court observed that, when talking about his wife and his apparent resentment of her “distrust,” appellant spoke with an “edge” to his voice. That is the kind of observation, unobtainable from reading a cold transcript, to which we owe some deference.” Id. at 190. But ultimately it was still too speculative:

Appellant had no history of prior manic episodes. Despite apparently credible threats, appellant attempted no violence; when he needed to be restrained in the hospital, it was not because he was in the process of trying to act out his delusional role but because he was denied the opportunity to shower. At the time of the hearing, he was lucid, his thoughts were organized, and his affect was normal. One mental health examiner deemed him to be harmless. Further, his belief that he was chosen by God to heal an imperfect world differed in degree, but not in kind, from similar beliefs held by perfectly sane adherents to traditional religions, not to mention prophets, oracles, seers, saints, and other generally venerated figures in traditional apocalyptic religious literature; by itself, such a belief does not justify the significant loss of liberty imposed by involuntary mental commitment. In short, the record supports the conclusion that appellant underwent a single decompensation brought on by fatigue and stress, during which he made a few ultimately idle threats. Seen in that light, the evidence that appellant was likely to repeat those threats and actually carry them out—that, in other words, he was a danger to others—is not clear and convincing.

Id. at 191. State v. R.H., 212 Or App 479 (2007): AMIP with bipolar disorder, alcohol abuse and frequent hospitalizations, removed his mother from a care facility without her medications and without permission. The removal was in the midst of a series of inappropriate, scary and threatening calls to the care facility and specific workers therein. Taken together with the strong warnings of the examiners and his extremely focused homicidal ideation at the hearing there was highly convincing evidence of his dangerousness.

He wanted to “sacrifice” his 1 year old child and told his pregnant wife that she and the baby would die because they were evil. Here the standard was met.

E. Basic Needs

1. The General Standard

“The legislature’s ‘basic needs’ commitment standard focuses on the capacity of the individual to survive, either through his own resources or with the help of family and friends. The state must establish by clear and convincing evidence that the individual, due to a mental disorder, is unable to obtain some commodity (e.g., food and water) or service (e.g., life-saving medical care) without which he cannot sustain life.” State v. Jayne, 174 Or App 74 (2001), quoting State v. Bunting, 112 Or App 143 (1992). A speculative threat alone, such as what may happen if the AMIP discontinues taking his or her prescribed medications, is insufficient to show inability to care for basic needs. “The essential question is whether the AMIP is able to access the resources necessary for continued survival.” State v. Shorett, 194 Or App 587 (2004).

The appellate court recently ruled in the Kaspercase that the trial court must “determine whether the record shows that, if released, the person would likely not have survived in the near future”:

To do that, we must take into consideration evidence of what was likely to happen after the hearing if the person were released. We cannot conclude that a person would likely not survive in the near future if the record shows that, if released, the person would receive appropriate care, even if the record also shows that the person had not been receiving assistance from others at the time of the hospitalization. In other words, we will affirm a judgment ordering commitment on “basic needs” grounds only if there is clear and convincing evidence that the person is unwilling or unable to obtain help from others upon release.

State v. Kasper(In re Kasper), 203 Or App 797, 803 (2006). The appellate court reversed the commitment even though there was a determination by both examiners and the judge that the AMIP’s “inability to interact is so great that she would be an immediate risk of death or serious physical injury.” Id. at 801.

The basic needs criterion is often abbreviated to “inability to care for basic needs,” but there are two separate requirements: 1) the AMIP does not have the ability to acquire basic needs like food or water, and 2) the AMIP is not receiving those basic needs in some other way. If the person either has the ability to care for basic needs or is receiving necessary care, then the basic needs criteria are not met. If the needs will be provided then the person is “receiving such care as is necessary for health or safety.” This issue arises frequently where there are friends or family willing to assist the person in finding shelter and sustenance. See, for example, State v. Johnson, 117 Or App 237 (1992). See also State v. Stanley, 117 Or App 327 (1992). Additionally, if the AMIP is being held in a jail or hospital, then basic needs are being met because the jail or hospital will provide necessary food, water, shelter and medication. In order to secure commitment, the state would have to present additional proof that the allegedly mentally ill person would be released in the near future and at that point the person would not receive necessary resources.

2. The Danger Must Be Imminent,Not Speculative

There must be a “likelihood that the person probably would not survive in the near future because the person is unable to provide for basic personal needs and is not receiving care necessary for health and safety.” State v. Cori Aron,176 Or App 342(2001), quoting Bunting. “The statute does not express a standard by which the imminence of the threat to life is to be measured. A speculative threat…is not itself sufficient.” State v. Jayne, 174 Or App 74 (2001), quoting Bunting, 112 Or App 143 (1992).

“However, the state need not postpone action until the individual is on the brink of death. The goal of the commitment statute is safe survival, not merely the avoidance of immediate death.” State v. Jayne, 174 Or App 74 (2001)In Jayne, the AMIP’s delusions prevented her from managing her finances and keeping her house, caused her to be unable to eat properly, as shown by a 25-pound weight loss in the previous year, and, because of her refusal to take medications, caused a “very severe deterioration from a highly functional state…to the degree of inability to cope.” Id. AMIP was committed.

In State v. Miller, 198 Or App 153 (2005), Ms. Miller was found mostly nude and stuck in some bushes behind her home. Police noted that she was frail and emaciated, suffering from dry and cracked skin. When police tried to help her out of the bushes, she hit an officer with her cane and otherwise resisted. During later hospitalization and at the hearing, she was incoherent and unresponsive. The only other evidence regarding her inability to acquire basic needs was an expression of concern by the officer about whether she would be able to find food or water if released. The court found that such evidence requires too great an “inferential leap” to meet the clear and convincing proof standard and reversed her commitment.

The difference between Jayneand Milleris that in Jaynethere was evidence of both a significant decline and a current inability to cope. It was entirely possible that Ms. Miller had been surviving in her then-present state for quite some time. There was evidence that Ms. Miller was emaciated but no evidence that this was caused by her mental illness. The only evidence of an inability to cope was the officer’s expression of concern. In Jayne, on the other hand, there was evidence that Ms. Jayne’s delusions prevented her from eating. There was evidence that, as a result of these delusions and the subsequent lack of food, she had lost a dangerous amount of weight in a short period of time, and her continued refusal to take medications would very likely result in a downward spiral.

3. Medical Treatment May Be Used as a Basic Need

Medical treatment is considered a basic need when necessary for survival and the mental illness is causing the AMIP to forego treatment. In State v. Brooks, 35 Or App 347 (1978), the AMIP took a 50-hour bus ride while suffering from pneumonia. When he eventually arrived at the hospital he behaved disruptively and refused to take tranquilizers. This behavior was sufficient for commitment because it made treatment of his life-threatening medical problems impossible. In State v. Ayala, 164 Or App 399 (1999), the AMIP had gestational diabetes which she refused to treat. However, there was no evidence that the condition threatened her life or the life of her fetus, and thus commitment was inappropriate.

Failure to take medications is, by itself, not enough if the threat is only speculative. The state must show that the failure will impact the AMIP’s survival in the near future. State v. Baxter, 138 Or App 94 (1995). In State v. Woods, 119 Or App 502 (1993), the AMIP refused to take prescription medications necessary for his survival. Despite his willingness to take over-the-counter medications, the refusal to take prescription medications was sufficient for commitment because it was caused by his mental illness.

Treatment of medical problems caused by chronic alcoholism may be considered a basic need, and the chronic alcoholism itself may be considered the mental disorder causing a person’s inability to seek medical treatment. In State v. Jacobson, the AMIP had chronic alcoholism resulting in seizures, liver dysfunction and increased risk of hip fracture. This was sufficient for commitment because the AMIP had little insight into the potential dangers of continued drinking. The court found that (1) the alcoholism was a mental disorder which (2) caused a serious threat to health so that (3) treatment was necessary for safe survival, and (4) the AMIP was unable to obtain such treatment because of his complete lack of insight into either his illness or the consequences of not treating it. Jacobson, 142 Or App 371 (1996).

4. Weight Loss

Weight loss must be life-threatening to be used as proof of a person’s inability to care for their basic needs.

In State v. Beil, 196 Or App 501 (2004), the AMIP’s weight had decreased by approximately thirty pounds in the two to three months before the hearing. This was an insufficient basis for commitment because there was no evidence that this was the sort of weight loss which was life-threatening. In fact, in this specific case, the AMIP had been obese prior to the weight loss, thus it may well have been life-saving.

5. The Basic Needs Plan

A plan to care for basic needs is easy to create and essential for release. In order to be released, the AMIP must have a plan in place for how he or she is planning to meet his or her basic needs. The Oregon Court of Appeals has recently summarized the requirements of such a plan as follows:

[W]hen an individual can articulate a reasonable plan for obtaining the necessities of life and has a demonstrable ability to support himself or herself, a civil commitment is inappropriate because there is no clear and convincing evidence that the individual cannot provide for his or her basic needs.” State v. Cunningham, 190 Or App 202, 213. In State v. Shorett, 194 Or App 587, 596–97 (2004), we noted that “we have held in the past that an allegedly “mentally ill person has a sufficient plan where she simply ‘recognizes the necessity of some activity on [her] part to provide for [her] survival’” (quoting State v. Strasburger, 138 Or App 409, 416 (1996) (brackets in Shorett)). Furthermore, we have also held that, “[a]lthough the lack of certain shelter is not a good plan, we cannot say that homelessness by itself is sufficient grounds for commitment.”

State v. Baxter, 138 Or App 94, 99 (1995). In State v. North, the AMIP was never asked what he would do if he were evicted from his apartment, but his responses to other questions demonstrated that he understood the need for shelter, as well as the intricacies of obtaining it, which was sufficient for the court to reverse his commitment. 189 Or App 518, 524 (2003).

In State v. Shorett, the court reversed a commitment when appellant’s efforts to secure housing demonstrated that she recognized a need for shelter. 194 Or App 587 (2004).

6. Homelessness

“Homelessness, alone, is not sufficient grounds for a ‘basic needs’ commitment… Rather, the record must persuasively establish that, because of the totality of the circumstances, ‘appellant [if homeless] probably would not survive in the near future.’” State v. Hayes, 202 Or App 63, 67 (2005) (quoting Bunting, 112 Or App 143, 146). Here, as in Hayes, although appellant’s “prospects of finding housing…were, at best, uncertain,” id., the record does not establish the requisite threat to appellant’s survival in the near future.” State v. M.A.B., 212 Or App 400 (2007).

7. The Inquiry Must Focus on Conditions at the Time of the Hearing

As with dangerousness to self or others, the ability to care for oneself must be analyzed in light of existing as opposed to past or potential future conditions. State v. Bunting, 112 Or App 143 (1992). See also State v. Woolridge, 101 Or App 390 (1990), and State v. Arnston, 47 Or App 477 (1980). Thus, even if an AMIP was unable to care for her basic needs when she entered the hospital one week before, she should not be committed if, on the day of the hearing, she is likely to be able to survive into the near future.

AMIP had been cared for by his mother until she died a few months before the hearing. State v. Cunningham (akaState v. M.C.), 190 Or App 202 (2003): Refusal to take medication is not sufficient, by itself, to prove an inability to provide for basic personal needs if condition for which medication is prescribed is not life-threatening.

State v. Saephan (akaState v. State v. Nguyen (akaState v. H.N.), 180 Or App 541 (2002): AMIP’s untreated diabetes and high cholesterol were not life threatening in the near future so failure to maintain treatment could not be a basis for commitment.

State v. Stephens (akaState v. A.S.), 178 Or App 31 (2001): AMIP with history of schizophrenia was willing to take meds, able to pay bills and had some insight into his mental illness. There was insufficient evidence to establish that his delusions would prevent him from acquiring that which was necessary for life.

State v. Cori Aron, 176 Or App 342 (2001): AMIP who, without assistance, might have met criteria should not have been committed where she had food to eat a place to stay, health insurance, friends and family willing to help, and a willingness to ask for help.

State v. Blanding, 174 Or App 238 (2001): AMIP was taken into custody after she emptied her purse on a restaurant table, laughed loudly and poured a cup of coffee on herself. AMIP indicated that she would not take medications and would rather shoot herself than go back to the hospital. But evidence was insufficient to warrant commitment where AMIP owned her own home, did her own shopping and cooking and had $15,000 in her bank account. Her statement about shooting herself was at least as likely to be an expression of frustration as a legitimate threat.

State v. DeMartino, 164 Or App 331 (1999): AMIP with bipolar disorder had rotting food in her refrigerator, many bags of garbage in her garage and mice in her home. She had also missed recent mortgage payments and did not believe she had a mental disorder. However, nothing in the record indicated that AMIP’s ability to survive in the near future was in doubt. State v. Ayala, 164 Or App 399 (1999): AMIP had gestational diabetes but there was no evidence that the condition was life threatening or immediately harmful to AMIP or her fetus. The conjecture of the examiners did not sufficient evidence make.

State v. White, 155 Or App 288 (1998): Civil commitment is not a paternalistic vehicle for “saving people from themselves.” AMIP “is, unquestionably delusional; her lifestyle is, at least by some lights, unconventional. But despite her disorder, she gets by—on her own terms.”

State v. State v. Headings, 140 Or App 421 (1996): AMIP was at risk of losing her house because her mother was no longer going to make the mortgage payments. However, AMIP testified that she understood that she would have to make payments on her own and planned on obtaining a job. The risk of losing one’s house in the future is neither likely enough nor clearly soon enough.

State v. Strasburger, 138 Or App 409 (1996): A difference in personal philosophy from that which is perceived by society as normal is not a basis for commitment. AMIP wanted to rely on the goodness of others, rather than his money, for survival. Evidence showed he had access to soup kitchens, shelters and funds if he chose to use them.

State v. Kuenzi, 137 Or App 207 (1995): AMIP who suffered from delusions was losing the relationships she had because of her less than harmonious disposition. The examiner testified there would be continued decompensation without hospitalization but there was no evidence that AMIP would be unable to survive in the near future.

State v. Baxter, 138 Or App 94 (1995): Tenuous housing, sleeplessness, hernia, dehydration and refusal to take medications for schizophrenia is not enough where the constellation of symptoms do not convincingly point to an inability to survive in the near future.

State v. Whitman, 132 Or App 596 (1995): AMIP was uncooperative and refused to speak. The examiners opined that the refusal to communicate was due to a mental disorder and indicated an inability to care for self. Such speculation does not rise to the level of clear and convincing evidence.

State v. Johnson, 131 Or App 561 (1994): Where AMIP’s family could provide housing and aid in seeking treatment and AMIP was agreeable to that help, there was little evidence that AMIP would not survive.

State v. Tardanico, 119 Or App 166 (1993): “In the light of the facts that [AMIP] earns income from rental property, has a job, and has a plan for where she will live in the future, the evidence offered by the state does not meet” the standard required for a basic needs commitment.

State v. Stanley, 117 Or App 327 (1992): State failed to show that homeless person was unable to care for himself given evidence that he had thought about where he would live, eat and get his medication, that he received Social Security, and that he had family members who could assist him.

State v. Brungard, 101 Or App 67, mod 102 Or App 509, rev den 311 Or 427 (1990): Where there was clear evidence that AMIP would probably not take her medications if released, there was insufficient evidence of an imminent threat even when combined with recent sleeplessness, regular eating and a past history of suicidality while unmedicated.

State v. Woolridge, 101 Or App 390 (1990): AMIP threatened to burn the Mayor’s house down and was violent, disjointed and disorganized when taken into custody. At the hearing AMIP was significantly improved. Speculation regarding an inability to survive in the near future was not clear and convincing.

State v. Nance, 85 Or App 143 (1987): Severe weight loss and recurring sleeping problems were insufficient where experts testified only of possible health difficulties rather than a high likelihood that AMIP would not survive.

State v. Garibbo, 77 Or App 321 (1986): It is not enough that AMIP has a mental disorder and needs psychiatric help. The examiners conclusions that AMIP was too disorganized to take care of himself and unwilling to take medication or see a doctor were merely conjecture, unconnected to any facts presented at the hearing.

State v. Arnston, 47 Or App 477 (1980): At the time of the hearing, AMIP was in the process of being evicted but the only evidence that she would be unable to find housing was that she had become confused three months earlier when she had tried to look for housing. This is insufficient where AMIP had a place to stay at the time of the hearing and was cognizant of the need to move in the near future.

State v. Heintz, 26 Or App 935 (1976): AMIP’s lack of income, food, and refusal to apply for welfare did not render her unable to care for her basic needs where she was alert at the hearing and seeking unemployment and there was no indication of malnutrition, ill health or poor dress.

9. Basic Needs Cases Affirmed (with an opinion)

State v. Jayne, 174 Or App 74 (2001): AMIP’s delusions prevented her from managing her finances and keeping her house; caused her to be unable to eat properly, shown by a 25 pound weight loss in the previous year; and instigated a “very severe deterioration from [a] highly functional state. . . to the degree of inability to cope.” She also continued to refuse to take medications so there was little evidence she would be able to break out of the downward spiral.

State v. Herdan, 129 Or App 24 (1994): Evidence was sufficient where AMIP’s mental disorder resulted in lack of awareness of serious weight loss, failure to take medications, inability to maintain housing and unwillingness to cooperate with case manager.

State v. Bunting, 112 Or App 143 (1992): AMIP “suffers from a bi-polar disorder and has been a patient in mental hospitals at least ten times in the last 13 years. Three weeks before the commitment hearing, he rode a bus from Portland to Vancouver, Washington, but forgot or could not decide why he did that. He returned to Portland and checked himself into a hospital. While there, he was agitated, confused and, at times, aggressive. The staff had to isolate him periodically to prevent odd behavior, such as rolling himself into a ball and removing all his clothes. He refused to eat, claiming without factual basis that someone was poisoning the food. Doctors believed that defendant’s medication was causing an organic problem that might be influencing his disorganized thinking and behavior but were unable to monitor his medication, because he was unable to communicate or cooperate due to his mental disorder.” A speculative threat is not sufficient. However the state need not postpone action until the individual is on the brink of death. The goal of the commitment statute is safe survival, not merely the avoidance of immediate death.

State v. Evjen, 111 Or App 368 (1992): The medical examiners recommended against commitment on the basis of appellant’s relative lucidity at the hearing, her resumption of medication after an altercation with an apartment owner and her stated willingness to continue taking her medication. However, appellant had a history of mental illness, a habit of failing or refusing to take needed medication, which resulted in hostile or dangerous behavior and a tendency to abuse drugs. AMIP’s case manager testified persuasively (to the trial and appellate court) that the history of AMIP’s illness and the inadequacy of discharge would lead to an inability to survive in the near future.

State v. Harpole, 101 Or App 405 (1990): Grossly unsanitary living conditions and lack of personal hygiene may be sufficient.

F. The Nexus

It is not sufficient for the state merely to prove that a person has a mental disorder and that the person is dangerous to others, dangerous to self or unable to provide for basic needs. In addition, the state must also prove a causal nexus between the mental disorder and at least one of the ORS 426.005(1)(e) criteria. State v. Gjerde, 147 Or App 187 (1997). The most common way this comes up is in cases where the allegedly mentally ill person, who clearly has a mental disorder, used drugs and did something stupid and dangerous. The nexus question for the court is whether the mental disorder caused the dangerous activity or if the drug use caused it. Because if the only nexus is between drug use and past dangerous activity then the statutory requirements are not met despite the existence of both a mental disorder and dangerousness.

The overarching standard was nicely summarized in the recent case of State v. N.A.P., 216 Or App 432 (2007):

A court ordered “dangerous to self” mental commitment requires clear and convincing evidence that a person has a “mental disorder.” The state must then show causation—that current indicators, symptoms, or behaviors of that mental disorder show that the threatened harm is likely to occur or follow a similar pattern that led toward harm in the past. State v. Puha,208 Or App 453, 464 (2006). The condition of “dangerous to self” must be caused by the person’s present mental disorder to justify intervention to protect her from her own dangerous state. Id. As we stated in Puha, “without proof of the element of causation, we risk judging and committing individuals based solely on their history of mental health problems rather than to satisfy a current need for protection.” Id. The causation standard is the “product of fundamental recognition of the priority of preserving personal liberties in civil commitment cases. The tension between the protection of personal liberties and the provision of medical help to persons with mental disorders can be relieved only if courts strictly adhere to the statutory requirements for involuntary commitment and ensure that there is an evidentiary basis that satisfies each of the requirements.”

State v. Hambleton, 202 Or App 526, 534. In State v. Gjerde, 147 Or App 187 (1997), the AMIP made a free, knowing, and rational choice to undertake an action with existing attendant risks. While not necessarily the most prudent choice, it was hers to make. It is not the state’s prerogative under the civil commitment statutes to interfere with that choice. Unless a mental disorder has impaired autonomous choice, civil commitment cannot be a vehicle for “saving people from themselves.”

In State v. Pike, 197 Or App 508 (2005), the state was able to prove that the AMIP had a mental disorder and that he had a hard time providing for his basic needs, however, it was unable to prove the causal nexus between the two. The AMIP had been completely cared for by his mother his entire life. He had not left her home in over five years. Isolation was just as likely his mental disorder making it difficult for him to live on his own. “Commitment cannot be used to achieve desirable social goals such as providing custodial care so that a person can master the skills of life to live on his own.” Id.

In State v. Phyll, 36 Or App 627 (1978), the AMIP was shown to have a mental disorder and to have been engaging in unsafe activities, such as sleeping in a bus shelter. However, the court reversed her commitment because the activities “were just as likely a result of appellant’s financial position as of her mental condition.” Id. at 630.

In State v. Lucas, 31 Or App 947 (1977), the appellate court reversed on the basis that there was no nexus shown between the mental disorder and the dangerous activity. This, however, is a case in which the court got it wrong. Both future harm and mental disorder were lacking, not the nexus. This type of confusion is not unusual. Mr. Lucas was hallucinating while in jail and as a result had been injuring himself. The court said that because the self-injurious behavior may have been a result of taking LSD, there was an insufficient nexus between the alleged disorder and the facts supporting self-harm. The court should have found no mental disorder because the LSD wore off by the time of the hearing with no lasting psychotic effects. Even if Mr. Lucas had an LSD-induced disorder when in jail, it was no longer present. There was also no likelihood of harm in the near future because the LSD was no longer present and, therefore, no longer causing self-injurious behavior. The one requirement that was present was a nexus. The psychotic symptoms clearly caused the self-injurious behavior.

In State v. Sickler, 133 Or App 50 (1995), evidence existed of “cocaine and alcohol abuse in the past and of bizarre behavior immediately preceding the time of the hearing. What was missing was evidence of ingestion of alcohol or cocaine around the time of hearing that would demonstrate a nexus between appellant’s previous behavior patterns and his recent behavior. Although it is inferable from the evidence that appellant’s behavior resulted from chronic alcoholism, it is equally inferable, as suggested by the examiner, that it resulted from other causes, such as lack of impulse control or antisocial tendencies.”

In State v. Gibson, 196 Or App 57 (2004), the appellate court found that paraphilia could be a disorder but that the nexus was crucial in distinguishing between sex offenders for whom commitment was appropriate and those for whom it was not. Ensuring that the function of a civil commitment is properly limited does not require a demonstration that a dangerous individual is completely unable to control his behavior. Proof of serious difficulty in controlling behavior, together with a severe mental abnormality, is what distinguishes the dangerous sexual offender whose serious mental illness, abnormality, or disorder subjects him to civil commitment from the dangerous but typical recidivist convicted in an ordinary criminal case. In Gibson the record contained clear and convincing evidence of the required nexus between the AMIP’s mental disorder of paraphilia and his dangerousness to others. This included an expert’s sex offender evaluation that clearly linked his “entrenched” and “reinforced” “thought processes” relating to “forcing women to have sex with him and hurting them if they resist” to his clear “potential for acting out aggressively on females.” Id.

G. Chronic Mental Illness—The Expanded Criteria

1. People Well Known to the Mental Health System

There are some people with severe and persistent mental disorders that enter the commitment process with such regularity that the progression of behavior during times of decompensation and increased symptoms is an established matter of record. The point of the expanded criteria is to catch such people during the downswing—before lives are in danger but late enough that the person may actually become dangerous or unsafe in the near future.

2. Limited Tool for the State

Expanding the traditional criteria was an excellent idea in theory which has turned out to be enormously difficult to apply in an actual hearing. In fact, as a practical matter, the chronic mental illness criterion is rarely useful for the state. If the judge can predict that a person will be committably dangerous in the near future then in the context of a commitment hearing the person seems actually dangerous now. After all, in every commitment case the judge must decide whether there is a serious risk of harm in the near future. The judge won’t assume a time lag between the point of becoming dangerous and actual harm. Prediction of the future is notoriously difficult. The decision of the judge in any commitment hearing is usually abbreviated to a more general question of whether there is a serious risk of harm in the near future, not whether the person is dangerous now. A person who meets the requirements for the expanded criteria will meet the abbreviated standard (serious risk of harm in the near future) every time.

A second reason that the expanded criteria are less than useful for the state is that they are cumbersome to prove. There are five statutory elements to a finding of chronic mental illness under 426.005(1)(e)(C):

–The person must be 18 years of age or older. ORS 426.495 via ORS 426.005(1)(C)(i).

–The person must have been diagnosed by a psychiatrist, licensed clinical psychologist or certified examiner with a chronic mood disorder or a chronic psychotic disorder other than those caused by substance abuse. ORS 426.495 via ORS 426.005(1)(C)(i).

–Within the previous three years, the person must have twice been placed in a hospital or inpatient facility under ORS 426.060 (i.e., successful outpatient commitments, successful conditional releases, and Chapter 427 MRDD commitments do not count). ORS 426.005(1)(C)(ii).

–The person must be exhibiting symptoms or behavior substantially similar to that which preceded and led to one or more of the placements. ORS 426.005(1)(C)(iii).

–Unless treated, the person will continue, to a reasonable medical probability, to deteriorate to the point of being a danger to self, others or unable to care for basic needs. ORS 426.005(1)(C)(iv).

In addition, as noted above, it must be probable that deterioration to the point of being genuinely dangerous or unsafe will occur in the “near future.” State v. Brungard, 101 Or App 67, 73 (1990). There are no commitment-specific evidentiary exceptions that assist the state in such cases. Thus, witnesses are needed to shore up the diagnosis of chronic mental illness and to provide accounts of the previous hospitalizations, the similarity of symptoms and the medical probability of dangerousness in the near future. This set of requirements, all of which must be proved by clear and convincing evidence, is far more burdensome than the traditional criteria.

3. Continued Deterioration Should Not Include People with Currently Improving Symptoms

ORS 426.005(1)(C)(iv) requires the state to prove that “unless treated, the person will continue, to a reasonable medical probability, to deteriorate to the point of being a danger to self or others or unable to care for basic needs. By using the words “continue…to deteriorate,” the statute seems to require that there be deterioration immediately prior to the hearing. For example, expanded criteria may not be applicable to a case where there has been a period of improvement prior to the hearing but it is still probable that there would be immediate deterioration if released. The statute appears to require that the AMIP be as symptomatic or more on the day of the hearing then when initially detained. It can be argued that the words “unless treated” buttress such an argument. If a person has been taken into custody and forcibly treated for a week before the hearing then the person has been treated. The statute seems to envision that such cases will be handled out of custody. After all, in order to hold a person there must be a decision by a doctor and a judge that probable cause exists to believe the person meets the criteria.

4. Expanded Criteria Does Not Violate the Constitution

The Oregon Court of Appeals has held that the chronic mental illness (or expanded criteria) framework does not violate due process or equal protection. State v. Brungard, 101 Or App 67, 73 (1990).

5. The Investigator May Testify to Prior Commitments and Symptomology

Most of the elements can be satisfied by the testimony of the investigator if the investigator has personal knowledge of the symptoms or behavior prior to the past commitments. State v. Belli, 120 Or App 94 (1993).

6. Two Prior Inpatient Placements Does Not Mean Two Commitments

In State v. Hilliard, 195 Or App 538 (2004), the court made it clear that the requirement of two previous hospitalizations or inpatient placements did not mean there needed to be two prior commitments. Mr. Hilliard was found chronically mentally ill. He had once been committed to the hospital and once been revoked off of conditional release status and placed in the hospital. That these two placements were made in the same 180-day commitment period was irrelevant to the requirements of the statute.

H. Speculation

Whether it is mental disorder, basic needs, danger to others or danger to self, the appellate courts have made it clear that a legal finding may not be based on apprehension, speculation or conjecture. It is not easy, however, to distinguish valid conclusions from invalid speculation.

State v. Sea, 137 Or App 333 (1995), is illustrative on this matter. Despite the AMIP’s significant recent weight loss, blackouts, delusions of “noisy” produce that made going to the grocery store difficult and refusal to let her caseworker enter her home, the court found the evidence insufficient for commitment because the symptoms were never established as genuinely dangerous. Id.

The court pointed out that, while weight loss is potentially meaningful, here “there is no evidence of how much appellant weighs. Thus, we have no meaningful referent for the significance of the loss of 10 pounds over a period of several months. Nor, in a related sense, did the state adduce any evidence that the weight loss had adversely affected appellant’s health, that it was likely to continue, or that, if it did, it was likely to affect her health in the immediate future.” Id.

Delusions could also be legally significant. The court explained that, “although the evidence suggests that appellant’s delusions have, on occasion, prevented her from completing errands, including grocery shopping, there is no evidence as to how frequently those episodes occur and whether they prevent appellant from obtaining an adequate supply of food.” Id.at 337.

“Appellant’s change in conduct toward her caseworker was a consequence of her Capgras Syndrome, i.e., she perceived that different individuals were trying to impersonate him, which, in turn, caused her to be unsure of who was visiting or calling when her caseworker attempted to contact her. Here again, however, the record is deficient in that it does not show the consequences, if any, of the caseworker’s inability to contact appellant. In particular, it is unclear whether appellant maintains contact with other persons besides the caseworker, including those who could provide, or arrange for her to receive, health care as needed. There is no evidence that appellant has failed to seek medical treatment when necessary.” Id.

Finally, “although appellant testified that she experiences “blackouts,” there is no evidence as to the frequency, severity, or consequences of such episodes. Nor is there is any evidence that appellant has ever been endangered as a result of such episodes.” Id. at 338.

In summary, the necessary showing for commitment requires specific facts that form an extraordinarily persuasive foundation for the court to conclude that a particular symptom threatens the person’s survival in the near future.

The Commitment Process

I. Initiation

The first step is that someone has to initiate the legal process of civil commitment. This can happen in a number of ways. The most common method is where a doctor places a hold after the person ends up at the hospital. Sometimes family or friends bring the person to the hospital, sometimes the police bring the person to the hospital in lieu of arrest and sometimes the person is already being treated on a medical unit. The important thing is that once an allegedly mentally ill person is at the hospital, a physician decides whether the person is dangerous to himself or others and is in need of emergency treatment for a mental disorder. The physician then consults with another physician or a qualified mental health professional and writes a notice of mental illness and emergency hold. ORS 426.232.

A much less common way of initiating the commitment process is that two people in the community file a written, sworn petition with the court stating that the person is mentally ill, in need of treatment and a resident of the county in which they file the petition. This is usually called a two-party petition. They may also but need not request a warrant of detention, which would hold the person in custody pending the investigation. A judge must sign off on the warrant of detention and may only do so if “there is probable cause to believe that failure to take the person into custody would pose serious harm or danger to the person or to others.” ORS 426.070(5)(b)(A).

Another uncommon but possible method of initiating commitment proceedings is a magistrate’s hold. That is, a judge can sign the same ORS 426.070 petition as the two-party hold as well as the warrant of detention. As an aside, ORS 426.288 and 426.233 grant police officers and county mental health designees the power to hold a person long enough to get them to a hospital and be evaluated by a physician. However, only the physician, with consultation, can file the paperwork to start an investigation.

ORS 426.090 requires that a citation be given to the AMIP stating the “nature of the information filed concerning the person,” “the specific reasons the person is believed to be mentally ill,” a “notice of the time and place of the commitment hearing,” “the right to legal counsel” and to have counsel appointed if requested, “the right to subpoena witnesses” and “other information as the court may direct.” This notice and advice of rights is separate from, and in addition to, the rights that are to be read at the hearing under ORS 426.100.

ORS 426.070(5)(a) requires that, following the investigation and in order to set a hearing, a court must make a finding that there is probable cause to believe that the AMIP is mentally ill.

The petition or notice of mental illness must be continuing at the time of hearing. In State v. VanTassel, 5 Or App 376 (1971), one signor of a two-party notice attempted to withdraw notice. The appellate court held that the trial court cannot refuse such a withdrawal and the commitment should have been dismissed.

When a person is being held involuntarily, a hearing must be held within five judicial days of the time he or she is taken into custody, not counting the first day. Technically, the hearing must be held within five days of the citation or warrant of detention, which is required in order to take a person into custody under the 426.070 scheme. Thus, it is the same as five days from the time they’re taken into custody. ORS 426.095(2)(a)(b). If the hearing is pursuant to a physician emergency admission, ORS 426.232(2) states, “Under no circumstances may the person be held for longer than five judicial days.” The various other holds all require that a decision be made pursuant to 426.232, thereby falling under the subsection (2) deadline.

ORS 426.180 sets out a separate set of rules for an emergency state hospital admission of Native Americans living on federal reservations free from state jurisdiction. The commitment process is initiated by any two people who sign an affidavit stating that they believe the person is so mentally ill as to be in need of immediate hospitalization. The affidavit must be accompanied by a recitation of recent circumstances, an application for admission to the hospital, a medical history and the name and address of the nearest relative or guardian. Assuming a doctor at the hospital consents, the person is admitted to the hospital. Within 48 hours of admission, two physicians at the hospital must perform a mental health examination and certify that the person is mentally ill and in need of treatment. If they cannot so certify then the person must be discharged. Within 48 hours of the certification, the superintendent must either 1) obtain permission for a voluntary admission, or 2) file a complaint requesting an ORS chapter 426 civil commitment in the normal course.

II. Setovers

Prior to a hearing, a request for a postponement of five judicial days may be made by the AMIP, the guardian, defense counsel, or the state. However, if the AMIP is being held in custody a state’s request for a postponement must result in release, unless it is agreed to by the AMIP or defense counsel. According to ORS 426.095(2)(c):

If a person is detained before a hearing under ORS 426.070, 426.180, 426.228, 426.232 or 426.233 and the hearing is postponed under this paragraph, the court, for good cause, may allow the person to be detained during the postponement if the postponement is requested by the person or the legal counsel of the person.

That is, the court may only grant a setover and also continue to detain a person when the setover request has been made by the defense.

III. Investigation—Overview

The investigator has a difficult job. She receives the petition on day one. She then has approximately 48 hours to go to the hospital, evaluate the client, read the chart, speak to hospital staff, interview family, friends and witnesses, review police reports or additional medical records and research the mental health history of the allegedly mentally ill person. If the investigator concludes that the allegedly mentally ill person does not meet the statutory requirements for mental illness then she must release the AMIP. She may do so by writing a recommendation to the court that a commitment hearing should not occur. If she believes that the statutory requirements are met, then she may either recommend a diversion—essentially an extension of the 5-day hold to 14 days—or a hearing.

Qualifications: The investigator must be certified by county mental health.

A. Role of the Investigator

1. Determine if there is probable cause to believe the person is mentally ill as defined by statute.

2. Determine the least restrictive treatment option. OAR 309-033-930.

3. Make a recommendation to the court:

  • a commitment hearing should not occur
  • a commitment hearing should occur
  • commitment criteria are met but a diversion is appropriate.

4. If a hearing is determined, write a report to give it to parties and examiners.

C. A Story

Somewhere in the Middle: County Investigators

The men and women in charge of determining which holds in Multnomah County end up in civil commitment hearings begin each day by meting out the holds that have come in overnight. They slowly gather, coffee cups in hand, in a cubicle hosting an easel posting all of the names and locations of their new clients. The unit supervisor of the Involuntary Commitment unit pulls their business cards out of a hat at 8:30 a.m. every morning, determining the order in which the 14 workers choose new clients. They tell me that they keep each other honest with evenly sharing the load and that they generally try to pick up new clients by location or by history. It turns out that almost half of all clients referred for hold—46 percent in the 2006–2007 fiscal year—are referred to the county investigators more than once. Some savvy consumers reportedly “hospital hop” after being released from holds against their will. They tell a few amusing stories of clients with Borderline Personality Disorder involving cranes and other dramatic cries for attention before hunkering down to work. Today there are 13 new holds, meaning one new client per investigator—one guy is on vacation.

The investigators seem to be a friendly lot, happy to be interviewed for the purposes of this book, and two of them bring me to a window cubicle to chat about their place in the system. Although the investigators acknowledge a natural tension between the public defenders arguing for their clients’ civil liberties and the doctors, social workers and families who are concerned about the same clients’ safety, the workers with whom I spoke did not disparage the system. After spelling out the basics of their jobs, two investigators respond to my questions regarding what they would like lawyers in the hearings to know about their role. The two at first chuckle over the question they get most often from attorneys—“Did you think of___?” (insert obvious community resource)—and then state that, as no one in their unit has worked there less than four years, yes, they have certainly thought of whatever “it” is. They posit that it may not be obvious, but they have four whole days of work together before the lawyers meet their clients.

Investigators usually work with the same clients when they recognize their names come up on the roster, and have histories with many of them (last year the highest number of repeat holds on the same person was 16). As investigating these holds is the sole responsibility of people in this position, the workers consult family members, police, nurses, social workers, neighbors, psychiatrists, and others to both gather information and generate any plan for keeping the clients safe without facing a civil commitment. Along the same lines, the investigators stress the difficult position of family members and involved professionals who appear as witnesses in these hearings, and become discouraged and severely inconvenienced with repeated setovers. They stated—many times, actually—that for most involved persons and certainly for all county investigators, going to a hearing is the absolute last resort.

Their second answer to my question also has to do with perception. One stated that he was surprised after talking with a lawyer from the public defenders’ office who conveyed the impression that county investigators are a branch of the district attorney’s office. The two again laughed and admitted that they don’t know the name of anyone in the DA’s office. They said that they write their reports based on whether or not they believe their clients meet criteria for commitment based on the OARs, turn the reports over to the DA’s office, and are called as witnesses in maybe one out of ten cases. Again they stressed that hearings are their last resort, and that the hearings are the least fun part of the job.

After the morning routine with the business cards, the first step of the investigation is to visit each new client. This could take a worker to any combination of the seven area hospitals—Emanuel, Good Samaritan, Oregon Health and Sciences University, the VA, St. Vincent’s, Providence, Adventist, or Mt. Hood Medical Center. Today I travel with one of the investigators first to the Emergency Department of Emanuel Hospital as he jokes that the office once received two calls in a matter of hours responding to the “How am I driving?” query on his passenger side.

This is day zero for a woman of indeterminate age lying on a bed in the dark, severely depressed. The investigator checks in with the hospital social worker and reads over the file. He lets me know from first glance that this is not a case that will end up in a hearing as the client called 9-1-1 on her own, a move signaling her ability to care for her own needs. He turns on her light and asks a few questions. The woman has razor blade–inflicted but superficial lacerations up and down her legs and arms but notably absent from her wrists. She remains on the bed with her head on a pillow and her arm over her face and cries intermittently throughout the interview. She is surprisingly candid and willing to talk to a stranger about the crisis leading to this hospitalization involving the loss of her dog. The details are many, but the investigator manages to elicit this woman’s hopes, which include access to psychiatric medications (she recites numerous past psychiatric diagnoses, including Borderline Personality Disorder), therapy, and a place to stay. He assures her that he will talk to the hospital social worker to try to get a plan in place for these concerns. He again consults with the social worker and comes up with a plan to assist this woman in finding voluntary treatment. The woman may not have a job or a place to live, but she is willing to engage in treatment and apparently able to care for her basic needs. The hold is dropped.

The second stop of the day is a brief walk from the ER to the psychiatric ward at Emanuel to visit a client on day two of a hold. The investigator explains that this client is a different story. He is 20 years old, schizophrenic with negative symptoms (absence of speech and movement), noncompliant with medications, and homeless. He’s been on three holds previously. Two of the holds were referred for hearings as he was only eating when both directed and closely supervised. He was released on both occasions because his father admitted that he would be willing to take care of him if no other options existed, removing the imminence of the threat to his safety. I’m told that this is one of the situations that is not well served by the system in that schizophrenia affects the decision-making part of the brain. Therefore, the client’s desire to be released in previous hearings—a desire which has been lawfully represented by his lawyers—is biologically flawed and has prevented him from getting the earliest possible treatment and best hope for intervention with schizophrenia. The investigator has kept this client in mind, trying on numerous occasions to find appropriate treatment even when he is not an official client.

The investigator checks in with the hospital staff about how the client has been doing on the unit (“He’s been quiet.”) and then walks to his room, after asking the nurse to keep the room on screen just in case. I accompany the investigator and am warned that this client can be unpredictable and has punched holes in the walls. Today, the young man is lying on a bed facing the wall, completely unresponsive to conversation. He’s curled into a fetal position and doesn’t even flinch at his name being called loudly. The investigator tells me that he spoke with the client’s father the day before, and that he is no longer willing to allow his son to live at home out of fear for the safety of the family; nor is he willing to be a witness in future hearings. Although the investigator knows it is not a slam dunk, this case is again being referred for a hearing. He’s hoping that without the option of returning home, and perhaps an examination in court mirroring our lack of interaction today, it will be determined that this actively schizophrenic homeless youth legally meets criteria for a civil commitment by virtue of being “unable to care for basic needs” and may finally get treatment.

We then travel to the last stop of the day, the behavioral unit at Good Samaritan Hospital to visit a client on day four of a hold. This man diagnosed with Bipolar Disorder started off in the Intensive Care Unit at Emanuel after an overdose on alcohol and pills but moved units and hospitals when the attending physician placed a hold. This is the fourth day that the investigator has met with this client. He was ready to release the hold yesterday, but a call from the attending doctor convinced him that a day of further mood stabilization would be helpful. Today, the man recites his plan for returning home and setting an appointment with his regular psychiatrist. The investigator tells me afterwards that this gentleman had demonstrated tremendous mood volatility in previous conversations, alternating between congenial and tearfully vulgar with no warning. However, the hospital social worker helped him to set up an appointment with his psychiatrist, his wife was planning to pick him up, and he was expressing no suicidal ideation. The hold was dropped.

Today’s ratio of cases being sent to hearing is actually high. Between July 2006 and the end of June 2007, 4,083 hold investigations were referred. Of those cases, only 8 percent were sent to a hearing—92 percent of the investigations ended in dropped holds (14-day diversions, voluntary treatment, lack of probable cause, or withdrawn petitions).

When asked what they would change about the civil commitment process, the investigators said that they don’t see a need to settle the tensions inherent in the system as it currently functions—as safety and liberty are equally important. In fact, there seemed to be a recognition that those who truly need the protection of a civil commitment will cycle back through the system and eventually get the help they need. They did admit to a “perfect world” scenario in which all involved professionals were allowed and encouraged to collaborate over each client.

'D. The Hearing

The investigator usually assists the state in organizing evidence and choosing which witnesses to subpoena or call. His role is similar to that of the police in a criminal case who are charged with both neutrally investigating and then successfully building a case for the district attorney or county counsel.

IV. Defense Investigation

A. Talk to the Hospital Staff

Talk to hospital staff regarding your client. The people participating in your client’s care will generally be your best source of information on the client. They are also the only people who will warn you if the client has any specific triggers, racial biases, and so forth. In your discussions with the staff, try to get the following information.

  • General behavior on the ward: isolative or mixing in the milieu, and if the later, are interactions good or not? Cooperative or disruptive?
  • Progress made during week stay, if any.
  • Medications prescribed. It’s not critical that you get all the medications they’re on, but you’ll want to know if there are any particular irregularities.
  • Medication compliance. This will come up in court as it is relevant to their ability to treat voluntarily.
  • Signs of discharge planning, if any.
  • Notable comments from treating psychiatrist, or treating physician, if any.
  • ADLs, or lack thereof. This stands for Activities of Daily Living, and refers to bathing and hygiene issues.
  • Medical issues, if any.

Note: Creating and maintaining good relationships with hospital staff will make your job easier and more pleasant, and will commonly result in you getting more information that is helpful to your client. In that spirit, I recommend not asking the staff whether they believe that your client should be in the hospital. Many people, including staff, will blame us for clients getting released, and it is counterproductive to have these discussions. Asking staff whether they think a person can take care of their basic needs is a much more productive way to get the same information.

B. Check the Chart

The chart is, generally, supplementary to staff interviews, but in certain cases the relevant staff will not be present or there may be information in the chart from an attending psychiatrist or similarly useful person. Take note of the same information given above, noting especially any discrepancies.

C. Client Interview

This is the most critical part of the pre-hearing investigation and also the most difficult. While it is always desirable to get information, the main goal is to see how the client is likely to react in court, and therefore it is more productive to allow yourself some leeway, both in questions asked and in general approach. In time you will find both a general approach that works well for you and specific approaches that work well with certain types of clients. Take the following as a rough guide.

1. Greet the client. Generally, if you’re not being introduced by staff, greet the client after having opened the door a bit; this allows the client to choose to talk to you, and prevents you from getting into the same room with the occasional extremely hostile client.

2. Ask if they know about the hearing they’re going to. If not, explain briefly and segue to the next question.

3. Ask about client’s understanding of why he or she is in the hospital. For some clients, this will launch a long recitation of the events that brought them in, which is useful but which you will sometimes have to interrupt.

4. Explain criteria, and that the court will be trying to determine whether any of these criteria apply. Ask client if any of them apply, if it seems appropriate.

5. Explain 1) the right to a hearing, 2) the right to a setover, 3) the possibility of a stipulation, and 4) potential outcomes. Ask client which they would prefer, but be sure not to actively encourage any of the options. Legal assistants can’t give advice, and mentally ill clients should not be pushed towards options they may not even understand.

6. If the client wants a stipulation, explain again that they would be confined to the hospital for an unknown length of time and would have to comply with medications and staff directions. If they reiterate their desire for a stipulation, you can likely skip the remaining steps. If you are not confident that they really understand a stipulation, however, you should do the rest of the interview in case they change their mind and desire a hearing. Many clients will change their minds from day to day.

7. In the case of a setover request, explain again that they will have to stay in the hospital in the intervening time and will still need a hearing at the end. Explain also that the investigator will have to sign off on a no-transport setover. If they again request a setover, you can skip the rest of the interview.

8. Ask whether client thinks he or she has a mental illness.

9. Ask whether client has or has had any mental health diagnoses.

10. Ask whether client thinks he or she has mental health issues/symptoms/voices.

11. Hone in on essential issues of case from the report and ask about how that’s going. Remember to ask specific questions about the allegations, for example: For an allegation of dangerousness to self, ask the client whether he or she is feeling suicidal. Note whether or not their answer is convincing. For an allegation of dangerousness to others, ask if the client wants to hurt anyone. Is he or she angry with anyone? What does he or she plan to do about it? What can be done about problems of anger?

12. Ask whether client is working with a caseworker or provider. If so, who is the caseworker and how is the relationship? Does the client want to keep working with that person?

13. Ask about discharge planning.

a.SSD or SSI, or any other source of income. Who is the payee? Where does the client pick up the money?

b.Housing. If possible, get specific. You may need to prove the client can get to wherever they live, so see if the client knows the bus route or plans on getting a ride from someone.

c. Treatment. Reiterate details about any mental health treatment the client is willing to participate in. The more it’s repeated, the more likely it will be mentioned during their hearing.

d. Community support, if any. This may be a place in which the client will divulge potential witnesses.

e. Ask whether the client believes his or her medications (if any) are helping, and if the client will continue to take them. If not, why not?

f. Ask specifically there are any witnesses the client may want to have at the hearing. They need to be people who exist, can be located, and have had some recent contact.

14. Give client the info sheets on mental health services/homeless resources, if relevant.

15. Ask if client is a U.S. citizen, if relevant. If the client is not, inquire about immigration status.

D. Interview Tips

1. Watch the client, not the checklist. Your primary job is to convey to the attorney the demeanor and appearance of the client. The specific information conveyed to you by the client is of secondary importance. Moreover, some clients are unpredictable and you will need to monitor their behavior closely for your own safety.

2. Do not write down all the responses verbatim. Clients are very sensitive to how much time someone spends writing things down and are more open and trusting with people who do not seem concerned with recording the responses. Jot down brief notes to help you remember later.

3. Try to limit digressions. Many clients, schizophrenics and manic people especially, can be hyper-verbal. Try to redirect them back to the matter at hand, without upsetting them in the process. Sometimes this will not be possible, but it still yields useful information for the attorney.

4. The clients do not have to talk to you, and many of them will choose not to. Don’t push it.

5. Try to think in more complex terms than simply whether the client is crazy. It is helpful for the attorney to know how they react to certain questions, whether their thinking is disturbed on some subjects but not others, and so forth.

V. The Commitment Hearing

A. Constitutional Issues

1. Due Process

The Oregon Court of Appeals has held that the procedural requirements of the statutes governing involuntary commitment proceedings provide the necessary and requisite protection of due process rights for the allegedly mentally ill individual. Matter of Mills, 36 Or App 727 (1978).

2. Advice of Rights

ORS 426.100(1) requires that certain advice must be given to the AMIP at the hearing:

(a) The reason for being brought before the court. This means only that the purpose of the hearing is to determine mental illness and, if so, whether commitment for up to 180 days is appropriate. State v. Buffum 166 Or App 552 (2000).

(b) The nature of the proceedings. This means advice regarding the essential character of the hearing. It does not encompass particularized advice about legal or evidentiary standards, such as the criterion for mental illness. “It will be the state’s burden of showing that you are mentally ill, as that expression is defined in the statutes, and they’ll do that by calling witnesses and perhaps presenting other evidence.” Buffum, 166 Or App 552 (2000).

(c) The possible results of the proceedings.

(d) The right to subpoena witnesses. The court must be specific. The statement that the “court was willing to hear any witnesses called by the AMIP” was considered reversible error because it did not specifically mention the right to force witnesses to come to court. State v. Grellert, 144 Or App 201 (1996).

(e) The person’s rights regarding representation by or appointment of counsel.

The aforementioned ORS 426.301 rights must be recited for recommitments, State v. Montgomery, 147 Or App 69 (1997), and stipulations, State v. Allison,129 Or App 47 (1994).

The rights do not need to be read at a trial visit revocation hearing. State v. Vonahlefeld, 140 Or App 248 (1996). It follows that they would also not need to be read at conditional release revocation hearings or outpatient commitment revocation hearings.

The rights can neither be waived by silence, State v. May, 131 Or App 570 (1994), nor by a specific oral waiver. State v. Burge, 167 Or App 312 (2000). A valid waiver of the advice requires a judicial examination regarding the knowing and voluntary nature of the waiver. State v. Vonahlefeld, 140 Or App 248 (1996).

If the court leaves even one of the aforementioned rights out of the recitation it is grounds for reversal. ORS 426.100 is designed to ensure that AMIPs receive the benefit of a full and fair hearing. Eliminating just one right denies that benefit. State v. Allison, 129 Or App 47 (1994). However, recent opinions have found that where the AMIP was given a list of rights pre-hearing and was represented by an attorney at the hearing, not all rights need be recited.

In State v. Bartel-Dawson, 176 Or App 519 (2001), the court found that it was harmless error that the AMIP was not told of his right to subpoena witnesses when given a pre-hearing citation with the statutory rights listed, counsel was appointed and a witness was called at the hearing. The AMIP clearly understood his rights.

In State v. Cach, 172 Or App 745 (2001), the court found it was harmless error that the AMIP wasn’t told of his right to retain counsel where counsel had already been appointed and in making the appointment it was determined that the AMIP had no money with which to retain counsel.

In State v. Scharf, 201 Or App 71 (2005), the judge excused the AMIP from the hearing at the AMIP’s request and then allowed the AMIP’s attorney to waive the reading of the rights. The appellate court reversed because “there is no evidence in this record from which we can conclude that appellant was adequately advised of his rights or that he, in fact, exercised those rights at the hearing.” Id. at 74

Further additions to the recitation of advice could be made, such as a review of the allegations in the Notice of Mental Illness, the right of self-representation, the consequences of commitment, a brief definition of the criterion, or a brief statement distinguishing the evidentiary standard of “clear and convincing evidence” from other standards of proof. However, an expansion of the recitation of rights could be counterproductive. Each additional right creates the potential for misunderstanding and inappropriate behavior. Each additional right is another sixty seconds during which the AMIP’s mind can wander toward a potentially self-destructive topic, and which takes away from the approximately thirty minutes for which the average AMIP is able to “hold it together.” In any case, attorneys should be giving all such advice to clients prior to the start of a hearing.

3. Right to Remain Silent

There is no constitutional right to remain silent in a civil commitment hearing except where the AMIP’s testimony might implicate the person in a criminal matter. Matter of Matthews, 46 Or App 757 (1980); State v. Whitman, 132 Or App 596 (1995). In Matthews, the trial judge ordered the AMIP to speak so that he could be evaluated. The appellate court found that because of significant due process protections already in place, as well as the practical need of the judge and examiners to determine mental illness, there was no absolute right to remain silent. In Whitman, the AMIP refused to speak to the doctors, the investigator, the examiners or the judge. The examiners and the judge all stated on the record their inference of mental illness from the fact that the AMIP was “nearly mute.” The Whitman court reversed the commitment, noting that while there was no right of the accused to remain silent, there was also no right of the state to infer mental illness from such silence, in the absence of sufficient additional evidence in the record.

4. Jury Trial

There is no right to a trial by jury in civil commitment proceedings. In light of the procedural requirements imposed under the statutes governing involuntary commitment proceedings, a jury trial is not a necessary element of fundamental fairness guaranteed by the Due Process Clause. Matter of Mills, 36 Or App 727 (1978); Addington v. Texas, 441 US 418 (1979).

5. Right to Counsel

The Fourteenth Amendment guarantee of due process entitles an allegedly mentally ill person to representation by counsel at a commitment proceeding. State v. Collman, 9 Or App 476 (1972). Additionally, the AMIP or a person acting on his behalf must be fully advised of his right to counsel and such right must be guaranteed unless intelligently and understandingly waived. Id.

The trial court is not, however, required to advise the AMIP of his right to retain private counsel, particularly where the court has already appointed counsel after determining that the AMIP lacks resources to hire a private attorney. State v. Cach, 172 Or App 745 (2001). The trial court is also not required to tell the AMIP that he has a right to represent himself. Id.

6. Vagueness

The Oregon Court of Appeals has also held that assuming the civil commitment statutes are strictly construed, they are neither void for vagueness nor an invasion of privacy. State v. O’Neill, 274 Or 59 (1976).

7. Pre-hearing Detention

When a person is being held involuntarily, a hearing must be held within five judicial days of being taken into custody, not counting the first day. Technically, the hearing must be held within five days of the citation or warrant of detention, which is required in order to take a person into custody under the 426.070 scheme. Thus it is the same as five days from the time they’re taken into custody. 426.095(2)(a)(b). If the hearing is pursuant to a physician emergency admission, ORS 426.232(2) states, “Under no circumstances may the person be held for longer than five judicial days.” The various other holds all require that a decision be made under 426.232, thereby falling under the subsection (2) deadline.

Sometimes a hospital places a “double hold” on an AMIP. In this situation, the paperwork for a second five-day emergency admission is submitted in the waning moments of the fifth judicial day. A hospital may do so for various reasons. The language of 426.232 is tied to the initiation of custody, not the submission of paperwork.

Occasionally, however, as a person is being released her actions create a new basis for an emergency admission. If there is a period of freedom between the end of the first hold and the initiation of the second, the statutory requirements may be satisfied.

If a new basis for a hold arises prior to the time that the first hold is dropped but after the deadline for the submission of the investigator’s report, the hospital must release the person. The appellate court has been unusually clear on the five-day limit. In State v. Bradsteen, 196 Or App 634 (2004), the appellate court reversed because the hearing was held outside the five days. The appellate court was blunt in its ruling: when a person is held for more than five judicial days without a hearing, the trial court must dismiss the case. In State v. Dezort, 208 Or App 751 (2006), the court accepted the Attorney General’s concession but still wrote a short opinion to point out that the phrase “under no circumstances may the person be held for longer than five judicial days” (without a hearing) doesn’t leave much room for a second hold, no matter how good the hospital’s reason.

A writ of habeas corpus may be required to get a person into court so a dismissal can be requested.

B. The Role of the Defense Attorney

There is an ongoing legal debate about the extent to which defense attorneys should be adversarial in civil commitment hearings. For example, if it is clear to the attorney that hospitalization would be in the client’s best interest, when is it the attorney’s duty to join the state in arguing for hospitalization? The clear answer to this question, at least in Oregon, is never. There is never an occasion in which the defense attorney should argue for a personal assessment of the client’s “best interest” if it contradicts the expressed wishes of the client. There are, of course, occasions on which the client will express a desire to acquiesce to the commitment, and in these situations it is perfectly appropriate to join the state in arguing for hospitalization.

The debate reminds me of the “ethical” question criminal defense attorneys are often asked: how do you represent a client you know is guilty? Any experienced criminal defense attorney will say: “without difficulty.” The difficult cases are the ones in which the client is innocent. The guilty clients are easy because the job of the defense attorney is to hold the state to its burden. If the state has insufficient evidence to prove a person guilty beyond a reasonable doubt, it shouldn’t get a criminal conviction, nor the correlative costs in liberty and resources.

A similar situation arises in civil commitment hearings. There is often a paternalistic instinct on the part of examiners and judges to get the person “help” through commitment. Moreover, because the case has come to a hearing, the investigator is arguing for deprivation of liberty and, almost surely, so is the doctor in the hospital where the client is being held. The AMIP’s family almost invariably sides with the investigator and the doctor because, to the family, commitment usually seems like a mechanism for gaining additional resources in a system where resources are absurdly scarce. Sometimes the chorus for commitment seems overwhelming. If the defense attorney does not argue for liberty, nobody will. It is the duty of the defense attorney to make sure that the paternalistic instincts of those with power do not overshadow the demands of the Constitution and the written laws. If the state cannot establish the level of dangerousness or severity of mental illness required by the law, it should not be able to use that law to deprive a person of their freedom.

There is little difference between choosing the goal in a criminal case and choosing the goal in a civil commitment case: 1) the client decides whether to go to trial or settle, and 2) until the client decides, or if the client is unable to decide, the presumption should be to fight against deprivation of liberty. In neither criminal nor civil commitment cases should the attorney’s vision of what is in the best interest of the client be substituted for the aforementioned presumption. The only difference between the two is that in civil commitment cases the state intends to revoke the client’s liberty via a hospital rather than a jail. This, however, is of little import since it is no less a loss of liberty.

First, civil commitment entails a massive loss of liberty, which includes both loss of freedom as well as adverse social consequences to the individual. See, e.g., Vitek v. Jones, 445 US 480, (1980).

Second, it should not be assumed that a hospital psychiatric ward is more pleasant or therapeutic than jail. Forced medications often have unpleasant side effects, psychiatric wards tend to be loud and chaotic, and the research is ambiguous regarding the benefits of hospitalization for all but the truly dangerous. Psychological Evaluations for the Court, Melton, et al., p. 320 (1997, updated 2005). To the extent that such benefits are a result of medication and confinement, jails may be equally beneficial.

Moreover, criminal defense attorneys often decline the opportunity to act paternalistically when the client is a drug addict who, if released, will immediately return to using dangerous substances. Even where a judge decided to hold a person in jail for their safety, any defense attorney would consider it their duty to zealously strive for release when requested by the client.

An adversarial defense attorney is the linchpin to a fair and just civil commitment hearing. The leading handbook on mental health law points out that the meaningfulness of a civil commitment hearing “is likely to turn largely on whether the attorney assumes an adversarial stance; indeed, the research evidence is clear that civil commitment procedures and standards will be just so many words unless attorneys behave adversarially.” Melton, et al, Psychological Evaluations for the Court.

The importance of an adversarial stance is supported by Oregon’s performance standards for representation in civil commitment proceedings. Standard 4.2.1 states:

It is counsel’s duty to ascertain the client’s ultimate goals and objectives in the proceedings.

a.It is the unusual circumstance where the client cannot express the ultimate goal, e.g., “I want out of this hospital/jail/custody situation.” Counsel should nearly always be able to ascertain if the client’s desire is liberty. If the client desires liberty, then counsel should work to achieve liberty.

b. Counsel should assume the role of advocate for the client’s rights and not attempt to take on an additional role. Counsel is not the judge, the consensus of the community, the client’s family member, the prosecutor or the mental health provider.

c.In those rare instances when a client, because of mental status, cannot express his or her ultimate goals and objectives in the commitment proceedings, it is the role of counsel to protect the client’s constitutional and statutory rights.

C. Prepping the Client for Hearing

The most important thing defense counsel can do in a commitment case is thoroughly prepare the client for the hearing. The performance of the client in the hearing is the single most important factor in a civil commitment proceeding. Because the medications can work quickly, and because the person must meet the criteria on the day of the hearing in order to be committed, an improvement in the day or two before the hearing can negate dangerousness and defeat the commitment. Recent improvement combined with an appropriate disposition and coherent answers will often outweigh otherwise convincing pre-hospitalization evidence that now seems outdated.

The difference between lying and coaching of emotions and appearance is not a subtle one. A clinician wears a suit to court instead of a t-shirt and jeans because the clinician wants to be thought of in a professional way. When a clinician testifies, he or she will use more professional words and have a different demeanor than when hanging out with friends. Dress, style, manner of speech, emotion, body language and appropriate use of jargon will all make a huge difference in judging credibility. People in commitment hearings come into court at a huge disadvantage: not only is their credibility being judged but also the ethereal quality of “having it together.” For this judgment, everything counts.

The attorney must advise the person about what the rest of the court already knows. So, for example, when the examiner asks what his or her plan is, the examiner is looking for something specific, generally for the AMIP to indicate a place to stay and a way to get food and water and some indication that those things are necessary. If the person hears the word “plan” and begins talking about his aspirations for the future, or how he’d like to smoke a cigarette, this could spell disaster. After all, when asked “What’s your plan for when you get out of the hospital?” what normal person would start talking about eating and sleeping? It’s counterintuitive. But if he starts talking about how he wants to be an architect someday, it could be considered delusional ideation, evidence of disorganization and psychosis, perhaps grandiose thinking. If the AMIP says she wants a cigarette, the examiners may conclude that she is incapable of thinking about her basic needs.

The idea of “coaching” may cause concern to new clinicians because the examiners are doing a mini evaluation during the court hearing, and the defense attorney is affecting the evaluation in a way that is, clinically speaking, ill-advised. But no self-respecting clinician would set up an evaluation like a trial—on the record, in front of witnesses and a judge, interrupted by arguments over evidence and other objections, etc. There’s no way a clinician could get an accurate feel for someone during a trial. Even the law recognizes the essential ineffectiveness of an in-court evaluation—by requiring the examination process to begin prior to the hearing and for examiners to receive the investigator’s report 24 hours before the hearing so they can talk to medical staff and witnesses. In some counties, where costcutting is a priority, that does not happen and the “evaluation” happens during the trial. But, because it is a trial, the defense attorney is obliged to make sure the witnesses understand the rules of the game.

 

For example, assume a client was suicidal when he came into the hospital a week before the hearing. Now he’s saying he’s not suicidal and wants out of the hospital. It is the attorney’s job to get him out of the hospital. So, in terms of coaching, the attorney should first explain how suicidality plays out in a commitment hearing. The court is looking for a lack of current intent, but also for a lack of repetitive thoughts and to make sure the client is not considering a plan or method for suicide. This is the thoughts/plan/intent hierarchy. The attorney should also coach the client as to the need for social support. The examiners will want to know that if the person starts feeling bad again he will call someone rather than fall deeper into despair. This is what is meant by “plan” in a suicidality case. If there are medications, the examiners want to know that the person is going to take them.

But these are all words. Emotions triumph over reason in both commitment and criminal court every time. So the client needs to be able to strike the right tone. That is, the examiners and judge are looking for a particular tone. The correct tone in a suicidal case is not happiness but rather some regret for past actions and an acknowledgement that life is worth living. The client shouldn’t lie. If the client really can’t think of any reason life is worth living then he shouldn’t talk about it. If the client doesn’t have anybody to call, then he shouldn’t make up a name. But, by the same token, it is malpractice to allow the client to simply be surprised by such questions during the hearing.

The structure of civil commitments does not, overall, make for a clinically accurate picture of the allegedly mentally ill person. Commitment hearings are, like trials, about whether the state can prove their case by clear and convincing evidence. Therefore, appearances and underlying emotions are critical.

Clients should be prepared in the following areas:

1. General incoherence and variability.

If a client is truly incoherent, the level of disorganization is probably too great for preparation to be of much benefit. However, although the client is delusional or tangential, frequently he or she is fully capable of understanding that it is best to minimize the outward display of certain types of behavior or discussion of certain topics at the hearing. More importantly, it is impossible to know prior to the hearing which client will be capable of following your advice and which one does not understand you at all. I have had many clients who, in the pre-hearing meeting, looked like they did not hear a word I said and then followed my advice perfectly during the hearing.

2. Idiosyncratic and unhelpful quirks like excess verbosity.

A number of people are able to answer questions well but seamlessly segue into increasingly tangential and delusional topics after answering the question. Sometimes such folks sincerely don’t understand the line where a topic has ended and another begun. One approach with such clients is to tell them that the additional information is very helpful to the attorney-client relationship, but that the psychologists in the courtroom will want short, simple answers. It is important to practice the idea a couple of times until the client is able to follow the advice. In case the client forgets or misunderstands where the line is during the hearing, you should set up a back-up plan. For example, if answers are getting too long, you can give the client’s forearm a comforting squeeze. Again, practice this a couple times. Make sure the client understands that you are not saying anything bad about the client’s style of speaking or way of being, just that you have been through many civil commitment hearings and know from experience what the psychologists and the judge want to hear. If you adequately practice, you likely will not have to squeeze the client’s arm. The client will give short, succinct answers to the questions and, by the end, release will be the judge’s only option. On the other hand, the client will likely be committed if too much tangential rambling finds its way into the courtroom.

3. Attitude toward the crucial issue.

It is vital for the attorney to identify the underlying emotion which is said to be causing the need for commitment. The client should be repeatedly advised and shown how to model the countervailing emotion.

For example, if the issue is one of suicidality caused by depression, it is important for the client to say that they no longer want to hurt themselves. But it is equally important for the client to show some passion or desire for life. Note that the countervailing emotion to suicidal depression is not happiness. Happiness or elation ends up looking like psychosis or mania when juxtaposed directly with depression. Drastic changes correlate to suicidal risk. Most importantly, happiness is an insufficiently serious emotion. If the AMIP tried to commit suicide because she decided that life was hopeless or overwhelming or that there was nothing to live for and, one week later, is saying that “life is great” and seems happy or carefree, it would, at the very least, indicate a lack of insight and perhaps a more serious undiagnosed psychosis. If, however, what she says is that the hospital has given her a chance to think about what suicide really means and she no longer wants to kill herself (for whatever specific reasons are meaningful for her), the change of heart seems more realistic, especially if the person has been taking medications in the hospital. The underlying emotion here is one of passion, yearning, desire to live.

If an allegedly mentally ill person is accused of being angry and violent, it is crucial that he not be easily provoked. He should think of the examiners’ questions as a function of their role. The examiners know nothing about the person except what they have very recently learned, so the AMIP should be tolerant of their asking questions that seem annoying or provocative. “Your job,” you might tell such clients, “is to answer the questions as if a child you loved were asking them. Be patient, answer short and simple and don’t get angry.” If witnesses are testifying, their job is to sit quietly and listen. You might tell people directly that their ability to follow this emotion related advice will be the primary factor for determining whether or not they return to the hospital.

If the person is accused of unintentionally doing something dangerous to herself, ideally her emotion would be regret. Perhaps she was walking around in traffic or running naked through downtown screaming about the coming apocalypse. Perhaps she even has a reason for everything, like the fact that her nudity symbolized the naked truth of her warnings. Here, the examiners and the court will be looking for increased insight. Thus, the client must show regret for whatever actions brought her to the hospital. Her words should reflect the emotion: “Yeah, that was really dumb. I won’t be doing that again.” Additionally, the delusions should be contained, though they need not be excised entirely from the hearing. So the client may say, “Yeah, I definitely believe the end is coming soon and that people should be warned, but running around naked was really dumb. I won’t be doing that again.” Client should also be trained not to go on for too long about the delusion, even when directly asked, and should not work it into the answer to every question (e.g., Q: “Where do you live?” A: “On Terwilliger, in a house built before the evil Lord Tarmac took over the earth and sought to create minions of all the...”).

4. The thirty-minute rule.

The risk of commitment skyrockets when the hearing lasts longer than thirty to forty-five minutes. This is primarily because some people can follow the aforementioned advice or “hold it together” for a short period of time, but only for a short period of time. Some can only last twenty minutes, some can last a couple hours. But the average is a little over thirty minutes. Every extra question, topic or witness should be carefully considered based on the risk that it will take the client past the point of collapse.

D. Examiners

1. Examiners Are Experts

The examiner is a court expert. Thus the examiner may rely on the client interview, a review of medical records or statements or anything else which he or she believes is appropriate. Moreover, examiners are allowed access to medical records, physicians, nurses or social workers in their preparation. None of this information is privileged for the purposes of the examination. ORS 426.120(3).

One examiner must be automatically appointed by the court. ORS 426.110(1). A second examiner must be appointed if the defendant requests one in writing. ORS 426.110(1). The state is not authorized to request a second examiner.

2. Examiners’ Duties

ORS 426.120(1) requires that: Persons appointed under ORS 426.110 to conduct the examination shall do the following:

(a) Examine the person as to mental condition;

(b) Initiate the examination process prior to the hearing. Any failure to comply with this paragraph shall not, in itself, constitute sufficient grounds to challenge the examination conducted by an examiner;

(c) Make their separate reports in writing, under oath, to the court; and

(d) Upon completion of the hearing, file the reports with the clerk of the court.

The examiners’ reports must:

  • Be in writing and under oath. ORS 426.120(1)(c).
  • Contain a reasoned opinion as to whether the person has a mental disorder. ORS 426.120(2)(a).
  • Contain a reasoned opinion as to whether the person meets the criteria for commitment. Id.
  • Contain an opinion as to whether the person would cooperate and benefit from voluntary treatment. Id.
  • Contain a recommendation for the appropriate treatment facility. ORS 426.120(2)(b).

In addition to the explicit requirements of ORS 426.120, the statute also requires that the examiners’ “reports shall contain the information required by the Department of Human Services by rule.” ORS 426.120(c). OAR 309-033-0960 is such a rule and it creates a lot of requirements for the examiners:

OAR 309-033-0960

Mental Health Examiner’s Report to the Court

(1) Examiner assessment of evidence. The examiner shall provide in a report to the court the examiner’s opinion whether the evidence supports or contradicts:

(a) The allegation that the person has a mental disorder;

(b) The allegation that the person is a danger to self or others, or is unable to provide for basic personal needs and is not receiving such care as is necessary for health and safety; and

(c) That the person would cooperate with and benefit from voluntary treatment.

(2) Mental status examination and psychosocial history. In addition to considering other evidence presented at the hearing, the examiner shall conduct a mental status examination and a psychosocial history to determine whether the person alleged to be mentally ill has a mental disorder.

(a) Mental status examination. A mental status examination shall include review of the presence of indicators of mental disorder in the following areas:

(A) Appearance. Features of the person’s dress, physical condition which may indicate the presence of a mental disorder.

(B) Behavior. Features of the person’s behavior, movement or rate of speech which may indicate the presence of mental disorder.

(C) Thought content. Features of the content of the person’s speech such as delusions and hallucinations which may indicate the presence of a mental disorder.

(D) Thought process. Features of the person’s expressed thoughts which may indicate that the person is unable to think in a clear logical fashion and which may indicate the presence of a mental disorder.

(E) Insight. Features of the person’s understanding of his/her current mental state which may indicate the presence of a mental disorder.

(F) Judgment. Features of the person’s judgment about social situations and dangerous situations which may indicate the presence of a mental disorder.

(G) Cognitive testing. Features of the person’s ability to concentrate, ability to remember recent and historical events, ability to use abstract thinking, and ability to use or remember generally known information which may indicate the presence of a mental disorder.

(H) Emotions. Features of the person’s emotions, such as being inappropriate to the situation, which may indicate the presence of a mental disorder.

(b) Psychosocial history. A psychosocial history shall consider the presence of indicators of mental disorder in the following areas:

(A) Psychiatric history.

(i) History of psychiatric or mental health treatment;

(ii) History of commitments for mental disorder including verification from the Division if available; and

(iii) Current participation in mental health treatment.

(B) Family history.

(i) Members of the person’s family who have a history of psychiatric or mental health treatment;

(ii) Members of the person’s family who have a history of commitment for mental disorder; or

(iii) Reports of family members who appear to have had an untreated mental disorder.

(C) History of alcohol or drug abuse.

(i) History of abusing alcohol or drugs;

(ii) Behaviors the person may have displayed during the course of the investigation which are substantially similar to behaviors that indicate the presence of a mental disorder that may be attributable to the use of alcohol or drugs; or

(iii) If the person appears to have a mental disorder, the effect of the person’s current use of alcohol or drugs on behaviors that may indicate the presence of a mental disorder.

(D) History of a loss of function.

(E) Social function.

(F) Personal finances.

(i) Availability of financial resources to provide for basic needs such as food and shelter;

(ii) Use of financial resources to meet needs for food and shelter; and

(iii) Other features of the manner in which the person uses money which would indicate the presence of a mental disorder.

(G) Medical issues.

(i) Medical conditions that may produce behaviors which are substantially similar to behaviors that indicate the presence of a mental disorder; or

(ii) Medical conditions which contribute to the seriousness of a mental disorder which appears to be present.

(3) Assessment of dangerousness and ability to provide basic needs. In addition to considering other evidence presented at the hearing, the examiner shall conduct an assessment of the danger the person represents to self or others and an assessment of the person’s ability to provide for basic personal needs.

(a) An assessment of dangerousness to self shall consider the following areas:

(A) History of thoughts, plans or attempts at suicide;

(B) Presence of thoughts, plans or attempts at suicide;

(C) Means and ability to carry out the plans for suicide;

(D) The potential lethality of the plan;

(E) The probable imminence of an attempt at suicide; and

(F) Available support systems which may prevent the person from acting on the plan.

(b) An assessment of dangerousness to others shall consider the following areas:

(A) History of thoughts, plans, attempts or acts of assaultiveness or violence;

(B) Presence of thoughts, plans, attempts or acts of assaultiveness or violence;

(C) Means and ability to carry out the plans for assaultiveness or violence;

(D) The potential lethality of the plan;

(E) The probable imminence of an attempt at assault or violence; and

(F) Available support systems which may prevent the person from attempting an assault or an act of violence.

(c) An assessment of the person’s ability to provide for basic personal needs shall consider the following areas:

(A) History of the person’s ability to provide for basic personal needs;

(B) The person’s current use of resources to obtain food, shelter, and health care necessary for health and safety;

(C) Behaviors which result in exposure to danger to self or other;

(D) Available support systems which may provide the person care necessary for health and safety; and

(E) If the person appears to lack capacity to care for self, the availability of a guardian who can assure the provision of such care.

3. Casting Doubt on the Examiners’ Diagnosis

First, look at the diagnosis and see if the elements are consistent with the symptoms stated in the examiner’s report. The diagnosis is frequently flat-out contradicted by the examiner’s own statement of the symptoms. For example, if the examiner notes catatonic symptoms but writes down a diagnosis of paranoid schizophrenia, the diagnosis is incorrect. The DSM-IV is clear that where there are catatonic symptoms, the appropriate subtype of schizophrenia is catatonic. This may initially seem like a hypertechnical difference, since in either case the AMIP has a mental disorder. It is not. A doctoral-level evaluator is presumed to be able to follow the most basic directions of his diagnostic manual. Otherwise, he is just making it up as he goes along. More importantly, different disorders should lead to different predictions of future behavior. Violence, for example, would be consistent with paranoid schizophrenia but inconsistent with catatonic schizophrenia.

Second, look to see if the elements of the diagnosis are all stated in the report. While occasionally the examiner has the wrong diagnosis, more often there is no supportable diagnosis at all. The examiner is required by statute to support his diagnosis in the report. Otherwise it is merely a “probable” mental disorder, which is insufficient for commitment. For example, suppose the examiner writes a diagnosis of schizophrenia but all he cites for support is “disorganized speech, angry presentation, nonresponsive answers, no plan for self-care.” The problem with this diagnosis is that schizophrenia has certain characteristic symptoms listed in Criterion A under diagnostic criteria for schizophrenia in the DSM-IV; there must be at least two of the following: delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior and negative symptoms. Our hypothetical examiner has only listed disorganized speech. Thus, a diagnosis of schizophrenia is unsupported. He also fails to mention anything about marked impairment of a major area of functioning, as required by Criterion B. Nor does he bother to rule out mood disorders, medical conditions or developmental disorders as required by Criteria D, E and F.

Third, consider whether the disturbance has been long-lived enough to meet diagnostic criteria. Going back to the previous hypothetical, a diagnosis of schizophrenia requires quite a lot of durational knowledge. The characteristic symptoms, of which there must be two, must be present for a significant portion of a one-month period. The impairment of a major area of functioning must have lasted for “a significant portion of the time since the onset of the disturbance,” and it must be more impaired than prior to the onset. This, of course, requires knowledge of when the onset occurred and what the person was like prior to onset, knowledge rarely available at a commitment hearing. Finally, Criterion C requires that “continuous signs of the disturbance persist for at least 6 months.” These “signs” do not need to be active-phase symptoms like those listed in Criterion A, but they must be symptoms of a disordered mind. This requires knowledge of a six-month period of the AMIP’s life, information which is, again, often unavailable at a commitment hearing.

Fourth, check to see if the listed symptoms and other support for the diagnosis have a basis in the investigator’s report or the hearing. At times, examiners may misrepresent words from the report to make it fit with their take on the disorder. More often, there will be some exaggeration that will not withstand challenge and may reveal insufficient support for the diagnosis.

Fifth, note whether the diagnosis is limited by a cautionary proviso such as “NOS” (not otherwise specified), “provisional” or “by history.” Each of these phrases means that the examiner probably does not have enough evidence before them to make a confident diagnosis. It is important to make the examiner state on the record what these phrases mean. They each have specific definitions in the DSM-IV, so make sure you have these definitions at hand during the cross-examination. “NOS” is a catchall diagnosis that is not inappropriate when a person’s symptoms fit the general scheme of a section of the DSM-IV but not the specific requirements of any particular disorder. However, civil commitment examiners and investigators often use the term because there is simply not enough time or information to make a proper diagnosis.

Sixth, consider how different the diagnosis might be if the examiner were working in her own practice rather than at a commitment hearing. For example, the examiner would likely take the following steps, and it is worth cross-examining on these points:

  • Conduct multiple meetings with a private client.
  • Talk with family and friends.
  • Administer psychological testing.
  • Ask questions for more than ten minutes.
  • Review medical records from past hospitalizations.
  • Review a basic medical workup and/or toxicity screen.
  • Review school records of a young patient.

The point here is twofold: 1) examiners often rely entirely on the investigator’s hearsay-riddled report at a commitment hearing when they would require substantially more confirmation in their own private practice, and 2) examiners are usually satisfied with an “examination” that involves a complete absence of testing and far fewer questions than would be considered appropriate in private practice.

Seventh, check the medications being prescribed in the hospital and consider whether there are any side effects that might account for some of the symptoms used to meet the elements of a diagnosis. The most common side effect of most psychotropic medications is sedation. If a person nods off during the hearing, drools or is slow to respond, this is likely caused by a sedating medication. Often, however, multiple medications are prescribed. Scant research has been done on the side effects of multiple medications taken together. I frequently see clients who are prescribed multiple antipsychotics, an anti-anxiety agent, an antidepressant and multiple anti-cholinergics. The type of drugs which have been prescribed may also point to other or different diagnoses. There is almost no research to indicate what effect this powerful mixture of mind-altering medications will have on a particular individual. The possible drug interactions rise exponentially with each added psychotropic medication. In addition to the type of medication being prescribed, you should also check the amount of each medication to see if the client is taking more than the normal therapeutic range. Drugs affect every person differently. One person’s therapeutic dose might cause substantial side effects with little additional benefit in another.

4. Challenging the Examiners’ Reports

ORS 426.120(b) requires the examiner to “initiate the examination process prior to the hearing.” However, the same section states that “[a]ny failure to comply with this paragraph shall not, in itself, constitute sufficient grounds to challenge the examination conducted by an examiner.” The words “in itself” imply that the failure to abide by the timeliness requirement might, in combination with some other inaction on the part of the examiner, be sufficient for exclusion, although it is an open question what other evidence, in combination, would provide such a basis. Therefore, it is a worthwhile objection to make.

For example, a failure of ORS 426.075(2) in conjunction with a tardy initiation might be sufficient. ORS 426.075(2) mandates that the court:

shall appoint examiners…sufficiently long before the hearing so that they may begin their preparation for the hearing. The records established by the Dept. of Human Services by rule and the investigation report shall be made available to the examiners at least 24 hours before the hearing in order that the examiners may review the medical record and have an opportunity to inquire of the medical personnel concerning the treatment of the allegedly mentally ill person relating to the detention period prior to the hearing.

ORS 426.075(2). The failure of any one of these requirements in combination with a failure to initiate the examination process prior to the hearing could form a basis for challenging the reports.

Counsel should also add to such a challenge any failure of the requirements of ORS 426.120 generally. For example, examiners often fail to include “a recommendation as to the type of treatment facility best calculated to help the person recover from mental illness” as required by ORS 426.120(2)(a).

Counsel should also include any failure of the requirements of OAR 309-033-0960 to complete a full mental status examination and psychosocial history.

The examiner may not submit his report until the end of the hearing: “Persons appointed under ORS 426.110 to conduct the examination shall do the following: … (d) Upon completion of the hearing, file the reports with the clerk of the court.” Often, the examiner will submit his report when it is deemed sufficient but before all possible evidence has been submitted. In that situation, it is worth objecting to the examiner’s report on that basis as well.

E. Cross Examination

In cross-examining an examiner, consider the following:

1. Who wasn’t interviewed—family, doctors, witnesses:

“What was the client’s baseline level of functioning prior to hospitalization?

—In forming an opinion of the allegedly mentally ill person’s current functioning, would it be helpful to know what he was like six months ago? Twelve months ago?

—In fact, some diagnoses like schizophrenia require that you know something about the person’s symptoms over the last six months.

—That’s information that a family member might know, correct?

—But you didn’t have a chance to speak with his ___” father/mother/sister/brother.

2. When were the allegedly mentally ill person and chart seen?

“What time of day did you see the client?

—Do you know what time he gets his medicine?

—What medications is he on?

—What are the side effects of this particular medicine?

—What level of acclimation would you say that the examinee had on this medication?

—Might your impressions of the examinee change if he had not yet taken his dosage at the time of the assessment?

—Are you able to prescribe psychiatric medications?

—Do you have medical training in psychopharmacology?

—Do you know the possible side effects of combining the medications the client is taking?

—Are psychologists recognized as experts in the study and practice of psychopharmacology?

—Would you consider yourself an expert in psychotropic medication?”

3. Chart questions:

“Did you review the chart?

—Can you describe the documents that you reviewed in the chart?

—Is it possible to provide confident diagnostic impressions if documents explaining alternative diagnosis and levels of functioning are not available?

—Is it possible that a previous evaluator who had undertaken a more comprehensive evaluation featuring psychological testing, collateral interviews, and multiple meetings with examinee would have arrived at different diagnostic findings?”

4. Diagnosis (in addition to the discussion above):

“I see in your report you have written down a diagnosis of _______.

—That’s the same diagnosis as was in the investigator’s report?

—You are relying, at least in part, on that diagnosis, correct?

—Are you aware of any diagnostic testing done by the investigator?

—In fact, it’s likely that the investigator’s diagnosis comes from the hospital?

—Are you aware of any diagnostic testing done by doctors at the hospital?

—Do you know which doctor made the diagnosis?

—Do you know that a doctor made the diagnosis?

—Is it possible that this is not a diagnosis independently made by this hospital but rather a diagnosis made at a previous time and found in the medical records?”

Note that the county mental health investigator is a county employee of the mental health division, not a physician. Nor, generally speaking, do they have a Ph.D. While they aren’t necessarily qualified to render an opinion of diagnosis, they can include a physician’s diagnosis in their report. Examiners may then adopt that opinion. State v. Brungard, 102 Or App 509 (1990). However, Brungard assumes that an actual doctor made a careful diagnosis at some point in the process.

5. Was there a mental status examination completed?

According to the OARs (309-033-0940(3)(a)), a mental status examination must include an assessment of:

  • Appearance
  • Behavior
  • Thought content
  • Thought process
  • Insight, judgment
  • Cognitive awareness
  • Emotions

6. Was there a psychosocial history completed?

According to the OARs (309-033-0940(3)(b)), a psychosocial history must include:

  • Treatment history
  • Commitment history
  • Current participation in treatment
  • Family history of treatment
  • Mental disorders or commitments
  • History of substance abuse
  • History of a loss of function
  • Social functioning
  • Personal finances
  • Medical issues

If the attorney has the benefit of any past psychological evaluations conducted by the evaluator, disparities in length and thoroughness may be apparent. Such disparities may raise questions regarding how complete the present psychosocial history is.

In cases where supportive documents are limited, an evaluator may be compelled or tempted to become overreliant on one data source. This situation creates ethical dilemmas about arriving at a diagnosis based on a single source of data. When this occurs, professionals are compelled to discuss the limits of their findings in the body of the report. It is unlikely that a psychologist will be able to give a confident diagnosis if supportive and collateral data are extremely limited.

Attorneys are encouraged to ascertain how long the evaluator actually spent with the client by asking the client questions and reviewing hospital/jail sign-in sheets. For a relatively verbal individual, it would be extremely difficult to conduct a thorough psychosocial evaluation in less than 90 minutes.

If salient data has not been memorialized in the report, note whether the examiner asked the requisite questions, or why not.

7. Challenging the examiner’sreport:

In Multnomah County, examiners never receive notice of a particular case until they arrive at the hearing. Sometimes it can be helpful to start the cross-examination by asking the examiner if she initiated the examination process before receiving the investigator’s report just prior to the hearing. An attorney might ask the following questions of the examiner to elicit this information:

“Have you spoken to any doctors or social workers or nurses about the client?

—Have you reviewed any medical records?

—Have you spoken to the client prior to today?

—Are you aware that Oregon law requires you to start the examination process prior to the hearing? When was the first time you heard the name of the client?

—Is it correct that you were not provided any documents or information of any kind about the client prior to arriving at the courthouse?

—Have you been the examiner at other commitments in this county?

—Have you ever been provided with reports or medical records the day before the hearing?”

8. Does the public nature of the examination affect the outcome?

  • The fact that it’s recorded?
  • The fact that there is a judge and a district attorney present and watching?
  • The fact that a legal outcome is on the line?
  • The fact that it is in the courthouse?
  • The fact that the allegedly mentally ill person was brought to court in handcuffs?

F. Evidence at the Hearing

1. Psychiatric Medications

ORS 426.075(1) requires that the court be notified of “all drugs and other treatment known to have been administered to the allegedly mentally ill person that may substantially affect the ability of the person to prepare for or function effectively at the hearing.” But once notified, what should a judge do with such knowledge? The examiners may be helpful, but they are not required to have any psychiatric knowledge. The judge must recognize that psychotropic medications may affect the allegedly mentally ill person’s behavior. For example, weight loss or unwillingness to eat may count against the AMIP in a basic needs inquiry; however, if the nausea or decreased appetite is a side effect of medication, then it is not evidence of mental illness. Other signs that can result from medication instead of illness are drowsiness and delayed responsiveness.

Some of the older antipsychotic medications cause long-term side effects that remain after medication is discontinued. Chief among these is tardive dyskinesia, an involuntary repetitive muscle movement that usually occurs with the tongue or jaw. There are other “extrapyramidal” long-term side effects, but they are rare enough that it is sufficient to simply be aware that they exist.

Psychiatry is an imprecise science. Because each person reacts so differently to any medication, under- or over-medication is extremely common. The difficulty of prediction is exponentially increased as additional medications are added. Doctors are often unable to achieve the correct balance of medication during the five- to seven-day period between when the AMIP is held and the hearing. In fact, it may take many months to discover the right combination and dosage of medications to treat the illness while minimizing side effects.

An important thing to remember is that the AMIP likely has past experiences with taking psychotropic medications. The AMIP may know more about which medications work best for him than a doctor who has only worked with the AMIP for a short period of time. Occasionally an AMIP refuses medication, saying he has taken it before and it was ineffective while causing significant side effects. The doctor may be reflexively treating the symptoms with medications which the AMIP has previously experienced. Also, side effects themselves vary widely according to the individual.

The sale of psychiatric drugs is booming. In 2004, there were over $14 billion worth of antidepressant sales. The most popular antipsychotic drug, Zyprexa, netted more than $4.4 billion that same year. There is an enormously powerful incentive to start the process from the wrong end, assuming the efficacy of certain drugs from a few studies and determining how the human brain works based on the brain’s interactions with the drugs. However,

Before there can be a neurochemical theory of mental pathology, there must be a neurochemical theory of mental health. Medical science has yet to explain the homeostatic mechanisms through which the brain regulates chemical and receptor processes. Without a fundamental understanding of how the brain controls these functions, it remains futile to discuss the chemical substrates of mental distress as the basis of treatments presumed to correct them. Maybe there is no evidence of a biochemical imbalance because there can be no evidence. Maybe human behavior cannot be defined or determined solely in physical terms, without relational and contextual considerations.

Duncan, Miller, Sparks, The Heroic Client(2004). More controversially, it is not at all clear that psychiatric medications are helpful to most people. The use of psychiatric medications is based on a chemical-imbalance theory of the brain. According to this theory, if one is depressed it is because the brain is producing insufficient amounts of serotonin. If one is psychotic, the brain is producing too much dopamine. Sending drugs into the brain that encourage the production of serotonin or block the production of dopamine essentially cures people of their symptoms. However, the precise mechanisms by which psychiatric drugs diminish symptoms and, therefore, the exact effects on the body are still being discovered. For example, we know that antipsychotics block the dopamine receptors, focusing on the D2 dopamine receptor, thereby increasing the amount of dopamine in the brain.

Older antipsychotics such as Haldol and Thorazine caused long-term side effects similar to those seen in Parkinson’s disease: facial tics, uncontrollable and repetitive body movements, a feeling that you wanted to crawl out of your skin. Interestingly, researchers have found that those people suffering from actual Parkinson’s disease also have very low levels of dopamine. They have concluded that a correlation exists between low levels of dopamine and Parkinsonian symptoms. The newer generation of antipsychotic medications, such as Clozaril and Zyprexa, appear to have diminished long-term Parkinsonian side effects. This effect is somewhat surprising, however, as the newer antipsychotics also block dopamine reception. These newer antipsychotics are weaker dopamine blockers but have a more complex pharmacology. In particular, they block serotonin in addition to dopamine. An additional property of the newer generation is that they dissociate faster from the D2 dopamine receptor. They get in and block dopamine and get out before the process which causes Parkinsonian side effects has a chance to occur.

On the other end of the dopamine spectrum are illicit drugs like methamphetamine, cocaine and ecstasy. These drugs create addictive pleasure by stimulating the dopamine system and flooding the brain with dopamine. High levels of amphetamine can induce psychosis.

Interestingly, no study has concluded that untreated schizophrenics suffer from too much dopamine. That is, there is only circumstantial evidence that dopamine is the cause of any of the symptoms of schizophrenia. Nonetheless, reducing levels of dopamine does appear to be effective in reducing some of the symptoms of schizophrenia in a large percentage of schizophrenics. There are several problems with this treatment. First, the precise mechanism of action remains a mystery. We are tinkering with the brain’s neurotransmitters with only the vaguest sense of causes and effects. There is some evidence that when we weaken the dopamine system by blocking the receptors, the ever-adaptive human body will adapt, creating additional, more sensitive receptors. The brain may become an overproducer of dopamine to compensate for what it perceives to be an assault on its dopamine system.

One clue that antipsychotics mask some of the symptoms of schizophrenia rather than treat the illness itself is that none of the negative symptoms of schizophrenia are affected by the drugs. Hallucinations, delusions and some types of disorganization can be significantly abated, depending on the person and the drug. However, the drugs do not alter the lack of pleasure, poverty of speech, flat affect and other negative symptoms that are the clearest markers of schizophrenia.

2. Stipulations

Some counties commonly allow an allegedly mentally ill person to stipulate to the elements of mental illness if they want to go back to the hospital. This is simply not appropriate in the vast majority of cases. If the person desires hospitalization, then a stipulation to the admission of evidence may be appropriate. A stipulated summary of expected evidence may also be used. And, where necessary, the client may also tell the judge why he or she is dangerous on that day. For example, a suicidal person may tell the judge that he still feels like killing himself.

These stipulations, however, are very different than stipulating to the ultimate issues of fact. First, stipulating to the evidence rather than the judgment will preserve the right to appeal. This also places the burden of the ultimate decision regarding commitment on the court rather than the AMIP, who may regret the decision in subsequent weeks and months. Finally, it is rarely clear that an AMIP is sufficiently competent to enter into and understand the ramifications of the contract that is a stipulation. A judge should review the consequences of the decision carefully with an AMIP who wishes to stipulate to anything.

In any case, a client should never stipulate to the dispositional phase of the hearing. Judges should be forced to confront the thorny issue of voluntary treatment. If the person is willing to go back to the hospital and there is a bed available, then the person is willing, able and likely to participate voluntarily in treatment. If the issue is that resources are available for hospital treatment on an involuntary commitment but not on a voluntary commitment, then that evidence should be presented at the hearing. There may also be a legal issue that should be raised. The statutory goals are clear in their preference for the least restrictive treatment. If more restrictive treatment is imposed due to bureaucratic distinctions, then there is a violation of the spirit, if not the letter, of ORS chapter 426.

ORS 426.130 imposes some obligations on the judge to review all the evidence as well as the examiners’ reports and make a series of decisions regarding judgment and disposition. There is no statutory exception to these obligations where there is a stipulation. However, in State v. Waters, 165 Or App 645 (2000), the court said that it is “reasonably in dispute” whether a trial court can take a stipulation without consulting with the examiners. In that case, the examiners had written reports but the reports were unsworn.

If there is some doubt as to the AMIP’s competency to stipulate, an alternative method is to engage a discussion with the AMIP wherein he or she states a preference for going back to the hospital and the facts which lead to a belief of danger or inability to care for self. The examiners and judge can then make the actual decision. This method generally requires only a minute or two more than a stipulation and it does not assume the competency of the mentally ill person.

3. Civil Procedure

Civil commitment is a civil action—O’Neill, 274 Or 59 (1976); Waters, 165 Or App 645 (2000)—regulated by the Oregon Rules of Civil Procedure (ORCP), the Oregon Rules of Evidence (ORE) as they apply to civil cases, and the Uniform Trial Court Rules (UTCR). Some distinctions are made part of the statutory scheme. However, where ORS Chapter 426 does not specifically distinguish itself, the ORCP, the Oregon Rules of Evidence and the Uniform Trial Court Rules should be applied as strictly as in any other civil proceeding. This includes, for example, identification of parties, service of pleadings and identification and processing of judgments.

One issue that may arise is whether or not the court may prohibit oral arguments. For example, this author has been told that some judges have barred opening arguments. However, ORCP 58A requires the court to proceed in the same manner as a jury trial, allowing opening and closing statements “unless the court, for good cause stated in the record, otherwise directs.” A court is clearly allowed to deny argument in a particular case based on particularized good cause. Whether the Constitution and the ORCP allow a court to deny oral arguments in a single case, however, is a wholly different question than whether a judge may presumptively deny all parties in a civil arena the right of allocution. The ORCP would seem to indicate the illegality of such a rule. And, while there is no case on point, it is hard to believe that the Constitution would allow for such a rule when the U.S. Supreme Court has repeatedly emphasized the massive curtailment of liberty involved in a commitment and the correlative due process which is required.

4. Confidential Nature of Proceedings

During the hearing, the courtroom may be open and must be on the record. ORS 426.160. A courtroom includes any place in which a judge calls a court to order, including the hospital bedroom of a patient.

The account of the proceedings and transcripts of testimony must be delivered to the court clerk who must seal the record. The sealed record may only be disclosed upon request of the AMIP or her legal representative, or for the court to inform the two persons in a two-party petition, if they requested it, of the disposition of the hearing and whether a firearm restriction was imposed. Additionally, the entire record is given to the treating facility if the person is committed.

5. Privilege

Rules of privilege for psychotherapists, physicians, nurses and social workers do not apply during any stage of commitment or recommitment proceedings.

The investigator is “allowed access to physicians, nurses or social workers and to medical records compiled during the current involuntary prehearing period of detention to determine probable cause and to develop alternatives to commitment. If commitment is proposed because the person appears to be a mentally ill person…the investigator shall be allowed access to medical records necessary to verify the existence of criteria.… The investigator shall include pertinent parts of the medical record in the investigation report. Records and communications described in this paragraph and communications related thereto are not privileged under ORS 40.230 [for psychotherapists], 40.235 [for physicians], 40.240 [for nurses] or 40.250 [for social workers].” ORS 426.074(2)(c).

The examiners are also “allowed access to physicians, nurses or social workers and to medical records compiled during the current involuntary prehearing period of detention and the investigation report. Records and communications described in this subsection and communications related thereto are not privileged under [the Oregon Evidence Code].” ORS 426.120(3).

At a civil commitment hearing, “The [Oregon Evidence Code rules of privilege for psychotherapists, physicians, nurses and social workers] shall not apply to and the court may consider as evidence any of the following:

(a) Medical records for the current involuntary prehearing period of detention.

(b) Statements attributed by the maker of the medical records or the investigation report to witnesses concerning their own observations in the absence of objection or if such persons are produced as witnesses at the hearing available for cross-examination.

(c) The testimony of any treating physicians, nurses or social workers for the preheating period of detention. Any treating physician, nurse or social worker who is subpoenaed as a witness for the proceeding shall testify as an expert under the provisions of ORS 410, 40.415, 40.420 and 40.425 and is subject to treatment as an expert witness in the payment of witness fees and costs.

(d) The investigation report prepared under ORS 426.074.”

ORS 426.095(4). Finally, at recommitment hearings, “[The Oregon Evidence Code rules of privilege for psychotherapists, physicians, nurses and social workers] do not apply to the use of medical records from the current period of commitment or to testimony related to such records or period of commitment in connection with hearings under this section. The court may consider as evidence such reports and testimony.” ORS 426.307(5).

ORS 426.120 appears to conceive of a separate investigation by the examiners in which charts would be examined, conversations had with medical staff, and the AMIP examined prior to the hearing. As a result, the examiners have access to medical records and information that would otherwise be privileged. Of course, as is true of any expert, the examiners are not limited by any rule of evidence. However, in any other civil or criminal trial the expert would form an opinion prior to trial. Two oddities result from the fact that the examiners in a civil commitment are reviewing the evidence as the hearing proceeds: 1) exhibits and other evidence may be permitted for use by the examiners but not the court, and 2) the judge may take into account an examiner’s opinion but must be vigilant that the judicial opinion is based solely on permissible evidence. The judge must exclude from the findings and conclusions all inadmissible evidence.

6. Prior Commitments

The court may not take judicial notice of files from prior commitment hearings. State v. O’Neill, 274 Or 59 (1976). However, the best evidence rule does not require prior commitment orders. State v. Brungard, 101 Or App 67 (1990). Testimony as to prior commitments may be elicited from the AMIP or other witnesses with knowledge. Questioning the AMIP regarding prior commitments is relevant to determine the nature and extent of the mental disorder and to predict future conduct.

7. Notice of Treatment

ORS 426.075(1) requires that the court be “fully advised of all drugs and other treatment known to have been administered to the allegedly mentally ill person that may substantially affect the ability of the person to prepare for or function effectively at the hearing.”

The issue that occasionally arises is whether a notation from the hospital that the AMIP refused medications is allowed under this section. Defense counsel should argue that by its plain language, the statute only allows admission of “administered” drugs or treatment that “may substantially affect the ability of the person to prepare.” A refusal is neither drugs nor treatment. It is not administered, and it is not an action taken by the hospital that would affect the ability of the person to prepare. According to the creators of ORS 426.075(1):

This section is included as a result of the events leading up to the Collman case, which is representative of similar cases. Because the allegedly mentally ill person’s ability to defend himself may be affected by certain drugs and treatment prior to the hearing, the court should be fully advised of the facts and should take such facts into account in reaching a conclusion as to the mental condition of the person in question.

Commentary to SB 510, section 8, Senate Judiciary Committee. In Collman, referred to in the commentary,

Defendant was given three injections of a drug called Inapsine at the hospital crisis unit prior to the commitment hearing. The doctor in charge at the clinic diagnosed defendant as a paranoid schizophrenic and his condition was described as “agitated.” The last injection of Inapsine was at 2:30 a.m. Defendant contends the drug was administered without his consent and affected his ability to respond to questions at the hearing. When the commitment hearing started defendant was still physically obstreperous, causing the judge to conduct the hearing in a holding room of the courthouse to which defendant had been brought, rather than having the hearing in the courtroom. The two psychiatrists accompanied the judge. The evidence was that the court and one of the doctors informed defendant of his right to counsel, which advice defendant ignored. The only coherent thing witnesses remembered him saying was, “I want a doctor. I want help.”

State v. Collman, 9 Or App 476, 450 (1972). The absence of medication and the reason for the absence of medication is irrelevant to the considerations of this section. The judge and the examiners need to be aware of situations like those in Collmanin which a recently injected mind-altering medication might be driving the behavior of the AMIP. A notation of refusal of medication falls under neither the spirit nor the letter of ORS 426.075(1).

The state may argue that the information is part of the medical record and, therefore, admissible under ORS 426.095(4). However, the Notice of Treatment is not part of the medical records. It was prepared solely for the purpose of the hearing. The information it contains may or may not be contained in the medical chart. Without the author of the document present to cross-examine, it is impossible to know whether it was a true refusal or if, for example, the AMIP was asleep and could not be woken up.

For a further discussion of medication, consult the preceding section, “Psychiatric Medication.”

8. The Hospital History

Another exhibit that may cause some dispute is a printout of the prior hospitalizations of the AMIP, called the hospital history. Defense attorneys should argue that the hospital history is hearsay. The information may come in through the investigator if the investigator has knowledge. Also, case law allows the AMIP to be questioned directly about it. However, there is no evidentiary exception that would allow a printout of historical information to be admitted directly.

9. The Investigator’s Report

The non-hearsay portions of the investigator’s report may be admitted without foundation if the investigator is present at the hearing:

Subject to the following, the investigation report shall be introduced in evidence:

(A) Introduction of the report under this paragraph does not require the consent of the allegedly mentally ill person.

(B) Upon objection by any party to the action, the court shall exclude any part of the investigation report that may be excluded under the Oregon Evidence Code on grounds other than [that of medical privilege].

(C) Neither the investigation report nor any part thereof shall be introduced into evidence under this paragraph unless the investigator is present during the proceeding to be cross-examined or unless the presence of the investigator is waived by the allegedly mentally ill person.

ORS 426.095(4)(d). Defense counsel should object specifically to any hearsay statements which are not marked as non-hearsay. The investigators are not lawyers, nor are they trained in the intricacies of the hearsay rules. Often, hearsay sneaks into parts of the report unintentionally. The Court of Appeals will consider there to be no objection to this hearsay if the only objection is “no objection to the investigator’s report, absent hearsay.”

G. After a Finding of Mental Illness

1. Voluntary Treatment

Where a person is 1) willing and able to participate in voluntary treatment, and 2) will probably do so, the trial court is “required to dismiss” the proceeding under 426.130(1)(b). State v. M.C.M.212 Or App 158 (2007).

The appropriate standard for determining whether an allegedly mentally ill person is willing and able to seek voluntary treatment is preponderance of the evidence, as it is for every decision during the dispositional phase of the proceeding. State v. Brenhuber, 146 Or App 719 (1997).

The burden of proof lies with the AMIP to show that he or she is able and willing to engage in voluntary treatment. In State v. T.M.,229 Or App 325 (2009), the Court of Appeals sorted out previously incongruent case law by declaring that an AMIP has the burden to show that they are able to benefit from and participate in voluntary treatment by a preponderance of the evidence.

ORS 426.130(1)(b) follows a more circuitous path in the determination of voluntary treatment than such case law would seem to indicate. Sections (A)(i) and (ii) say that if the judge decides that the AMIP is mentally ill then a consideration of voluntary treatment must follow. If the judge finds that the AMIP is (1) “willing and able” to participate in treatment on a voluntary basis and (2) “will probably do so,” then the judge “shall order the release of the individual and dismiss the case.” This “shall,” however, is tempered by section (C) which says that the judge may still order commitment if a finding is made that voluntary treatment is “not in the best interest” of the mentally ill person.

The courts have never addressed a situation in which a judge found that while an allegedly mentally ill person was willing and able to participate and would probably do so, it was nevertheless not in their best interest to discharge them.

2. Term of Commitment

The judge may commit the person for any period of time up to 180 days. Thus, the judge may commit the AMIP for 30, 60 or 90 days for any reason whatsoever. There is no legal presumption favoring a 180-day period. On the other hand, most judges in the state do issue orders for a 180-day commitment, except in unusual circumstances.

On a 180-day commitment, a person may be held only for as long as the AMIP continues to meet the statutory criteria, but no more than 180 days. For example, the average stay on a Multnomah County commitment is approximately three weeks.

3. Conditional Release

After deciding that the person is actually mentally ill, the judge has the option of conditionally releasing the person to a friend, relative or guardian. The court may only release if the following findings are made:

a.Release is requested by a legal guardian, relative or friend for the period of commitment.

b.The place where care will be given is satisfactory to the court.

c.The court is satisfied that the requesting person has the ability to care for the AMIP.

d.The court is satisfied that adequate financial resources are available for care.

The court may then establish whatever conditions it feels are appropriate.

If the mentally ill person fails to abide by the conditions ordered at the hearing, he or she may be revoked from conditional release. The person is entitled to a new hearing at which the only factfinding issue is whether the conditions were violated. The judge then makes a dispositional decision whether to continue the conditional release if it is still available or to revoke release and commit the person to the custody of County Mental Health for the remainder of the commitment period. Whether or not the person is still mentally ill is not at issue at a conditional release revocation hearing. The person’s rights need not be read at the beginning of a revocation hearing.

Of course, if a new notice of mental illness or physician’s hold has been filed and all parties are aware that the issue of mental illness will be revisited, there is no reason why a standard commitment proceeding couldn’t be held at the time set for a conditional release revocation hearing.

4. Trial Visit

After a person has been committed to the custody of the Dept. of Human Services mental health division, the division has several options: 1) discharge the person at any time, 2) continue to keep the person in the hospital, or 3) set up a trial visit. In a trial visit, a contract is made with the mentally ill person in which the mental health division allows the person to be released to a suitable facility or home and the person agrees to abide by all the conditions set by the division. If all parties agree the person is released and a trial visit monitor is assigned.

If the mentally ill person fails to abide by the conditions in the contract, the division may request a revocation hearing. The scope of the hearing is limited to whether the person violated the contract and if so, what the disposition should be. The state need not prove that the person is still mentally ill. State v. Bryant, 127 Or App 68 (1994). Of course, if a new notice of mental illness or physician’s hold has been filed and all parties are aware that the issue of mental illness will be revisited, there is no reason why a standard commitment proceeding could not take place.

If the court finds that the conditions were violated it may keep the person on the visit, assuming it is still available, or revoke and return the person to inpatient care for the remainder of the commitment period. If the visit is continued the judge may modify or add to any of the conditions.

An advice of rights, pursuant to ORS 426.100, is not required. State v. Vonahlefeld, 140 Or App 248 (1996).

5. Outpatient Commitment

In an outpatient commitment, the mentally ill person is taken to an outpatient facility in lieu of being sent to the hospital. The judge does not make this decision. The judge commits the individual to the custody of the mental health division, which may immediately decide to send the committed person, with consent, to an outpatient facility. The rules of the outpatient commitment are put on the record at the hearing. Just as with trial visit revocations and conditional release revocations, if the person does not comply with the conditions of the outpatient commitment a hearing may be set in which a judge determines whether the mentally ill person failed to meet the conditions and, if so, whether the outpatient commitment should be continued or revoked. The state need not prove that the statutory definition of mental illness is still met at the time of a revocation hearing. State v. Bryant, 127 Or App 68 (1994).

To get into the Portland branch of the Oregon State Hospital, you must ring a doorbell and state your name and purpose before being buzzed in. Two sets of glass doors only open with the push of a button from a distant desk, and the first set must close before the second set grants you entrance. The lobby is helpfully peppered with pamphlets such as “Coping with Crisis,” “Empathic Guidelines,” “How to Help Someone who Resists Taking Medications” and “Helpful Hints for Dealing with Persons with Mental Illness” (in which such advice is dispensed as “Try to adopt an attitude of benign indifference”).

There are seven social workers who work at the state hospital—four in charge of admissions and three designated as discharge specialists. A particularly friendly and energetic discharge social worker named Clem explains the system after spending a morning frantically trying to get a housing situation secured for a patient who will be discharged the coming weekend.

She tells me that there is one patient at the hospital who has been there for over ten years. Apparently staff have moved him to different floors in attempts to motivate him towards a different living situation, but each move has only made him happier with the hospital environment. Clem describes that this patient is the exception, that the quickest discharge she has facilitated occurred after only two months, and that most clients stay at the hospital for at average of six months. At any given time, there are 92 patients at the hospital, and there is always a waiting list stocked with admissions at local hospitals.

Her morning mini crisis exemplified the greatest challenge of her job—finding living situations for the patients upon their discharge. When asked about family and friends, Clem states that some are fortunate enough to have this resource, but that most have “burned their bridges.” The majority of clients are discharged to secured or unlocked residential treatment facilities (RTFs). However, these facilities are few and far between, and have the right to use discretion in accepting residents (disqualifying clients with a recent history of aggression, any history of fire-setting, and those who aren’t so keen on complying with medication regimens). Also, a client at the state hospital can be placed on a waiting list for an RTF but lose their spot if in the intervening six months they happen to shove a fellow client in a scuffle. When asked what she would like attorneys to know, she recommends as much communication as possible with the staff who work with clients on a daily basis would be helpful as mental illness can foster unpredictable behavior, leading to a false perception of sickness or wellness just before a hearing. Recommitments

I. No Automatic Hearing

At an original commitment hearing, the court usually commits the person to the department for a period not to exceed 180 days. This period is somewhat misleading because at the end of 180 days the department may request a further 180-day period of commitment. The mentally ill person must be notified of such a request, but no review will take place unless the person objects to the recommitment. If the person does nothing or takes no position then no attorney will be appointed, no judge will review the recommitment for probable cause, and no hearing will be scheduled:

If the person does not protest a further period of commitment within 14 days of service of the certification, the department or facility shall so notify the court and the court shall, without further hearing, order the commitment of the person for an additional indefinite period of time up to 180 days.

ORS 426.301. The recommitment framework does not violate the Due Process Clause because, according to the appellate court, the person has already been adjudged mentally ill at the time of the original commitment hearing and a new hearing would be of little value in reducing the possibility of unnecessary commitments. At the same time, the state has a significant interest in providing care for mentally ill patients and protecting the community from dangerous people. Any additional procedural process would divert resources from that significant interest. State v. Johansen, 125 Or App 365 (1993).

II. Timing of the Hearing

If the person does protest, either orally or by signature, then an attorney is appointed and a hearing “shall be conducted as promptly as possible.” ORS 426.307(1). The motion for recommitment must be filed prior to the end of the original 180-day period of commitment, but the hearing need not take place within the original 180-day period. The circuit court does not lose jurisdiction over the case at the end of the 180th day. Matter of G., 26 Or App 197 (1976).

III. Legal Standard

The question for the court is whether the person “is still a mentally ill person and in need of further treatment.” ORS 426.307(6). The phrase “mentally ill person” has the same meaning as it does at an original commitment hearing. That is, the state must prove that the person has a mental disorder which is currently causing the person to be: (A) dangerous to self, (B) dangerous to others, (C) unable to provide for basic personal needs and is not receiving such care as is necessary for health or safety, or (D) the person is chronically mentally ill, has twice been hospitalized by the department in the previous three years, is exhibiting substantially similar symptoms to those that led to the previous hospitalizations and, unless treated, will continue to deteriorate to a point of dangerousness or inability to care for basic needs. ORS 426.005(e).

IV. Expanded Criteria

Defense counsel should be particularly wary of an argument under the expanded criteria for chronic mental illness. The point of ORS 426.005(e)(C) (aka expanded criteria) is not to create a repeat offender law but to treat people who are rapidly decompensating toward an expected and previously experienced negative outcome. The key component is the downward trend implied by the statutory phrase “will continue to deteriorate.” When a person has been hospitalized for 180 days, there is rarely such a trend. If there is such a deterioration, it can typically be attributed to some action by the hospital, such as a medication change. Counsel should argue in that situation that the phrase “unless treated” implies that the person has been deteriorating because of a lack of treatment, not as a direct result of treatment.

V. Further Treatment

The court must find, in addition to the criteria for “mentally ill person,” that the person is “in need of further treatment.” It is unclear what “in need of further treatment” means. Presumably, the legislature assumed that if the hospital personnel think the person should stay in the hospital, they also believe more treatment is necessary. Sometimes, however, a generally low-functioning person has achieved his “baseline,” having obtained the maximum benefit that treatment has to offer him. The hospital should be pressed to demonstrate what further treatment is needed. If the person is simply waiting for a bed, the hospital should be pressed to show why continued hospitalization is the least restrictive alternative.

VI. Burden of Proof

The burden of proof for the state at a recommitment hearing is clear and convincing evidence. State v. Linde, 179 Or App 553 (2002).

VII. Setovers

Requests for postponement and appointment of counsel are the same as for an original commitment. ORS 426.307(2)(3).

VIII. Court Examiners

Appointment of examiners is slightly different for recommitment hearings. The allegedly mentally ill person may request an examination by “a physician or other qualified person” who is independent from the hospital or facility in which the AMIP is placed. Such an independent examiner must be appointed if the person does not have funds to hire one privately. There is no provision for examiners at a recommitment hearing outside of the independent examiner.

IX. No Privilege for Current Commitment

At the hearing, no medical, therapist, social worker or other privileges apply “to the use of medical records from the current period of commitment or to testimony related to such records or period of commitment.” Also, “[t]he court may consider as evidence such reports and testimony.” ORS 426.307(5).

X. The Record from the Original Commitment Hearing

The records from the original commitment hearing are not automatically incorporated into the record of a recommitment hearing as evidence of continuing mental illness. State v. Hitt, 179 Or App 563 (2002).

The investigator’s report from the original hearing is not admissible unless the investigator is present at the recommitment hearing and available for cross-examination. State v. M.A.B.,212 Or App 400 (2007).

XI. Reading of the Rights

The specific rights listed in ORS 426.301 must be read to the AMIP at a recommitment hearing. If the rights are not read it is reversible error. State v. Montgomery, 147 Or App 69 (1997).

XII. Stigma

It is important to recognize that both stigmatization and a person’s liberty are at stake in a recommitment proceeding. In discussing why a recommitment appeal is not mooted by release from commitment, the Oregon Court of Appeals said: “the stigma that arises from commitment is a function not only of the fact of commitment but, also, of its duration. Bluntly, the longer someone is committed for a mental illness—six months versus one year; one year versus three years—the greater the attendant stigma. Like Chief Judge Schwab in Van Tassel, we decry the ignorance that generates such attitudes among many in our society. But they are undeniable.” State v. Linde, 179 Or App 553 (2002).

State v. Linde, 179 Or App 553 (2002)

No attorney should handle a recommitment proceeding without reading State v. Linde, 179 Or App 553 (2002). The Lindecase contains common recommitment facts. At the time of the further commitment hearing, appellant was 22 years old, had a history of substance abuse, and was diagnosed as having chronic paranoid schizophrenia. In his report, which accompanied the certificate and was submitted to the trial court, Axer [the examiner] described two “barriers to discharg[ing]” appellant:

“1. [Appellant’s] symptoms of schizophrenia include severe disorganization of thought process and persistent auditory hallucinations. He is irritable especially when his voices are louder and more disturbing. [Appellant] doesn’t acknowledge having psychotic symptoms. He believes that he was hospitalized just because he didn’t have any other place to stay. He doesn’t understand the need for his further pharmacological treatment.

“2. [Appellant] has a history of serious substance abuse problems. He had [a] few episodes of secretly inhaling glue at Hugo Hills until the staff realized what was going on. [Appellant] is unaware of the risks associated with street drugs. He also states a desire to drink beer when released from Hugo Hills[.]”

At the hearing, Axer and Dr. Wes Garwood, the treating psychiatrist at Hugo Hills, testified consistently with the concerns described in Axer’s report. More particularly, Axer and Garwood advanced three related reasons why appellant’s commitment should be continued. First, they reiterated their concerns about appellant’s interest in drinking beer and using illegal drugs—they specifically pointed to staff reports of a “few” glue-sniffing incidents—and expressed their belief that appellant did not appreciate the risks associated with such abuse. Axer testified: “He indicated to me that he would not take street drugs if he is discharged, but he would drink beer, and that he doesn’t really see the…any reason why medication and alcohol could be a poisonous mixture.”

Second, Axer and Garwood testified that appellant did not view himself as being mentally ill and that he had been “grudgingly” compliant in taking his medication. Third, and most globally, both doctors were concerned that, outside the structured environment of the hospital, appellant would be generally unable to appreciate and deal with the risks he would encounter because of mental illness. In that regard, Garwood testified that, although appellant hoped to be able to live on his parents’ property upon discharge, appellant’s parents had been “very clear” that “that is not a viable option.”

State v. Linde, 179 Or App 553 (2002). The Lindecourt ruled that this was insufficient:

[T]he “worst case scenario” the state posits is that appellant, upon discharge, would discontinue his medication, indulge in substance abuse, and perhaps ultimately end up on the streets.… the state has adduced no proof that appellant’s failure to take medication would be in any way life-threatening, much less in the near future.… the state’s concerns about appellant’s potential homelessness—even if that came to pass—are speculative.

* * *

[T]he alleged “danger to self” seems to pertain to appellant’s potential consumption of alcohol and drug abuse. Two concerns, one general and one more precise, seem to underlie the state’s argument. The more general concern is that alcohol and drug abuse can be harmful and that appellant does not appreciate the risks of such abuse. The difficulty with that argument is that the state has not established the requisite nexus between appellant’s mental illness and the potential substance abuse or failure to appreciate the risks of such abuse. That is, nothing in this record demonstrates that appellant will abuse drugs or alcohol because of his mental condition or that his appreciation of those risks (or, conversely, his choice to engage in such abuse) has been materially impaired because of his mental illness.

* * *

The second, and more precise, concern seems to be that, if appellant abuses drugs or alcohol while continuing to take his prescribed medications, the combination could be dangerous.The only support in the record for this asserted danger is Axer’s passing remark that “medication and alcohol could be a poisonous mixture.” That single, unamplified comment was insufficient to constitute “clear and convincing” evidence warranting continuation of appellant’s commitment on the ground that he was dangerous to himself.

Mental Retardation and Developmental Disabilities (MRDD)

I.Mentally Retarded Person

For the purpose of the commitment statutes, “unless the context requires otherwise, ‘mentally retarded person’ applies only to a person who, because of mental retardation, is or is alleged to be either:

(1) Dangerous to self or others; or

(2) Unable to provide for basic personal needs and not receiving care as is necessary for the health, safety or habilitation of the person.

ORS 427.215. According to the plain words of the statute, a person is a “mentally retarded person”—and therefore eligible for commitment—if the person is “alleged to be” dangerous. However, this aspect of the definition has to be unconstitutional. The U.S. Supreme Court set forth a minimum standard required in order to take away a person’s liberty for the protection of society rather than as punishment. O’Connor v. Donaldson, 422 US 563 (1975). The Court in Donaldsonruled that there is no constitutional basis for confining persons involuntarily if they are dangerous to no one and can live safely in freedom. If the state could simply allege that a person was dangerous and dispense with proving it, then the U.S. Constitution’s Due Process Clause would surely be offended.

ORS 427.215 is better read as limiting the definition of “mentally retarded person” to chapter 427 but requiring that a person actually be dangerous or unsafe to be committed.

Note: A person with mental retardation is simply a person who meets the definition of mental retardation set out below. But a “mentally retarded person” is one who is both mentally retarded and dangerous or unsafe as a result. “Mentally retarded person” is the final legal conclusion which includes, among other things, a finding that the allegedly mentally retarded person (AMRP) is a person with mental retardation. This is an incredibly confusing statutory scheme and counsel should make sure that all parties are clear on what the statute actually requires. It is really no different than the chapter 426 framework in which “mental disorder”—the psychiatric diagnosis—is one element of the final legal conclusion of “mental illness” (mental disorder + dangerousness or inability to survive + nexus). In chapter 427, the legislature made it even more confusing by using phrases which are even more similar to each other than mental disorder and mental illness. “Mental retardation,” a diagnosis, is one element of the final legal conclusion of “mentally retarded person” (mental retardation + dangerousness or inability to survive + nexus).

II. Mental Retardation

“Mental retardation” means “significantly subaverage general intellectual functioning existing concurrently with deficits in adaptive behavior and manifested during the developmental period. People of borderline intelligence may be considered mentally retarded if there is also serious impairment of adaptive behavior. Definitions and classifications shall be consistent with the Manual on Terminology and Classification in Mental Retardation of the American Association on Mental Deficiency, 1977 revision. Mental retardation is synonymous with mental deficiency.” ORS 427.005(11).

“Intellectual functioning” means “functioning as assessed by one or more of the individually administered general intelligence tests developed for the purpose.” ORS 427.005(10).

“Significantly subaverage” means “a score on a test of intellectual functioning that is two or more standard deviations below the mean for the test.” ORS 427.005(16).

“Adaptive behavior” means “the effectiveness or degree with which an individual meets the standards of personal independence and social responsibility expected for age and cultural group.” ORS 427.005(1).

AMRP—allegedly mentally retarded person, a statutory phrase for the “defendant” in an MRDD commitment proceeding. The acronym is used as shorthand.

These individuals reside in eight homes on a small campus. These homes are staffed 7 days a week, 24 hours per day. In addition to direct care staff, each home has a manager and other professional staff, including nurses, physicians, a psychologist, and occupational and physical therapists as well as dental, psychiatric and vocational and recreational services. Fairview Training Center, open since 1911, was at one time called the State Institution for the Feeble-Minded. Fairview closed in February of 2000. At the time, the Justice Department had a pending lawsuit against the center and the institution was losing money rapidly. The residents were moved into community placements. The statutes 427.330 to .345 were written in 1999 to provide funding and a framework for community housing. Proceeds from the sale of the Fairview Training Center have been directed toward community housing.

IV. Initiation of an MRDD Commitment

There is no mechanism for a doctor’s or magistrate’s hold for an AMRP. This makes for a particularly cumbersome situation when a defendant is found permanently unfit to proceed in a criminal case. The sole mechanism for initiating a chapter 427 civil commitment is a two-party hold. The two-party hold works similarly to chapter 426. The two parties file a sworn written petition with the probate court stating that “a person within the county is a mentally retarded person in need of commitment for residential care, treatment and training.” ORS 427.235(1). Note that the phrase “mentally retarded person” includes mental retardation, dangerousness or inability to care for basic needs, and the nexus between the two.

The circuit court forwards the petition to the community mental health and disabilities program in the county “if it finds the notice sufficient to show the need for investigation.” This implies that the circuit court conducts some sort of probable cause review. However, the statute sets forth neither a standard for the review nor a suggestion as to the person or persons who may conduct the review. ORS 427.235(1).

If the AMRP is in a state training center, the petition is forwarded to the training center to conduct an investigation. ORS 427.235(1).

V. Investigation

The investigation begins with an examination or interview of the AMRP. The examination/interview should take place in the person’s home or a familiar place. ORS 427.235(3). If warranted, the investigator should schedule a diagnostic evaluation (see below) and interview witnesses such as relatives, neighbors, teachers or the physician. Id.

The physician may provide relevant information, at the investigator’s request, only in response to a court order or the permission of the AMRP. ORS 427.235(6).

The investigation must include, in addition to a determination of whether the person is a “mentally retarded person,” a determination of whether there are alternatives to commitment and whether the person is incapacitated and in need of a guardianship or a conservatorship. ORS 427.235(3).

The investigation report must be submitted within 30 days of notice from the court. ORS 427.235(4).

Copies must be given to the AMRP “as soon as possible after completion but in any case prior to a hearing held under ORS 427.245.” ORS 427.235(4).

The timing of the investigation for an AMRP being held in custody is not specified in the statute. The only timing requirement for the investigation is the 30 days specified in ORS 427.235(4) and the requirement that the copies of the report must be served on the AMRP prior to the hearing. The hearing statute states that if a person is taken into custody the hearing must be held within seven judicial days. The grammar of the statute and the similarity of the chapter 427 framework to the chapter 426 framework suggest that the hearing must be held within seven judicial days of the person being taken into custody on the warrant of detention. ORS 427.245(2).

VI. Diagnostic Evaluation

Contents of the evaluation: “A diagnostic evaluation shall include, but not be limited to, the following:

(a) A social history;

(b) A psychological evaluation, including an appropriate individual test of intellectual capacity, an academic achievement test, a social development assessment and an adaptive behavior assessment;

(c) A medical evaluation including prenatal, natal, early postnatal and other past and family history, a complete physical examination including tests of visual function, and any specialized examinations necessary;

(d) A speech and hearing screening; and

(e) A dental screening.

(2) The diagnostic evaluation shall also attempt to determine the existence of related conditions such as epilepsy, cerebral palsy, autism and specific learning disorders and to outline the most appropriate services for the treatment and training of the person, whether those services are immediately available or not.

ORS 427.105. If the report finds that the AMRP is a mentally retarded person, the report shall include a recommendation as to the type of treatment or training facility best calculated to habilitate the person and whether the person is eligible and appropriate for voluntary treatment. ORS 427.270(1).

Evaluator: The evaluation must be conducted by a facility which is approved by the Developmental Disability Diagnosis and Evaluation Service, except insofar as the facility may “contract with qualified persons to perform components of the evaluation.” ORS 427.104, 427.105(3). Also, the facility may use components of an evaluation written within the previous year in accordance with the proper guidelines. ORS 427.270.

VII. Judicial Citation—ORS 427.245(2)

After the completion of the investigation, the investigator must turn in a report to the circuit court. The court reviews the report and determines whether there is probable cause to order a hearing. If there is probable cause, the court issues a citation and delivers a copy to the AMRP prior to the hearing. The citation shall state:

  • The time and place of the hearing.
  • The specific reasons the person is believed to be mentally retarded.
  • The right of the AMRP to have legal counsel, including appointed counsel.
  • The right of the AMRP to have counsel appointed immediately upon request.
  • The right to subpoena witnesses.
  • The right to cross-examine witnesses.
  • Such other information as the court may direct.

Someone from the Mental Health and Developmental Disabilities Division or Community Mental Health Program, presumably the investigator, must explain to the AMRP prior to the hearing the purpose of the citation and the possible consequences of the hearing.

VIII. Pre-hearing Detention

The standard: “If the court finds that there is probable cause to believe that failure to take an allegedly mentally retarded person into custody pending an investigation or hearing would pose an imminent and serious danger to the person or to others, the judge may issue a warrant of detention.” ORS 427.255.

The maximum: “If the person is detained pursuant to ORS 427.255, the court shall hold the hearing within seven judicial days.” ORS 427.245. Unfortunately, the statute does not specify a time from which the seven judicial days should be measured. It is unclear whether the first of the seven days is the day the person was taken into custody, the day after the person was taken into custody, the day the judge receives the investigator’s report, or some other day entirely. If the statute is interpreted similarly to chapter 426, as it is in most counties, day one is the day after the AMRP is taken into custody because it is considered the first full judicial day.

The quirk: There is no obvious place for a person with mental retardation to be held pending a hearing. As a result such persons often end up in psychiatric wards, an expensive and inappropriate solution to the problem.

IX. Setovers—ORS 427.265(4)

Who may request a setover? “…the allegedly mentally retarded person, the legal counsel, parent, guardian, an examiner or the court.”

How long may the setover be? “…not more than 72 hours.”

What standard should the court use in deciding whether to grant the setover? “The court may, for good cause, postpone the hearing for not more than 72 hours in order to allow preparation for the hearing.”

If someone other than the AMRP requests the setover, may the judge order that the AMRP remain in custody pending the setover? “The court may, for good cause, order the continuation of detention authorized under ORS 427.255, during a postponement.” The answer is yes. The court could, for example, order a setover sua sponte and hold the person in custody for the duration of the setover.

X. The Hearing

The Standard: “After hearing all of the evidence, and reviewing the findings of the investigation and other examiners, the court shall determine whether the person is mentally retarded and because of mental retardation is either dangerous to self or others or is unable to provide for the personal needs of the person and is not receiving care as is necessary for the health, safety or habilitation of the person.” ORS 427.290. Note that the standard essentially repeats the definition of “mentally retarded person.”

Thus, ORS 427.290 allows a court to commit a person if it finds by clear and convincing evidence:

(1) The AMRP has significantly subaverage intellectual functioning, in that he or she received a score on a test of intellectual functioning that is two or more standard deviations below the mean. ORS 427.005(17).

(2) The AMRP has a concurrent deficit in the effectiveness or degree with which he or she meets the standards of personal independence and social responsibility expected for age and cultural group.

(3) Both deficits manifested themselves during the developmental period, presumably prior to the age of eighteen.

(4) The person is:

(a) Dangerous to him or herself.

(b) Dangerous to others.

(c) Unable to provide for basic personal needs and not receiving care as is necessary for the health, safety or habilitation of the person.

(5) The danger or lack of necessary care is caused by the intellectual or adaptive deficiencies. Where it is just as likely that the danger or inability is caused by an independent source, such as a physical disability, the evidence is insufficient for commitment. State v. Grandy, 50 Or App 239 (1981).

As an alternative to (1) and (2), the court may find the following, pursuant to ORS 427.005(11):

(1) “Borderline intelligence.”

(2) “Serious impairments in adaptive behavior.”

(3)–(5) same as above.

Neither “borderline intelligence” nor “serious impairments” is defined in chapter 427. One can only assume from the addition of the word “serious” that “serious impairments” refers to a greater impairment than “deficits,” given that “impairment” and “deficit” are virtually synonymous.

Evidentiary Rules: There is no provision similar to the one in chapter 426 declaring that the rules of civil procedure apply unless otherwise stated. The only evidentiary statute is ORS 427.285, Witnesses required at hearing; cross-examination:

The investigator and other appropriate persons or professionals as necessary shall appear at the hearing and present the evidence. The allegedly mentally retarded person shall have the right to cross-examine all witnesses, the investigator and the representative.

Witnesses are required to be present and available for cross-examination. The rules of evidence, however, do not apply. If there is no Oregon Evidence Code then it would seem to follow that there is no hearsay rule. But just the opposite should actually be true. If witnesses are required to be present and there is no evidence code then there is a hearsay rule without any hearsay exceptions. Either the witness is there and the evidence is admissible or the witness is not there and it is not admissible. Moreover, hearsay itself should be thought of in the strictest possible sense. If the evidence is not being used to prove the truth of the matter asserted, it should be inadmissible regardless of whether it falls into one of the typical exceptions.

On the other hand, the statute isn’t as clear as it could be about whether all witnesses are required. The problem is with the vague and undefined phrases “appropriate persons” and “as necessary.” The statute would be clear if it read “The investigator and other witnesses shall appear at the hearing.” Instead the statute says that only the investigator and “appropriate persons and professionals” shall appear “as necessary.” The word “appropriate” and the phrase “as necessary” do seem to modify the extreme evidentiary position previously posited. At the very least the statute requires the presence of the persons and professionals who write the reports which are admissible at the hearing. But what if the reports rely on statements from witnesses or secondhand accounts of an event? Do the primary factual witnesses need to be present? Counsel should be prepared to argue that the due process clauses of the federal and Oregon constitutions require that a witness statement cannot be used to revoke a person’s liberty unless the witness is present and available for cross-examination.

Admissibility of recent evaluations: Where a previous evaluation was performed within the previous year for the purposes of a civil commitment “the results of such evaluation may be introduced in court in lieu of repetition of those components by the examining facility.” ORS 427.270(1).

XI. The Treatment Report—ORS 427.280

The director of the facility in which the person is held, or the investigator, if the person is not currently being held, must “fully advise” the court “of all treatment known to have been administered to the allegedly mentally retarded person after a citation has been issued to the person.” Note that this is a similar, but much broader, provision to the chapter 426 medication report. Here the court must be informed about “any treatment,” rather than just medications, and the time frame includes the entire time the person’s case is pending rather than just an amount of time recent enough for medication to substantially affect the person.

Independent report: If the AMRP requests, the court shall appoint one additional physician or one additional psychologist or both to examine the AMRP and make recommendations to the court.

XII. Right to Counsel—ORS 427.265

If the AMRP requests counsel then counsel must be appointed. ORS 427.265(1).

If any guardian, counsel, relative or friend of the AMRP requests counsel for the AMRP then counsel must be appointed. ORS 427.265(1).

If no request for counsel is made then counsel must be appointed. ORS 427.265(2).

“In all cases legal counsel shall be present at the hearing and may examine all witnesses offering testimony, and otherwise represent the person.” ORS 427.265(3).

When one adds up the various requirements for appointment of counsel at the hearing, it appears that the only way for an AMRP to avoid the appointment of counsel is to hire private counsel.

Additionally, if the AMRP is held in custody pending the hearing, she has a right to have counsel appointed immediately if requested.

XIII. Advice of Rights—ORS 427.265(1)

Just as in Chapter 426, the judge is required to advise the allegedly mentally retarded person of certain specific rights:

The court shall advise the person of the reason for being brought before the court, the nature of the proceedings and the possible results of the proceedings. The court shall also advise the allegedly mentally retarded person of the right to subpoena witnesses and to suitable legal counsel possessing skills and experience commensurate with the nature of the allegations and complexity of the case during the proceedings, and that if the person does not have funds with which to retain suitable legal counsel, the court shall appoint such legal counsel to represent the person.

XIV. Disposition—ORS 427.290

If the person is not mentally retarded, the court shall discharge.

If the person is not dangerous to self or others and is receiving necessary care, the court shall discharge.

If the court finds the following then it shall order release of the person and dismiss the case:

  • The AMRP is a mentally retarded person.
  • The person can give informed consent.
  • The person is willing and able to participate in treatment and training on a voluntary basis.
  • The person will participate in voluntary treatment and training.
  • Voluntary treatment is in the best interest of the person.

The court may commit and order conditional release if it finds the following:

  • A friend, relative or legal guardian requests to care for the person for one year.
  • There is a place of care which the court finds satisfactory.
  • The friend, relative or guardian is able to care for the person.
  • There are adequate financial resources available to care for the person.
  • A conditional release is in the best interest of the person .

If the person is not eligible for discharge, voluntary treatment, or conditional release then the court may commit the person to the Department of Human Services for a period not to exceed one year.

XV. Guardianship

If at the time of the commitment hearing, it is “the opinion of the court [that] the mentally retarded person may be incapacitated, the court may appoint a legal guardian or conservator pursuant to ORS chapter 125. The appointment of a guardian or conservator shall be a separate order from the order of commitment.” ORS 427.290(4).

“Incapacitated” means “a person is unable, without assistance, to properly manage or take care of personal affairs or is incapable, without assistance, of self-care.” ORS 426.005(7).

XVI. Sealing of the Record—ORS 427.293(2)

Just as in chapter 426, the account of the proceedings, transcript and reports are required to be delivered to the clerk or court administrator who “shall cause them to be sealed.” The records shall only be disclosed upon the request of the person or their representative or by order of the court.

Conclusion

Improve Community Mental Health Over Expanding Civil Commitment System

I was recently interviewed for a newspaper article about civil commitments. The reporter, an industrious, intelligent guy, was exploring the question of whether the civil commitment laws need to be loosened to allow more people to be committed. The article was centered around a number of folks with mental illness who had recently died. If they had been committed might they still be alive?

This was the provocative question posed by families of folks with mental illness as well as by various people in power. From their perspective, they see a way to get someone help and a law that is unnecessarily restricting that help. Over the years, I’ve seen many dozens of family members in civil commitment court with the same basic view: a commitment is a mechanism to get their son/daughter/father/sister more resources they can’t seem to access in the community. These folks aren’t wrong in their assessment that some people with mental illness desperately need help. And if it were my family member, I would also seek whatever additional resources exist.

But it is wrong to talk about expanding the commitment system before we provide basic resources through community mental health. We aren’t even providing all the folks who would willingly take medications with those medications. We have a “lottery system” that knowingly allows people to decompensate into florid psychosis instead of providing them with medications. This is simply inhumane and cruel. Even looked at from a purely economic perspective, why would we spend 100 dollars on expanding hospitalization when we could spend 1 dollar on medications? Unfortunately, the people who are talking about loosening the laws for civil commitment aren’t talking about spending more money anywhere, which means they’re assuming an extra capacity of inpatient hospital beds in the state. Which there isn’t.

An enormous portion of the mental health system has been shifted to the criminal justice system so that jail and prison beds are essentially being used as hospital beds throughout the state. Despite this shift, the supply of hospital beds is far outstripped by the demand for those beds. A not infrequent occurrence in Multnomah County is for a local hospital to go into “divert” status because not only are all the psychiatric beds being used but the entire Emergency Department is also being used by people waiting to move into those beds. A person having a heart attack or other life-threatening emergency then gets “diverted” to the next nearest hospital. Our commitment clients are sometimes held in hospitals up to seven or eight hours away because no beds are available any closer to Portland. Oregon State Hospital is rightfully building a new, more humane structure; but in doing so they will lose a significant number of beds. Taking into account the state’s budget woes, it’s unlikely that we’ll be adding to the capacity of hospital beds anytime soon. Thus, if we actually did loosen the commitment laws, we would significantly expand the already high demand for beds without expanding the supply at all. It would be disastrous and wrongheaded.

It is important for defense attorneys to keep this bigger picture in mind when representing commitment clients. Sometimes, as a defense attorney, one can rationally understand that the default position is to prevent a person’s freedom from being taken away, but the sympathies and emotions of the case can muddle the mind. The family desperately wants to get the person help, the person clearly needs help, and the judge, examiners, investigator and prosecutor are aligned in their desire to use the law to get the person help. Only the defense attorney stands in the way and demands that the law be taken seriously, that the allegedly mentally ill person’s freedom and constitutional rights be taken seriously, that the state be held to their requirements of proof just as much as in a criminal case. After all, some folks end up in the hospital on a single commitment for decades. Sometimes, it’s also helpful to remember that the inpatient psychiatric component of our mental health system has already exceeded its maximum. The high burden on the state is one of the few things keeping our system intact.

What the system needs far more than hospital beds or looser laws is a saner community mental health system that provides medications to those who need them, respite beds for those who occasionally decompensate, a place for officers to take folks instead of the jail, and a humane caseworker-to-client ratio. Until we start fixing the real problems with the mental health system, it is absurd to talk about expanding the commitment system— whether on a wholesale or case-by-case basis.
