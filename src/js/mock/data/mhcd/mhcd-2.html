Fitness To Proceed

Twelve Elements You Should Know About Fitness To Proceed

1. The Legal Standard

(ORS 161.360 + Dusky):

A defendant must have sufficient present ability to:

(a) Factually understand the nature of the proceedings.

(b) Rationally understand the nature of the proceedings.

(c) Assist and cooperate with counsel with a reasonable degree of rational understanding.

(d) Participate in the defense of the legal case.

ORS 161.360(2): A defendant may be found incapacitated if, as a result of mental disease or defect, the defendant is unable:

(a) To understand the nature of the proceedings against the defendant; or

(b) To assist and cooperate with the counsel of the defendant; or

(c) To participate in the defense of the defendant.

Dusky v. US, 362 US 402 (1960), in its entirety with emphasis added:

Upon consideration of the entire record we agree with the Solicitor General that “the record in this case does not sufficiently support the findings of competency to stand trial,” for to support those findings under 18 U.S.C. sec 4244 the district judge “would need more information than this record presents.” We also agree with the suggestion of the Solicitor General that it is not enough for the district judge to find that “the defendant [is] oriented to time and place and [has] some recollection of events,” but that the “test must be whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.”

In view of the doubts and ambiguities regarding the legal significance of the psychiatric testimony in this case and the resulting difficulties of retrospectively determining the petitioner’s competency as of more than a year ago, we reverse the judgment of the Court of Appeals affirming the judgment of conviction, and remand the case to the District Court for a new hearing to ascertain petitioner’s present competency to stand trial, and for a new trial if petitioner is found competent.

2. A Practical Standard

There is nothing more essential to being a criminal defendant than choosing whether to go to trial or plead guilty. Such is the nature of the modern criminal justice system that almost everyone receives a plea offer; almost every defendant has to weigh the risks and potential benefits of going to trial against the known quantity of the offer. Ethically, defense counsel cannot make the decision to plead guilty for the client. But the decisional process is a complicated one which requires the defendant, at a minimum, to be able to:

  • Participate in discussions with counsel.
  • Understand the roles of the parties and the structure of the justice system enough that the decision has coherence and meaning.
  • Utilize enough working memory to hold in mind multiple options and multiple future possibilities while weighing pros and cons.
  • Cognitively process the idea of constitutional rights like the right to remain silent at trial.

If counsel suspects that the defendant may not be up to any of these tasks then it’s probably best to hire a forensic expert to delve into the issue in greater detail.

3. An Even More Practical Approach

Immediately after appointment or retention, counsel should attempt to discern whether the client has a mental illness or cognitive disability and the nature of the client’s impairment. Counsel can use this checklist as a guide:

  • Is the defendant oriented in the sense of knowing who he is, where he is and when it is?
  • Can the defendant track a simple conversation without losing the thread or veering off into an irrelevant topic?
  • Is the defendant fixated on a single topic to the exclusion of all others?
  • Is the defendant expressing strong beliefs in bizarre or impossible ideas?
  • Is the defendant attending to auditory or visual hallucinations?
  • Is the defendant unwilling to meet with, talk to or engage in meaningful conversation with counsel?
  • Is the defendant so concrete in his thinking that he doesn’t seem to understand the idea of an uncertain future or a reasoned choice?

Counsel might consider the following strategies during an intake interview:

  • Ask the client if he knows where he is, why he’s there and what the date and day are.
  • Ask the client to summarize a simple idea just explained by the attorney. If the defendant cannot accomplish the task then repeat the explanation to see whether the problem lies in the defendant’s inability to understand or to communicate.
  • Try to vary up questions so that sometimes they require a no, sometimes a yes and sometimes an explanation. Don’t assume the client understands just because the client says he understands; most folks with cognitive disabilities have learned coping mechanisms like feigning understanding in order to get through common interactions.
  • Don’t be afraid of asking direct, nonjudgmental questions about mental health symptoms, prior treatment or medications. Do not ask clients whether they are mentally ill or have a specific disorder. It is a much more controversial topic with very little information to be gained.

4. Avoid Using the State Hospital for the Initial Evaluation

There is little to be gained and lots to lose from using the state hospital for the initial evaluation. ORS 161.365 allows a judge to commit a defendant to the hospital for a 30-day period to get an initial opinion on competency. Despite the clear statutory language that the defendant is committed to the hospital, what actually happens is that the defendant is held in the local jail and taken to the hospital by the local sheriff to undergo the evaluation, returning back to the jail the same day. Unless otherwise ordered by the judge, the hospital evaluator will only consider the issue of current fitness. Specific reasons not to initially use .365 include:

  • The risk involved in submitting your client to a state evaluation in which the defendant’s statements can be used against him at trial.
  • The control gained in choosing your own expert to fit the particular case, including a specialized expert like a neuropsychologist where needed.
  • The advantage given to your expert by seeing the client before the state’s evaluator and as close as possible in time to the incident. Ideally, your expert should begin considering GEI and mental state issues while determining fitness to proceed. Moreover, a follow-up interview after the hospital evaluation allows for a possible sandwiching of the state’s evaluation between two defense evaluations, both of which will be more thorough and empirically backed than the state’s evaluation.
  • With your own expert you will be able to frame and discuss the specific concerns you have about your client’s fitness to proceed. You will also be able to frame and discuss potential trial issues in order to begin building your case. Conversely, the hospital evaluator see the police reports first and will only be available for minimal discussion if you go down to Salem for the evaluation.

5. Competency Must be Determined in Context

This defendant at this time with these charges and this evidence. For example, competency to participate in a complex fraud case is higher than competency to participate in a simple possession case because it requires more of the defendant.

The American Psychiatric Association, author of the DSM-IV-TR, weighed in on this issue in a brief to the Supreme Court in 2008: “Mental competency is not a unitary concept. An individual can be competent for one purpose and not another. Respect for autonomy supports the widespread legal recognition of such differences. An individual’s inability responsibly to make one kind of decision, or to perform one role, is not generally reason enough to override other decisions, or entitlement to perform other roles, for which different relevant capacities are sufficiently intact.” Brief for the American Psychiatric Association and the American Academy of Psychiatry and the Law in Support of NeitherParty, Indiana v. Edwards, 554 US 164 (2008).

6. Competency May be Raised by the Defendant, the DA or the Judge at Any Time

As the U.S. Supreme Court stated: “Even when a defendant is competent at the commencement of his trial, a trial court must always be alert to circumstances suggesting a change that would render the accused unable to meet the standards of competence to stand trial.” Drope v. Missouri, 420 US 162 at 181 (1975). In Drope, the court ruled that “the correct course was to suspend the trial until such an evaluation could be made. That this might have aborted the trial is a hard reality.…” Id.

A trial court judge has the independent duty to protect a defendant’s due process right to competency. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals recently stated, “Where the evidence before the trial court raises a ‘bona fide doubt’ as to a defendant’s competence to stand trial, the judge on his own motion must conduct a competency hearing.” Maxwell v. Roe, 606 F 3d 561, 568 (9th Cir 2010). In that case, the defendant’s murder conviction was reversed because the judge did not, sua sponte, hold a competency hearing to readdress an issue the judge thought was settled. The court also held that defense counsel’s failure to raise the issue cannot be treated as an admission of competency: “[C]ounsel is not a trained mental health professional and his failure to raise petitioner’s competence does not establish that petitioner was competent.” Id. at 574 (quotations omitted).

The issue of competency is timely “at any time before or during the trial in any criminal case.” State v. Gilmore, 102 Or App 102, 105 (1990) (quotations omitted). It is error for a trial judge to deny a motion for a mental health examination as untimely: when the issue is raised, the court must exercise its discretion to decide whether there is a reasonable doubt as to the defendant’s fitness to proceed. Id.at 104.

7. Treat Until Fit in the Community

The judge may “release the defendant on supervision” instead of committing the defendant to the state hospital if “it determines that care other than commitment for incapacity to stand trial would better serve the defendant and the community.” ORS 161.370(2).

8. The Forensic Evaluation Service (F.E.S.)

The F.E.S. is the group of psychologists (currently headed by a psychiatrist) that performs all forensic evaluations for the state hospital. Their offices as well as the evaluations are located in building 48 of the state hospital in Salem. The evaluators are almost exclusively PhD-level psychologists early in their career. Generally speaking, the psychologists are intelligent, hard-working people who are trying to do the best job they can with the information they have. The F.E.S. is not involved in direct treatment or patient care and considers their independence a point of pride. Counsel should be aware of the following tendencies of the F.E.S.:

  • Formal testing is rarely used.
  • There is some pressure from the hospital generally and ward staff specifically to get folks out of the hospital.
  • The standard evaluation format is somewhat focused on factual understanding and relationship with the attorney at the expense of rational understanding and decisional competency.
  • The psychologists generally have little direct experience with the criminal justice system. Thus, they are usually unaware of concepts such as the Sentencing Guidelines and the realities of plea bargaining.

9. The Hospital Evaluation

There is a basic evaluation format used by hospital evaluators. The first section gathers information about the defendant’s social history. Topics include, for example, history of abuse, drug use and mental illness, academic and job history and current family situation. The second section addresses potential mental health symptoms by first doing a mini mental status examination and then asking the client if he has any of a list of symptoms. Examples of symptom questions include: “Do you ever hear voices that aren’t there?” “Do you receive messages from the television?” “Do you have any special abilities or powers?” “Have you recently felt that you wanted to hurt yourself?” The third section focuses on factual understanding of the criminal justice system and ability to get along with defense counsel. Most hospital evaluations begin and end with the three standard sections.

10. The .370 Class

There is a class at the state hospital for those defendants who have been committed to the state hospital to be treated until fit. The class is, of course, in addition to medical treatment such as psychiatric medications. The written materials can be found in an appendix at the end of this chapter. The class is a good idea in the abstract. For example, it is helpful for some folks to learn the basics like the role of the judge and the DA. There is no doubt that if a person does not understand the basic subjects taught in the class then the person is very likely unfit. However, the reverse is not true: a person who has a factual understanding of the system may very well be unfit. But because of the class, there is a tendency for everyone, including attorneys and judges, to focus on factual understanding at the expense of rational understanding. Defense counsel should understand this tendency and hold all parties to the full legal standard. Other problems with the class include the fact that some of the material is just plain wrong. And much of the rest is so uni-dimensional as to be misleading.

11. There Are Three Different Paths for Fitness to Proceed Dismissals

A. Unlikely to be fit in the foreseeable future.

ORS 161.370(9) requires the court to dismiss the case without prejudice if it “determines that there is no substantial probability that the defendant, in the foreseeable future, will gain or regain the capacity to stand trial.” Dismissal in such a situation is required by Jackson v. Indiana, 406 US 715 (1972), in which the U.S. Supreme Court held that a defendant committed for incompetency “cannot be held more than the reasonable period of time necessary to determine whether there is a substantial probability that he will attain that capacity in the foreseeable future.”

B. Maximum time has been served.

ORS 161.370(9) requires the court to dismiss the case where the defendant is entitled to discharge from the hospital under section (6). ORS 161.370(6) states that “in no event shall the defendant be committed for longer than whichever of the following, measured from the defendant’s initial custody date, is shorter: (a) three years; or (b) a period of time equal to the maximum sentence the court could have imposed if the defendant had been convicted.”

C. Unjust to resume case

ORS 161.370(2) allows the court to dismiss the case where “so much time has elapsed [ ] that it would be unjust to resume the criminal proceeding.” One possible reason for such an injustice might be that the defendant has served far more than the likely sentence he would have been given if he were not mentally ill. Another possibility is the set of arguments used in speedy trial motions to dismiss such as loss of witnesses or change of circumstances.

12. The Time Your Client Is at the State Hospital Is Not Dead Time

In addition to preparing for trial in all the usual ways, “The fact that the defendant is unfit to proceed does not preclude any objection though counsel and without the personal participation of the defendant on the grounds that the indictment is insufficient, that the statute of limitations has run, that double jeopardy principles apply or upon any other ground at the discretion of the court which the court deems susceptible of fair determination prior to trial.” ORS 161.370(12)

Discussion

I. Introduction

A. The Crux of the Issue

The crux of fitness to proceed is whether the person charged currently has the capacity to be a defendant in a criminal case in the sense of understanding what’s going on, making decisions and participating in the process with the assistance of an attorney. A defendant has a basic due process right to participate in the case. If a mental illness, a physical disorder or a cognitive disability are preventing the defendant from being able participate in the case, Due Process requires that the criminal case be postponed until the defendant is well enough to participate. If the defendant is unlikely to be able to participate in the foreseeable future, then the case must be dismissed. Jackson v. Indiana, 406 US 715 (1972).

B. What’s in a name?

1.“Fitness to proceed”is the proper legal term in Oregon. This is the title of ORS 161.360 (“Mental disease or defect excluding fitness to proceed”), ORS 161.365 (Procedure for determining issue of fitness to proceed), and the term most commonly used in Oregon case law. A search of other state’s statutes reveals that most other states also use the phrase “fitness to proceed.” Like “capacity,” the word “fitness” is an empty vessel ready to be filled with the skills needed for an individual defendant in particular case.

2.“Capacity to stand trial” is the statutory term for the psychological decision made by the state hospital and other mental health experts. Thus, while lawyers are directed in section 1 of ORS 161.370 that “When the defendant’s fitness to proceed is drawn in question, the issue shall be determined by the court,” mental health experts are directed in section 3, for example, to determine “whether there is a substantial probability that, in the foreseeable future, the defendant will have the capacity to stand trial.” ORS 161.370(3). This distinction gets somewhat muddled in parts of the statute which direct the court to decide, after an evaluation, “whether the defendant presently has the capacity to stand trial.” ORS 161.370(8).

3.“Aid and Assist” is the most common colloquial term in Oregon, occasionally even used by the courts (see, e.g., State v. Gallegos, 2001). It appears to be a phrase which is unique to Oregon. In addition to the fact that it is not the correct legal term, there are two other potential problems with the phrase “aid and assist.” First, it is an abbreviated reference to one part of the capacity analysis under both Oregon statute and Supreme Court case law. It subtly implies that factual and rational understanding are not as important as the relationship with the attorney. Second, it implies that the defendant is the agent of the attorney, needing only to aid and assist the attorney. In fact, the attorney is the agent of the defendant—ethically required to defer to the defendant’s decision on fundamental questions such as whether to plead guilty and whether to waive jury.

4.“Competence to Stand Trial” (or CST) is the phrase most often used by the U.S. Supreme Court and in the psychological literature. The phrase would seem to imply that courts should focus exclusively on the defendant’s capacity to understand and participate in a criminal trial. However, neither the opinions of the Supreme Court nor the psychological literature support such an implication. For example, the seminal U.S. Supreme Court case of Dusky v. US requires that a defendant, among other things, must have the capacity “to assist in preparing his defense,” Dusky, 362 US 402 (1960). Moreover, the psychological literature (as well as the formal competency tests) has focused on a variety of skills necessary to be a defendant, many of which apply pretrial: the ability to work with one’s attorney, the ability to make decisions, the ability to understand the roles of the parties, etc.

C. The Oregon Standard

There are two sources for the Oregon standard for fitness to proceed by reason of incapacity.

First, the U.S. Supreme Court’s opinion in Dusky v. US, in which the Court ruled that:

it is not enough for the district judge to find that “the defendant [is] oriented to time and place and [has] some recollection of events,” but that the “test must be whether he has sufficient present ability to consult with his lawyer with a reasonable degree of rational understanding—and whether he has a rational as well as factual understanding of the proceedings against him.”

Dusky v. US, 362 US 402 (1960). Despite its brevity (clocking in at less than a page of content), Dusky laid down a reasonably clear two-prong test for determining trial competency. Almost fifty years later it is still the most important legal case on the subject. The first prong is whether the defendant can have a meaningful relationship with his or her defense attorney. The second prong is whether the defendant has the ability to understand and participate in the case.

All 50 states now use a competency standard which is based on Dusky. Oregon is no exception. ORS 161.360(2) states:

A defendant may be found incapacitated if, as a result of mental disease or defect, the defendant is unable:

(a)To understand the nature of the proceedings against the defendant; or

(b)To assist and cooperate with the counsel of the defendant; or

(c)To participate in the defense of the defendant.

Note that the Oregon statute adds a component of “participation in the defense” that is not specifically stated in the Dusky opinion. Until recently it was unclear whether the Dusky standard required an ability to actively participate in the case by, for example, making decisions or if passive understanding was sufficient. The forensic literature generally assumed there was a requirement of active participation to be found within the idea of rational understanding. After all, what would be the point of rationality if it wasn’t used to make decisions, give input and otherwise participate in the case? The Supreme Court clarified matters in Godinez v. Moran, 509 US 389 (1993). It found that if the defendant was competent to stand trial, he was automatically competent to plead guilty or waive the right to legal counsel.

The Dusky standard also has an articulated element that ORS 161.360 does not. Duskyrequires “rational,” as well as “factual” understanding, a distinction presumably subsumed within the Oregon statute requirement of “understanding,” written after Duskywas issued. The requirement of rational understanding is an important one for courts and attorneys to keep in mind. It is not uncommon for courts, attorneys and even experts to focus on questions of factual understanding (like roles of the parties) to the exclusion of questions of rational understanding (like the ability to use and apply basic knowledge about the justice system). The Oregon Court of Appeals has indicated that a defendant cannot be said to have “understanding” unless, “considering the totality of the circumstances, the record reflects that he substantially appreciates the material risks” involved in the decisions he is making. State v. Lasarte, 203 Or App 222, 224 (2005) (addressing the term “understanding” in the context of a defendant’s decision to waive his right to counsel).

Note that Oregon also uses the words “assist and cooperate” with counsel, rather than the Dusky phrasing of “consult” with counsel. “Assist and cooperate,” like “aid and assist,” frames the issue as one where the attorney has the power and needs only for the defendant to cooperate or give a little assistance. Conversely, a person who “consults” an expert like an attorney is a person with the power to make decisions with the assistance and cooperation of the expert. It’s difficult to know whether the frames were intentionally chosen but there is no doubt that the attorney is merely the agent of the defendant. As the Supreme Court has said, a defendant has “the absolute and primary right to conduct one’s own defense.” Faretta v. California, 422 US 806, 816 (1975) (quotations omitted). Attorneys and judges should therefore eschew any implication that the attorney can handle everything for the defendant if the defendant merely understands. First, the defendant must be the one to make the fundamental decisions in the case like plea or trial and testify or remain silent. Second, to the extent that “consult” does mean something more than “assist and cooperate” then the higher federal standard should be incorporated into the Oregon standard. Third, the Oregon standard separately requires that the defendant be able to participate in the case. Participation is a separate requirement from assistance and cooperation. Thus, it must mean something additional.

Ultimately, the standards set forth in Dusky and the Oregon statute beg more questions than they answer. What is a “sufficient” present ability? What does it mean to “consult”? What is a “reasonable” degree of rational understanding? What must the defendant rationally understand? What must he factually understand?

D. Competency Is a Due Process Right

Despite the permissive language in ORS 161.360(2) that “a defendant may be found incapacitated if…,” competency is a fundamental due process right. The Supreme Court has stated, “the conviction of an accused person while he is legally incompetent violates due process, and [. . .] state procedures must be adequate to protect this right.” Pate v. Robinson, 383 US 375, 378 (1966) (citations omitted). The Oregon courts have similarly recognized competency as a fundamental right: “If an indicted person is not sane, the court cannot go on with the case; or if he becomes insane after the trial commences, he can neither be sentenced, nor, if sentenced, punished, while his insanity continues.” State v. Bostrom, 2 Or App 466, 467 (1970) (quoting State v. Henke, 196 Wash 185 (1938)).

E. Burden of Proof

Nobody bears the burden of proof in Oregon when determining whether or not a person is competent to stand trial. The court is simply directed to make a decision. It makes sense not to place the burden on the defendant since either party or the judge can raise the issue and a finding of incapacity does not redound to the defendant’s legal benefit. “The Oregon statutory scheme does not allocate any burden to a criminal defendant in these circumstances.” State v. Cunningham, 197 Or App 264, 288 n 12 (2005).

II. Thinking Practically About Trial Competency

ORS 161.360 sets out a standard for capacity to stand trial. However, the standard is so broad that it’s very difficult to use in any particular situation. In fact, as a practical necessity, most defense attorneys simply refer clients for competency evaluation when there is a sufficient level of mental illness and then defer to the expert. A “sufficient” level of mental illness is defined differently by every attorney since it is not a legal term but rather a gut feeling by the attorney.

Deferring entirely to the evaluator is tempting but problematic. First, if the defense attorney understands the issues and has a tentative game plan before the evaluation, then the issues can be framed for the evaluator and potentially affect the outcome. Second, competency evaluations are contextual. Only the defense attorney really knows how complicated it will be for the defendant to think through and participate in the particular case. Thus, the defense attorney is in the best position to roughly sketch out the defendant’s deficiencies in the context of the case. The expert can then flesh out the details, perform testing for confirmation (or denial) and rewrite the initial ideas in the language of psychology. Third, issues of competency are legally separate from but intertwined with the defense of the case. The earlier defense counsel can formulate a rough defense, the sooner a focused investigation and preparation of the case can begin.

Fortunately, there are easier and more finely honed ways to think about the competency of a defendant than coming up with an internal cutoff line. In many ways the general approach is like a legal problem. First, spot the issue. Second, get a handle on the basic facts. Third, think about the facts in relation to the law. Fourth, apply a rule to come to a rough conclusion.

A. Spotting the Issue

Though there are no formal criteria that guide an attorney in spotting the possibility of a mental illness, the following are excellent factors to use as an easy indicator to investigate the matter further:

  • Defendant’s ability to understand generally the fact that he or she has a criminal case and will have to make a decision.
  • Defendant’s ability to follow a simple conversation (the alternative being delusions, hallucinations, disorganization or limited cognitive ability which prevent logic, linearity or sustained attention).
  • Defendant’s unwillingness to engage in simple discussions about the case.

B. Honing the Facts: The Mental Status Examination (MSE)

Attorneys are not trained to perform a full and complete mental status examination. However, it is a helpful way for lay attorneys to think about mental illness in a more nuanced way. After spotting the possibility of mental illness, the attorney (or a legal assistant) should go through the elements of a mental status examination like a checklist to narrow down the issues. There are approximately 13 parts to a typical MSE:

1. Appearance: How is the person dressed? How is their grooming?

2. Mood: How does the person report feeling—in general and on that day (sad, bitter, happy)?

3. Affect: How does the person appear to feel? For example, many people with schizophrenia have a “flat” tone to their voice even while expressing anger or sadness. Another possibility is lability: the rapid cycling between emotions.

4. Behavior: How do they hold themselves in terms of body posture or unusual facial expressions like grimacing?

5. Speech: How is the person’s volume, rate and flow of speech? Are there any unusual verbal mannerisms or tics? For example, a person in a manic episode will likely have “pressured” speech.

6. Thought process: Are the thoughts linear and connected or are they tangential and disconnected? Are the thoughts coming so quickly that the person can barely keep up?

7. Thought content: Is the person expressing thoughts that seem paranoid, delusional, or obsessive? Is the person fixated on a particular topic or phrase?

8. Perception: Is the person’s sense of reality distorted by, for example, auditory or visual hallucinations, or bizarre beliefs, like special powers or thought insertion?

9. Cognition: Is the person able to think abstractly in a basic way? Is the person able to understand basic concepts of the criminal justice system? How is the person’s education, intelligence and ability to concentrate or focus on the conversation?

10. Orientation: Is the person aware of who he is, where he is and when it is?

11. Memory: How is the person’s long- and short-term memory?

12. Judgment: Is the person able to think through simple life decisions or ethical quandaries? For example, a classic judgment question is “If you smelled smoke in a theater, what would you do?”

13. Insight: Is the person aware of the unusual psychological aspects that you’ve noted?

C. Applying Facts to Law: Grisso’s Five Components of Competency

Thomas Grisso, PhD, is the author of many of the most important books and articles on trial competency including Evaluating Competencies: Forensic Assessments and Instruments, the seminal book on conceptualizing competencies. His model for thinking about competency has five components: functional, causal, interactive, judgmental and dispositional. It’s a useful place to start thinking about the nuance of trial competency.

1. The Functional Component

The functional component is the one that attorneys would most easily recognize: can the defendant perform the functions required by the criminal process? But what functions exactly are required by the criminal process?

a. The Grisso List

Dr. Grisso sets out a list of functional abilities organized by the Dusky standard:

Consulting and Assisting Counsel

  • Understanding that counsel works for defendant
  • Understanding counsel’s inquiries
  • Capable of responding to counsel’s inquiries in a manner that provides relevant information for defense
  • Providing consistent account of events relevant to charges and a defense
  • Managing the demands of trial process (stress, maintaining demeanor)
  • Capable of testifying if necessary

Factual Understanding

  • That the defendant is accused of a crime
  • That the court will decide on guilt or innocence
  • That the trial could result in punishment
  • Of the various ways that defendant may plead
  • That certain sentences are possible (their nature and seriousness)
  • Of the roles of the various participants in the trial process
  • Of the general process of trials

Rational Understanding (including decisional abilities)

  • Beliefs about one’s own trial process are not distorted by delusional beliefs
  • Appropriate motivation to further one’s defense
  • Reasoning ability sufficient to process relevant information during decision making

Thomas Grisso, Evaluating Competencies, p 84 (Kluwer Academic, 2003), hereinafter referred to as Grisso.

b. The Rogers List

Richard Rogers and Daniel Shuman also have a list they created to combine clinical considerations and subcategories of the Dusky prongs:

Consult with Counsel

  • Inability to communicate one’s thoughts coherently

– Formal thought disorder, disturbances of speech, incoherence

  • Inability to participate in one’s defense

– Lack of autonomous thoughts (e.g., thought insertion, or withdrawal) and self-destructive motivations (e.g., delusions of guilt)

  • Incapacity to make decisions

– Cannot describe or recognize alternatives and severely impaired decisions (e.g., psychotic interference)

  • Expectations about the case and defense counsel

– Psychotically based expectations

Factual Understanding of the Proceedings

  • Lack of understanding of the defense counsel’s role

– Unaware of counsel’s efforts to help the defendant and minimize punishment

  • Lack of understanding of judge’s role

– Unaware of judge’s role in running the court, verdicts, and sentencing

  • Lack of understanding of his or her criminal charges

– Unaware of the seriousness of the charges and possible penalties

  • Lack of understanding of the prosecutor’s role

– Unaware of prosecutor’s role in bringing charges, guilty verdicts or maximizing punishment

  • Lack of understanding of the jury’s role

– Unaware of jury’s role regarding verdict and possible sentencing

  • Lack of understanding of the courtroom process

– Unaware of trial process (e.g., opening statements, evidence, and jury deliberation)

Rational Understanding of the Proceedings

  • Inability to rationally participate in defense

– Unaware of possible defense strategies; participation influenced by psychotic symptoms or motivated by self-harm

  • Lack of awareness of his or her involvement as the defendant

– Denial of being a defendant; denial of the possibility of being found guilty and sentenced

  • Lack of understanding of the adversarial process

– Inability to understand conflicting roles between prosecution and defense

  • Lack of investment in the trial’s outcome

– Uninterested in the verdict; cannot grasp its potential consequences

  • Self-defeating motivation

– Potentially interested in sabotaging case

Rogers and Shuman, Fundamentals of Forensic Practice, p 163 (Springer, 2005).

2. Causal Component

It is not enough under either Dusky or ORS 161.360 that a person is unfit to proceed. The person must be unfit “as a result of mental disease or defect.” In other words, there must be a nexus between a mental disorder or cognitive disability and the inability to understand, assist, cooperate or participate. Where there is no such nexus, a defendant will be found competent: State v. Cunningham, for example, states that “although defendant’s paranoia interfered with his ability to make wise choices and to understand the merits of his advisors’ opinions, those were results of his personality and not of a mental disease or defect. Accordingly, the court determined that defendant was competent to continue to stand trial.” 197 Or App 264, 284 (2005) (affirming the trial court’s ruling). The nearly unanimous consensus of legal and forensic experts is that such a nexus is important to exclude a nonnegligible amount of defendants whose inabilities or incompetence derives from something other than a mental disease or defect. For example, the most respected expert in the area of competency, Dr. Thomas Grisso, believes that a nexus is important to “assure that the functional deficits in understanding are beyond the control of the defendant” and to “assure that observed deficits in trial-related functioning are not merely a consequence of conditions that could be remediated easily without the need for psychiatric treatment.” Grisso, p 85. His example for the first reason is the malingerer who is simply feigning incompetence and his example for the second is the person who is merely ignorant of the nature of trial procedures.

This author respectfully disagrees with the nearly unanimous consensus generally and Dr. Grisso specifically. There is no need to require a nexus between mental disease or defect and functional inabilities because there are no defendants who lack capacity for reasons other than mental disease or defect. Grisso’s examples do an excellent job of showing why. The malingerer is not unable to understand or cooperate—he is unwilling to understand or cooperate; he is feigning or exaggerating symptoms to accrue some benefit (also called secondary gain). Thus, the malingerer is fit to stand trial. The person who is merely ignorant of trial procedure is not unable to understand—he is lacking knowledge; a situation which will be remedied by defense counsel without the assistance of psychiatrists. The inability, whatever it is, must be a mental disease or defect. Even an inability caused by drugs or alcohol is still an inability (and a disorder) for the period of intoxication (easily remedied by mandated or forced sobriety): the Oregon Supreme Court has held that if a defendant is deprived of his ability to comprehend the proceedings due to the narcotic effect of tranquilizers, he is not fit to proceed. “If during his trial or any part thereof this defendant because of the narcotic effect of the tranquilizers being administered to him was deprived of his ability in the absence thereof to comprehend the nature of the proceedings and to assist in his own defense, then it follows that his conviction cannot stand.” State v. Hancock, 247 Or 21, 28 (1967).

Of course, ORS 161.360 requires a causal nexus between disease or defect and incapacity. The point is not advocate for statutory change. After all, nobody is legally affected by the distinction. The point is to make sure attorneys and experts are not adding something more than is required because the nexus is unclear. As a practical matter, attorneys should focus on the functional component without concerning themselves with the causal component.

3. Interactive Component

The issue of competency cannot be considered in a vacuum. A defendant might be competent for the purposes of one case but not competent for the purposes of a second case. Grisso created this nonexhaustive list to give an idea of the sorts of contextual issues that affect the competency decision apart from the defendant’s abilities:

  • Complexity and multiplicity of charges
  • Particular events associated with the alleged offense
  • Range of possible penalties for this alleged offense, and probabilities of their occurrence
  • Range and types of evidence available to counsel without defendant’s report
  • Simplicity or complexity of the legal defenses available
  • Necessity for defendant’s own testimony at trial
  • Probable length of trial
  • Probable complexity of trial (e.g., types and numbers of witnesses)
  • Potential of trial to arouse emotion (e.g., due to the nature of offense relation of parties in the trial process)
  • Sources of social support for defendant during trial process

Grisso, p 88. I would add two more: the complexity of the plea offer and the complexity of possibilities if the plea offer is rejected. (As to the plea offer, it makes a difference if there is a sentence of discharge on the table as opposed to a complicated set of probationary conditions with significant additions such as treatment or sex offender registration for life. As to the rejected plea offer, perhaps the DA has made specific possibilities clear like the indictment of additional crimes or a referral to the federal government for prosecution.)

4. Judgmental and Dispositional Components

A conclusion must be made. This involves applying the functional abilities of the defendant with the functional abilities required by the particular criminal cases at hand. But it also requires thinking through the underlying reasons for competency requirements like fairness, justice and due process. It would be an unfortunate triumph of form over substance if attorneys, experts and judges ignored the dispositional aspects of finding competency such as a significantly greater deprivation of liberty

D. Double Check the Focus on Competency Rather Than Severity of Illness

In a recent study, 273 forensic psychiatrists and psychologists completed a questionnaire about two hypothetical trial competency situations. Morris, et al, “Assessing Competency Competently: Toward a Rational Standard for Competency to Stand Trial Assessments,” Journal of the American Academy of Psychiatry and the Law Vol 32(3)2003, 311–328 (2004). The study found that clinicians overwhelmingly base competency decisions on the severity of the defendant’s mental disorder rather than the defendants’ relative ability to understand the system, assist their attorneys, or participate in their defense.

There are three possible lessons for attorneys and their experts to take from this study. First, clinicians should remain vigilant toward what is apparently a natural tendency to substitute severity of illness for a more meaningful inquiry.

Second, competency evaluations should generally include some objective testing. Clinicians have assumptions and biases that they cannot control, as well as schemas which order and make sense of the sensory input they receive. As professionals, they may be more confident in their abilities and conclusions than a sober objective analysis would warrant. Objective testing will assist clinicians in identifying and controlling subjective biases.

Third, the question of competency is often alien to the experience and training of the forensic expert. It is easier for the lawyers to understand the complexity of the defendant’s case and determine what it means to understand, assist and consult in the particular factual and legal matters involved. The attorney should assist the clinician in understanding what it will take for a defendant to get through the particular case at hand. The clinician should couch any opinion or recommendation in terms that acknowledge that he or she cannot take into account every aspect of the legal case relevant to the competency determination, because some such aspects are outside the experience and training of the clinician. Note that the complexity of the case and the need for client consultation is often unrelated to the severity of the case. For example, a murder case may be less complex than a low-level welfare fraud case.

III. Competency as a Defense Weapon

Competence to stand trial is not a defense tactic that should be chosen at the beginning of a case. There are too many negative repercussions for the client, not least of which is the possibility that the client will be sent to the state hospital to be treated until fit for a period not to exceed the statutory maximum. For some clients, this may be a better option than incarceration, but for most it would not be a choice they would make. However, once the issue is raised, there are three separate statutory means for dismissing the case.

A. Unfit for the Foreseeable Future. ORS 161.370

ORS 161.370(9) requires the court to dismiss the case without prejudice if it “determines that there is no substantial probability that the defendant, in the foreseeable future, will gain or regain the capacity to stand trial.” Dismissal in such a situation is required by Jackson v. Indiana, 406 US 715 (1972), in which the U.S. Supreme Court held that a defendant committed for incompetency “cannot be held more than the reasonable period of time necessary to determine whether there is a substantial probability that he will attain that capacity in the foreseeable future.”

It is not uncommon for the state hospital to send a still incompetent defendant back for trial, stating that the person is at their “baseline” and unlikely to improve beyond their current state. If that is indeed the situation, the defense attorney can request a hearing and argue that the state hospital is correct about the unlikelihood of further improvement but incorrect regarding the legal standard. Defense counsel should hire a forensic expert to assess (or reassess) the defendant and testify regarding the likelihood of further improvement and present fitness to proceed.

B. Maximum Time Has Been Served

ORS 161.370(9) requires the court to dismiss the case where the defendant is entitled to discharge from the hospital under section (6). ORS 161.370(6) states that “in no event shall the defendant be committed for longer than whichever of the following, measured from the defendant’s initial custody date, is shorter: (a) three years; or (b) a period of time equal to the maximum sentence the court could have imposed if the defendant had been convicted.”

C. Unjust to Resume Case

ORS 161.370(2) allows the court to dismiss the case where “so much time has elapsed [ ] that it would be unjust to resume the criminal proceeding.” One possible reason for such an injustice might be that the defendant has served far more than the likely sentence he would have been given if he were not mentally ill. Another possibility is the set of arguments used in speedy trial motions such as loss of witnesses or change of circumstances.

IV. Procedure

A. Raising the Issue of Competency

Competency to stand trial raises ethical issues for the legal system and difficult decisions for the defense attorney. A person who is found incompetent to stand trial is, by definition, pre-trial and thus presumed innocent. Yet, the defendant adjudicated incompetent is usually incarcerated at the state hospital and placed in legal limbo until such time as he or she becomes fit to proceed. Often the period of pretrial incarceration is greater than the sentence that the defendant would have received had he been found guilty.

Typically, in order to incarcerate a mentally ill person (even one who has been found guilty except insane) there must be a legal finding of dangerousness. But dangerousness is not a consideration in this area. Hospitalizing a defendant in order to restore competency is often justified as being the most fair outcome for the defendant. It is difficult, however, to reconcile the ideal of fairness with incarceration at the state hospital, a dismal place with cramped quarters and prisonlike security measures.

It is important for counsel to keep these ethical issues in mind when dealing with a defendant who may be incompetent to stand trial. Defense counsel is ethically bound to raise a serious concern about competency but, particularly given the current condition of the state hospital, defense counsel is also ethically bound to refrain from raising concerns about competency unless there are serious concerns.

Defense counsel may move for a judicial inquiry into the defendant’s fitness to proceed at any time. If counsel files a written motion for a determination of fitness to proceed then the court has a duty to review defendant’s fitness. State v. Gilmore, 102 Or App 102 (1990). Defense counsel is required to notify the court of a serious concern because a defendant has a right under the federal Due Process clause not to be tried unless competent. Pate v. Robinson, 383 US 375 (1966).

The court may conduct an inquiry at any time if it has “reason to doubt” the defendant’s competency. Drope v. Missouri, 420 US 162 (1975).

B. Initial Determination

Once the issue of competency is at issue the court must make an initial determination as to fitness to proceed under ORS 161.370(1). In doing so, the court may call any witness and may appoint an expert to examine the defendant. The court may also commit the defendant to the State Hospital for a period not to exceed thirty days in order to facilitate the court expert’s evaluation. For this reason, defense counsel should strongly consider hiring an expert to conduct a competency evaluation so that the report can be submitted at the initial hearing. That way, the judge can proceed directly to the initial determination rather than sending the defendant down to the hospital for the 30-day evaluation.

The state hospital evaluators are not authorized to consider any issue except capacity to stand trial unless the defense requests it and the court orders it. ORS 161.365(3). For example, it may save time for the hospital to consider GEI as well as capacity to stand trial. More likely, however, is that the parties will request a determination of whether the defendant, if they are unfit to proceed, will regain capacity in the foreseeable future.

Additionally, the expert may inquire as to whether the defendant may qualify for a defense of Guilty Except Insane if both the defense and the court request that the expert do so. ORS 161.365(3).

The expert, whether appointed directly or from the state hospital, must submit an examination report including a description of the examination, a statement of defendant’s mental condition, and an opinion as to whether defendant is incapacitated within the meaning of ORS 161.360. ORS 161.365(2).

If no party objects, the examination report may form a sufficient basis for the initial determination that defendant lacks capacity to proceed. If either party objects, the court must hold a hearing, and the objecting party must be allowed to summon and cross-examine the expert or experts who submitted the examination report. State v. King, 84 Or App 165, 174 (1987). This is a constitutional requirement: “A primary purpose” of the hearing is “to afford to the defendant the rights of confrontation and cross-examination guaranteed by the Sixth Amendment.” Brady v. Calloway, 11 Or App 30, 36 (1972). As a practical matter, hearings are rarely held for the initial determination. District attorneys rarely object because the defendant will be held in custody, albeit in a hospital setting. Defense attorneys rarely object because they typically raised the issue based on serious concerns which have now been confirmed by an expert.

C. Result of Initial Determination

If the court finds that a defendant lacks fitness to proceed, a decision must be made whether to commit the defendant to the custody of the state hospital or release the defendant on supervision. ORS 161.370(2).

The court may release the defendant on supervision if it determines that care other than commitment for incapacity to stand trial would better serve the defendant and the community. It may place conditions which it deems appropriate on the release, including the requirement that the defendant regularly report to the Department of Human Services or a community mental health and developmental disabilities program for examination to determine if the defendant has regained capacity to stand trial. ORS 161.370(2).

D. Commitment to the State Hospital—ORS 161.370

If the court commits the defendant to the state hospital, it does so for an indefinite period of time. The maximum period of commitment, however, may be no longer than three years or the maximum sentence the court could impose, whichever is shorter. ORS 161.370(6). The hospital must notify the court at least thirty days prior to discharge if they are planning to discharge the defendant because the maximum period is about to expire. ORS 161.370(7).

Within 60 days of commitment, the state hospital must evaluate the defendant to determine whether there is a substantial probability that, in the foreseeable future, the defendant will have the capacity to stand trial. ORS 161.370(3). Within ninety days of commitment, the hospital must alert the court as to whether there is a substantial probability that, in the foreseeable future, the defendant will have the capacity to stand trial. If there is a substantial probability, the hospital must estimate how long it will be until the defendant is competent and what treatment might be required. ORS 161.370(4)(b).

In addition to the initial evaluation, the Hospital must submit a progress report to the court no less than every 180 days. ORS 161.370(5).

If the hospital believes that the defendant has the capacity to stand trial, it may notify the court at any time. ORS 161.370(4)(b).

While at the hospital, “the defendant shall receive treatment designed for the purpose of enabling the defendant to gain or regain capacity.” ORS 161.370(5). As a practical matter, this means that the defendant will receive psychiatric treatment in the form of medications as well as the Oregon State Hospital’s “370 Class.” However, a court cannot order the hospital to medicate a defendant against his or her will solely for the purpose of rendering him or her competent, unless the court specifically concludes that involuntary medication is both “substantially likely to render the defendant competent to stand trial” and “substantially unlikely to have side effects that will interfere significantly with the defendant’s ability to assist counsel in conducting a trial defense.” Sell v. United States, 539 US 166, 181 (2003). The court must also conclude that involuntary medication is necessary to achieve that result, and that less intrusive alternative treatments are unlikely to be successful. Id.

The “370 Class” (see appendix) focuses on factual understanding to the detriment of the other three aspects of competence: rational understanding, assistance of counsel and participation in the legal case. For example, the class does not teach defendants how to think about decisions involving hypothetical risks, nor does it teach defendants about the elements of a crime or the idea that each needs to be proven beyond a reasonable doubt.

Moreover, the class materials are rife with mistakes. For example, page ten of the materials lists the five possible pleas:

  • Guilty Plea
  • Not Guilty Plea
  • No Contest Plea
  • Guilty Except for Insanity Plea
  • Diminished Capacity Plea

The first four are possible pleas, but there is no such thing as a “diminished capacity plea.” The diminished capacity defense does not exist in Oregon (see Chapter 3, “Disordered Mental State”). Even if it did, the applicable plea would simply be “not guilty.”

E. Final Determination

Each time the court receives a report, the defendant is entitled to a hearing, upon request, to have the court determine whether there is a substantial probability that the defendant, in the foreseeable future, will gain or regain the capacity to stand trial. If the court makes a determination that there is no substantial probability or that the maximum period of commitment has expired, the court must dismiss, without prejudice, all the charges against the defendant and discharge the defendant from the forensic commitment. The court may institute a civil commitment proceedings in accordance with chapter 426 for a defendant with a mental illness or under chapter 427 for a defendant with mental retardation or developmental disabilities.

V. Other Competencies

A. Competency to Plead Guilty

The Supreme Court held in Godinez v. Moran, 509 US 389 (1993), that the standard of competency for pleading guilty or waiving the right to counsel is the same as the standard for competence to stand trial defined in Dusky. However, there is a heightened requirement in the sense that a trial court “must satisfy itself that the waiver of [defendant’s] constitutional rights is knowing and voluntary. In this sense there is a ‘heightened’ standard for pleading guilty and for waiving the right to counsel, but it is not a heightened standard of competence.” Godinez at 400–01. If a court has doubts about the competency of a guilty plea, it must refuse/defer the plea pending an examination. Saddler v. United States, 531 F 2d 83 (2d Cir 1976). The Ninth Circuit has held that a court has the duty to conduct a hearing, sua sponte, whenever it doubts that the guilty plea was the result of a “reasoned choice among the alternatives presented.” Chavez v. United States, 656 F 2d 512, 517 (9th Cir 1981).

In Oregon, to satisfy itself that a defendant’s guilty plea was “voluntarily and intelligently made,” a trial court “must address the defendant personally” to determine that he understands the charges and the potential maximum sentence, and to inform him of the rights he waives by pleading guilty. State v. Peterson, 70 Or App 333, 340 n 4 (1984).

B. Competency to Waive Counsel

The Supreme Court, in Godinez,held that the competency standard for waiving counsel is the same as the standard for competency to stand trial, and also held that the right to dispense with counsel is protected by the Sixth Amendment, in Faretta v. California, 422 U.S. 806 (1975). However, this does not mean that a defendant who is competent to stand trial is universally permitted to proceed without counsel. There are situations in which the constitutional right to a fair trial conflicts with, and trumps, the right to self-representation—for instance, when the defendant engages in “serious obstructionist misconduct” in the course of self-representation. United States v. Johnson, 2010 US App LEXIS 13719 at *13 (9th Cir 2010).

The Supreme Court has recognized that competency is not a unitary concept, stating, “[o]ne might not be insane in the sense of being incapable of standing trial and yet lack the capacity to stand trial without benefit of counsel.” Indiana v. Edwards, 554 US 164 (2008). The Edwards Court stated that Dusky and its progeny assume representation by counsel, and therefore the competency requirement differs in situations where the defendant has decided to forego counsel. Id. When a defendant’s lack of capacity “threatens an improper conviction or sentence,” a court may insist on representation by counsel.Id.

Additionally, even when a defendant is allowed to proceed without counsel, a court may insist on appointing a “standby counsel” to advise the defendant on matters of procedure and protocol. McKaskle v. Wiggins, 465 US 168 (1983).

C. Competency to Be Sentenced/Punished

A defendant who becomes unfit to proceed cannot be sentenced, and a convicted defendant who becomes unfit after sentencing cannot have their sentence carried out. State v. Bostrom, 2 Or App 466, 467 (1970) (quoting State v. Henke, 196 Wash 185 (1938)). The Supreme Court has spoken to this issue in the context of capital punishment: an incompetent person cannot be put to death, and the test for competency is whether they “know the fact of their impending execution and the reason for it.” Ford v. Wainwright, 477 US 399, 422 (1986) (Powell, J., concurring). A prior finding of competence to stand trial in no way forecloses a current finding of incompetence to be executed. Panetti v. Quarterman, 551 US 930, 934 (2007) (“Prior findings of competency do not foreclose a prisoner from proving he is incompetent to be executed because of his present mental condition”).

D. Competency to Refuse an Insanity Defense

The D.C. Circuit, interpreting the Supreme Court, has held that if a defendant has been found competent to stand trial, the court is not permitted to impose an insanity defense against the defendant’s wishes. The Sixth Amendment protects a competent defendant’s right to conduct his own defense. “Unless the accused has acquiesced[,] the defense presented is not the defense guaranteed him by the Constitution, for, in a very real sense, it is not hisdefense.” United States v. Marble, 940 F 2d 1543, 1546 (D.C. Cir 1991) (quoting Faretta v. California, 422 US 806 (1975)).

E. Competency to Forego Appeals

To determine whether a defendant is competent to make the decision not to appeal a conviction, a court must inquire as to “whether he has capacity to appreciate his position and make a rational choice with respect to continuing or abandoning further litigation or on the other hand whether he is suffering from a mental disease, disorder, or defect which may substantially affect his capacity[.]” Rees v. Peyton, 384 US 312, 314 (1966).

The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals has indicated that this standard might be exactly the same as that of Dusky, but ultimately declined to resolve that question. Dennis v. Budge, 378 F 3d 880, 889 n 6 (2004). The Dennis court also held that in order to demonstrate incompetence in this context, it is not enough to show that the defendant’s decision was affected by mental illness. Rather, it must be shown that mental illness substantially impacted the defendant’s capacity to make a rational decision. A decision affected by mental illness, the court indicated, is not necessarily an irrational one. Id. at 890.

F. Competency to Waive the Right to Competency

In United States v. Morin, 338 F 3d 838 (2003), the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals addressed the question of whether a defendant who is presently competent may preemptively move to waive his right to competency (in anticipation of future incompetency resulting from a decision to stop taking medication). The court upheld the trial judge’s denial of that motion, stating that because the defendant’s competency was never challenged at trial, he never had a valid opportunity to waive the requirement. Morin at 842. This seems to implicitly create a catch-22 in which competency can never be waived: a competent defendant does not have the opportunity to waive the requirement, but an incompetent defendant is, of course, not competent to waive.

G. Competency to Confess

If a defendant’s Fifth Amendment rights against self-incrimination are violated, a confession is “incompetent because not voluntary.” Miranda v. Arizona, 384 US 436, 461 (1966). But the analysis of voluntariness is constrained to police conduct: where a defendant’s confession was prompted by imaginary voices, the confession was admissible because there was no coercive police conduct. Colorado v. Connelly, 479 US 157 (1986).

H. Competency to Consent to Search/Seizure

Competency to consent to a search does not require that the defendant have knowledge of the right to refuse consent. But lack of knowledge can be a factor taken into account when determining whether consent was voluntary. Florida v. Rodriguez, 469 US 1 (1984).

VI. Psychological Testing Instruments

[Note: For a thorough presentation of clinical tests, see Chapter 8, “Clinical Assessment & Testing”.]

A. Generally

In general, psychological testing is administered to help provide reasoning for incompetence, detection of malingering, and instructions for adaptive means of communication between attorney and client. It should be noted that psychological testing is not as commonly used in aid and assist assessments as in other types of forensic referrals. The specific nature of the referral question and the data being measured does not necessarily lend itself to a comprehensive psychological testing battery. Knowledge of the exact level of intellectual functioning or nature of thought disturbance are secondary to an examinee’s ability to meet the Duskyprongs.

Psychological testing may help elucidate the underlying reasons for incompetence. Intellectual testing helps to uncover profound levels of mental retardation. Neuropsychological testing may help to identify the presence of brain injury, which can explain deficits in competence. The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) is an instrument commonly used to assess competence. The Wechsler scale provides data on factors believed to be central to a defendant’s ability to participate in the legal process.Research conducted by Nestor, Daggett, Haycock, and Price (1999, cited in Stafford) showed significant differences in WAIS-R scales measuring verbal reasoning, episodic memory, and social judgment.

No matter which competency protocol is utilized it is incumbent upon the evaluator to spend ample time with an examinee to gain insight into his or her reasoning and ability to sustain attention. Such interaction may provide insight into an examinee’s capacities and thresholds for thoughtful discourse. Pointed follow-up questions help the evaluator gauge a client’s ability to reason. While an individual may be able to consistently respond “yes” or “no” to a variety of questions, it is the explanation which ultimately uncovers bizarre and loose associations.

B. Malingering Relevant to Competency Assessments

Instruments designed to measure competency (e.g., MacArthur or Competence Assessment Inventory) are often plagued by a high level of transparency. As such, these instruments frequently produce poor results.

Malingering can be measured on objective personality tests such as the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI-2) and performance-based tests like the Test of Memory Malingering (TOMM). Instruments such as the MMPI-2 and the Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI) possess validity scales to measure an individual’s propensity to feign or highly exaggerate his or her current level of functioning. The MMPI-2 contain scales which have been designed to detect feigning impairment. In essence, the MMPI-2 contains items that isolate symptoms and attitudes not frequently found in known clinical groups, items isolating obvious versus subtle symptomology, and items based on erroneous stereotypes. The F scale has been distinguished as being the most predictive of malingering. However, the MMPI-2’s F scale was not developed using a clinical sample. In other words, it does not necessarily distinguish between genuine and faked symptoms of pathology.

The PAI also contains scales designed to detect malingering. Unlike the MMPI-2 which offers interpretation within a range of scores, the PAI was developed with specific cut-off scores. This instrument has greater sensitivity to the aspects of malingering more relevant to forensic assessment. The PAI’s validity scales allow for different levels of false responding. The PAI utilizes two primary scales to help in the detection of feigning: Negative Impression Management (NIM) and the Malingering Index.

The professional literature strongly discourages the isolated use of objective personality assessment tools such as the MMPI-2 and PAI in the determination of forensic issues such as competency. They are most effectively used as adjuncts to help explain functioning.

C. Feigned Cognitive Impairment

According to Richard Rogers, the primary goal of a defendant who intentionally feigns intellectual impairment is to convince the examiner that his or her efforts to succeed on the test are sincere and that the impairment is genuine. This phenomenon is referred to as “effortful failure.” Tests such as the Bender-Gestalt and Wechsler scale can be “failed” intentionally with minimal effort.

Ultimately, the examiner must distinguish between limited effort and gross attempts at exaggeration and fabrication of cognitive ability. Rogers (2004) identifies the following as common reasons for suboptimal effort:

  • Decreased interest and effort as a result of genuine impairment.
  • Decreased interest and effort as a result of a co-occurring emotional or physical disorder such as depression or head trauma.
  • Expectations of failure.
  • Stress and preoccupation based on results of the evaluation.
  • Malingering.

The clinician must provide reasoning for his or her conclusion that suboptimal effort is due to any one of the above factors. Rogers recommends a thorough explanation of reasoning for suboptimal effort and proactivity in gathering data to explain this conclusion. He isolates two general domains of detection of malingering: 1) detection by excessive impairment (e.g., failures on extremely easy items and failures below levels of chance), and 2) detection by unexpected patterns (e.g. similar performance on very easy and very hard tasks and unexpected answers on forced choice formats).

VII. Relevant Oregon Statutes

Sections in bold indicate legal standards.

ORS 161.365. Procedure for determining issue of fitness to proceed.

(1) Whenever the court has reason to doubt the defendant’s fitness to proceed by reason of incapacity as defined in ORS 161.360, the court may call to its assistance in reaching its decision any witness who may help the court reach its decision and may appoint a psychiatrist or psychologist to examine the defendant and advise the court.

(2) If the court determines the assistance of a psychiatrist or psychologist would be helpful, the court may order the defendant to be committed to a state mental hospital designated by the Department of Human Services for the purpose of an examination for a period not exceeding 30 days. The report of each examination shall include, but is not necessarily limited to, the following:

(a) A description of the nature of the examination;

(b) A description of the mental condition of the defendant; and

(c) If the defendant suffers from a mental disease or defect, an opinion as to whether the defendant is incapacitated within the definition set out in ORS 161.360.

(3) Except where the defendant and the court both request to the contrary, the report shall not contain any findings or conclusions as to whether the defendant as a result of mental disease or defect was subject to the provisions of ORS 161.295 or 161.300 [GEI and Disordered Mental State] at the time of the criminal act charged.

(4) If the examination by the psychiatrist or psychologist cannot be conducted by reason of the unwillingness of the defendant to participate therein, the report shall so state and shall include, if possible, an opinion as to whether such unwillingness of the defendant was the result of mental disease or defect affecting capacity to proceed.

(5) The report of the examination shall be filed in triplicate with the clerk of the court, who shall cause copies to be delivered to the district attorney and to counsel for defendant.

(6) When upon motion of the court or a financially eligible defendant, the court has ordered a psychiatric or psychological examination of the defendant, a county or justice court shall order the county to pay, and a circuit court shall order the public defense services executive director to pay from funds available for that purpose:

(a) A reasonable fee if the examination of the defendant is conducted by a psychiatrist or psychologist in private practice; and

(b) All costs including transportation of the defendant if the examination is conducted by a psychiatrist or psychologist in the employ of the community mental health and developmental disabilities program established under ORS 430.610 to 430.670.

(7) When such an examination is ordered at the request or with the acquiescence of a defendant who is determined not to be financially eligible, the examination shall be performed at the defendant’s expense. When such an examination is ordered at the request of the prosecution, the county shall pay for the expense of the examination.

ORS 161.370. Determination of fitness; effect of finding of unfitness; proceedings if fitness regained; pretrial objections by defense counsel.

(1) When the defendant’s fitness to proceed is drawn in question, the issue shall be determined by the court. If neither the prosecuting attorney nor counsel for the defendant contests the finding of the report filed by a psychiatrist or psychologist under ORS 161.365, the court may make the determination on the basis of such report. If the finding is contested, the court shall hold a hearing on the issue. If the report is received in evidence upon such hearing, the party who contests the finding thereof shall have the right to summon and to cross-examine any psychiatrist or psychologist who submitted the report and to offer evidence upon the issue. Other evidence regarding the defendant’s fitness to proceed may be introduced by either party.

(2) If the court determines that the defendant lacks fitness to proceed, the proceeding against the defendant shall be suspended, except as provided in subsection (12) of this section, and the court shall commit the defendant to the custody of the superintendent of a state mental hospital designated by the Department of Human Services or shall release the defendant on supervision for so long as such unfitness shall endure. The court may release the defendant on supervision if it determines that care other than commitment for incapacity to stand trial would better serve the defendant and the community. It may place conditions which it deems appropriate on the release, including the requirement that the defendant regularly report to the Department of Human Services or a community mental health and developmental disabilities program for examination to determine if the defendant has regained capacity to stand trial. When the court, on its own motion or upon the application of the superintendent of the hospital in which the defendant is committed, a person examining the defendant as a condition of release on supervision, or either party, determines, after a hearing, if a hearing is requested, that the defendant has regained fitness to proceed, the proceeding shall be resumed. If, however, the court is of the view that so much time has elapsed since the commitment or release of the defendant on supervision that it would be unjust to resume the criminal proceeding, the court on motion of either party may dismiss the charge and may order the defendant to be discharged or cause a proceeding to be commenced forthwith under ORS 426.070 to 426.170 or 427.235 to 427.290.[civil commitment for mentally ill persons and mentally retarded or developmentally disabled persons]

(3) The superintendent shall cause the defendant to be evaluated within 60 days from the defendant’s delivery into the superintendent’s custody, for the purpose of determining whether there is a substantial probability that, in the foreseeable future, the defendant will have the capacity to stand trial.

(4) In addition, the superintendent shall:

(a) Immediately notify the committing court if the defendant, at any time, gains or regains the capacity to stand trial or will never have the capacity to stand trial.

(b) Within 90 days of the defendant’s delivery into the superintendent’s custody, notify the committing court that:

(A) The defendant has the present capacity to stand trial;

(B) There is no substantial probability that, in the foreseeable future, the defendant will gain or regain the capacity to stand trial; or

(C) There is a substantial probability that, in the foreseeable future, the defendant will gain or regain the capacity to stand trial. If such a probability exists, the superintendent shall give the court an estimate of the time in which the defendant, with appropriate treatment, is expected to gain or regain capacity.

(5) If the superintendent determines that there is a substantial probability that, in the foreseeable future, the defendant will gain or regain the capacity to stand trial, unless the court otherwise orders, the defendant shall remain in the superintendent’s custody where the defendant shall receive treatment designed for the purpose of enabling the defendant to gain or regain capacity. In keeping with the notice requirement under subsection (4)(b) of this section, the superintendent shall, for the duration of the defendant’s period of commitment, submit a progress report to the committing court concerning the defendant’s capacity or incapacity, at least every 180 days as measured from the date of the defendant’s delivery into the superintendent’s custody.

(6) A defendant who remains committed under subsection (5) of this section shall be discharged within a period of time that is reasonable for making a determination concerning whether or not, and when, the defendant may gain or regain capacity. However, regardless of the number of charges with which the defendant is accused, in no event shall the defendant be committed for longer than whichever of the following, measure from the defendant’s initial custody date is shorter:

(a) Three years; or

(b) A period of time equal to the maximum sentence the court could have imposed if the defendant had been convicted.

(7) The superintendent shall notify the committing court of the defendant’s impending discharge 30 days before the date on which the superintendent is required to discharge the defendant under subsection (6) of this section.

(8) When the committing court receives a notice from the superintendent under either subsection (4) or (7) of this section concerning the defendant’s progress or lack thereof, the committing court shall determine after a hearing, if a hearing is requested, whether the defendant presently has the capacity to stand trial.

(9) If under subsection (8) of this section the court determines that the defendant lacks the capacity to stand trial, the court shall further determinewhether there is a substantial probability that the defendant, in the foreseeable future, will gain or regain the capacity to stand trial and whether defendant is entitled to discharge under subsection (6) of this section, the court shall dismiss, without prejudice, all charges against the defendant and:

(a) Order that the defendant be discharged; or

(b) Initiate commitment proceedings under ORS 426.070 or 427.235 to 427.290.

(10) All notices required under this section shall be filed with the clerk of the court and delivered to both the district attorney and the counsel for the defendant.

(11) If the defendant regains fitness to proceed, the term of any sentence received by the defendant for conviction of the crime charged shall be reduced by the amount of time the defendant was committed under this section to the custody of a state mental hospital designated by the Department of Human Services.

(12) The fact that the defendant is unfit to proceed does not preclude any objection through counsel and without the personal participation of the defendant on the grounds that the indictment is insufficient, that the statute of limitations has run, that double jeopardy principles apply or upon any other ground at the discretion of the court which the court deems susceptible of fair determination prior to trial.

(13) As used in this section, “superintendent” means the superintendent of the state mental hospital of the Department of Human Services to which the defendant has been committed.
