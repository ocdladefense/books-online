Crossing Mental Health Experts'

This chapter will provide numerous helpful examples of cross-examining mental health experts in a variety of situations, but before turning to those suggestions and tips on preparing your cross, it is important to know and understand terms used in clinical research and to thoroughly “investigate” the mental health expert and the clinical assessment protocols and research used with your client.

Prerequisite Knowledge

I. Research Terms

It’s important to know a fewbasic research terms like validity and reliability.

Q: Is the test/measure/study trustworthy? There are two primary ways to answer.

1. Reliability – Is the test repeatable? If you step onto a bathroom scale five times in a row and get wildly different results, the scale is an unreliable measure of weight. If five different psychologists do the same test on the same person and get different results, then the test is not reliable. Sometimes this is called “inter-rater reliability.”

2. Validity– Is the test accurate? If you step onto a bathroom scale 20 times and each time it tells you that you weigh 500 pounds, you can be confident that the scale has low validity (assuming you do not actually weigh 500 pounds) but high consistency. If you use a good bathroom scale to measure IQ, you will have high reliability but very low validity. That is, it will be consistent but wrong. Technically this is called predictive or concurrent validity. There are other kinds of validity like content validity, construct validity, convergent and discriminant validity and criterion validity. Don’t worry about them. Using them in cross, even if you worked to understand them, would leave the judge behind. The idea here is just to be able to spot the issue when the test has not been proven to accurately measure what it says it measures.

Some reasons a test may have low validity in your case are 1) if it has little to no research showing that it measures what it says it measures, 2) if the research was done on people who are very different than your client, or 3) if the test has been validated but not for what the expert is using it for. Remember, these are just some of the possible scenarios where the test in your case may have low validity.

II. Diagnostic Terms

It’s important to know a few diagnostic terms like the multiaxial assessment, “provisional,” “rule out,” and NOS.

The primary manual for diagnosing mental disorders used in the United States is the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Disorders(DSM-IV-TR). It proposes an assessment along five axes:

Axis I: Clinical Disorders and Other Conditions

Axis II: Personality Disorders and Mental Retardation

Axis III: Medical Conditions

Axis IV: Personal Stressors

Axis V: A 1 to 100 score of how a person is doing right now.

Axis I – includes almost everything one normally thinks of as a mental disorder, including but not limited to psychotic disorders like schizophrenia, mood disorders like depression, cognitive disorders like dementia, substance-related disorders like addiction, sexual disorders like paraphilia, childhood disorders, anxiety disorders, eating disorders, dissociative disorders and impulse control disorders.

Axis II – The only mental disorders or cognitive deficits not coded on Axis I are personality disorders and mental retardation, which make up the entirety of Axis II. When the DSM first developed, personality disorders and retardation were treated differently because it was thought that they were permanent, unchanging and untreatable. But now the DSM-IV-TR warns specifically against assuming anything about Axis II.

The listing of personality disorders and mental retardation on a separate axis ensures that consideration will be given to the possible presence of personality disorders and mental retardation that may otherwise be overlooked when attention is directed to the usually more florid Axis I disorders. The coding of personality disorders on Axis II should not be taken to imply that their pathogenesis or range of treatment is fundamentally different from that for the Axis I disorders. DSM-IV-TR, p. 28.

Axis III – is used for medical conditions like thyroid disorders, cancer or diabetes. It’s important to think about medical conditions because they can have a profound effect on a person’s mental health, as a psychological reaction to the medical issue. Or the medical issue might be the physiological cause of the mental disorder. When the latter is true, the medical condition should be coded on both Axis I and Axis II. It used to be that the medical and psychological (the body and mind) were considered to be very separate arenas. But as with Axis II, the DSM-IV-TR warns against reading anything into the separate coding:

“The multiaxial distinction among Axis I, Axis II, and Axis III disorders does not imply that there are fundamental differences in their conceptualization, that mental disorders are unrelated to physical or biological factors or processes, or that general medical conditions are unrelated to behavioral or psychosocial factors or processes. The purpose of distinguishing general medical conditions is to encourage thoroughness in evaluation and to enhance communication among health care providers.” DSM-IV-TR p. 29.

Axis IV – is for personal stressors, or in DSM language “psychosocial and environmental problems.” A death in the family, poverty, illiteracy, homelessness, legal problems and job loss are all personal stressors that might exacerbate or even trigger a mental disorder.

Axis V –is a score of functioning where at 91 to 100, “Life’s problems never seem to get out of hand, [the individual] is sought out by others because of his or her many positive qualities. No symptoms.” From 1 to 10, the person is a “persistent danger of severely hurting self or others OR persistent inability to maintain minimum personal hygiene OR serious suicidal act with clear expectation of death.” The score is called a Global Assessment of Functioning or GAF. The GAF is little more than a snapshot of how the person is doing at the moment of the evaluation.

Within the two axes for mental disorders, there are multiple terms for hedging or qualifying the diagnosis:

“Provisional” means that the mental health expert is pretty sure the diagnosis is correct but there’s not enough evidence to meet the criteria. For example, “Axis I – Schizophrenia, provisional,” usually means that the symptoms are present but the mental health expert doesn’t have much information about the history of the disorder. It has been my experience in the legal arena that “provisional” is rarely used even when the mental health expert lacks information.

“Rule out” is used for disorders which are possible alternatives to the primary diagnosis. For example, “Axis I – Schizophrenia. Rule out Bipolar Disorder.”

“NOS” stands for Not Otherwise Specified. NOS is used either when there is an unusual pattern of symptoms that fall into a general class of disorders but don’t meet the criteria for any particular disorder; or when there isn’t enough information to diagnose anything beyond the class of disorder. For example, if the person clearly has psychotic symptoms but the diagnosis is being made in the ER with little time to know much beyond the psychosis, the proper diagnosis is “Axis I – psychosis NOS.”

The DSM-IV-TR contains multiple sections within each diagnosis.

Diagnostic Features – these are the criteria or “elements” of the diagnosis. Sometimes the criteria are listed under “Features.”

Associated Features and Disorders – includes clinical features often associated with the disorder but not considered essential to the diagnosis; other disorders associated with the disorder being discussed; and associated lab, physical exam and medical findings.

Differential Diagnosis– discusses how to differentiate this disorder from other disorders.

Specifiers/Subtypes– once a general diagnosis is made, the specifiers are a sort of subtype of the disorder, provide more information about a person’s condition and which treatments may be more effective, and aid the prediction of the course of the disorder. An example would be Schizophrenia – Paranoid type.

Specific Age, Culture and Gender features – whether the disorder is attributable to, or prevalent because of, these factors.

Course– describes the disorder’s duration and how it is expected to change.

Prevalence– lifetime risk to general population, recurrence, and incidence by setting.

Familial Pattern– whether the disorder more likely occurs in relatives of those with the disorder compared to the general population.

It is important to know the elements or “diagnostic criteria” for the diagnosis. But you also want to know how to differentiate other diagnoses with overlapping or similar criteria (differential diagnosis). You want to know how the disorder is expected to progress (course), how common it is (prevalence), whether it’s inherited (familial pattern), and what the criteria mean (diagnostic features). This seems obvious, however, my experience has been that most attorneys just skip to the diagnostic criteria like they are elements of a legal statute and the rest is just fluff. Think of the course, prevalence, familial pattern, and associated features as legislative history and interpretive case law and they may seem more relevant as to whether the diagnostic criteria are sufficient.

Note: Many different combinations of symptoms will get you to a particular diagnosis. For example, a diagnosis of Borderline Personality Disorder requires that a person meet five of nine criteria. Where there are nine variables and five required, there are exactly 15,120 different combinations of symptoms that could get you to that diagnosis. That’s without taking into account the general criteria for a personality disorder which must be found before moving on to the specific disorder. So when an expert testifies to “Borderline features,” don’t think of it as “Borderline lite.” Rather, think of it as a failure to meet any of the 15,000 combinations that could have resulted in an actual diagnosis, i.e. the person had one or some, but not the requisite five, of the nine features of Borderline Personality Disorder.

Finally, regarding diagnosis, it’s always important to remember that the science of psychiatry and psychology is in a very early stage of its existence. That’s why with each subsequent edition the DSM significantly changes. Diagnosis is much more a matter of art or guesswork than actual science. There was a point in the development of traditional medical knowledge where diagnosis was defined by clusters of symptoms rather than identifying underlying causes of the illness. That point was centuries ago. Now we’re able to test and verify. Symptoms are for issue spotting. Unfortunately, what this means for the mental health field is that the validity of the DSM is unclear and probably quite low since there is no way to test whether a diagnosis is accurate or not. One can’t even look toward medications because 1) psychiatric medications alleviate symptoms - they don’t go to the root cause of disorders, and 2) medications are also much more of an art than a science in that we know little about how they work and why they work for some people and not other similarly situated people.

III. Testing Terms

It’s important to know a few testing terms like cutoff score, standard error of measurement and base rates.

Any objective psychological test will produce a numerical score that provides information about the person. But in order for the score to mean something there has to be a cutoff. Above the cutoff, the score is clinically significant. Below the cutoff, the score is insignificant. No cutoff should be treated as a magic line. It may be that the expert has adjusted the cutoff. It may be that while plenty of studies validate folks with extreme scores, there is little evidence regarding folks near the cutoff. Note also that all tests and validations contain errors of measurement. So it may be that a person has a score which puts them just above the cutoff but it falls within a standard degree of error from being a clinically insignificant score.

Base Rates – The rate of positive hits tells you nothing about the accuracy of a test. You need to know the rate of false positives. In order to know the rate of false positives you need to know the base population and the overall rate for that population. [Also see the discussion in Chapter 6, “Dangerousness.”]

For example, Veronica Victim fits profile X of someone who has been abused. Eighty percent of those who fit the profile have been sexually abused. What is the probability that Veronica has been abused?

The answer to the Veronica question is that there isn’t enough information. But the answer is almost assuredly not eighty percent. We need to know how many children there are in the general population and how many have been abused. Assume there are fifty million children in the United States and the rate of child sex abuse is five percent. That means that of the fifty million children, two and a half million have been abused.

Now suppose you gave the profile X test to every child in the nation. You would get eighty percent of the abused children. Eight percent of two and a half million is two million. So there would be two million accurate or “true positive” hits. But if the test is accurate eighty percent of the time, that means it’s wrong twenty percent of the time. The problem is that it’s wrong on a much larger population. Twenty percent of the forty-seven and a half million nonabused kids is nine and a half million.

To figure out the probability that Veronica is one of the true positives (as opposed to a false positive), you take the number of true positives and divide by the total number of positives. Two million (true positives) divided by eleven and a half million (2 million true positives + 9.5 million false positives) = .17. That is, there’s only a 17 percent chance that Veronica has actually been abused, even assuming the test is exactly as accurate as it claims to be.

One more example of base rates to further understanding: Suppose I have a facial recognition camera that picks out psychopaths and I, as an employer, am going to parade each of my one thousand employees in front of this camera. Suppose also that the camera is accurate ninety percent of the time and the rate of psychopaths in the general community is one percent. Assuming my company fits with the average, there will be ten psychopaths among my one thousand employees. When I parade my one thousand employees in front of my camera it will pick up nine out of the ten psychopaths. Which is good. But it will also be wrong ten percent of the time. Which means that a hundred of the one thousand employees will be identified as psychopaths who aren’t. To figure out the probability that one of those people identified as a psychopath is actually a psychopath, you divide the true positives (nine) by the total positives (one hundred nine), resulting in a probability of .082 or eight percent. Eight percent actual accuracy compared to the advertised accuracy of ninety percent makes it a complete scam of a product.

IV. Human Thinking Errors

It’s important to know some of the basic human thinking errors, applicable to experts and other humans alike.

Confirmation bias, the most important one for our purposes, means that people unconsciously prefer information that fits with the story they have in their head. This leads people to collect, interpret and remember information in a biased way. So let’s say, for example, that an experienced psychologist forms the hypothesis that people with personality disorders are fundamentally bad parents. As a result of that hypothesis, the psychologist will likely filter out those patients that contradict the hypothesis, interpret other diagnoses so that they become consistent with the hypothesis, and subsequently recall primarily those patients that best fulfill the hypothesis. This is why investigators in legitimate scientific studies go to great lengths to construct double blinds, set up control groups and meticulously record by objective measures the results.

Pretrial Investigation

1. Talk to your expert about the state’s evaluation.Use your expert’s knowledge and experience.

2. Request testing datafrom the mental health expert you’re going to cross. Then double-check the results. Never, never, never take someone’s word that a test has been scored correctly. Whether it’s a P.O. doing an actuarial risk assessment or a forensic psychologist doing personality testing, you’ll be shocked how often the scores are simply wrong. You’ll also be surprised how subjective the scores are as well. Natural bias prompts experts of all kinds to unconsciously fudge the scoring in the direction that benefits the party that hired them.

3. Watch out for computer-based test scoring.An evaluation based on a computer-scored MMPI, for example, is like having a cop testify about blood testing done by the state lab without bringing in someone from the state lab. The MMPI corporation has algorithms and methods for calculating these scores. We know little about the accuracy, the results, the meaning or even whether these are the right scores. Computer-based scoring generates standardized reports which are often contradictory to the evaluator’s report. The algorithms and formulas on which the scoring is produced is proprietary information unknown to either the mental health expert or the court. The mental health expert is often not an expert in the conclusions generated by the computer. And, the computer-generated report is unable to take into account aspects of the individual that make the symptoms or descriptions mean something.

4. Get a feel for what it’s like to take the teststhat were done by the state’s expert. What’s an example question? How many questions are there? How difficult or subjective is it? It’s like going to the scene of the crime in a criminal case. It doesn’t seem like it will make that much difference until you’re there and you realize that you pictured everything quite differently. Once you’re able to picture things appropriately and you understand the details, you will notice questions and inconsistencies that you previously would have brushed aside. Take the MMPI, for example. It is the most commonly used personality test with voluminous research backing it up. Before I knew more, I assumed a lot of involvement by the psychologist administering the test. This is incorrect. The MMPI-II is a 567-question true/false test in which you sit down with the book of questions and answer them on a bubble sheet in order. If you sustain a clip of six questions a minute, then you can finish the test in a little less than two hours. As an example, here are questions 1 through 10 of the MMPI-II:

1. I like mechanics magazines.

2. I have a good appetite.

3. I wake up fresh and rested most mornings.

4. I think I would like the work of a librarian.

5. I am easily awakened by noise.

6. I like to read newspaper articles on crime.

7. My hands and feet are usually warm enough.

8. My daily life is full of things that keep me interested.

9. I am about as able to work as I ever was.

10. There seems to be a lump in my throat much of the time.

5. Pull the psych research on the tests that were performed by the mental health expert.

6. Pull the psych research on the conclusions made by the mental health expert.

7. Find out everything you can about the mental health expert.Request the expert’s curriculum vitae or resume. Google the expert. Pull prior publications, if any. If you see the same experts over and over, consider pulling transcripts of their testimony. Pull their disciplinary history, etc. Sometimes you’ll find a golden nugget. But if nothing else, you demonstrate to both the expert and the judge, right at the beginning of your cross, that you know everything about them. It’s impressive and, for the witness, disconcerting.

8. Understand the cultural and linguistic limitationsof the tests, studies or personal experience of the expert. Stated in the inverse, ask whether it would be more appropriate for the client to have culturally specific tests, studies, or evaluators.

9. Read the full DSM description for each of the proposed diagnoses.

10. Look at whether there is convergence in the data.Think about whether the various tests all point in the same direction. Do they agree with the diagnosis? Do they agree with the observed data from police reports, interviews and evaluations? Does everything culminate into an obvious conclusion? Or, alternatively, do the tests point in different directions? Is there mixed support from collateral sources or a diagnosis that doesn’t quite match? Knock out a single pillar and sometimes the whole structure collapses.

11. Have the appropriate ethics codes with you. These can be useful for impeachment. There are general ones and ones that are specific to different professions that might be relevant to the conduct of a mental health expert. The two most obvious are those that apply to psychologists:

“Specialty Guidelines for Forensic Psychologists” is not binding but it is a useful ethical code for those psychologists who perform work in the court system. It can be found online at the American Psychology–Law Society, a division of the APA (www.ap-ls.org). See Chapter 12 for Assessment.

“Ethical Principles of Psychologists” is a code of conduct imposing certain requirements upon its members, and can be found at www.apa.org/ethics. See Standard 9 for Assessment.

Preparing Your Cross

I. Know Your Point of Attack

There are three steps in the mental health expert’s evaluative process: data gathering, conclusions and opinion. For example, a psychologist’s data (interviews, testing and collateral documents) might lead her to a conclusion that Father is impulsive and antisocial. The conclusion of antisocial impulsivity might lead her to an opinion that Father’s parenting skills will never improve and that his presence is harmful to the child’s development. Note that there are five possible points of attack (three steps and two links):

  • Problems with or deficits in the data.
  • The data does not support the conclusions.
  • Regardless of evidentiary support, the conclusions are inherently problematic.
  • The conclusions do not support the opinion.
  • There is something inherently problematic with the opinion.

For example, the data may be deficientif the interview were only 10 minutes long, or if the mental health expert didn’t review significant reports or medical documentation, or if the only testing was inappropriate for the client.

The link between the data and the conclusions may be deficientif the testing has never been validated for the conclusions the mental health expert is making, or if there is no convergence in the data in that it seems to point in different directions, or if the mental health expert is downplaying some data points while favoring, or even exaggerating, the data points that support the conclusion.

The conclusions may be deficientif they are faux diagnoses like “antisocial” detached from the DSM definitions, if the conclusions rely on generalizations or lay definitions, or if the degree of certainty expressed is completely unwarranted by the literature on the subject.

The link between the conclusions and the opinion may be weak where there are no studies or evidence to support the link. Who says that impulsive, antisocial people can’t be great parents? One can certainly imagine a person who is callous toward those he doesn’t know but protective and loving toward his own family. In fact, we all have that basic propensity toward greater empathy as familiarity and closeness increases. The link between conclusion and opinion is often scientific evidence and cannot rely on instincts or presumptions about human behavior. There must be a showing of reliability.

II. Decide Who You Want Your Expert To Be

Is he Newbie Guy making a mistake? Is she Old Expert simply fitting your client into a premade mold? Is he a nice guy and a good evaluator who simply made a mistake in this case? Is she lazy, inexperienced, burned out, out of her league? These decisions must be made before the cross begins so that you are consistent in the story you tell in cross and the way it fits into your larger narrative.

III. Default Method for Cross

Your default method for cross should be short, tight statements introduced by a signpost. The point of the expert is to say “Yes” over and over. If the expert goes off on a tangent or gives a longer answer, simply ask the same question again. If you can’t easily recall your last question verbatim, that’s an excellent sign that your questions are too long or convoluted. And long, convoluted questions will result in a long, convoluted cross.

The goal of cross, like the rest of the trial, is to persuade. Persuade the judge that your theory of the case is the correct one and that you are on the side of justice. The only way to truly persuade is to tell a story and look good doing it.

''The MacCarthy System1: Short + Statements = Control. Control = you looking good.

1. Eliminate prefixes (like, “Isn’t it a fact…”) and suffixes (like, “…isn’t that correct?)

2. Use introductory transitions (like, “I want to ask you some questions about what you saw when you left Murphy’s Bar about three o’clock in the afternoon on May 8th, you understand?”). These aren’t chapters. They’re a way to increase the clarity of your story.

3. Don’t ask questions, make the shortest possible statements (like, “You saw a car. It was green. You knew the driver. The driver was Bob Bobbins”).

Other points about the MacCarthy method:

  • Cross is not a dialogue. There is only one narrator: You. You’re telling the story. The witness is there to nod.
  • It is important to elicit Yes Mode—a trance like repetition of yes, yes, yes, yes, yes, so they stop thinking very hard and you look like you know everything.
  • Don’t succumb to Perry Mason syndrome (“Oh, Counsel, I can’t live this lie any longer—your client didn’t commit this crime, that guy did.”) Cross is won on points, not with a knockout.
  • Looking good is the most important goal of cross. Ordinary people listen with their eyes and think with their emotions. They see who is “winning” and, ultimately, that matters more than anything. But note that if you’re a jerk, you’re losing.
  • Start strong and end strong. It matters just as much here as in closing. 
  • You must tweak a witness that isn’t giving you a Yes or No. Simply repeat the question.

Example of a MacCarthy Cross:

“I’m going to ask you some questions about how much time you spent with Mr. Client, you understand?

—You saw Mr. Client in April of this year.

—On the 24th.

—You went to the jail.

—You met with him in a locked room in the jail.

—You spent two hours with him in the jail.

—You also saw Mr. Client in May of this year.

—On the 14th.

—You came to my office.

—You met with him in one of my conference rooms.

—You spent about thirty minutes with him.

—Those are the only times you met with Mr. Client.

—You haven’t spoken with him except on those two occasions.”

IV. Examples of Introductory Transitions

Begin each transition with, “I’m going to ask you about…” and finish with, “You understand?”

Background Transitions:

  • Professional Qualifications – I’m going to ask you some questions about your experience administering the MMPI, you understand?
  • Research – I’m going to ask you some questions about your knowledge of the research supporting the validity of the MMPI, you understand?
  • Evaluation – I’m going to ask you some questions about how you gathered data for this psychological evaluation, you understand?
  • Lack of Testing – I’m going to ask you some questions about the objective psychological tests you administered, you understand?
  • Complete and Truthful Report Impeachment – I’m going to ask you some questions about the completeness of your report, you understand?
  • Scientific Evidence – I’m going to ask you some questions about whether conclusions about parenting ability are accepted science, you understand?
  • The DSM – I’m going to ask you some questions about differential diagnoses for Borderline Personality Disorder, you understand?

Logical and Scientific Errors:

  • Confidence Level/Standard Error of Measurement – I’m going to ask you some questions about the standard error of measurement for the PRI, you understand?
  • Experiential Conclusions – I’m going to ask you some questions about your study of Borderline Personality and bad parenting, you understand?
  • Base Rates/Samples – I’m going to ask you some questions about the base rate for bad parenting, you understand?
  • Double-checking the Testing/Response Styles – I’m going to ask you some questions about your scoring of the MMPI, you understand?
  • The Mouse Trap: Science vs. Law – I’m going to ask you some questions about the scientific method, you understand?
  • Diagnosis – I’m going to ask you some questions about the DSM-IV definition of Schizoaffective Disorder, you understand?
  • Reliability and Validity – I’m going to ask you some questions about the validity of the PRI for predicting parenting ability, you understand?
  • Judgment Biases – I’m going to ask you some questions about the phenomenon of confirmatory bias, you understand?

The Client:

  • Lack of Convergence – I’m going to ask you some questions about whether the data you gathered all points in the same direction, you understand?
  • Facts/Tests -> Diagnosis – I’m going to ask you some questions about the facts supporting your diagnosis of Depression, you understand?
  • Diagnosis -> Conclusion – I’m going to ask you some questions about your conclusion that Borderline Personality Disorder causes, you understand?
  • Positive Parenting – I’m going to ask you some questions about the positive aspects of my client’s parenting, you understand?

V. Looping

Use looping to emphasize information favorable to your case. Execute this trick by simply repeating a word or phrase from the previous question that you want to highlight. For example:

“You reviewed some rights with Mr. Client.

—You did that prior to beginning your interview with Mr. Client.

—These rights are printed out on a standardized form.

—You review them before every interview.

—With every new client.

—They’re important rights.

—Like the lack of confidentiality.

—They set the boundaries of the psychologist-patient relationship.

—In fact, you’re ethically required to review these rights.

—When you reviewed these rights with Mr. Client, he told you he didn’t understand.

—He didn’t understand what confidentiality meant.

—Because he didn’t understand confidentiality, you talked about it with him.”

VI.Challenge Testimony

Be prepared to challenge testimony as not meeting evidentiary requirements for scientific evidence.

In criminal cases this is usually a fairly weak argument. But for dependency cases it may be an excellent argument. There are almost no studies linking any sort of behavior, personality profile or diagnosis to bad parenting. Or, for that matter, to good parenting. So the expert’s testimony will have the sound of scientific validity without any science at all to back it up—the very definition of unreliable junk science. Usually, what scientific evidence is going to come in has been decided in limine at a 104 hearing, but if something new comes up, make the usual “Scientific Evidence Lacking Foundation” objection.

Summary of Admissibility of Scientific Evidence in Oregon

A.State v. Brown,297 Or 404 (1984)

In 1984, the Oregon Supreme Court decided in State v. Brown that the admissibility of scientific evidence should be analyzed under traditional evidence law as it is codified in the Oregon Evidence Code.297 Or. 404. Browndescribed scientific evidence as drawing its convincing force from some principle of science (or mathematics). “Typically, but not necessarily, scientific evidence is presented by an expert witness who can explain data or test results and, if necessary, explain the scientific principles which are said to give the evidence its reliability or accuracy.” Brownlooked at other available admissibility tests—Kersting, Frye, and Relevancy—and rejected them in favor of weighing the probity of the evidence against the possible prejudicial effect of the evidence.

The Kersting test, developed by the Oregon Court of Appeals in 1981, allows scientific evidence to be admitted if it is reasonably reliable. The idea behind the standard is that there needs to be credible evidence on which the judge can make an initial determination that the scientific technique is reasonably reliable for admission, and the jury can then decide what weight to give it. The Brown court rejected the reasonably reliable test because the analysis focuses on the expert testimony supporting the evidence, not the evidence itself.

The Frye admissibility standard requires that the scientific technique have obtained general acceptance within the relevant scientific community. The Browncourt rejected this standard as too restrictive and ambiguous. The ambiguity lies in determining at what point “general acceptance” occurs as a cutoff point. Courts have a hard time applying the standard with more precision than requiring more than the expert’s own acceptance but less than every expert in the field’s acceptance.

Finally, the Relevancy test treats scientific evidence more or less the same as other evidence—by weighing its probative value against countervailing dangers and considerations. Brown focused on critiques of the Relevancy test’s lack of definiteness. The court recognized that relevancy is part of a larger determination of admissibility, but the test needed an additional screening function similar to the one employed in the Fryetest.

Ultimately, Brown focused on the OEC rules and simply added some structure to an analysis under them. To add the structure lacking in the Frye and Relevancy tests, the court looked at seven factors to define relevancy and determine helpfulness under the OEC. The seven factors are not exclusive, and the court emphasized that most important is the process of examining the evidence’s probative value, not lockstep affirmation of each factor. The factors listed were:

1) The technique’s general acceptance in the field

2) The expert’s qualifications and stature

3) The use which has been made of the technique (frequency of use)

4) The potential rate of error

5) The existence of specialized literature

6) The novelty of the invention

7) The extent to which the technique relies on the subjective interpretation of the expert.

These factors are neither dispositive nor exhaustive, and the court mentioned other possible factors in the opinion’s footnotes.

B.State v. O’Key, 321 Or 285 (1995)

In 1995 the Oregon Supreme Court applied the Brown analysis to determine whether a field sobriety test (the horizontal gaze nystagmus, or HGN test) was (1) based on scientifically valid principles, (2) pertinent to the issue (a DUII), and (3) not unfairly prejudicial. O’Key reasoned that because expert scientific testimony has an unusually high degree of persuasive appeal to jurors, the scientific assertions must be supported by appropriate scientific validation. The empirical factors of validity the court considered were a combination of both the Brownfactors and factors from the 1993 SCOTUS case Daubert. 509 US 579. The Brown factors applied were the expert’s qualifications, the use that has been made of the test or technique, the novelty of the technique, and the extent to which the test relies on the subjective interpretation of the officer administering the test. Factors which had been employed by both Daubert and Brown were peer review and publication (the existence of specialized literature), the known or potential rate of error, and the degree of the technique’s acceptance in the relevant scientific community. The testability or falsifiability of the technique in question was taken from the Daubertanalysis.Additionally, the presence of safeguards in the test, the existence and maintenance of standards governing the use of the HGN test, and the extent to which other courts admit HGN test evidence were considered in the court’s validity assessment.

After determining that the test was valid to show the person was under the influence of intoxicants but not for a particular blood alcohol content, the court applied the OEC 401 “Relevant” and OEC 702 “Helpful” standards to the evidence. Evidence is relevantif it would tend to make more or less probable the existence of a consequential fact. The evidence is helpfulif it is pertinent to the issue to which it is directed and will assist the jury in factfinding. Even where the scientific evidence is found to be sufficiently reliable, pertinent to the issue in question, and will assist the trier of fact it can be inadmissible if it is unfairly prejudicial. O’Key emphasized the importance of OEC 403 in retaining the essential function of jury as factfinder in the face of scientific evidence. If the jury is likely to accept the evidence because of an air of scientific infallibility, then OEC 403 could prevent its introduction as an improper basis for decision. The rule states that evidence should not be admitted if the prejudicial effect of its introduction “substantially outweighs” its probative value. The “substantially outweighs” phrasing in OEC 403, in effect, places the burden on the party seeking exclusion of the evidence. Because the court found that the HGN test was not the kind of evidence that would cause the jury to accept its conclusions without question, nor would its introduction unduly delay the trial, it was admissible. Admissibility was subject to a foundational showing: that the officer who administered the test was properly qualified, the test was administered properly, and the test results were recorded accurately.

C. Subsequent Case Law

Federal case law has expanded the Daubert factors to apply not only to scientific evidence, but also to “technical” or “specialized” testimony more generally. Kumho,526 US 137 (1999). In Oregon however, Brown and O’Key apply only to evidence that draws its persuasive force from the principles of science. Testimony that Oregon courts have found specifically requires a Brownfoundation include the characteristics of sex abuse victims, the characteristics of sex abusers, and “syndromes,” generally (including Battered Woman Syndrome). The proponent of the scientific evidence has the burden of establishing its validity. State v. Perry, 347 Or 110 (2009). Both Oregon and the federal system have specifically held that the Brown and Daubert factors are not exhaustive and should not be mechanically applied, and that the inquiry itself is flexible. The point of the inquiry is to make sure the scientific evidence is reliable so that its persuasive appeal is legitimate. Marcum, 345 Or 237 (2008). Additional factors for the court’s consideration are analogy to other scientific techniques whose results are admissible, the nature and breadth of the inference adduced, the clarity and simplicity with which the technique can be described and its results explained, the extent to which the basic data are verifiable by the court and jury, and the availability of other experts to test and evaluate the technique. Id.

Southard, an Oregon Supreme Court decision in 2009, broke with convention and held that the probative value of an expert’s diagnosis of a child as “sexually abused” was outweighed by the risk of prejudice. 347 Or 127. Whereas most medical diagnoses give jurors information beyond their common experience, the court reasoned that a diagnosis of sex abuse is more like a credibility determination that jurors make for themselves. The diagnosis had met the standard of validity and relevancy, but was nonetheless excluded because it “did not tell the jury anything that it was not equally capable of determining on its own” but had a substantial risk of overly impressing the jury. Id. at 140–141.

A series of Oregon appellate cases followed on Southard’s heels, heightening the requirements for admissibility determinations. For a diagnosis to be admissible there must be some physical evidence which supports that diagnosis. The cases are probably most useful for sex abuse cases or dependency cases. The first two held that a medical expert’s diagnosis related to sex abuse (or even an opinion which sounds to a jury like a diagnosis) requires some confirming physical evidence. Lovern, 234 Or App 502 (2010); Merrimon, 234 Or App 515 (2010). In the third case, a pediatric nurse practitioner from CARES gave a diagnosis of sex abuse “to a reasonable medical certainty,” despite the complete absence of physical evidence, and this warranted reversal of conviction. State v. Clay, 235 Or App 26 (2010). A trial judge subsequently ruled that a CARES diagnosis was not scientific evidence and did not require an in limine review. The Court of Appeals found that it was reversible error not to hold a hearing in limine to determine whether the evidence was scientific and reliable, and, separately, that the judge should have held a hearing to decide whether the evidence was more prejudicial than probative. State v. Evans, 2010 Or App LEXIS 906. Evans is useful when making an objection to the introduction of scientific evidence which wasn’t reviewed in a 104 hearing.

Additionally, the Oregon Court of Appeals decided that the vertical gaze nystagmus (VGN) test was not valid scientifically based only on the Oregon Supreme Court’s endorsement of the HGN test in Brown and the introduction of a Department of Transportation manual which asserted that “field experience” had shown the VGN to be reliable. State v. Bevan, 235 Or App 533 (2010).

VII. Don’t Assume Specifics

Don’t assume specifics when a mental health expert uses a psychological or diagnostic term. A word like “impulsive” means almost nothing without details about the degree of impulsivity, the context in which it arises, the direction it leads the person and examples of how it has played out in the past. Everybody is impulsive to some extent. We assume that because a psychologist is talking about it, it must be clinically significant, and that, in turn, leads us to assume the sort of uncontrollable impulsivity we’ve seen in different cases in the past. But, first, that may not be the case at all. And, second, the expert may not have any justification for such a symptom.

Similarly, one should not assume from an expert’s conclusion or statement of fact that the expert has personal knowledge or verifiable information supporting the conclusion or statement. Often the expert will simply assume the truth of information contained in collateral information like chart notes or past reports. A single meritless statement in a decades-old report can create a sort of snowball effect that shades all the information that comes after it. It is important to press the expert where it’s clear that a statement or conclusion is, in part or whole, based on information he or she is unable to verify.

VIII. Causation

Much of the faulty logic from experts in court comes down to mistaken conclusions of causation. Here are the four classic logical errors related to causation:

A. Missing Data

To say that A causes B requires knowing four data points:

  • How often the existence of A precedes B.
  • How often A occurs without B.
  • How often B occurs without A
  • How often neither B nor A occur.

Missing any of these data points will put a conclusion on shaky ground no matter how good it sounds. For example, it has been said that marijuana is a gateway drug for heroin. That is, marijuana use causes, or leads to, heroin use. The evidence for this is that if you ask heroin addicts whether they used marijuana before they used heroin they will almost all say yes. So we presumably know the number of times in our sample that A (marijuana use) preceded B (heroin use). And we know how often B (heroin use) occurred without A (marijuana use). But we don’t know how often marijuana use doesn’t lead to heroin use. (We also don’t know how often neither occur.) Without that information we know nothing. Out of a hundred people who used marijuana, how many became heroin addicts? If the answer is 1 or 2, then there’s not much of a correlation. Out of a hundred random people, how many used marijuana?

The missing information is easier to see where the gateway “drug” seems less reasonable. Like milk, for example. Is milk a gateway drug for heroin addiction. If you surveyed heroin addicts, you would likely find that more than 99 percent of them drank milk as a child. And the number of heroin addicts who didn’t drink milk would surely be less than 1 percent. So does that prove the proposition that milk is a gateway drug? Of course not. You can’t ignore the rest of the population that drank milk as a child and didn’t end up addicted to heroin.

This comes up all the time because unless you’re conducting a scientific study, you’re not going to have a control group. Which means you’re only looking at one side of the equation.

B. Correlation Is Not Causation

Police officers tend to pull over more red sports cars for speeding. Does this mean that the police have it out for people who drive red sports cars? Or is it more likely that the sort of person who chooses a red sports car is also the sort of person who is more likely to speed? The truth is that without more information it’s impossible to know. But correlation, not causation, should be the default assumption until causation is proven.

C. The Causal Arrow Might Be Going in the Opposite Direction

For example, it is a well known fact that single people are far more likely to be involved in crime than married people. Can we then conclude that if we manage to increase marriage rates we can decrease crime rates? Unfortunately not. It is just as likely that the sort of person who becomes a criminal is also the sort of person who doesn’t make a particularly attractive mate. That is, criminality is likely driving singlehood (for those people). Being single doesn’t make a person into a criminal. 

D. There May Be a Third Variable

That is C might be causing A and B. For example, there is great evidence that women who smoke during pregnancy tend to have children with lower IQs. However, it is also true that women with lower IQs are much more likely to smoke during pregnancy. And it is true that women with lower IQs are much more likely to have children with lower IQs. IQ is mostly a genetic thing. So it is likely that the 3rd variable of women with lower IQs is driving both the smoking during pregnancy and the lower IQ children. A more simplistic example is that a Martian observing cars would note that right before cars turn left, a blinking light on the left side of the car makes it happen.

IX. Try to Isolate the Particular Problem With the Expert’s Argument or Conclusions

Look out for these common problems with experts and testing:

1. Expert uses a test for one thing to do something else entirely. For example, the HCR-20 was designed to predict the risk of violence for mental patients in an inpatient setting. It has been studied in the correctional setting as well. But it should not be used to predict the risk of bad parenting. This is like using a scale to predict IQ – a validity problem.

2. Expert uses a diagnostic test or diagnosis to make predictions of future behavior. For example, concluding that a personality disorder will result in bad parenting is completely unwarranted by the diagnosis itself. This is a causation problem of several types.

3. Expert uses a test with a study population very unlike your client. For example, the HCR-20 study population was people with acute mental illness. It was later extended to prisoners. It has never been studied with the average person on the street. Another validity problem.

4. Expert uses anecdotal experience to make a conclusion. For example, “In my 30 years of experience doing this test thousands of times I can tell you that high scores predict bad parenting.”

5. Expert uses a test incorrectly. For example, by diagnosing personality disorder without the required knowledge of past behavior or without all the elements having been met. 

6. Expert uses information that is supposed to be for treatment purposes to make legal recommendations.The Cautionary Statement in the DSM preceding the main text enumerates the manual’s use as a guideline for diagnosis, study and treatment. It specifically denies meeting any legal criteria for what constitutes a mental disease, disorder, or disability; and “may not be wholly relevant to legal judgments . . . such as individual responsibility, disability determination, and competency.”
