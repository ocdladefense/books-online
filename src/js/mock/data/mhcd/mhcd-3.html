Mental Health and Criminal Defense

2010 edition

Disordered Mental State

Justice Breyer and the majority in Clark v. Arizona, 548 US 735 (2006), seem to be making two points about the modern state of psychological evidence. First, diagnostic labels are misleading at this stage of the development of the science of psychology. Knowing a person’s specific diagnosis tells very little about the person, their symptoms or prognosis. Therefore, excluding such information is largely a good thing, or is at least acceptable. Second, “diminished capacity” is a concept suited better to the insanity defense than to mens rea. Determining whether the defendant had a particular mental state at a particular time is very different from determining his generalized “capacity” to form a mental state. Neither of these points is terribly controversial. Neither point should be ignored by defense attorneys in Oregon.

Discussion

I. Statutes

A. ORS 161.300 Evidence of Disease or Defect Admissible as to Intent

Evidence that the actor suffered from a mental disease or defect is admissible whenever it is relevant to the issue of whether the actor did or did not have the intent which is an element of the crime.

B. ORS 161.295(2) Effect of Mental Disease or Defect

(2) As used in [this chapter] the terms “mental disease or defect” do not include an abnormality manifested only by repeated criminal or otherwise antisocial conduct, nor do they include any abnormality constituting solely a personality disorder.

C. ORS 161.309(2–3) Notice Prerequisite to Defense; Content

(2) The defendant may not introduce in the case in chief expert testimony regarding partial responsibility under ORS 161.300 unless the defendant gives notice of intent to do so in the manner provided in subsection (3) of this section.

(3) A defendant who is required under subsection (1) or (2) of this section to give notice shall file a written purpose at the time the defendant pleads not guilty. The defendant may file such notice at any time after the plea but before trial when just cause for failure to file the notice at the time of making the plea is made to the satisfaction of the court. If the defendant fails to file notice, the defendant shall not be entitled to introduce evidence for the establishment of a defense under ORS 161.295 or 161.300 unless the court, in its discretion, permits such evidence to be introduced where just cause for failure to file the notice is made to appear.

D. OS161.085 Definitions with Respect to Culpability

As used in chapter 743, Oregon Laws 1971, and ORS 166.635, unless the context requires otherwise:

(1) “Act” means a bodily movement.

(2) “Voluntary act” means a bodily movement performed consciously and includes the conscious possession or control of property.

(3) “Omission” means a failure to perform an act the performance of which is required by law.

(4) “Conduct” means an act or omission and its accompanying mental state.

(5) “To act” means either to perform an act or to omit to perform an act.

(6) “Culpable mental state” means intentionally, knowingly, recklessly or with criminal negligence as these terms are defined in subsections (7), (8), (9) and (10) of this section.

(7) “Intentionally” or “with intent,” when used with respect to a result or to conduct described by a statute defining an offense, means that a person acts with a conscious objective to cause the result or to engage in the conduct so described.

(8) “Knowingly” or “with knowledge,” when used with respect to conduct or to a circumstance described by a statute defining an offense, means that a person acts with an awareness that the conduct of the person is of a nature so described or that a circumstance so described exists.

(9) “Recklessly,” when used with respect to a result or to a circumstance described by a statute defining an offense, means that a person is aware of and consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the result will occur or that the circumstance exists. The risk must be of such nature and degree that disregard thereof constitutes a gross deviation from the standard of care that a reasonable person would observe in the situation.

(10) “Criminal negligence” or “criminally negligent,” when used with respect to a result or to a circumstance described by a statute defining an offense, means that a person fails to be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the result will occur or that the circumstance exists. The risk must be of such nature and degree that the failure to be aware of it constitutes a gross deviation from the standard of care that a reasonable person would observe in the situation.

E. ORS 161.105 Culpability Requirements Inapplicable to Certain Violations and Offenses

(1) Notwithstanding ORS 161.095, a culpable mental state is not required if:

(a) The offense constitutes a violation, unless a culpable mental state is expressly included in the definition of the offense; or

(b) An offense defined by a statute outside the Oregon Criminal Code clearly indicates a legislative intent to dispense with any culpable mental state requirement for the offense or for any material element thereof.

(2) Notwithstanding any other existing law, and unless a statute enacted after January 1, 1972, otherwise provides, an offense defined by a statute outside the Oregon Criminal Code that requires no culpable mental state constitutes a violation.

(3) Although an offense defined by a statute outside the Oregon Criminal Code requires no culpable mental state with respect to one or more of its material elements, the culpable commission of the offense may be alleged and proved, in which case criminal negligence constitutes sufficient culpability, and the classification of the offense and the authorized sentence shall be determined by ORS 161.505 to 161.605 and 161.615 to 161.655.

F. ORS 161.115 Construction of Statutes With Respect to Culpability

(1) If a statute defining an offense prescribes a culpable mental state but does not specify the element to which it applies, the prescribed culpable mental state applies to each material element of the offense that necessarily requires a culpable mental state.

(2) Except as provided in ORS 161.105, if a statute defining an offense does not prescribe a culpable mental state, culpability is nonetheless required and is established only if a person acts intentionally, knowingly, recklessly or with criminal negligence.

(3) If the definition of an offense prescribes criminal negligence as the culpable mental state, it is also established if a person acts intentionally, knowingly or recklessly. When recklessness suffices to establish a culpable mental state, it is also established if a person acts intentionally or knowingly. When acting knowingly suffices to establish a culpable mental state, it is also established if a person acts intentionally.

(4) Knowledge that conduct constitutes an offense, or knowledge of the existence, meaning or application of the statute defining an offense, is not an element of an offense unless the statute clearly so provides.

G. ORS 161.125 Intoxication as Defense; Drug Use or Dependence as Defense

(1) The use of drugs or controlled substances, dependence on drugs or controlled substances or voluntary intoxication shall not, as such, constitute a defense to a criminal charge, but in any prosecution for an offense, evidence that the defendant used drugs or controlled substances, or was dependent on drugs or controlled substances, or was intoxicated may be offered by the defendant whenever it is relevant to negative an element of the crime charged.

(2) When recklessness establishes an element of the offense, if the defendant, due to the use of drugs or controlled substances, dependence on drugs or controlled substances or voluntary intoxication, is unaware of a risk of which the defendant would have been aware had the defendant been not intoxicated, not using drugs or controlled substances, or not dependent on drugs or controlled substances, such unawareness is immaterial.

II. Statutory Issues

A. “A ‘Diminished Capacity Defense’ Immediately Breaks Down”

ORS 161.300 is an evidentiary rule allowing admission of almost any psychological evidence relevant to the mental state element of the crimes charged. Neither the word “diminished” nor the word “capacity” appear in ORS 161.300. Equally absent are the words “partial” and “responsibility.” Such labels, so often used by defense attorneys themselves, make it seem as though ORS 161.300 is a defense which requires proof that the defendant is incapacitated to some extent; as if the defendant can win by proving that the defendant’s capacity was diminished.

In practice, a “diminished capacity defense” immediately breaks down because it raises the questions of “What capacity?” and “How diminished?” Presumably, we’d be talking about the capacity to form mental state. But even the most acutely ill folks have the capacity to form the intent to eat or to sit up. The question of “how diminished” is even more absurd – 30 percent? 40 percent? The point is that the defendant’s capacity to intend or know shouldn’t be at issue. The defendant’s mental illness simply makes it less likely that the defendant actually did have the required mental state on the day in question. ORS 161.300 does nothing more than specifically allow the defense to introduce evidence of mental illness as it relates to the State’s burden to prove mens rea beyond a reasonable doubt.

B. The State Must Prove That Specific Content Was in Defendant’s Mind

ORS 161.300 allows the defense to properly rebut the state’s inferencesregarding mental state. The state must prove that specific content was in the defendant’s mind at the moment of the actus reus. The state cannot see into the defendant’s mind; therefore, the state must rely on evidence of defendant’s actions, statements and context to infer the content of defendant’s mind. Such inferences are often questionable even where the defendant’s mind works in a normal and predictable fashion. Such ideas as “intentional action” are losing their coherence with every passing year of cognitive science research.

But where the defendant suffers from a chaotic, disordered mind beset with delusions, disorganization, PTSD terror, cognitive impairment or any number of other disorders, there is usually little to support the state’s inferences other than the prosecutor’s emphatic voice and the jury’s lack of understanding. For example, if an officer pointed a gun at me, I would do what the officer said. If I started gesticulating wildly and approached the officer, one might properly infer that I had a death wish. But any crisis intervention–trained officer will tell you that when interacting with a person with psychosis, the last thing you want to do is raise the tension level by pulling out a gun. It’s not that people with psychosis have a death wish; rather, generally speaking, they are not thinking through their actions at all. Raising the tension level raises their tension level which exacerbates whatever behavior is already present. We gained this knowledge of how police should behave in these situations from repeated experience. But the real point is that even when you know how to appropriately manage the tension level and behavior of a person with acute psychosis, you still don’t know what’s in their mind.

Remarkably little is known of the disordered mind which would allow the sort of inferences which the state must use to prove mental state beyond a reasonable doubt.

Take, for example, a disorderly conduct case where the client is alleged to have been screaming nonsensical statements and shaking his fists at passersby. The state will have to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant had the intent to cause public inconvenience, annoyance or alarm (or that he thought about such risks and consciously disregarded them). How are they going to do that? How are they going to pluck specific, ordered content out of this guy’s clearly disordered mind. The answer is that they’ll try to do it by ignoring the obvious symptoms of mental illness. They’ll say that it is common sense that anyone shaking their fist around and yelling must know they’re causing public inconvenience. Which would be a reasonable enough inference if the guy had a healthy mind. But common sense is another way of asking the jury to ignore the obvious symptoms of disorder: “Ask yourself what you would be thinking if you were shouting and shaking your fist.” The inference relies on an underlying presumption of a healthy mind. With the aforementioned client, we don’t have any idea what was in his chaotic, disordered mind. Banana peels, George Bush, fractals, absolute chaos, nothing? I defy the state to prove beyond a reasonable doubt exactly what he was thinking.

The following excerpt from a 1998 “Onion” article exemplifies the absurdity of assuming intentionality in a person who is clearly mentally ill:

Alhambra, California – Area crazy man Dennis Fife held a press conference Tuesday to announce that on Oct. 8, he will stand in the doorway of the office building at 2600 Kenilworth and yell at cars all day. Addressing reporters, the 47-year-old Fife said, “At approximately 9:30 a.m. on the day in question, shortly after I finish lunging at dogs, I will proceed to the Simmons Building and yell loudly for nine hours. The screaming will be broken by a 15-minute fit of rigid catatonia, most likely in the late afternoon.” Among the topics Fife plans to address during his nine hour rant: the ace of diamonds, bookshelves, the man trying to kill him, those goddamn bananas, people from St Louis, closed up straws, Trapper John, MD and papers, papers, everywhere. “I may briefly stray from my agenda to urinate at the building’s entrance,” Fife said, “but, for the most part, I will focus on the task at hand and spend the bulk of the day yelling at the various passing cars.

The article is funny because it is absurd. It is absurd because folks suffering from florid psychosis do not plan their every move. They’re not thinking through their actions – they’re just acting. But I can tell you, because I’ve seen many such cases, if Mr. Fife knocked over a bicyclist while lunging at dogs, the DA would charge him with assault. And their argument would be that it’s common sense that if you’re lunging around on a public street or sidewalk there’s a high likelihood you’re going to knock someone down. Or, alternatively, that you don’t just knock over a bicyclist without knowing what you’re doing. The natural pull toward an assumption of intentionality is strong enough to make these arguments appealing even in the most unlikely situations.

C. Evidence of Disorder or Loss of Functioning in the Mind Is Even More Relevant to the Recklessness Calculation Than to Knowledge

ORS 161.300 applies to mens rea generally, not just to crimes which require the mental state of “intentionally.” State v. Smith, 154 Or App 37 (1998). Smith’s reasoning but not its holding extends ORS 161.300 to recklessness in addition to knowledge. Moreover, recklessness is clearly a higher order mental state than knowledge or intent. It requires more cognitive ability generally and frontal lobe functioning specifically to disregard a substantial risk of a hypothetical future event or fact than it does to simply know a present fact. Every pet, for example, “knows” when they have not been fed but rarely has a pet considered and disregarded the substantial risk that there will not be a feeding that night. The point is that evidence of disorder or loss of functioning in the mind is even more relevant to the recklessness calculation than to knowledge. See motion in Appendix B.

D. ORS 161.300 Does Not Require an Expert

Friends, family members, caseworkers, random bystanders and even the state’s witnesses can talk about the symptoms they’ve observed. The most effective witness is often a family member who can talk about the progression of symptoms over the tragic arc of the defendant’s life. No notice to the state is required unless an expert is presented. An effective tactic with an expert is to ask the expert if it is possible to be sure about what was in the defendant’s mind at the time of the crime. Most experts will be extraordinarily reluctant to testify about the existence of a legally required mental state because they simply don’t know. The expert’s knowledge of the difficulty of discerning mental state from the chaos of mental disorder is the most important thing that an expert can communicate in a disordered mental state case.

E. ORS 161.300 Applies to Any “Mental Disease or Defect”…

…except “abnormalities manifested only by repeated criminal or otherwise antisocial conduct, nor…any abnormality constituting solely a personality disorder.” ORS 161.295. That is, any Axis I mental disorder or cognitive disability may form the basis for such evidence. For example, Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder, Attention Deficit Disorder, depression, adjustment disorders and eating disorders are all Axis I disorders that may be used, in addition to the more typical psychotic disorders like Schizophrenia, Bipolar Disorder and Schizoaffective Disorder. The only “abnormality” that the statute specifically excludes is a personality disorder when there is no other kind of disorder. If there is both a personality disorder and an Axis I disorder, then the rule applies. If the client’s mental illness is 90 percent personality disorder with a little PTSD, then it applies. If the client’s mental illness is likely 100 percent personality disorder but you can make an argument that there is an Axis I disorder or mental retardation, then the rule should apply and any arguments against admissibility would more properly go to the weight of the evidence. (Technically, the statute also excludes evidence that the defendant is a psychopath. However, exclusion of psychopathy only serves to protect defense counsel from themselves.)

F. A Jury Instruction on Disordered Mental State Evidence Is Unnecessary and Risky

It’s unnecessary because ORS 161.300 is an evidentiary rule not a special defense. The jury is almost never informed about the basis for admissibility of evidence. Moreover, the jury is already instructed on the state’s burden on mental state. A special jury instruction risks an impression that ORS 161.300 is more like an affirmative defense than a rule on establishing a central element of the crime.

But it is important to make mental state and accomplice liability instructions as clear and strong as possible. That means, at a minimum, using the updated mental state instructions based on State v. Woodman, 341 Or 105 (2006), and State v. Crosby, 342 Or 419 (2007). Crosby makes it clear that when the state is proving a reckless crime, they must prove more than general recklessness:

The statutory definition thus begins with the qualification that it applies when the term “recklessly” is used in either of two situations, namely, when it is used “with respect to a result. . . described by a statute defining an offense” or when it is used with “a circumstance described by a statute defining an offense.” The statute then provides that the term means that a person is “aware of and consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the result will occur or that the circumstance exists.” Plainly, “the result” or “the circumstance” refer back to the beginning of the sentence, in which there is reference to a “result” or “a circumstance described by a statute defining an offense.” Thus, not just any result or circumstance may be considered in determining whether a person acts recklessly, only a result or circumstance “described by a statute defining an offense.” As a result, the meaning of “the result” or “the circumstance” as used to define “recklessly” within the meaning of ORS 161.085(9) will depend on the particular offense at issue.

Crosby, 204 Or App at 80. The state must prove that the defendant was reckless toward the result or circumstance that constitutes the crime. Thus, for example, in a DUII manslaughter the state would have to prove that the defendant was aware of and consciously disregarded a substantial risk that someone would die because the defendant was driving. This is a far more difficult task for the state than proving general recklessness or even disregard of a substantial risk that there would be an accident.

G. Not-Bad-Person Evidence

It is quite powerful to present evidence that counters the implication by the government that the defendant is a bad person. The more you can shift the jury to an understanding of the client and the horrors of his/her life, the closer you are to a not guilty verdict. Attribution theory is a field of research based on the fact that people tend to have a default assumption that what another person does is based more on what “kind” of person he is, rather than the social and environmental forces at work on that person. For example, if a person cuts in front of you in a car, then the default assumption is that he’s a jerk or a bad driver, not that he’s distracted and didn’t see you. More individualistic cultures are more likely to make the error, and the U.S. is the most individualistic of cultures. Moreover, those with a heavy cognitive load, like jurors, are even more likely to make the error. Americans are also far more likely to ascribe intentionality to action (rather than negligence) than are most other cultures. Peng/Nisbett. Finally, the more unfamiliar one person is with another person or his culture (as for example, with cross-racial judgment), the more likely he is to make both the fundamental attribution error and to ascribe intentionality to behavior.

Thus several unconscious assumptions are combining to create an immediate assumption that the defendant intendedhis results and did so because he’s a bad person. Fortunately, there are several “debiasing techniques” that are quite effective in overcoming these presumptions. The most important debiasing technique is to point out unseen factors. For example, if you knew more about the situation or context of the person that cut in front of you, you’re much more likely to ascribe causation to situational factors rather than dispositional factors. Moreover, the more one knows about a person’s situation the closer one gets to making a presumption that is like the one people make about themselves, i.e., that situational factors caused the result. People rarely ascribe traits to themselves (especially bad-person traits) as a result of an incident. In fact, we naturally look around for situational factors that might have caused whatever situation we find ourselves in. Once we understand the context of another person we are much less likely to believe they are a “bad” person. And people who aren’t “bad” generally don’t intend to do “bad” things.

Disordered mental state evidence provides an excuse for presenting evidence of context and history as well as the legal path for finding the person not guilty. Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder is a particularly effective disorder to use in this way as a history of the defendant’s traumatized life can change the defendant from presumptively “bad” to sympathetic very quickly in the eyes of the jury. Once a person is labelled as sympathetic (or even as a victim herself), the jury will look for a reason to acquit.

III. The Dual Diagnosis Defense: Disordered Mental State Is a Direct Path to an Intoxication Defense

=

ORS 161.125 provides that:

(1) The use of drugs or controlled substances, dependence on drugs or controlled substances or voluntary intoxication shall not, as such, constitute a defense to a criminal charge, but in any prosecution for an offense, evidence that the defendant used drugs or controlled substances, or was dependent on drugs or controlled substances, or was intoxicated may be offered by the defendant whenever it is relevant to negative an element of the crime charged.

(2) When recklessness establishes an element of the offense, if the defendant, due to the use of drugs or controlled substances or voluntary intoxication, is unaware of a risk of which the defendant would have been aware had the defendant been not intoxicated, not using drugs or controlled substances, or not dependent on drugs or controlled substances, such unawareness is immaterial (emphasis added).

Both intoxication and addiction are explicitly admissible to bear on the mental states of intent and knowledge. Moreover, evidence of addiction to drugs would also be admissible under ORS 161.300. Substance addiction, substance withdrawal and (hard to believe as it is) substance intoxication are all Axis I diagnoses. That is, they are diagnosable mental disorders which are not personality disorders. (DSM-IV-TR, Substance Related Disorders.)

At the same time, an enormous proportion of drug addicts have co-occurring mental disorders, and an even larger percentage of mentally ill people are also addicted to drugs or alcohol. A co-occurring Axis I mental disorder and substance addiction is usually called a “dual diagnosis.” According to the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA), 37 percent of alcohol abusers and 53 percent of drug abusers also have at least one serious mental illness. Of all people diagnosed as mentally ill, 29 percent abuse either alcohol or drugs. A recent confidential study of dual diagnosis issues in the Marion County Jail found that more than 80 percent of those people who had been diagnosed with mental illness self-admitted to having used methamphetamine in the past. Not everyone who uses is an addict, but since this was a self-admission study run by the government, the numbers had to be on the low side. For women, it was almost 90 percent. (Testimony by Chris Hoy, Commander of the Marion County Jail, 2-17-06.)

Two simultaneous factors create the enormous percentages of co-occurring disorders. First, many people with psychotic and mood disorders “self-treat” their mental disorders with street drugs. There’s actually evidence that, initially at least, there are some therapeutic benefits to this approach. At the same time, use of drugs can trigger otherwise nonexistent or dormant mental illness such as methamphetamine psychosis or bipolar disorder.

The trick to this defense is showing that at no step of the process was there anything that could be considered voluntary. The Axis I mental illness caused the Axis I addiction disorder to initiate its process and to continue without the defendant having sufficient insight into the havoc it was creating in his life. The addiction caused the intoxication in this particular case. And the effects of the intoxication, combined with the symptoms of the mental illness, caused the defendant to act irrationally.

As a side note, evidence of addiction or intoxication is not admissible to prove GEI. The appellate court has stated that the GEI statute “provides that a person is guilty except for insanity if he or she proves that the lack of substantial capacity is ‘a result of mental disease or defect’ at the time of the crime. The statute does not provide that the defense is available upon proof of a lack of capacity as a result of mental disease or defect in combination withother factors such as voluntary alcohol consumption. When the legislature wants to require proof of either a single cause or a combination of causes, it generally does so.… In this instance, the legislature did not do so. It stated only that insanity may be claimed upon proof that a defendant’s condition is a result of a mental disease or defect.” State v. Peverieri, 192 Or App 229 (2004).

Practice Tip

You can also use disordered mental state evidence as a legal hook when the tactic is to shift blame away from a mentally ill defendant to a more “responsible” person that could have more easily prevented the incident from taking place. This works especially well in resisting arrest/assaulting a public safety officer cases. The argument here is that the cop was responsible for the defendant’s behavior because of the choices the cop made. As defense lawyers, we know the cop could be at fault and also the client guilty. But the jury doesn’t know it – fault and intent are too intertwined in common understanding. The DA will never argue that the cop acted wrongly because DAs, for various reasons built into the criminal justice system, feel obligated to stand up for their police officers. Judges never catch the distinction and even if they did, you can just say that you’re arguing mens rea.

Mental state is inextricably linked with moral judgment. Unless told otherwise, criminal juries do not think about mental state as content in the mind so much as legalese for “blameworthiness.” The question for the jury is the extent to which the defendant is at fault. If blame or fault can be shifted to another person for causing or failing to prevent the ultimately tragic event, then the defendant is, by logical extension, less blameworthy.

Use the Power of Counterfactual Thinking

The propensity for constantly thinking about what could have happened in order to interpret what did happen is called “counterfactual thinking.”

Human Thinking Quirk #1 – Closer calls seem more tragic.

The classic study is Kahneman and Tversky’s hypothetical:

“Mr. Crane and Mr. Tees were scheduled to leave the airport on different flights, at the same time. They travelled from town in the same limousine, were caught in a traffic jam, and arrived at the airport 30 minutes after the scheduled departure time of their flights. Mr. Crane is told that his flight left on time. Mr. Tees is told that his flight was delayed, and just left five minutes ago. Who is more upset, Mr. Crane or Mr. Tees?” It turns out that 96 percent of people believe Mr. Tees would be more upset.

Once the attorney is aware of this fact, you can emphasize a close call to make it seem more tragic or present evidence that a situation wasn’t a close call to make it seem less impactful. Where the defense can position the mentally ill client as the victim of an officer or the immediate circumstances, then it may be helpful to make the situation seem as tragic as possible.

Human Thinking Quirk #2– When unnecessary action is taken to step into the path of misfortune, it seems more tragic than when no action is taken.

For example, imagine Bob gets to the airport two hours early with no lines and so he changes his flight to one that leaves thirty minutes earlier. If the plane Bob is on crashes it seems more tragic than for those who arrived and took the plane in an expected and normal pattern. Of course, the opposite is true as well. If Bob switches his flight away from one that ultimately crashes, he seems luckier or more fortunate than the other people on his flight who are also in a plane that doesn’t crash.

Human Thinking Quirk #3 – When choices are made, the chooser is seen as more responsible.

Thus if Bob chooses to leave thirty minutes earlier and, as a result, finds himself on the plane that crashes, he will be seen as more responsible for his fate than those who never made that last-minute choice. Americans especially like to believe that choice and responsibility are integrally intertwined, even for unforeseeable events. This is true even if a choice is made to do nothing. Thus, for example, “Jurors will view the auto manufacturer that ‘almost’ increased its roof strength but didn’t as more responsible than the auto manufacturer that never considered improving roof strength. But on the flipside, they will also view the plaintiff who ‘nearly’ bought a safer car as more responsible than the plaintiff who never considered it. In both cases, the key is the ease with which jurors can imagine the alternative.” Your Counterfactual Strategy, Broda-Bahm (2001).

Using the Power– “The cop made a choice not to volunteer for CIT training. The cop made a choice to confront this mentally ill individual rather than talk to him. The cop made a choice to hurl mandates and threats of violence at this person with paranoid delusions. Imagine for a second if, instead of raising the aggression level, he calmed the situation down. He admitted on the stand that he considered other options but he went forward with his tragic choice. If only…” Emphasizing choice will lead the jurors to assign responsibility. If they assign responsibility to the officer, they will not assign responsibility to the defendant. Or, maybe instead of the cop at the scene it was a healthcare worker or doctor that morning that saw defendant’s increasing symptoms and chose not to step in. For example, I had a case where a client accused of attempted murder went to the hospital the morning of the incident in a floridly psychotic state, having just attempted suicide. They released him within a couple hours and a few hours after that the incident occurred. The hospital really is much more at fault in that case than my client whose mind was a chaotic mush of delusions, hallucinations and misfiring neurons. The client did everything he could to stop the illness that was controlling his life. He even tried to take his own life. He had no power at all in the face of his command hallucinations. The hospital, on the other hand, had the power to contain this ugly thing. They could have treated the real problem. Or they could have kept him and protected him and us from this thing that had taken over. It is important to externalize the illness to make it clear that the client is choosing the illness and it’s symptoms any more than a stroke victim chooses her illness and the paralysis that might come with it.

Use the Power of Hindsight Bias– “Hindsight bias refers to people’s tendency to view an event as more inevitable and foreseeable after the event has occurred (Fischhoff, 1975, 1977; Hawkins & Hastie, 1990; Leary, 1982). Knowing the outcome of an uncertain event makes that outcome seem to have been more likely in the first place (i.e., in hindsight), compared to likelihood estimates made before knowing the outcome (i.e., in foresight). This retrospective distortion presumably results from an automatic cognitive assimilation of new and old information (Fischhoff, 1977).” Bryant and Brockway, 1997. Thus, for example in the APSO/blame the cop case, the jury will see the sequence of events and presume they were inevitable and foreseeable. If the officer should have foreseen that his actions would create an incident then the officer is far more blameworthy than the defendant, who was largely being controlled by the external force that is his mental illness. It’s all about responsibility and the officer is responsible for making choices that led to the foreseeable result.

IV. Some Practical Advice in Using Disordered Mental State Evidence

A. Notice

Notice of disordered mental state evidence must be filed whenever expert testimony will be used. Notice need not be filed if expert testimony will not be used. The notice need not be specific. For example, it can be as simple as the following:

Pursuant to ORS 161.300 and 161.309(2), defendant hereby serves notice on the state and the court that she intends to introduce expert testimony and other evidence that defendant suffers from a mental disease or defect. Such evidence will be relevant to the issue of whether defendant did or did not have the intent which is an element of the crime.

B. Use of Experts

An expert is not a prerequisite to presenting disordered mental state evidence. Sometimes, depending on the manner of use, it may be advantageous to simply present mental health history and contextual perspective through friends, family and caseworkers. Also, experts usually review documents which would be inadmissible in trial. So it may be that the expert has a somewhat more negative view of the client than the jury might. The prosecutor may also ask the expert about the basis of his or her opinion, and otherwise inadmissible evidence might therefore slip into the trial.

In any case, defense counsel should not be afraid to use an expert simply because the expert is unable to conclusively state that the defendant lacked capacity to form intent or didn’t have the required intent at the time of the crime. In fact, for mens rea, one of the most helpful conclusions that can be drawn by an expert is that it is almost impossible to confidently state what the intent of the defendant was at the time of the crime. After all, if a psychologist with far more training, experience, time and access to resources than the jury cannot make a confident conclusion, then what hope can the jury possibly have of coming to an airtight conclusion?

This is what I like to do in closing in this situation:

“Beyond a Reasonable Doubt” is the amount of confidence you must have in your verdict in order to come back with a guilty verdict. The problem is that the words are cumbersome and unwieldy. The DA will tell you that the phrase isn’t “beyond an unreasonable doubt,” meaning that you shouldn’t consider unreasonable or absurd doubts or arguments. Which is true. “Beyond A Reasonable Doubt” is the amount of “sure” you would need to be if you were packing your parachute to jump out of an airplane. You wouldn’t check for ghosts or other unreasonable possibilities. But if I pointed out to you that there was a hole in the parachute, would you still jump? What if you noticed that there was a rip in the seam? What if some guy told you about the parachute but didn’t show it to you? Would you tell that person that you’re not going to jump unless you get to see the parachute and check it yourself?

Folks, a parachute expert has come into court to tell you that they’ve looked at this parachute and there’s a huge, gaping hole right in the middle of the fabric of this case. You know you wouldn’t jump with your own life. Please don’t jump with Mr. X’s life.

C. The Bigger Issue

Mental state is about whether the State can prove the appropriate criminal intent. We don’t punish people as criminals unless they have the criminal intent that the law requires. The thing I like most about a disordered mental state strategy is that it cleanly fits with the broader point that this person does not belong within the criminal justice system. This is a person who should be treated, not punished. He’s not guilty, precisely because he didn’t have the required criminal mindset; he had the mind of an incredibly ill person who needs help. That’s why he’s innocent.

Don’t be afraid to link up to the bigger issue. “This defendant is but a pawn in the much bigger shift of mentally ill people from the mental health system to the criminal justice system. You should vote not guilty because Mr. X is not guilty, but you will also be making a statement that the state can’t just shove off their responsibility to care for our more fragile citizens onto the criminal justice system and into the prisons. We’re talking about basic human decency here.” Most people are at least vaguely aware of diminishing local resources for mental health and the sad state of the Oregon State Hospital, a decrepit institution way back in 1974 when it was used to film One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest.

D. State’s Evaluation

If the defense gives notice of intent to call an expert in support of a claim of mental disease or defect as required by ORS 161.309, then the state has the right to request an independent mental examination of the defendant under ORS 161.315. In order to exercise that right, the state must first “file notice with the court of its intention to have the defendant examined.” Once notice is filed, the court “in its discretion” may order the defendant committed for examination for up to 30 days.

The judge is not required to take the defendant into custody for a state evaluation. State v. Corbin, 15 Or App 536 (1973). In fact, if the defendant is out of custody, the preferred method should be a “consent examination” in which the defendant goes to a site chosen by the parties to be evaluated by the psychologist chosen by the state. The noncustodial evaluation has the simultaneous advantages of being considerably less expensive for the state and impinging much less on the defendant’s rights.

The defendant may object to the examiner chosen by the State. If the court believes there has been “good cause shown,” it may direct the State to choose another examiner. ORS 161.315.

Note that the state’s right to examination is only triggered by the defense’s intent to call an expert. The defense may present “observation evidence” of a mental disorder through nonexperts like family members, friends and neighbors without risking a state’s examination. This is because the state’s right to examination under ORS 161.315 is triggered by “filing of notice…as provided in ORS 161.309(3).” For mental state evidence, ORS 161.309(3) refers back to ORS 161.309(2) which only requires filing of notice when the defense intends to “introduce in the case in chief expert testimony.”

E. Oregon Defense Attorneys Would Be Well Served Following Clark v. Arizona

The United States Supreme Court addressed the issue of mental state evidence in the Clark v. Arizona,548 US 735 (2006), opinion June 29, 2006. This was the first time that the court addressed evidence of mental illness in the context of either the insanity defense or mens rea in over two decades. Amicus brief of American Psychiatric Association, p. 1. Technically, the case has little relevance to Oregon law since the issue before the court was the constitutionality of an Arizona Supreme Court ruling that purported to “channel” mental health evidence entirely into the insanity defense. The basis for the Arizona Supreme Court’s finding was that the Arizona legislature had adopted the Model Penal Code (like Oregon) but rejected the model penal code provision identical to Oregon’s ORS 161.300 which specifically declares the admissibility of evidence of mental disease or defect.

The U.S. Supreme Court ruled that a state is allowed to channel mental health evidence into the insanity defense but not all mental health evidence. In the most interesting part of Justice Breyer’s opinion, the Supreme Court gives birth to a typology of mental state evidence. As the opinion states, “Understanding Clark’s claim requires attention to the categories of evidence with a potential bearing on mens rea.” Clark, 548 US at 757. According to the Court, there are three types of such evidence: observation evidence, mental-disease evidence and capacity evidence. This division becomes important because the majority believes that Arizona purported to exclude only mental-disease and capacity evidence but not observation evidence. Since Mr. Clark had a bench trial and his counsel made no offer of proof, it was not clear whether there was any observation evidence excluded in Mr. Clark’s case, and the Supreme Court failed to make a finding either way.

1) Observation Evidence is “testimony from those who observed what Clark did and heard what he said; this category would also include testimony that an expert might give about Clark’s tendency to think in a certain way and his behavioral characteristics. This evidence may support a professional diagnosis of mental disease and in any event is the kind of evidence that can be relevant to show what in fact was on Clark’s mind when he fired the gun. Observation evidence in the record covers Clark’s behavior at home and with friends, his expressions of belief around the time of the killing that “aliens” were inhabiting the bodies of local people (including government agents), his driving around the neighborhood before the police arrived, and so on. Contrary to the dissent’s characterization…observation evidence can be presented by either lay or expert witnesses.” 548 US at 757–58.

2) Mental-Disease Evidence is “opinion testimony that Clark suffered from a mental disease with features described by the witness. As was true here, this evidence characteristically but not always comes from professional psychologists or psychiatrists who testify as expert witnesses and base their opinions in part on an examination of a defendant, usually conducted after the events in question. The thrust of this evidence was that, based on factual reports, professional observations, and tests, Clark was psychotic at the time in question, with a condition that fell within the category of schizophrenia.” 548 US at 758.

3) Capacity Evidence is evidence “about a defendant’s capacity for cognition and moral judgment (and ultimately also his capacity to form mens rea). This too is opinion evidence. Here, as it usually does, this testimony came from the same experts and concentrated on those specific details of the mental condition that make the difference between sanity and insanity under the Arizona definition.” Id.

The Supreme Court’s ruling is that a state may channel mental-disease and capacity evidence into the insanity defense: “Nothing that we hold here is authority for restricting a factfinder’s consideration of observation evidence indicating state of mind at the time of a criminal offense (conventional mens rea evidence) as distinct from professional mental disease or capacity evidence going to ability to form a certain state of mind during a period that includes the time of the offense charged.” 548 US at 765, n 34. “[W]e read the Arizona Court of Appeals to have done nothing more than rely on Mott to reject the claim that due process forbids restricting evidence bearing on ‘ability to form mens rea’” 548 US at 763 (brackets and emphasis omitted). Note that ability to form mens rea is different than the question of whether the defendant did form mens rea at the time in question.

Justice Breyer and the majority seem to be making two points about the modern state of psychological evidence. First, diagnostic labels are misleading at this stage of the development of the science of psychology. Knowing a person’s specific diagnosis tells one very little about the person, their symptoms or their prognosis. Therefore, excluding such information is largely a good thing, or is at least acceptable. Second, “diminished capacity” is a concept suited better to the insanity defense than to mens rea. Determining whether the defendant had a particular mental state at a particular time is very different from determining his generalized “capacity” to form a mental state. Neither of these points is terribly controversial. Neither point should be ignored by defense attorneys in Oregon.

Defense attorneys in Oregon should strive to focus their disordered mental state cases on observation evidence. Drifting into general predictions from diagnosis opens the door for cross-examination on the impotency of psychiatric diagnosis, a door that need not be opened. It is much more powerful to say, “This is what it’s like to see through the defendant’s eyes: these are the sorts of delusions that this defendant has and this is the way it makes him act,” than to say, “This defendant has Schizoaffective Disorder manifesting itself in psychosis and mania.” The particular diagnostic label is almost never necessary (or even helpful) to an argument related to mens rea.

In Arizona v. Clark, the fact that the defendant was delusional and hallucinatory at the time of the crime was relevant to the question of whether the defendant knew he was shooting a police officer. That is, his perception and logical abilities were hindered at the time of the crime. Thus, any inferences and circumstantial evidence used by the state to prove mens rea would need to be examined in light of such evidence. It is not different in kind (though different in complexity) to a situation involving blindness. A blind defendant is not incapable of knowing that the person he shoots is a police officer. However, evidence that the officer was wearing a uniform and standing ten feet from the defendant at the time of the shooting is not very good evidence of a blind defendant’s actual knowledge. It is impossible to separate the value or inferential meaning of the state’s evidence from the fact of defendant’s blindness. This is the difference between capacity for knowing and actual knowledge.

In Clark, the state presented evidence that the defendant was driving around the block multiple times in the middle of the night blaring loud music. The state argued that the inference to be taken from this evidence was that Mr. Clark was luring a police officer to the scene so that he could kill a police officer. The defense argued that this was evidence of schizophrenic hallucinations and Mr. Clark’s attempt to dull them.

Under the court’s distinction between observation evidence and mental disease/capacity evidence, the defense has a constitutional right to present evidence that Mr. Clark commonly had hallucinations; that they were very distracting to him; that he was also delusional and disorganized; that he had not been taking his medications and was likely more delusional, disorganized and hallucinatory than usual; that he was prone to repetitive actions like driving around the block; that he tended to play loud music to drown out his hallucinations, etc. The defense could present this evidence through an expert or laypersons. The defense does not have a constitutional right to present an expert that diagnosed Mr. Clark with schizophrenia to say that people with schizophrenia often have hallucinations and schizophrenics with hallucinations often feel the need to drown out such hallucinations. The defense does not have a constitutional right to present an expert to testify that defendant lacked the capacity to know whether a person was a police officer.

V. Mental States Generally

A. The Model Penal Code Mental States; Case Law

1.Generally

a. History.In 1970, the legislatively commissioned Criminal Law Revision Commission undertook a sweeping reform of Oregon’s criminal code. Its revised statutes were based on the Model Penal Code and adapted the MPC’s structure for mental states. Under the MPC, and now under the Criminal Code of Oregon, a defendant may not be found guilty of an offense (except under certain isolated exceptions) unless he is found to have acted intentionally, knowingly, recklessly or criminally negligently with respect to each material element of the alleged offense.

b. Results, conduct and circumstances. Per their respective statutory definitions, the mental states of intentionally, knowinglyand recklesslyeach apply only to two of these factors. “Intentionally” applies to results and conduct (you can intend to cause a result or to engage in conduct, but you cannot intend that circumstances are what they are). “Knowingly” applies to conduct and circumstances (you can know that your conduct is such as it is or that circumstances are as they are, but you cannot know that your conduct will have a particular result). “Recklessly” applies to results and circumstances (you can be aware of and consciously disregard a risk that your conduct will have a certain result or that circumstances might be a certain way, but you cannot be aware of but disregard a risk that your conduct is such as it is).

c, The criminal statute fills in the particular results, conduct or circumstances to which the mental state must be applied. It’s never left for the jury to figure this out on its own. State v. Crosby, 342 Or 419, 432 (2007), provides a recent example of this rule. The case is noteworthy for two reasons. First, it makes it absolutely clear that the risk which must be consciously disregarded refers back to the statute. So, in a manslaughter statute that says “recklessly causes death,” the state must prove that the defendant was aware of and consciously disregarded a substantial risk of death. Death is the particular result with which the statute is concerned. Ms. Crosby had done a terrible job of taking care of her elderly mother, who got bedsores that became septic and ultimately caused her untimely demise. The state proved that Ms. Crosby was aware of the distinct possibility of bedsores but not that that she was aware of the risk that the bedsores would cause death.

Second, the defense attorneys let something happen which should be assiduously avoided. They let the judge read a jury instruction that did not refer to a specific result, circumstance or conduct: “A person acts recklessly if that person is aware of and consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk either 1) that a particular result will occur, or 2) that a particular circumstance exists.” Among the many problems that arise, the worst is that there is no circumstance in manslaughter toward which the defendant must be reckless. There’s only a result. The defendant must consciously disregard a substantial risk of death. So, in Crosby the jury deliberated for awhile and came back with a question: Does the phrase “particular circumstance” refer to death or to the bedsores? The judge then got confused by the fact that he hadn’t clarified the instruction and responded to the jury by saying that circumstance can mean many things and it is for the jury to figure out. The Supreme Court disagrees. Death is a result, not a circumstance. So the phrase “particular circumstance” not only does not mean many things, it doesn’t mean anything at all in the context of manslaughter. Ms. Crosby was convicted for recklessly causing bedsores when the statute requires proof that she recklessly caused death.

Practice Tip

Always attach the word “criminal” to the mental state when you’re speaking with a jury. For example, the DA must prove “criminal intent” or “criminal recklessness.” Adding the extra word makes it clear that there is a legal definition for these words that is weightier than the lay definition. Additionally, it gets at the idea that mental state isn’t some technicality, and deciding someone’s guilt isn’t a bureaucratic exercise of checking boxes. We’re trying to decide whether to punish the defendant as a criminal. But a person is only a criminal if they have a criminal mind. After all, we don’t punish accidents. Nor do we punish five-year-olds. Even if the accident causes death or the five-year-old steals. We only punish people who commit a criminal act with a criminal mind.

2.Intentionally

– ORS 161.085(7): “‘Intentionally’ or ‘with intent,’ when used with respect to a result or to conduct described by a statute defining an offense, means that a person acts with a conscious objective to cause the result or to engage in the conduct so described.”

a.Case Law

Direct evidence of a defendant’s disordered mind is relevant to counter the prosecution’s claim that the defendant acted intentionally. In this case, a bizarre, incoherent letter written by the defendant and delivered along with a forged check should have been admitted at trial as relevant to whether she was able, at the time of the alleged offense, to form the requisite intent to defraud. State v. Gallegos, 174 Or App 549, 556 (2001).

Evidence that defendant’s hired hand took the victim from one room to another while robbing her in her home was insufficient to establish either defendant or his hired hand’s intent to interfere with victim’s liberty, as required for a kidnapping conviction. The movement from one room to another was “merely incidental” to the other, intended offenses. State v. Zweigart, 344 Or 619,636–37 (2008).

Defendants’ intent to deprive the state of its property could be reasonably inferred by their act of taking the state-owned van from caseworkers, even when they had their own car by which they could have left the scene, and also by their act of abandoning the van in a secluded spot, such that the state might not have recovered it. State v. Christine, 193 Or App 800, 810–11 (2004).

Jury could infer defendant’s intent to kill infant-victim from evidence suggesting a motive. A written note and past behavior indicated that defendant was frustrated with the victim’s chronic crying and jealous over the amount of time the victim’s mother spent with the child. In addition, prior incidents of mistreatment made it less likely that the death was an accident. State v. Beason, 170 Or App 414, 426 (2000).

Note:Watch out particularly for the distinction between intent and knowledge, as in State v. Moreno, 197 Or App 59 (2005). Mr. Moreno was charged with possession of a precursor substance with the intent to manufacture. The state proved that he possessed a large amount of pseudoephedrine which he intended to sell on the street, knowing that any potential buyer would use it to manufacture methamphetamine. The court ruled, however, that there was no evidence that Mr. Moreno intended to personally manufacture methamphetamine. Therefore, he was not guilty. “At most . . . he intended to sell the Sudafed to someone else who would manufacture methamphetamine, which establishes his knowledge that the Sudafed would be used in that way but not his intent that it be so used.” Moreno, 197 Or App at 64. The court further rejected the argument that Mr. Moreno could have intended to aid and abet in the manufacture of methamphetamine by the potential buyer. A prosecution under an aid-and-abet theory would still require the state to prove that Mr. Moreno intended the manufacture of methamphetamine, which leads back to the fact that there was no demonstrated intent.

Note that the result would be the same even if there was no evidence that the defendant intended to sell the substance on the street. The state would still have to prove that defendant’s intent was to personally manufacture rather than possibly sell the pseudoephedrine to somebody else. In the absence of evidence either way, the defendant should be found not guilty.

This logic should apply to other situations as well. For example, people are commonly prosecuted for identity theft when they are in possession of forged or real drivers licenses in another person’s name. However, there is often no way for the state to prove that the defendant intended to use the identifications himself, rather than sell them to somebody else. Thus, there is no way to prove that the defendant had “the intent to deceive or defraud,” as required under ORS 165.800(1). Just as in Moreno, the state would be able to prove, at best, that the defendant knew that deception or fraud would occur.

b.How lay people (aka jurors) understand “intentionally”

We see intention everywhere around us, even though we believe just the opposite about ourselves. The fundamental attribution error is the name for the error we make when we attribute character traits to people we don’t know. When somebody cuts you off while you’re driving, you think to yourself, “What a jerk, that guy is a bad driver.” You don’t think to yourself, “That guy is probably distracted, maybe he’s on his way to the hospital because something happened to his wife.” You see the action and you attribute a character flaw. One time I was telling a friend about a manslaughter case in our office. The client had been distracted, ran a red light and killed someone. He was in no way impaired, had a perfect driving record and was cooperative and appropriately remorseful with the police. There was no evidence of negligence, much less recklessness. My friend said, “I don’t know. It’s just really difficult for me to believe that the guy wasn’t a terrible driver all the time.” That’s the fundamental attribution error – the guy did this thing, therefore, he’s like that all the time. Therefore, he’s a reckless, dangerous person who needs to be stopped. It all happens automatically. The first way it must be countered is by offering as much evidence as possible that the client is normally a responsible, safe driver. Second, it must be countered by offering as much evidence as possible that the client is a responsible, safe person. Third, it must be countered by introducing the jury to the client as a person. After all, we don’t make the fundamental attribution error about people we know. We only do it in a vacuum of knowledge about a person. The point here is that the fundamental attribution error is a useful shortcut that allows us to perceive the world correctly a large portion of the time. But also leads us into blazingly incorrect misperceptions as well.

Americans are especially likely to believe others are acting intentionally. The belief that others act intentionally is inextricably intertwined with the value of and belief in individual responsibility. Unfortunately for defense attorneys, there may be no nation in the history of the world that so deeply believes in individual responsibility.

Our moral judgment of an action also determines whether we view someone as acting intentionally. In one recent series of studies, subjects were asked whether a person was acting intentionally toward a result when the person didn’t care whether the result occurred or not. It turns out that how you feel about the result determines whether you think the person acted intentionally. Where the result is bad, the person is seen as acting intentionally. When the result is good, the person is not seen as acting intentionally. An example of intentional action is the following:

The vice-president of a company went to the chairman of the board and said “We’re thinking of starting a new program. It will help us increase profits, but it will also harm the environment. The chairman answered “I don’t care at all about harming the environment. I just want to make as much profit as I can. Let’s start the program.” They started the program and, sure enough, the environment was harmed.

An example of non-intentional action is the following:

The vice-president of a company went to the chairman of the board and said “We’re thinking of starting a new program. It will help us increase profits, and it will also help the environment. The chairman answered, “I don’t care at all about helping the environment. I just want to make as much profit as I can. Let’s start the program.” They started the program and, sure enough, the environment was helped.”

“Intention” is better than “intentionally.” In the previous example, far fewer people see intent when they are asked, “Was it the Chairman’s intention to harm/help the environment?” than when they are asked, “Did the Chairman intentionally harm/help the environment?” There is still a difference between harm and help, but significantly more people answer “No” when “intention” is used rather than “intentionally.” Rather than a large majority of people thinking the Chairman who harmed the environment acted intentionally, only about a quarter of people believe it was the Chairman’s intention to harm the environment. McCann (2005). Assuming jurors act similarly to other lay people, the difference would translated to approximately 3 or 4 jurors out of 12.

When harm has resulted, reckless behavior is seen as intentional. The previous set of studies was not set up to test the legal definition of recklessness, but they might as well have been. Recklessness is the conscious disregard of a substantial risk that a future event will occur. It’s like saying “I know that’s likely to happen but screw it.” This is exactly what the Chairman is saying: “I know that harm to the environment is likely to happen but screw it.” It was this attitude that subjects saw as intentionally harming the environment. Thus, defense attorneys should be very careful asserting a defense that the client didn’t act intentionally, he acted recklessly. There is a real likelihood that the jury will believe all the facts on which the defense is based but still believe the defendant was acting intentionally.

When choices are made, the chooser is seen as more responsible. For example, if Bob chooses to switch planes in order to leave 30 minutes earlier and, as a result, finds himself on the plane that crashes, he will be seen as more responsible for his fate than those who never made that last-minute choice. Americans especially like to believe that choice and responsibility are integrally intertwined, even for unforeseeable events. This is true even if a choice is made to do nothing. This propensity for constantly thinking about what could have happened in order to interpret what did happen is called counterfactual thinking.

3.Knowingly

ORS 161.085(8): “‘Knowingly’ or ‘with knowledge,’ when used with respect to conduct or to a circumstance described by a statute defining an offense, means that a person acts with an awareness that the conduct of the person is of a nature so described or that a circumstance so described exists.”

a.Case Law

In a prosecution for felon in possession of a firearm, the factfinder could reasonably infer the defendant knew he was a felon, where the prosecution presented evidence that he had plead not guilty and been instructed on his rights to appeal in his earlier felony trial – events that necessarily involved his participation. State v. Lockamy, 227 Or App 108, 114 (2009).

In a prosecution for criminal mistreatment, defendants were deemed to have knowingly withheld adequate physical care from their young children, where, for an extended period of time, their home was strewn with pieces of plastic that they admitted were choking hazards. State v. McCants, 231 Or App 570, 584 (2009) (petition for review granted by the Oregon Supreme Court, 348 Or 114 (April, 2010).

In a charge of encouraging child sex abuse, evidence that defendant took affirmative steps to locate a pornographic image online, view it, and then close the window, was sufficient to find that he had knowingly controlled a prohibited visual recording. State v. Ritchie, 228 Or App 412, 418 (2009).

In a charge of unauthorized use of a motor vehicle, evidence proved only that defendant had been driving a car reported stolen and told officers he rented the car from a stranger for $50. This was arguably sufficient to establish that he should have known the car might be stolen, but it was insufficient to establish actual knowledge that the car had been stolen. State v. Bell, 220 Or App 266, 271–72 (2008).

Where the judge in a status hearing revoked defendant’s release status and told him he had been “reduced to custody,” evidence was sufficient to find that defendant knew the courtroom had, in effect, become a “correctional facility.” When he subsequently escaped from the courtroom he was therefore liable for second-degree escape, which includes escape from a correctional facility. The State did not have to prove that the defendant understood the specific statutory difference between escape from custody and escape from a correctional facility. State v. Lane, 341 Or 433, 440–41 (2006).

The crime of endangering the welfare of a minor requires defendant’s knowledge of the minor’s age. Here, the 17-year-old minor did not offer his age, the defendant did not ask his age, the minor’s friend did not volunteer his age, and there was no other circumstantial evidence suggesting defendant knew the minor’s age. Evidence was therefore insufficient to prove the defendant knew that the alleged victim was a minor. State v. Dixon, 191 Or App 503, 506, 510 (2004).

In a prosecution for failure to appear, the state must prove the defendant knew she had to appear on the date in question. Here, the officer’s certification that he served the defendant with notice, and the fact that the service copy of the notice, which is ordinarily given to a defendant, was missing from the court file, constituted sufficient evidence for the trial court to find that the defendant had been served with notice and to therefore infer her knowledge of the obligation to appear. State v. Rogers, 185 Or App 141, 147 (2002).

Police officer’s acts of repeatedly showing up at a female citizen’s place of employment, following her home, and telling her that he believed she had been speeding in an intimidating way were sufficient to infer that he knew he was using his office in an unauthorized capacity. State v. Gove, 128 Or App 239, 243 (1994).

b.How lay people (aka jurors) understand “knowingly”

The most important thing to be noted about deciding whether someone “knew” is that it immediately devolves into the question of whether the person should have known. Both intent and recklessness are essentially about a person’s attitude toward a situation. Intent is an affirmative desire to see something happen. Recklessness is a risk assessment followed by “damn the risks, I’m doing it anyway.” Knowledge, however, is simply awareness of what’s out there. No matter our attitude, we’re aware of some things and unaware of other things. For a jury, there’s no way to address the question of awareness except to first figure out whether the defendant should have been aware, i.e., the circumstances or clues that would have made any person aware. The problem is that unless we as criminal defense attorneys push back, this is usually where it stops. “Should have known” gets substituted for “Did know.” This is true even though “should have known” is, at most, negligence. The only way to push back is to get jurors to understand how complicated awareness and knowledge are.

First, it’s impossible to unravel awareness from memory. For example, my wife told me last week that this Friday we’re going to a play. I listened to her but somehow it didn’t stick and by the time Friday rolls around I’ve completely forgotten about the play. Was I aware on Friday that I was going to a play? Of course not. I consciously thought through my evening and “going to a play” was not one of the possibilities I contemplated. Should I have been aware of the play? Absolutely. I would like to be the sort of husband that remembers everything his wife tells him. Besides, the fact that I was told seems like excellent evidence that I knew at a later date. This comes up with failure to appear and failure to register cases all the time. Just because a person was told at some point in the past doesn’t mean they knew on the day in question.

Which brings us to the second point: awareness is also intertwined with attention. Have you ever been reading a book and realized that you hadn’t actually processed the last page or so? You have to go back and read again what you just read. What was going on there? Your eyes were moving across the lines of the page but “you” weren’t paying attention. Your conscious mind had been drawn to other things like the laundry or the friend you’re meeting for lunch. But doesn’t the fact that you looked exactly like you were reading seem like excellent proof that you were aware of the ideas written on the page? There’s a wonderful attention study in which subjects are asked to watch a video of some high school basketball players and count the passes. During the video, a gorilla walks into the center of the screen, pounds his chest and walks away. A majority of people don’t see the gorilla. Yet wouldn’t the fact that you watched the video be excellent evidence that you saw the gorilla? Of course, if you know about the gorilla you’ll see the gorilla. But you still won’t notice other changes. (Check out the video yourself by searching for “The Monkey Business Illusion” on YouTube or by going to Daniel Simons’s website, [../../../Publications/mental%20health%20book/%202010%20edition/%20chapters%202010/chapter%203%20mental%20states%20-%20final%20pdf%20done/www.theinvisiblegorilla.com theinvisiblegorilla.com]. )

The truth is that we attend to only a small fraction of the information that floats through our immediate awareness. We process for immediate understanding even less. And we remember only a small portion of the information we process, most of which we’ll forget over time. So how does the DA prove that a person was consciously aware of a particular fact at a particular time?

An additional issue stemming from the aforementioned proof problem is hindsight bias: once we do know, we believe we should have known. Hindsight bias refers to people’s tendency to view an event as more inevitable and foreseeable after the event has occurred (Fischhoff, 1975, 1977; Hawkins & Hastie, 1990; Leary, 1982). Knowing the outcome of an uncertain event makes that outcome seem to have been more likely in the first place (i.e., in hindsight), compared to likelihood estimates made before knowing the outcome (i.e., in foresight). This retrospective distortion presumably results from an automatic cognitive assimilation of new and old information (Fischhoff, 1977). Bryant and Brockway, 1997.

This happens in almost every single criminal case. We know what happened because we’re looking at a past event. Because we know what happened it seems almost fated to have happened. But more importantly, it seems like the fated path should have been obvious to everyone before it happened. We know now, therefore, they knew then. It’s a real problem that has to be vigorously addressed by a defense attorney. The only way to counteract this very powerful bias is to use storytelling techniques to get the jury to feel what it was like to be the defendant at the time. Get them to feel the fear or the indecision or the hesitation before the unknown. It’s not like we, as humans, are unfamiliar with our ignorance. It’s just that once we know the answer or see a pattern, it seems obvious.

4.Recklessly

ORS 161.085(9):“‘Recklessly,’ when used with respect to a result or to a circumstance described by a statute defining an offense, means that a person is aware of and consciously disregards a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the result will occur or that the circumstance exists. The risk must be of such nature and degree that disregard thereof constitutes a gross deviation from the standard of care that a reasonable person would observe in the situation.”

One cannot attempt a reckless crime. State v. Smith, 21 Or App 270, 279 (1975) (“The legislative scheme, as a whole, is complete and consistent with the approach that ‘one cannot attempt to act recklessly’”).

a.Case Law

Allowing a two-year-old to wander the streets alone for six hours is not necessarily reckless. Here, evidence was insufficient to establish that defendant-mother did not search for her child, and therefore she may not have consciously disregarded the risk of harm. Moreover, even if she did not look for her child, the evidence still would only support a finding of negligence, as the defendant may not have recognized the risk of harm even if she should have. State v. Nelson, 224 Or App 398, 404 (2008).

Where the defendant testified that he intended to strike the alleged victim, the jury could not have found that he acted recklessly. Therefore, the trial court’s refusal to instruct the jury on the lesser-included offense of Assault III, a reckless crime, did not constitute error. State v. Taylor, 207 Or App 649, 666 (2006).

Evidence that defendant knew people worked in the building at all hours and that the victim-janitor was visible from the parking lot where suicidal defendant waited before driving his car into the building, was sufficient to infer that defendant acted recklessly in killing the janitor. State v. Schlender, 199 Or App 253, 258–59 (2005).

A decedent or third party’s contributory negligence is irrelevant to a charge of manslaughter, which is concerned only with whether the defendant acted recklessly. Here, testimony that construction site failed to meet safety standards was evidence of contributory negligence and was therefore irrelevant . Defendant’s testimony that he was aware construction was in progress was sufficient to find that he acted recklessly by driving into the construction zone. State v. Curtiss, 193 Or App 348, 352-54 (2004).

Defendant did not act recklessly where his attempt to stamp out a fire only spread it further, as there was no evidence that he was aware of the risk that stamping might spread the fire. Additionally, because defendant did not start the fire, he had no legal obligation to report it, and thus his failure to report it also could not constitute recklessness. State v. McBean, 189 Or App 235, 242 (2003).

The crime of murder by abuse requires that defendant have been reckless as to the risk of death. The same offense may count as aggravated murder if defendant intended to maim or torture the victim. However, the state’s burden to prove that defendant intended to maim or torture does nothing to change the lesser mental state requirement of recklessness as to the risk of death. State v. Compton, 333 Or 274, 280–81 (2002).

A jury instruction equating recklessness with gross negligence constituted reversible error, where the defendant was charged with Assault III, which requires the higher mental state of recklessness. The jury might have convicted with a finding of lesser culpability than that required under the statute. State v. Hill, 298 Or 270 (1984).

b.Voluntary Intoxication, ORS 161.125(2)

“When recklessness establishes an element of the offense, if the defendant, due to the use of drugs or controlled substances or voluntary intoxication, is unaware of a risk of which the defendant would have been aware had the defendant been not intoxicated, not using drugs or controlled substances, or not dependent on drugs or controlled substances, such unawareness is immaterial.”

This statute does not relieve the state of its burden to prove mens rea. It essentially says that reckless can be proved either by 1) establishing that the defendant consciously disregarded a substantial and unjustifiable risk of a particular result or circumstance; or 2) establishing that defendant (a) was intoxicated, (b) was unaware of the relevant risk because of the intoxication, (c) would have been aware of the risk had he not been intoxicated, (d) the risk was substantial and unjustifiable. The way this usually plays out is that the state will talk about how risky the behaviour is and then argue that evidence of extreme intoxication is irrelevant. But in that case the state has neither proven recklessness by the traditional means nor have they proven the four prerequisites for ORS 161.125(2) to be triggered.

The most difficult of the prerequisite elements for the state to prove is that the defendant would have been aware of the risk if not for the intoxication. The way the state proves this up is by establishing that a reasonable person would have realized the risk. But that doesn’t speak to the defendant in the particular case. If actually held to their burden, it would be almost impossible (and a little absurd) to prove what would be the content of a person’s mind if they existed in an alternate universe where they behaved exactly the same but weren’t intoxicated. It’s hard enough to prove the mental state of an existing defendant. How does one go about proving the mental state of an alternate universe defendant?

c.How lay people (aka jurors) understand recklessly.

Recklessness is not a heightened form of negligence. To the lay person, recklessness may indicate a kind of “negligence plus,” and indeed this is how recklessness began its life in the law. But in fact recklessness under the Oregon Criminal Code is a higher order mental function. To be criminally reckless, a defendant must have been aware of and then consciously disregarded the risk of a specific result coming to pass or the risk that certain circumstances existed, at the time of the alleged offense. This is a mental state entirely distinct from negligence, which is when a defendant should have recognized a risk but didn’t. Here, the state must prove that the defendant did recognize a risk and chose to proceed anyway. Rather than simply assert that there existed a risk a reasonable person would have recognized, as in a charge of negligence, for recklessness the state must prove that the defendant was able to and in fact did recognize the risk at the time of the alleged offense, and then consciously chose to disregard it. This is high order mental functioning, so evidence of an impaired mental state should be especially relevant for the factfinder’s determination of mens rea in such cases.

5.Criminally Negligent

ORS 161.085(1): “‘Criminal negligence’ or ‘criminally negligent,’ when used with respect to a result or to a circumstance described by a statute defining an offense, means that a person fails to be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk that the result will occur or that the circumstance exists. The risk must be of such nature and degree that the failure to be aware of it constitutes a gross deviation from the standard of care that a reasonable person would observe in the situation.”

Defendant’s failure to secure all the doors in the house, which allowed her four-year-old child to escape and ride his tricycle in the street, did not constitute criminal negligence, as it could not be considered a “gross deviation from a standard of care that a reasonable person would observe in the situation.” State v. Savage, 214 Or App 343, 347 (2007).

Jury could find that defendant acted with criminal negligence, where she left her one-year-old daughter in the car with the windows rolled down in a high-crime neighborhood. State v. Obeidi, 211 Or App 377, 383 (2007).

Defendants could not be held criminally negligent for leaving their seven- and nin-year-old children at home alone for 45 minutes while they took another child to the hospital. Although there may have been a possibility of danger, the danger was not sufficiently likely to make the parents’ conduct criminally negligent. State v. Paragon, 195 Or App 265, 271–72 (2004).

6.Strict Liability

Felony murder requires no culpable mental state as to the death of the victim. Rather, the commission or attempted commission of the predicate felony establishes, as a matter of law, the culpable mens rea for felony murder. State v. Blair, 348 Or 72, 80 (2010).

The crime of providing alcohol to a person under 21 is governed by strict liability and does not require a specific mental state. State v. McCathern, 211 Or App 171, 180 (2007).

B. DA Pleading

1.A higher mental state suffices to prove a lower one. ORS 161.125(3).

2.The DA is stuck with the mental state pled.

State v. Jones, 223 Or App 611, 622 (2008), “[D]efendant was indicted for “unlawfully and knowingly” taking a vehicle. Thus, the state was required to prove that defendant knowingly took the trailer in order to convict him of unauthorized use a vehicle.”

The state must prove precisely what it alleges in the charging instrument, even if the statute allows for conviction under lesser or alternate circumstances. Here, the state alleged that defendant had purchased an angling license while suspended. It was undisputed that he had an angling license while suspended, and the statute prohibited merely obtaining a license at such time. However, there was no evidence offered to prove that he had purchased the license, as alleged. Therefore, the trial court erred in denying the defendant’s motion for judgment of acquittal. State v. Remsh, 221 Or App 471, 475 (2008).

“Having alleged that defendant acted knowingly, the state was required to prove defendant’s knowledge.” State v. Lane, 341 Or 433, 440 (2006). But see State v. Rutley, 343 Or 368, 378 n 9 (2007), “Our disposition of the issue should not be read as accepting the Court of Appeals’ proposition that the State may be bound by the words of an indictment to prove a particular mental state respecting an offense, even if the legislature did not intend to require such an offense.” Between the statements in Lane and Rutley, it remains unclear where the Oregon Supreme Court stands on this issue. Rutley was handed down one year after Lane, and its Footnote 9 seems to contradict the quote from Lane immediately above, though it does not expressly overrule or even cite to Lane. It almost seems as if the Rutleycourt forgot what it had said a year earlier in Lane.

Moreover, the state cannot simply amend a grand jury indictment by striking the language regarding mental state (“knowingly” or “intentionally” as the case may be), as this would impermissibly alter the availability to the defendant of certain defenses. State v. Wimber, 315 Or 103, 114 (1992).

The state need not necessarily allege the requisite mental state for each element of an offense. In this prosecution for felon in possession of a firearm, the court found error where the charging instrument did not specifically allege that defendant knew he was a felon. However, it deemed this error harmless, as the charging instrument did generally allege that the defendant had acted “knowingly,” and the trial court instructed the jury that the state had to prove the defendant’s knowledge of his prior felony conviction. State v. Eberhardt, 225 Or App 275, 281 (2009); see also State v. Anderson, 233 Or App 475, 480 (2010).

C. All elements of an offense, excepting purely procedural elements such as venue, require a culpable mental state – unless the legislature specifically indicates otherwise.

1.ORS 161.095(2): “Except as provided in ORS 161.105, a person is not guilty of an offense unless the person acts with a culpable mental state with respect to each material element of the offense that necessarily requires a mental state.”

2.Commentary to the third draft of 161.095: “The phrase, ‘that necessarily requires a culpable mental state’ has been added to make it clear that the draft does not require scienter with respect to an element relating solely to the statute of limitations, jurisdiction, venue, etc., which may be elements of the crime but do not require a culpable mental state on the part of the actor.” (Proper attribution goes to Judge Rebecca Duncan from a lecture she gave at the 2008 OCDLA Winter Conference.) In other words, a material element necessarily requires a mental state if it is not jurisdiction venue or the like. All other material elements require a mental state.

3.ORS 161.115(1): “If a statute defining an offense prescribes a culpable mental state but does not specify the element to which it applies, the prescribed culpable mental state applies to each material element of the offense that necessarily requires a culpable mental state.”

4.Felon in Possession: The state must prove that, at the time the defendant was in possession or attempted to be in possession of a firearm, he knew or should have known that he had been convicted of a felony. State v. Rainoldi, 236 Or App 129, 140 (2010).

5.Theft: The state does not need to prove that defendant intended to steal property amounting to a particular value. Here, defendant was charged with Theft I, which required at the time of this incident that the property stolen had a value of $750 or more (now statutorily increased to $1000 or more). The state had to prove that defendant intended to deprive the owner of property, but did not have to prove that defendant knew the value of the property equalled $750 or more. State v. Jones, 223 Or App 611, 619 (2008).

6.Delivery of Controlled Substances Within 1000 Feet of a School:The state need not prove that defendant knew he was within 1000 feet of the school at the time of the offense. To require such knowledge would incentivize drug dealers to remain ignorant as to whether they were operating near a school, and would place an unrealistic burden on the State in its efforts to prosecute drug dealers and protect children from harm. State v. Rutley, 343 Or 368, 376–77 (2007).

7.Unlawful Possession of a Concealed Firearm: The state must prove that defendant knew the object he was carrying had the characteristics of a firearm, i.e., that it was capable of being fired. State v. Schodrow, 187 Or App 224, 231 (2003); see also State v. Andrews, 174 Or App 354, 356 (2001) (holding that the State must prove defendant knew firearm was loaded in prosecution for unlawful carrying of a loaded firearm per Portland city ordinance).

8.Failure to Perform Duties of a Driver Toward Injured Persons: The state must prove defendant knew he had been in an accident, but only that he acted with criminal negligence as to the risk that other people might be injured. State v. Hamlett, 235 Or App 72, 80 (2010).

9.Possession of a Controlled Substance: The state must prove that defendant knew she was in possession of a controlled substance but need not prove that she knew specifically which controlled substance it was. State v. Engen, 164 Or App 591, 595 (1999).

10.Assault: In a charge of knowingly causing injury to another, the state need only prove that defendant knew his conduct was assaultive and that his conduct did in fact cause injury. State v. Barnes, 329 Or 327, 338 (1999).

11.Rape: In charge for sexual intercourse with a person incapable of consent by reason of mental defect, the state does not need to prove defendant knew that the alleged victim was incapable of consent. The legislature has indicated that the knowledge of the alleged victim’s incapacity to consent is not a material element of the offense. State v. Phelps, 141 Or App 555, 558–59 (1996).

D. If the Statute Doesn’t Specify a Mental State Then Apply One

ORS 161.125(2): “Except as provided in ORS 161.105, if a statute defining an offense does not prescribe a culpable mental state, culpability is nonetheless required and is established only if a person acts intentionally, knowingly, recklessly or with criminal negligence.”

E. Non–Criminal Code Crimes: a mental state is not required if there’s a clear legislative intent to dispense with a mental state.

1.ORS 161.105(1): “Notwithstanding ORS 161.095, a culpable mental state is not required if: (a) The offense constitutes a violation, unless a culpable mental state is expressly included in the definition of the offense; or (b) An offense defined by a statute outside the Oregon Criminal Code clearly indicates a legislative intent to dispense with any culpable mental state requirement for the offense or for any material element thereof.”

2.Furnishing Alcohol to a Minor, ORS 471.410(2). The wording of the statute, the history of the law, and case law going back to 1887 clearly indicate that the legislature intended Furnishing Alcohol to a Minor to be a strict liability crime, requiring no culpable mental state. State v. McCathern, 211 Or App 171 (2007).

F. The mental state required in a criminal statute doesn’t carry through to the subcategory factors for the Sentencing Guidelines crime seriousness scale

“In some cases, a culpable mental state can travel far down a statute, but no case or logic supports the idea that it can also travel into a completely separate, nonstatutory, text. In sum, we conclude that an offense-subcategory fact is not an element of an offense to which a culpable mental state must apply… at least when the fact is not described in the statute itself.” State v. Travalini, 215 Or App 226, 234 (2007).

G. Accomplice liability requires intent to aid and abet in the specific offense charged.

To be convicted as an accomplice, defendant must have intended to aid or abet in the commission of the specific offense charged. In this aggravated murder case, the court strikes down Oregon’s longstanding “natural and probable consequences” jury instruction (UCJI 1052). This instruction allowed the jury to find a defendant liable for any additional offenses committed by his accomplice, where those offenses could have been foreseen. The court holds that the instruction is not supported by the accomplice liability statute, ORS 161.155, which requires specific intent to aid or abet in the commission of an offense. State v. Lopez-Minjarez,Or AppNo C053660CR, WL 2976516 (July 28, 2010).

VI. Criminality Means Conscious Choice But Your Conscious Mind Is Not the Control Center of You

As V.S. Ramachandran said in A Brief Tour of Human Consciousness(2004): “Your conscious life, in short, is nothing but an elaborate post-hoc rationalization of things you really do for other reasons.”

Based on what we now know about the human mind, mens rea creates an impossibly high burden for the state in many cases – if we make them prove it. Generally speaking, people are not rational beings that intend every action and assess the risks of every consequence. Rather, humans are rationalizers, coming up with reasons for their actions after the fact. The great bulk of our thinking, processing, preferences and decision-making are done beneath the surface of conscious awareness. This is true of intelligent people who consciously think and act and persuade for a living. It is doubly true of our clients, whose brains have been damaged by abuse, deprivation, drugs, illness, injury or cognitive disability. In either case though, we should force the state to prove the full extent of what the law requires: conscious intention, conscious awareness or conscious risk assessment.

The law has inextricably intertwined itself with an outdated understanding of the human mind. This outdated model is largely based on the Freudian version which itself is an extension of the ancient Cartesian version. These Freudian and Cartesian models of the inner self have been around for so long they have become common sense. When the DA asks the jury to use their common sense with regard to mental state, it is this model of a constantly intentional, thinking person to which they are appealing. But people are not really Cartesian any more than they are like the annoying “reasonable person” of torts fame, constantly worrying about and taking precautions against remote possibilities.

The Cartesian version of the human mind is as a controller in our head. If you think about it, you can almost envision a little person behind your forehead, looking out through your eyes, driving your body around and making rational decisions about what to do from moment to moment. In addition to the fact that it has become embedded into our cultural assumptions, it’s a powerful illusion because evolution slowly built an emergent, conscious self that would feel in control. In other words, we feel consciously in control because it’s really useful to feel in control. It makes life seem doable despite the hurdles.

Consider the alternative. Everything that we think, feel and do emanates from brain activity. That activity occurs in signals between neurons. There are more than one hundred billion neurons and each neuron has approximately ten thousand contact points (synapses) with other neurons. It is a cacophony of chemical and electrical messages. Imagine receiving ten thousand e-mails at once from each and every person on the planet and you still don’t come close to the amount of information we’re talking about. There is more information constantly being sent throughout the brain than the conscious mind can possibly conceive much less assimilate and use. It is far preferable to have only the most important information and simplistic decisions bubbling up to a conscious mind which can attend to one thing at a time.

But an illusion is still an illusion no matter how right it feels. The earth is round even though it really looks flat, and, frankly, it makes little sense that the people in China aren’t falling off the underside. Yet there it is. As a result, we walk around our daily lives assuming we’re in control despite the million assumptions of the neocortical brain required to create that illusion of wholeness, control and self.

Criminality means conscious choice.

In the picture below is a guy who is probably acting intentionally. He acquired a ski mask and a matching outfit. He got the gun. He chose the bank. He thought through what he would say to the teller and probably an exit plan.

But most of our cases aren’t like a bank robbery. They’re more like the following image situation, filled with alcohol and emotion and interrelational or group dynamics. In sum, it’s basically impossible to figure out what was in anyone’s mind.

The law does not punish behavior with a negative outcome. It punishes criminal behavior. Behavior is only criminal if it is accompanied by the requisite mens rea or criminal mental state. In Oregon, as in most states, the possible mental states are those which were proposed by the Model Penal Code in 1962: intentionality, knowledge, recklessness, negligence and strict liability. We can set aside, for this discussion, strict liability and negligence since neither requires any conscious cognitive processing. Strict liability is the absence of mental state, and negligence is a failure to be aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk. The remaining three mental states make up the great bulk of crimes in Oregon.

The statutory definitions are taken directly from the Model Penal Code, completed in 1962. The American Law Institute, through the Model Penal Code attempted to codify the mental state definitions that had grown separately in each state. The idea of a mental state itself is at least as old as Blackstone, writing in the 1700s: “To constitute a crime against human laws, there must be first, a vicious will; and secondly, an unlawful act of consequent upon such vicious will.” William Blackstone, 4 Commentaries on the Laws of England 21 (1769), as quoted in State v. Rutley, 343 Or 368 (2007). We have come a long way from a “vicious will” to awareness of conduct. Nonetheless, the proposition that a criminal act is only punishable when it is concurrent with a criminal state of mind is older than the U.S. Constitution and as close to the core of criminal law as it’s possible to get.

The Model Penal Code was largely written in the 1950s. The group of people responsible for the code was a remarkable amalgamation of distinguished academics, practitioners, psychiatrists and prison representatives. However, even if we presume that the group was diligent in including the most up-to-date science on the human mind, it is still impossible to ignore the scientific sea change in neuroscience since the 1950s. There were no MRIs or EKGs, and consequently no capability for seeing the brain in action. There were few psychiatric medications and consequently little understanding of how chemicals in the brain affected mood and behavior. Psychology and therapy were mostly practiced by medical doctors. Neuroscience was barely conceived. The idea of a cognitive unconscious, in which brain structures like the amygdala and the hypothalamus are far more in charge than the conscious mind, would have been absurd had it been suggested at all. The legal requirement that the conscious mind must make an assessment for most every crime would have been an unremarkable aspect to the endeavor. But now mental states like conscious intention, conscious awareness and conscious risk assessment stand out as clearly distinguishable from the work done by the cognitive or adaptive unconscious. One could argue that the vicious will required in Blackstone’s time could be achieved without conscious reflection. But not the intentionality, knowledge and recklessness of the MPC and the 1972 Oregon Criminal Code: except in the cases of negligence and strict liability, if there’s no conscious reflection, there’s no crime.

Your conscious mind is not the control center of you.

The conscious “I” is largely a pawn in a game being played by the cognitive unconscious. We like to think of ourselves as in control of our destiny, our preferences, our perceptions, our daily decisions, our goals and our desires. And we are in control. Just not consciously.

Although you probably take it for granted, you already know that your body, with the aid of some sort of unconscious thinking mechanism, is in a constant state of regulating and adjusting a thousand internal systems: sugars, salts, hormones, metabolites, oxygen, temperature, blood flow, immune function, etc. Less apparent is that these systems are not internal closed loops, ambivalent toward the outside world – they are adjusting in accordance with what you see and sense. The thousand systems are reacting to your external environment according to your needs. If you come across something that requires alertness (like a snake or a potential mate) your body reacts: increased cortisol, increased catecholamines, increased blood pressure, increased temperature, faster respiration, etc. Your body, in the form of chemicals, electrical signals and brain processing, reacts apart from and well before your conscious recognition of the external stimuli. Often what the conscious mind notices first is the change in physiological status like heart rate or quickened breathing, and only after that absorbs awareness of the external stimuli. For our purposes here, the most important aspect of this everyday experience is that you often see, think, process and react without consciously seeing, thinking, processing or reacting.

One interesting example of how we see without consciously seeing is face recognition. You aren’t consciously aware, for example, of how to make a genuinely happy face – a Duchenne smile. If you try to do it right now you will be unable to adjust the correct combination of facial muscles to create a Duchenne smile. The key to a genuine smile is the contraction of the zygomatic muscles around the eye (aka crows’ feet) in addition to the classically upturned mouth. Nor can you consciously see the muscles on someone else making the crucial alterations unless you’ve trained yourself to do so. But you do see and react to the most subtle flashes of emotion displayed on the faces of the people around you. Moreover, it works in the opposite direction as well. If you train yourself to adjust the correct facial muscles for a particular emotion, you will cause the physiological feeling to occur. Working your face into a Duchenne smile will give you a subtle but very real feeling of happiness. We also preferentially remember smiling faces. Scientific American, June 2008. That is, internal mechanisms recognize the actual smile and encode it in a separate pathway in the brain than if it were a different expression. And, if the smile is that of an infant there will be a spike in activity in the medial orbito-frontal cortex, inducing the release of pleasure chemicals even before the cognitive unconscious is able to see and recognize the face as a face.

Before getting too much deeper, it’s probably time to step back and briefly think about the structure of the brain itself. Lewis, Amini and Lannon lay out the current scientific understanding of the brain in their book A General Theory of Love (2000). The human brain has evolved to contain three distinct layers. The innermost and oldest layer, they term the “reptilian brain” as we share this brain with our cold-blooded friends. The reptilian brain is little more than the top of the spinal chord, directs automatic bodily functions such as breathing and digesting, and has a relatively basic role in our emotional lives. This control center contains the startle response, or our “fight or flight” instinct, and is the first part of the brain to come on-line in the life of a human. Incoming information passes first through this control center, and if stimuli are not perceived as immediately threatening, we are left with an unconscious impression of positive or negative. Reptiles though, like our reptilian brain, are unable to feel or care. They are just as likely to leave behind or eat their young as they are to let them be.

When mammals evolved, a new area of the brain emerged to allow for attachment, nurture, play, and the ability to sense the emotional states of other mammals. The second layer of the human brain, the limbic brain, is the center of emotions and social intelligence. It is the limbic brain that allows for intuition, empathy, and social order. However, “while having emotions is under limbic control, speaking of them falls under the jurisdiction of the neocortex,” (p. 57) the most recent area of the brain to evolve. The outer layer of the brain is responsible for thought, abstraction, and language. While this is the system that most assume to have the greatest power and the seat of will, “words, good ideas, and logic mean nothing to at least two brains out of three. Much of one’s mind does not take orders” (p. 33). While these three systems are distinct, they are also interrelated. For example, once the limbic brain senses excitement, it sends a message to the reptilian brain with instructions on heart rate and breathing while coordinating a thought directive to the neocortical brain. Similarly, ideas formed in the neocortex can trigger a limbic emotional response. This advanced understanding of the workings of the human brain complicates our assumptions of human behavior.

In moments which are not relaxed calm, the conscious mind is more controlled than in control. That is, it is not just an ignorant bystander. It is one of the thousand soldiers under the direction of the cognitive unconscious. Thus, as Malcom Gladwell points out in Blink, a decision to invite a coworker over for dinner is almost surely a conscious one, but a decision to get in a fight with a coworker is almost surely an unconscious one. In the decision to fight, you are “carried away” by your anger or frustration, unable to “control” yourself. Such phrases allow us to speak of a situation where we know the conscious mind is not in control but they refer to a Cartesian/ Freudian world in which the conscious mind is like a driver who is occasionally thrown around by the inclement weather of feelings seeping out from the unconscious. It’s difficult to drive in the thunderstorms of anger, sadness or panic but that’s still the conscious you behind the wheel.

In fact, the feelings we consciously perceive are but one small part of system-wide action initiated and directed by the limbic system. An example is provided by Drs. Lewis, Amini and Lannon:

A man is riding to work on a bus, heading for the financial district in downtown San Francisco. A tattooed teenager with a shaven head boards the vehicle, glares at the commuter, and bumps by him. That sensory experience flashes to the limbic brain, which will sift the event for its significance and prepare physiology to meet that singular moment. Our man’s limbic brain will receive input about the intruder’s facial expression, his pupil size, his body posture and gait, and perhaps even his scent. The limbic brain evaluates the nature of the other’s intention – is it careless, aggressive, friendly, sexual, submissive, indifferent? A given limbic brain arrives at conclusions based on the collaboration of its genetically specified wiring scheme and past experience of similar situations. In this case, let us suppose our man’s limbic apparatus detects hostility and, to meet the situation, equips him with the emotion of anger.

Once the limbic brain has settled on an emotional state, it sends outputs to the neocortical brain, spawning a conscious thought (Who the hell does this guy think he is?). At the same time, limbic outputs to the premotor areas of the neocortex are directing action-planning. Meanwhile, outputs to the endocrine system will alter stress hormone release, which may impact the entire body for hours or days afterward. Limbic instructions to the lower brain centers will cause facial muscles to contract in the configuration of anger: eyes narrowed, brows drawn together, lips pressed, with the edges of the mouth turned down. The limbic brain will direct the reptilian brain to change cardiovascular function. Heart rate will increase, as will blood flow to the arms and hands – because the outcome of anger may be a fight, the limbic brain readies the physiologic systems most suited to fisticuffs. The entire maneuver is executed with the speed and grace of a ballerina’s pirouette. One moment a man is minding his own business – two seconds later, anger swells, brow furrows, and his hands start to clench.

A General Theory of Love (2000), p. 54–55. Even in times of calm logic, the conscious mind is almost always late to the game. The intellectual superiority and control of the conscious “I” is largely an illusion that gets us through the day. Sure, there is some long-term planning and abstract thinking that is largely the province of the conscious mind, but for day-to-day activities, decisions and thinking, the conscious “I” is neither in control nor able to intellectually keep up with the cognitive unconscious. Most of the time, the conscious mind is not even being controlled – it’s just irrelevant. Like the third-string basketball player who is let into the game in the waning seconds because the team is ahead by so much that who won is no longer in doubt. The cognitive unconscious is a vast thinking machine that does almost everything that the conscious mind does but faster and first.

An Iowa gambling experiment headed by neurologist Antonio Damasio is such a wonderful example of the cognitive unconscious doing what seems to be conscious thinking that it has been discussed in many of the best and most popular books on the subject from Damasio’s own classic, Descartes’ Error (1994) to Malcom Gladwell’s Blink (2005) and Dan Ariely’s Predictably Irrational (2008). The experiment simply requires the subject to pick from either of two red decks or two blue decks of cards. To add some incentive, the subject can win or lose money depending on which card is chosen. Unbeknownst to the subject, there is a simple pattern: it’s impossible to win from the red decks, which offer occasional big payoffs but even more big losses while the blue decks give out small amounts with few losses. On average it took subjects approximately fifty cards to have a “hunch” that the blue decks are preferable and about 80 cards to “figure it out.” But by the fiftieth card, it hardly mattered because the cognitive unconscious sees the pattern around the tenth card and starts adjusting behavior immediately. At roughly the tenth card, the subject’s hands start sweating, respiration increases, muscles tighten and heart rate quickens when reaching for the red deck. As a result, the subject shows an increasing preference for the blue decks almost immediately. By the time forty more cards have been chosen and a conscious hunch appears, the person is already picking most of their cards from the blue decks.

Optical illusions also give us a clue as to how the thinking process works. The wizard behind the façade is momentarily revealed where a mistake is made. For example, two identical, side-by-side pictures of the leaning tower of Pisa appear to the eye as if the tower on the right leans more. It also works with two pictures of angled railroad tracks going off into the distance. As Macknik and Martinez-Conde explain it in a July 2008 article in Scientific American:

The Leaning Tower Illusion elucidates the way in which the visual system uses perspective to help reconstruct 3-D objects. We say “reconstruct” because the visual system has no direct access to three-dimensional information about the world. Our perception of depth results from neural calculations based on several rules. Such rules include perspective (parallel lines appear to converge in the distance), stereopsis (our left and right eyes receive horizontally displaced images of the same object), occlusion (objects near us occlude objects farther away), shading, chiaroscuro (the contrast of an object as a function of the position of the light source), and sfumato (the feeling of depth that one gets from the interplay of in and out of focus elements in an image, as well as from the level of transparency of the atmosphere itself). The Leaning Tower Illusion shows that the brain also uses the convergence angle of two reclining objects as they recede into the distance to calculate the relative angle between them.

Macknik and Martinez-Conde, “A Perspective on 3-D Visual Illusions,” SciAm.com, July 8, 2008. Thus, while the conscious mind can relax in the false belief that it is “seeing” the world as it exists, the cognitive unconscious is doing myriad calculations based on millennia of inherited experience just to figure out depth. Of course, calculating depth is only a small aspect of the highly complex system of perceiving that goes on entirely beneath the level of consciousness.

An interesting demonstration that awareness and the feeling of awareness are two wholly different things is the phenomenon of blindsight. When the primary visual cortex is destroyed (through surgery or cancer for example), so goes the ability to consciously see. But people can often see nonetheless. When shown pictures or dots, some people who have no apparent vision at all are able to “guess” the correct location or texture at rates far above chance. With practice, almost all such people improve their blindsight abilities. And some are able to correctly intuit subtle differences in shading better than those who are normally sighted. Susana Martinez-Conde, “Subconscious Sight,” Scientific American Mind, May 2008.

Like perception, memory is also a recreation each and every time. And, not surprisingly, memory is very much a part of perception itself. When an object is perceived, some information is conveyed to the higher order sections of the brain through the senses. Much more information, as mentioned earlier, is being sent by the parts of the brain doing the various calculations and thinking through all of the assumptions and rules on which perception is premised. A third set of information is being sent from the parts of the brain responsible for memory. It attempts to answer questions such as “Are there shapes, colors, movements or patterns with which I should be familiar?” and “How should I feel about this object?”

Unfortunately, where there is memory and emotion there will also be bias, expectations and mistakes. So, for example, where people are briefly shown a picture of a black man holding a cell phone or a camera there is a reasonable likelihood that the wallet will be “seen” as a gun. Erica Goode, “Researchers Link Guns to Stereotypes,” New York Times, Dec. 10, 2002. “Numerous studies over the last 30 years have found that in ambiguous situations, blacks are more likely to be perceived as violent than whites performing the same actions. In one study, the subjects saw two men engaged in a discussion in the course of which one man lightly pushed the other’s shoulder. When a black man pushed a white man, the action was described by the subjects as violent. When the situation was reversed, the push was perceived as playing.”

Finally, bias and perception are deeply integrated into normal thinking processes as well. For example, a phenomenon called stereotype threat and stereotype lift has been well documented. In a series of studies from the 1990s, African-Americans at Stanford performed significantly worse on the GRE when they were told it was a test which measured innate intelligence. Similar findings have been made across a broad spectrum of groups. For example, Asian females do worse on math tests when cued for being female and better when cued for being Asian than if they are not told anything before the test. White men performed worse at golf when they were told they were being compared to African-American men on a scale of athletic ability and they did worse than the norm when told they were being compared with African-American men on a scale that tested their strategic skill. Haslam, Salvatore, Kessler and Reichler, “The Social Psychology of Success,” Scientific American Mind, June 2008, p. 28). It appears that when stereotype and aspiration coincide there is a certain synergy that elevates performance. And when they collide the conflict interferes with performance. These studies are noteworthy in this context because the common conception of the consciously intentional, autonomous self doesn’t make room for behavior and performance to be so easily affected.

One does not have to have a self-perceived strength or weakness to be primed to behave a particular way. “Priming” is where the subject is slipped thematic words during a conversation or test thereby altering immediate behavior. For example, people who take a word test which is peppered with words like “Florida,” “bingo” and “wrinkle” will walk away from the test slower, more hunched and subconsciously feeling a little like an old person. Malcolm Gladwell discusses a particularly interesting priming experiment in Blink:

For example, on one occasion [John] Bargh and two colleagues at New York University, Mark Chen and Lara Burrows, staged an experiment in the hallway just down from Bargh’s office. They used a group of undergraduates as subjects and gave everyone in the group one of two scrambled-sentence tests. The first was sprinkled with words like “aggressively,” “bold,” “rude,” “bother,” “disturb,” “intrude,” and “infringe.” The second was sprinkled with words like “respect,” “considerate,” “appreciate,” “patiently,” “yield,” “polite,” and “courteous.” In neither case were there so many similar words that the students picked up on what was going on. (Once you become conscious of being primed, of course, the priming doesn’t work.) After doing the test, the students were instructed to walk down the hall and talk to the person running the experiment in order to get their next assignment.

Whenever a student arrived at the office, however, Bargh made sure that the experimenter was busy, locked in conversation with someone else – a confederate who was standing in the hallway, blocking the doorway to the experimenter’s office. Bargh wanted to learn whether the people who were primed with the polite words would take longer to interrupt the conversation between the experimenter and the confederate than those primed with the rude words. He knew enough about the strange power of unconscious influence to feel that it would make a difference, but he thought the effect would be slight. Earlier, when Bargh had gone to the committee at NYU that approves human experiments, they had made him promise that he would cut off the conversation in the hall at ten minutes. “We looked at them when they said that and thought, You’ve got to be kidding,” Bargh remembered. “The joke was that we would be measuring the difference in milliseconds. I mean, these are New Yorkers. They aren’t going to just stand there. We thought maybe a few seconds, or a minute at most.”

But Bargh and his colleagues were wrong. The people primed to be rude eventually interrupted – on average after about five minutes. But of the people primed to be polite, the overwhelming majority – 82 percent – never interrupted at all. If the experiment hadn’t ended after ten minutes, who knows how long they would have stood in the hallway, a polite and patient smile on their faces.

Malcolm Gladwell, Blink (2005), p. 54–55. Once emotions get involved in the mix, the conscious mind is but a pawn, a stranger to the unemotional self. The mind which is aroused, for example by lust, anger or envy, is a mind which is barely known to the dispassionate mind. Dan Ariely, in his book Predictably Irrational, recounts a series of experiments in which subjects were asked a series of questions and then asked the same questions again after being aroused. According to Ariely:

Our experiment at Berkely revealed not just the old story that we are all like Jekyll and Hyde, but also something new – that every one of us , regardless of how “good” we are, underpredicts the effect of passion on our behavior. In every case, the participants in our experiment got it wrong. Even the most brilliant and rational person, in the heat of passion, seems to be absolutely and completely divorced from the person he thought he was. Moreover, it is not just that people make wrong predictions about themselves – their predictions are wrong by a large margin.

Dan Ariely, Predictably Irrational (2008), p. 99.

VII. Bringing It Home

Our clients are much less likely than us to have conscious control over their behavior. A limited sort of control can be brought about by recognizing the earliest signs of physiological arousal and withdrawing from the situation while using calming techniques. The stronger one’s frontal cortex, the easier it is to accomplish this task. Those who may be unable to accomplish such a task include people with major psychotic disorders, mood disorders, PTSD, ADD, cognitive disabilities, mental retardation or head injuries. That is, a huge portion of our clients.

Another large portion of defendants unable, perhaps, to consciously control their behavior when emotions are strong are those who have had disorganized, insecure or absent attachment patterns with their caretakers when in infancy. Bad parenting causes ill-formed frontal lobes and a more powerful reptilian structure. It turns out that the amygdala is on-line and doing computations of negative and positive valence from the first moment of waking life. The structures of memory as we know it in the hippocampus and other structures don’t start working until the second or third year of life. Our early emotional experiences in life can shape what we’re like later in our relationships and behavior even if we can’t consciously remember what happened. Dacher Keltner, “Emotions and the Brain,” UC Berkeley Psych 156 lecture, 2007.

So, conscious thinking often doesn’t precede action, especially with those whose executive functioning is compromised. That is, most of our clients. But still, you might reasonably ask: how is this knowledge going to help me in a trial?

A. Force the State to Meet Its True Burden

Forcing the state to meet its true burdenwould be a huge step forward. If the crime requires recklessness toward the result of physical injury, then the state has to prove beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant went through a conscious risk assessment as he was swinging his fist. I find it useful to state out loud the content which the state has to prove. In this case, the DA has to prove that the following content was in the defendant’s mind: “I know that there is a substantial risk that if I punch this person I will cause physical injury but I’m disregarding that risk and moving forward with this punch.” It is also useful to say it this way to the jury. The primary problem this tactic heads off is that the DA usually attempts to prove that the person should have known there was a substantial risk. But, obviously that’s not the state’s burden. The .A must prove that the defendant, at the time of the act, consciously thought about and then consciously disregarded a substantial risk of physical injury. If the defendant was only thinking about the punch then he wasn’t doing a risk assessment. If he was completely carried away by his emotions then he wasn’t doing a risk assessment. If his head was filled with psychosis, good luck proving what was in his head.

At this point, the most common response when I’ve had this informal conversation with people (defense attorneys and otherwise) is that if we make the state really prove mental state, it would destroy the criminal justice system. The state wouldn’t ever be able to win. My response is twofold. On the one hand, this is an overreaction. When most people think of criminals and criminal acts they don’t think of impulsive behavior, they think of truly conscious, intentional behavior: the stranger rapist waiting in the bushes, the identity thief that has meticulously stolen your financial life, the battering husband who uses his fists and threats to keep his victim cowering in fear, the robber who sticks a gun in your face and demands money, and even the shoplifter who takes a five finger discount. These are all people who are clearly thinking through their criminal actions. In fact, I would argue that “thinking through it” is at the very heart of what it means to be criminal. Juries instinctively know that not all bad acts or negative results are criminal cases. And the criminal cases that immediately come to mind are “thinking through it” cases. What the outside world doesn’t know is that a huge portion of criminal cases are ones that are either impulsive acts or ones where the state has almost no evidence of what was in the person’s mind: the felony elude case where the driver freaks out at the police lights and hits the gas, the defendant who fails to appear for court, the person who is in a place from which he was trespassed months or years before, the drunk couple who get in a fight, and any of a million aid and abet situations.

The second part of my response is: if it is true that a significant percentage of cases will be unwinnable, wonderful; because those are exactly the cases that shouldn’t be in the criminal justice system in the first place. Those are the cases that have been shifted over from the mental health system when the mental health system started to crumble. Those are the cases that get charged with little to no investigation. Those are the cases where the state is assuming the presence of the required mental state as if “should know” and “did know” were identical.

B. Use Disordered Mental State Evidence Whenever Possible

If even a healthy, ordered mind cannot be cleanly parsed and reconstructed, then the processes of a disordered mind – a mind suffering from mental illness – are virtually impossible to divine. By presenting disordered mental state evidence wherever possible, and by making it clear to the jury that this evidence goes straight to the heart of the mens rea inquiry, you can get a jury to understand how difficult it is for the state to meet their burden to prove specific mental content beyond a reasonable doubt. Be sure to highlight the fact that the mens rea alleged is an integral element of the offense. If the state has not established beyond a reasonable doubt what went through the defendant’s mind, then the verdict must be not guilty.
