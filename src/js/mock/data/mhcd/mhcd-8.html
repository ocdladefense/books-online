Mental Health and Criminal Defense

2010 edition

Clinical Assessment and Testing

Keith I Linn, PsyD

(edited by Melissa Masini, MA, CADC)

Defense attorneys working with mentally ill defendants are faced with diagnostic and therapeutic issues that would be difficult even for experienced psychologists and psychiatrists. The new attorney is commonly exposed to clients with pathology comparable to patients in inpatient facilities. Neither solutions nor prognosis can be given at a glance. A full assessment with testing is almost always necessary. This chapter is designed to give attorneys some basic knowledge about the tools used by mental health experts in a full and complete clinical assessment.

I. Introduction

Most clinical assessments involve both a medical and a mental health evaluation. The medical examination also known as the physical examination or clinical examination is the process by which the physician investigates the body of a patient for signs of disease. The medical examiner asks a series of questions about recent and past physical functioning in the service of developing a differential diagnosis (account of possible illnesses). The medical examiner will review the client’s past medical history, familial medical history, and history of medication use. The health professional may evaluate the client’s nervous system by testing reflexes, balance, and senses (hearing, taste, sight, smell, and touch).

A psychological evaluation commonly involves a review of records, a mental status examination, and psychological testing. The psychological testing piece is the variable which distinguishes psychological evaluation from other types of mental health assessments. The evaluation process generally includes the following major phases: 1) Establishment of Referral Question; 2) Record Review; 3) Clinical Interview; 4) Psychological Testing.

II. Referral Questions

The establishment of the referral question is one of the more important aspects of the forensic evaluation. The referral question represents a distillation of an attorney’s goals for the mental health professional’s role in the client’s defense. This also signifies a meeting of the minds between counsel and mental health professional as to the focus of the evaluation. Determining the clinical focus will likewise determine the nature of the clinical interview, the choice of psychological tests (if any), the use of collateral sources, and the use of legal references.

Referral questions may be broad, “Please conduct a psychological evaluation on my client” or quite detailed, “Please determine if my client’s level of mental impairment impacts his ability to aid and assist in his/her legal defense.” In general, a more detailed referral question provides the most guidance to the forensic examiner in structuring the evaluation to the most essential and relevant areas of interest. Counsel is encouraged to discuss the referral question with a prospective evaluator to ensure common understanding.

III. Record Review

Supportive documentation may also be referred to as third-party information or third-party data. Given the nature of forensic assessment, collateral data provides the evaluator a clear statement of the case facts. Of equal importance, the supportive documents and collateral sources allow the evaluator to rely on information from sources other than the examinee. (In forensic type evaluations, examinees are often prone to misrepresent aspects of their crimes and histories to enhance their chances of a favorable outcome. In addition, the subject of the examination may not be a reliable and/or competent historian due to mental or intellectual disturbance.)

The type and extent of records will depend on the nature of the referral question. For example, in an assessment of dangerousness or Measure 11 considerations, a comprehensive history of past legal records is critical. In an aid and assist assessment, documentation of recent functioning is especially important.

First and foremost, the examiner should be provided a copy of the legal records outlining the criminal allegations. It will be difficult for the examiner to judge the defendant’s competence if the examiner is ignorant to the reality of the defendant’s situation. While such records are important, attorney-examiner consultation may provide additional and nuanced information not found in the written documents. Melton (1994) suggested that the examiner should ask the attorney the following questions prior to the competency evaluation:

  • Describe the charges for which defendant is standing trial and the underlying facts.
  • Describe potential penalties.
  • Describe the specific behavior of the defendant which leads you to believe that he or she may be incompetent to stand trial or was suffering from significant mental abnormality at the time of the offense (e.g., detached and indifferent, depressed, hostile, chaotic, peculiar speech content, hallucinations, history).
  • Have you observed this behavior yourself? If not, who are the sources of these observations?

The examiner is also encouraged to pursue and obtain a release of information to discuss the examinee’s recent functioning in other contexts such as jail or a hospital milieu. Such consultation may help provide a point of comparison to help fully consider malingering.

Common Types of Third-party Information:

1. Documents

  • Arrest Report
  • Prior Mental Health History
  • Victim or Witness Statements
  • Prison Records
  • Prior Legal History: Adult and Juvenile
  • School Records
  • Employment Records
  • Medical

2. Collateral Interviews

  • Attorney
  • Family, Significant Others
  • Past and Present Therapists and Hospital Staff
  • Employers
  • AA/NA Sponsors
  • Spiritual or Religious Leaders

Attorneys should be wary of examiners who do not assertively request a complete account of the legal and mental health histories. This may reflect a lazy or ill-informed clinician who will be vulnerable to later cross-examination questions dealing with the impact of missing documents in his or her findings.

Grisso (1986) provides a useful summary of the data collection methodology of noted forensic examiners Seymour Halleck and David Shapiro:

  • A review of all available police reports and other legal documents related to the alleged offense.
  • Interviews with any individuals in contact with the defendant in the period of time leading up to, during, and after the alleged offense, or review of written statements by those individuals.
  • Interviews with family members and friends.
  • Review of the defendant’s criminal justice and mental health records prior to the event.
  • Review of jail and/or hospital ward notes and records concerning the defendant’s incarceration between the time of arrest and the assessment, and during the period of time over which the assessment occurs.
  • Interview with the defendant over many hours, with a focus on reconstructing the events surrounding the offense, the defendant’s mental state before and during the offense, and their relation to each other.
  • Interview with the defendant’s attorney (if appropriate with regard to the expert’s role in the case) to obtain information of which the expert may not be aware from other sources, as well as the attorney’s direct observations of the defendant.
  • Performance of neurological, psychological, and neuropsychological testing when such information may address specific questions raised by the data from other sources.

As noted previously, clues as to the examinee’s state of mind at the time of the instant offense may be evident from interviews with people who associated with the defendant prior to the offense or soon after her arrest. Police records such as victim and witness statements may contain allusions to erratic or bizarre behavior as well as statements attributed to the examinee during commission of the offense. In many cases, the examiner should pursue follow-up interviews with individuals who witnessed the offense after discussing it with the defendant’s attorney. Statements to the police must be considered within an adversarial context. That is, police officers’ reports are often biased in their favor and defendants may attempt to engage in impression management. Jail and/or hospital records offer a unique glimpse of functioning close to the time of the instant offense.

Third-party information obtained prior to the clinical interview allows the examiner to focus on issues germane to the referral question. Without ample discovery material at the time of the assessment, the chances of delay in the generation of the written report becomes likely.

IV. Clinical Interview

In a broad sense, clinical interviews are utilized by the evaluator to gain a first-hand account of an examinee’s life. This narrative will provide the reader with a sense of how the accused has come to this particular place in his or her life. In many cases, a comprehensive account of the accused’s childhood, as well as traumas or other adversities may provide a degree of sympathy and empathy for the subject.

A forensic examiner is looking, in a broad sense, for inconsistencies across the available data. Such inconsistency, however, does not necessarily equate to malingering. Through record review, the examiner may develop hypotheses regarding typical functioning and how certain behaviors manifested themselves in other contexts. For example, in a case involving murder, an evaluator would want to assess if a behavioral pattern including acts of physical violence directed toward others existed prior to the instant offense.

In clinical interviews involving MSO, the examiner is strongly encouraged to take verbatim or precise and detailed notes of the examinee’s questions regarding the instant offense. Admissions made in clinical interviews may carry as much weight or significant consequences as disclosures made during police investigations. Audio and/or video taping of the clinical interactions between examinee and examiner are often necessary. Differences in an examinee’s past and present behavior may be attributed to the following factors: (a) effects from psychotropic medication, (b) significant alterations of the examinee’s level of stress, (c) distortion, whether conscious or unconscious, of the defendant’s memory and perceptions of the incident, and (d) education from external sources regarding impression management (Goldstein et al., 2004).

Clinical interviews focus on reconciling inconsistencies between police reports and the examinee’s account. GEI assessments have a particular focus on explanation and insight into the behaviors and choices made during the instant offense. GEI assessments also place particular emphasis on general functioning during the week prior to the relevant criminal activity. It is presumed that the defendant would exhibit diminished functioning in other areas prior to and immediately following the instant offense. Rogers (quoted in Goldstein, 2004) encourages the examiner to reconcile functioning related to the criminal behavior with comparable emotional states preceding the offense. Questions such as the following may elicit useful data: “You said that you had felt this way before but never did anything like what you were accused of. What was there about this situation that was different?” Regardless of time constraints, an evaluator should not undertake a clinical interview of a defendant without having obtained and reviewed all relevant discovery materials.

Given the complexity and the relatively high number of tests suggested for GEI assessments, forensic experts such as Goldstein (2004) and Rogers (2005) recommend that such evaluations be spread across multiple sessions. This arrangement enhances examiner-examinee rapport and diminishes the possibility of fatigue. The examiner should consider use of nonleading and open-ended questions. Special emphasis should also be placed on the thoughts, feelings, and behaviors relevant to the instant offense.

Broadly speaking, GEI clinical interviews address several considerations: nature and quality of crime; wrongfulness of behavior; and conformity of conduct.

In the assessment of the defendant’s ability to aid and assist, it is incumbent on the forensic examiner to answer the question referred as well as provide a clear picture of current intellectual, characterological, emotional, and basic neurological functioning. Given the apparent simplicity of the question of competence, clinicians may tend to merely answer the question without providing a sense of diagnostic understanding or recommendations for amelioration of deficient functioning.

A traditional clinical interview may help provide valuable information into the examinee’s mental health history. Furthermore, by asking questions not directly related to the referral issue the examiner is able to establish rapport, which may mitigate aspects of performance anxiety connected with competency-type interviewing. Such rapport is especially necessary given that examiners are interfacing with often quite paranoid and/or hostile individuals. Understanding an individual’s social history may provide insight into the examinee’s capacity to interact with legal counsel.

Clinical interviews generally include the following:

  • Developmental history
  • Academic and employment history
  • Relationship history
  • Medical and mental health history

– Psychiatric hospitalization

– Suicidality

– Medication

– Head injury

  • Legal history

The examiner is interested in determining a baseline of emotional, intellectual, and behavioral functioning. However, given the nature of the key referral question, special emphasis is placed on recent functioning. For example, lines of inquiry around current medication regimen, mental health treatment, recent interactions with the legal system, head trauma, and institutional deportment warrant special attention. The clinical interview may also provide attitudes and data to serve as a point of comparison during portions of the assessment which may be more susceptible to malingering.

Rogers et al. (2005) recommends structured interview formats such as the Structured Interview of DSM-IV Disorders (SCID) and Schedule of Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (SADS). These instruments allow the examiner a structured means of addressing specific criteria related to diagnoses often relevant to issues of competence.

In conjunction with the clinical interview, aid and assist assessments should address aspects of current mental status. Mental status examinations commonly address the following traits: orientation to time, place, person, and current situation; long or short-term memory; fund of knowledge; attention/concentration; social judgment; and verbal abstract reasoning. Mental status examinations provide the examiner with a brief overview of the examinee’s level of functioning at the time of the assessment. Impressions generated from this clinical interaction may inform which types of psychological instruments should be incorporated into the battery of tests. Data generated in this portion of the examination may demonstrate the necessity for comprehensive intellectual testing (e.g. WAIS-R) or neuropsychological instruments.

Clinical interviews often begin with a Mental Status Examination (MSE). The purpose of a mental status examination is to assess the presence and extent of a person’s mental impairment. In cases where a potential examinee shows evidence of pronounced emotional or cognitive impairment, a clinical interview may need to be altered to accommodate the examinee’s ability. In addition, the MSE may provide information on the nature and type of psychological testing needed later in the evaluation. The MSE may be very structured and extensive or quite brief. The following represent major themes of a mental status examination from the Folstein Mini-Mental Status Examination (1975):

  • Appearance. The examiner notes the person’s age, race, sex, civil status, and overall appearance. These features are significant because poor personal hygiene or grooming may reflect a loss of interest in self-care or physical inability to bathe or dress oneself.
  • Movement and behavior. The examiner observes the person’s gait (manner of walking), posture, coordination, eye contact, facial expressions, and similar behaviors. Problems with walking or coordination may reflect a disorder of the central nervous system.
  • Affect. Affect refers to a person’s outwardly observable emotional reactions. It may include either a lack of emotional response to an event or an overreaction.
  • Mood. Mood refers to the underlying emotional “atmosphere” or tone of the person’s answers.
  • Speech. The examiner evaluates the volume of the person’s voice, the rate or speed of speech, the length of answers to questions, the appropriateness and clarity of the answers, and similar characteristics.
  • Thought content. The examiner assesses what the patient is saying for indications of hallucinations, delusions, obsessions, symptoms of dissociation, or thoughts of suicide. Dissociation refers to the splitting-off of certain memories or mental processes from conscious awareness. Dissociative symptoms include feelings of unreality, depersonalization, and confusion about one’s identity.
  • Thought process. Thought process refers to the logical connections between thoughts and their relevance to the main thread of conversation. Irrelevant detail, repeated words and phrases, interrupted thinking (thought blocking), and loose, illogical connections between thoughts, may be signs of a thought disorder.
  • Cognition. Cognition refers to the act or condition of knowing. The evaluation assesses the person’s orientation (ability to locate himself or herself) with regard to time, place, and personal identity; long- and short-term memory; ability to perform simple arithmetic (counting backward by threes or sevens); general intellectual level or fund of knowledge (identifying the last five presidents, or similar questions); ability to think abstractly (explaining a proverb); ability to name specified objects and read or write complete sentences; ability to understand and perform a task (showing the examiner how to comb one’s hair or throw a ball); ability to draw a simple map or copy a design or geometrical figure; ability to distinguish between right and left.
  • Judgment. The examiner asks the person what he or she would do about a commonsense problem, such as running out of a prescription medication.
  • Insight. Insight refers to a person’s ability to recognize a problem and understand its nature and severity.

A. Interview Tactics Specific to GEI: Capacity to Appreciate Criminality

In assessing whether the examinee was suffering from delusions at the time of the incident, the examiner is encouraged to allow the examinee to provide free and uninterrupted explanation of his actions immediately before, during, and soon after the offense. This prong seeks to determine what the criminal actions meant to the defendant at the time of the offense. In other words, what was this individual’s sense of the goals and consequences of his actions? Through this inquiry, the defendant may disclose delusional or nonsensical motivations without a logical sense of consequence. Questions such as the following may be useful:

  • Did you understand what could happen if you were caught doing this crime?
  • What did you hope would happen as a result of your engaging in this criminal type behavior?

Coaching and/or repeated questioning of the examinee may create a higher level of rational understanding than may have been present during the instant offense, and should thus be avoided. This prong seeks to determine the examinee’s knowledge of the immediate consequences of his/her actions at the time of the incident (Rogers et al., 2005).

Rogers and Shuman (2005) outline four common explanations of defendant behavior:

  • Self-defense
  • Officially sanctioned duties: Examinee may believe that his or her actions are part of governmental duties.
  • Misconstrued exigencies: The examinee may be operating under the belief that his or her behaviors are justified to avoid greater harm.
  • Divine authority: Examinee may believe he or she is following the direction of God or another divine being.

The defendant may have possessed a belief that his or her life was in jeopardy or that a higher power or agency had provided license for the criminal behavior to occur. These ideations may lead to an internal blurring of concepts such as “wrong” and “illegal.” For example, a defendant may appreciate that his or her behavior is illegal but believe that it is necessary because a divine authority says so. In this case, the evaluator should determine whether there is a complete and full belief in the divine agency. Such belief of agency alone does not necessarily justify a finding of insanity under the wrongfulness prong. Rogers (2005) suggests that the defendant must also believe that his need to act or sense of threat be immediate.

B. Interview Tactics Specific to GEI: Capacity to Conform Conduct

Rogers (2005) expressed strong opinions that clinicians could adequately distinguish between volitionaland cognitiveabilities. He recommends that forensic evaluators engage in “painstaking” questioning of the examinee’s level of planning, preparation, execution, and post-event actions. In essence, the examiner is seeking to distinguish between “compelled” behaviors and behaviors motivated by rational choice.

The examiner may ask questions to ascertain the presence of an “irresistible impulse” during the instant offense. In other words, did the examinee have capacity to withhold the criminal behavior, or was there uncontrollable impulse that stemmed from a mental disorder? Finally, Rogers (2005) looks at the dynamics of the offense through the “policeman-at-elbow rule.” In other words, would the disorder have made the defendant act similarly even if a police officer had been present?

V. Psychological Testing

Psychological testing comes in a variety of forms and uses. Regarding the forensic domain, the choice of such testing involves consideration of multiple factors. Clinical and diagnostic decisions based on such testing are subject to the scrutiny of legal decisions such as Daubert and Frye. Tests which may be appropriate for therapeutic use or general assessment of functioning may not be appropriate for use in court proceedings. A forensic evaluator who bases his or her clinical conclusions on instruments which are based on limited or controversial research is vulnerable to rigorous cross-examination and diminished influence to judge and jury. The reader is referred to the “Mental Measurement Yearbook” (Buros Institute of Mental Measurement or http://buros.unl.edu) as a resource to describe commonly used psychological testing instruments.

Psychological testing can be over- or underused in the assessment of dangerousness. For example, an evaluator who bases conclusions on an offender’s scores on the MMPI-2 runs the risk of false positive and false negatives. On the other hand, the evaluator who does not administer psychological testing does not provide the court with information to fortify diagnostic impressions or clinical needs. In forensic evaluations, psychological testing provides invaluable insight into an examinee’s defense structure.

In general, testing is used to develop clinical hypotheses regarding enduring personality traits, emotional state, and neurological functioning. No test can or should be used by itself to formulate DSM-IV-TR diagnoses. Similarly, the absence of pathology found in testing should not be confused with adequate functioning. Tests should be chosen on a client- and case-specific basis. Psychological testing, while commonly used in insanity evaluations, can be controversial. An individual presenting as either floridly psychotic or highly intact may or may not have possessed such functioning at the time of the offense. On the other hand, Goldstein et al. (2004) found that psychometric tests may help to determine neurological, intellectual, and character-based dysfunction. Regarding the R-CRAS and MSE, Grisso (1986) concluded, “[t]heir primary strength lies in their potential to structure various aspects of the assessment related to legally relevant description.” First, they can guide the collection of data so that it does not occur haphazardly and so that potentially useful information is not overlooked. Second, they produce some uniformity in the assessment process, potentially facilitating descriptive communication between examiners as well as comparisons across cases. Finally, their grounding in an analysis of the legal standards for insanity give them the potential to clarify relations between clinical descriptions and the legal question.

Assessment of insanity should include the use of traditional psychological testing instruments as well as protocols developed specifically for use in a forensic context. The preeminent forensic evaluators, Goldstein, Rogers, and Heilbrun, suggest use of psychological testing as part of a thorough assessment of issues related to insanity. However, testing cannot provide hypotheses as to the examinee’s mental state at the time of the instant offense. In cases where the examinee’s present emotional functioning is comparable to that in existence at the time of the offense, testing may provide clearer insights.

A minority of forensic psychologists (Morse, 2004) do not recommend the use of psychological testing instruments in addressing forensic issues related to GEI. They contend that clinicians will draw conclusions of mental status at the time of the instant offense from testing administered long after the fact. In addition, critics note that testing instruments such as the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI-2) and Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI) were not developed using populations relevant to the assessment of sanity. However, Goldstein (2004) states that most experts endorse judicious and case-specific use of psychological testing. Test results are generally given lesser weight than behavioral data. Given the exclusionary criteria of some mental disorders such as Antisocial Personality Disorder and/or substance abuse, the forensic examiner is encouraged to structure the evaluation to consider such issues.

Issues related to the assessment of GEI often include:

  • Presence of major mental illness
  • Presence of substance abuse
  • Presence of personality disorder (e.g., psychopathy or Antisocial Personality Disorder)
  • Presence of malingering and positive impression management
  • Presence of neuropsychological deficits (e.g., dementia, head injury)
  • Presence of developmental disability (e.g. mental retardation)

There is a broad consensus that ruling out the possibility of malingering and diminished intellectual functioning is important in assessing insanity. Stephen Lally (2003) surveyed forensic experts regarding “acceptable” tests used in the assessment of “Mental State at the Time of Offense Evaluations.” Sixty percent of experts (forensic diplomats) commonly used the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-III (WAIS-III), and 54 percent commonly used the MMPI-2.

Despite the rate of use and reverence of psychological testing in forensic arenas, a limited number have specific relevance to issues before the court. In addition, many psychological instruments were not normed on criminal populations. Thus, clinicians are vulnerable to making inferences about legal matters which may go well beyond the original intent and purpose of the testing. For example, the WAIS-III may clearly define a defendant as mentally retarded based on the I.Q. score. However, this same individual may be capable of aiding in his legal defense despite intellectual deficits. Legal counsel should inquire into the nature and development of the tests used to make clinical conclusions. In general, psychological testing is based on the premise that when an examinee produces a testing profile, the chances increase that this individual shares basic characteristics with other members of that profile group. The confidence of such correlations may be questioned if other behaviorally based documentation does not support the tests findings. It is incumbent on the attorney to reconcile the degree of similarity and disparity between the defendant and a test’s reference group.

As noted previously, research like that undertaken by Lally can help the forensic practitioner choose instruments which may satisfy Daubert and Fryecriteria of “general acceptance.” Lally’s research also concluded the following:

Acceptableinstruments in the measurement of Mental State at the Time of Offense:

  • Rogers-Criminal Responsibility Assessment Scales (R-CRAS)
  • Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI)
  • Stanford-Binet-Revised (intelligence protocol)
  • Halstead-Reitan (neuropsychological protocol)
  • Luria Nebraska (neuropsychological protocol)

Equivocalinstruments in the measurement of Mental State at the Time of Offense:

  • Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory-II (MCMI-II)
  • Rorschach Inkblot Test (projective personality assessment)
  • 16-PF (personality assessment)

Unacceptableinstruments of Mental State at the Time of Offense:

  • Projective Drawings
  • Sentence Completion
  • Thematic Apperception Test (TAT)

VI. Types of Psychological Testing

A. Structured Clinical Diagnostic Interviews

These are highly structured interviews that formalize inquiries about the nature and extent of symptoms which closely track diagnoses found in the DSM. In practice, the examiner will ask the examinee or collateral sources a series of questions designed to rule in or rule out a particular diagnosis. Examples: Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (SADS) and Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV (SCID).

B. Projective Personality Testing

Such tests require the examinee to view, interpret, and describe ambiguous stimuli such as an ink blot or seemingly neutral image. Through “projection” of their unique interpretations onto the stimulus, the examinee is said to reveal something about the ways in which she views and comprehends her environment. From the projected responses, the examiner draws inferences about diagnosis and personality traits. Despite efforts to standardize projective-type tests such as the Rorschach, their use in forensic matters remains controversial and not widely accepted in the forensic psychological community.

The use of projective instruments in forensic analysis is controversial. Research by Borum and Grisso (1995) and Lally (2003) suggests that projective tests are not adequate for forensic assessment. Tests such as the Thematic Apperception Test (TAT) are not equipped with validity measures to aid in the detection of impression management. In other words, there exists a greater potential for undetected feigning or malingering. Over the past fifteen years, the Rorschach Inkblot Test has been reformatted to offer standardized scoring, administration, and interpretation. Despite these improvements, however, the Rorschach has been the subject of ongoing criticism and debate regarding the reliability and validity of its measures. Examples: Rorschach Inkblot Test; Draw-a-Person test; Thematic Apperception Test (TAT).

C. Objective Testing/Personality Inventories

Such tests consist of objectively scored items (usually scored as true/false) which produce a set of correlative statements based on empirical research on individuals who present with relevant diagnoses, personality traits, and behavior. They allow the examiner to deduce likely characteristics of the individual test-taker based on his/her responses compared with the population on which the test was developed. In practice, the examinee completes a paper/pencil test comprised of dichotomous (true or false) items. Examples: MMPI-2, Millon Clinical Multiaxial Inventory (MCMI), PAI, and California Psychological Inventory.

Weaknesses:

  • These measures focus on current levels of functioning and do not necessarily translate to functioning at the time of the instant offense. The MMPI-2 and PAI do not necessarily correlate with specific DSM-IV-TR diagnoses.
  • Research has not found profile configurations associated MMPI-2 to be predictive of sanity or insanity.
  • PAI lacks the level of research on malingering that is part of the MMPI-2 (Rogers and Shuman, 2005).

D. Tests of Malingering

1. Overview

It should be noted that a determination of malingering does not necessarily preclude a true diagnosis of mental illness. Evidence that an examinee has exaggerated his or her symptoms on testing instruments does not rule out the possibility of mental illness at the time of the instant offense. A determination of malingering at the time of testing raises questions as to the accuracy and veracity of an examinee’s account of the instant offense. Thus, the clinician must temper statements implying “once a liar always a liar.” In Goldstein et al. (2004), the authors suggest that “malingering is most frequently interpreted by forensic psychologists as an attempt to cope with a threatening, negative situation, not necessarily as an aspect of antisocial personality.” Both the MMPI-2 and PAI may help the clinician to determine if more extensive assessment regarding malingering is necessary.

2. Malingering Relevant to Competency Assessments

Instruments designed to measure competency (e.g., MacArthur or CAI) are often plagued by a high level of transparency. As such, these instruments frequently produce poor results.

Malingering can be measured on objective personality tests such as the MMPI-2 and performance-based tests like the Test of Memory Malingering (TOMM). Instruments such as the MMPI-2 and the PAI possess validity scales to measure an individual’s propensity to feign or highly exaggerate his or her current level of functioning. The MMPI-2 contains scales which have been designed to detect feigning impairment. In essence, the MMPI-2 contains items that isolate symptoms and attitudes that are not frequently found in known clinical groups, obvious versus subtle symptomology, and items based on erroneous stereotypes. The F scale has been distinguished as being the most predictive of malingering. However, the MMPI-2’s F scale was not developed using a clinical sample. In other words, it does not necessarily distinguish between genuine and faked symptoms of pathology.

The PAI also contains scales designed to detect malingering. Unlike the MMPI-2, which offers interpretation within a range of scores, the PAI was developed with specific cutoff scores. This instrument has greater sensitivity to the aspects of malingering more relevant to forensic assessment. The PAI’s validity scales allow for different levels of false responding. The PAI utilizes two primary scales to help in the detection of feigning: Negative Impression Management (NIM) and the Malingering Index.

E. Intellectual, Neuropsychological and Achievement Tests

1. Overview

Such tests are based on multiple series of tasks consisting of problems to be solved. The items have objectively or normatively “right or wrong” answers. They generate scores which can be interpreted in terms of relative strengths and weaknesses in areas like cognitive functioning, general abilities, and overall intelligence. In turn, the scores are used to make more global pronouncements on functioning (e.g., mental retardation, dyslexia, ADHD, and brain injury). Examples: Intelligence Tests/Wechsler Scale (WAIS and WISC) and Stanford-Binet; Neuropsychological Tests/Halstead-Reitan Neuropsychological Battery and Luria-Nebraska.

2. Feigned Cognitive Impairment

According to Richard Rogers, the primary goal of a defendant who intentionally feigns intellectual impairment is to convince the examiner that his or her efforts to succeed on the test are sincere and that the impairment is genuine. This phenomenon is referred to as “effortful failure.” Tests such as the Bender-Gestalt and Wechsler scales can be “failed” intentionally with minimal effort.

Ultimately, the examiner must distinguish between limited effort and gross attempts at exaggeration and fabrication of cognitive ability. Rogers (2004) identifies the following as common reasons for suboptimal effort:

  • decreased interest and effort as a result of genuine impairment
  • decreased interest and effort as a result of a co-occurring emotional or physical disorder such as depression or head trauma
  • expectations of failure
  • stress and preoccupation based on results of the evaluation
  • malingering

The clinician must provide reasoning for his or her conclusion that suboptimal effort is due to any one of the above factors. Rogers recommends a thorough explanation of reasoning for suboptimal effort and proactivity in gathering data to explain this conclusion. He isolates two general domains of detection of malingering: 1) detection by excessive impairment (e.g., failures on extremely easy items and failures below levels of chance), and 2) detection by unexpected patterns (e.g., similar performance on very easy and very hard tasks and unexpected answers on forced choice formats).

F. Tests for Specific Disorders

This category of tests is used when an examiner wishes to determine the presence of a specific diagnosis, deficit, or personality characteristic. For example, the Beck Depression Inventory-2 (BDI-2) is a commonly used instrument which focuses directly on symptoms associated with depression. Another example is Hare Psychopathy Checklist-Revised (PCL-R) which requires the examiner to compare the examinee on series of behaviors, attitudes, and historical factors associated with extreme antisocial functioning.

G. Forensic Assessment Instruments

The field of forensic psychology has developed several instruments which directly address issues related to legal referral questions. Such tests, surveys, and questionnaires are referred to as forensic assessment instruments. They deal directly with skill sets and attitudes directly related to forensic issues. These include: Criminal Competency (Aid and Assist); CAI (Competency to Stand Trial Assessment Instrument); CST (Competency Screening Test); GCCT (Georgia Competency Court Test); IFI-R (Interdisciplinary Fitness Interview); MacCAT-CA (MacArthur Criminal Assessment Tool; Criminal Responsibility (GEI); MSO (Mental State at the time of the Offense Screening Eval); R-CRAS (Rogers Criminal Responsibility Assessment Scale).

H. Physiological Testing

In sex offender evaluations, psychological evaluations are often accompanied by physiological testing. Such testing is believed to provide data which is difficult to obtain from self-report or legal records. The polygraph, an instrument for the simultaneous recording of several involuntary physiological activities, including pulse rate and perspiration, is also used widely in North America to facilitate judgments about whether someone is being truthful. Combined with a sexual history questionnaire, it seems to facilitate fuller disclosure of past offending and so may indirectly indicate the direction of sexual interests (Ahlmeyer, Heil, McKee, & English, 2000). However, its usefulness is dependent upon the employment of highly trained polygraphers, which limits its availability. Polygraphy represents the oldest and possibly the most controversial psychophysiological assessment tool. As you recall, it was the polygraph which stood at the center of the Daubert ruling. During the presentence phase of psychosexual evaluations, the polygraph is utilized in two distinct ways. First, polygraph assessments may help clarify certain contested issues found in a victim’s statements. Second, this type of test may help the forensic evaluator rule out additional victims, predatory tendencies, and/or other suspected forms of sexual deviance. As noted earlier, sexual deviance represents a major consideration in assessing risk of sexual recidivism.

I. Psychophysiological Testing Instruments

Psychophysiological tools such as the Abel Assessment of Sexual Interest-2 (AASI-2), penile plethysmograph, or polygraph represent a separate stream of data to aid in the assessment and treatment of alleged and convicted sexual offenders. The AASI-2 is a psychological test designed to provide a self-report of a client’s sexual arousal patterns as well as an objective measure of that client’s sexual interests. Penile plethysmography measures sexual arousal to appropriate and deviant stimuli. Alone, these instruments cannot make a diagnosis of pedophilia, guilt or innocence of a particular sexual offense, or determine the likelihood of sexual recidivism.

J. Assessment of Competence

1. Overview

Powers-Stafford (2004) conceptualizes the assessment of competence on two levels: 1) The evaluator assesses the defendant’s understanding of his or her legal situation and ability to make decisions about it through interview, use of competency assessments, review of discovery materials, and input from legal counsel, and 2) the evaluator assesses relevant psychological conditions through self-report, clinical observation, collateral interviews, and psychological testing of intellect, psychopathology, and potential malingering.

While instruments such as the MacArthur Competence Assessment Tool (see below) have been designed to structure clinical interactions to help determine competence, this type of forensic evaluation should also entail traditional aspects of psychological assessment: clinical interviewing and psychological testing. The clinical interview may help establish rapport between examiner and examinee. Interviewing also provides data on levels of defensiveness and DSM-IV considerations.

Research on the reliability of competency evaluations has been promising. Validity research, on the other hand, is less clear. Clinicians have been found to erroneously conclude incompetence based on diagnoses of thought disturbance and developmental disabilities (McGarry in Melton et al.). Validity and reliability appears to be enhanced when evaluators are specifically trained in the assessment and determination of competence. It is legitimate to ask forensic examiners for their specific training and experience in conducting aid and assist evaluations at the time of referral or during cross-examination.

2. Common Traits of the Incompetent Client

Demographics:

  • Incompetent clients differ from other pretrial populations by history of past hospitalization, history of psychiatric treatment, lower intellectual functioning, and symptoms of depression and thought disturbance.
  • In one study, approximately 81 percent of defendants referred for competency evaluations had been previously hospitalized for psychiatric reasons. (Steadman).
  • Eighty-seven percent of incompetent defendants were diagnosed with psychotic-type symptoms.
  • Attorneys were more apt to pursue competency if clients faced serious charges and were unusually passive or rejecting of the attorney’s advice. (Monahan, 1994)
  • In their meta-analysis of thirty studies comparing competent versus incompetent defendants, Nicholson and Kugler (1991) found that the factors most strongly related to findings of incompetency were (a) poor performance on psychological tests or interviews that specifically related to competency, (b) a diagnosis of thought disorder, and (c) severe symptoms of psychopathology. These researchers also found that older defendants, individuals with past psychiatric history, and defendants diagnosed with more severe disorders were more likely to be found incompetent. (cited in Melton, 1994).
  • Although research has consistently found that individuals with psychotic disorders are at greater risk of being found unfit or incompetent to stand trial, few studies have examined the relationship between diagnosis, current psychiatric symptoms, and legal abilities. In one study, defendants with psychotic disorders (n = 66) and defendants with nonpsychotic disorders (n = 30) were administered two measures of legal abilities (Fitness Interview Test and MacArthur Competency Assessment Tool-Criminal Adjudication), and a measure of current psychiatric symptoms (Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale). Legal impairment was significantly associated with psychotic disorders, and the presence of psychotic symptoms, particularly conceptual disorganization. However, 60 percent of psychotic defendants were not impaired on any of the sections of the FIT, and approximately 80 percent were judged fit by clinicians and the court. The relationships between legal impairment and hallucinations, hostility, depression, and withdrawal were non-significant. (Vilojen, Zapf, 2004).

Research shows that somewhere between 19 (Cochrane, Grisso, and Frederick, 2001) and 30 percent (Nicholson and Kugler, 1991) of defendants pursuing a competency study are ultimately found not competent to assist in their defense.

Incompetence may be related to a variety of factors beyond severe thought disturbance and mental retardation, such as lack of education (especially regarding the legal system), poor attorney-client communication, and malingering (the intentional misrepresentation of one’s traits and aptitudes for secondary gain).

K. Assessments of Sexual Violence

Two meta-analyses have investigated the relative predictive effectiveness of actuarial versus clinical judgment in the prediction of sexual recidivism (Grove & Meehl, 1996; Grove, Zald, Snitz, & Nelson, 2000). These studies involved the analyses of 136 published reports involving 50 years of research. Each study came to a similar basic conclusion: “Mechanical predictions of human behaviors are equal or superior to clinical prediction for a wide range of circumstances.” However, the Grove et al. (2000) research found actuarial prediction to be about 10 percent more effective than clinical judgment.

Karl Hanson (1998) conducted a meta-analysis of 10 studies in which the predictive accuracy of clinical judgment was r = .10. Hanson found that predictive accuracy could be almost doubled (r = .19) by knowing if the person being evaluated had been convicted of a prior sexual offense. This researcher opined that clinical judgment was consistently inaccurate because examiners relied on factors which do not strongly correlate with offender recidivism. For example, forensic examiners reliant on a subject’s own history of sexual abuse may erroneously predict that this individual was more likely to sexually offend in the future. However, clinical research has not shown that factors such as a history of sexual abuse or low self-esteem are strongly correlated with sexual recidivism.

Research has not been able to identify historical factors or psychological testing scores as a reliant or consistent profile of a sexual offender. In other words, no amount of data can prove or disprove whether an individual committed a sexual crime or whether or not an individual will commit such an act in the future. Federal courts have consistently not allowed mental health experts to testify as to whether a defendant could or could not have committed a given crime (U.S. v. Robinson, 2000, from Conroy 2003).

In essence, if the probability of dangerousness is overestimated (false positive), a true low-risk offender may be incarcerated for an extended period of time at the tax payers’ expense. On the other hand, if risk of future dangerousness is underestimated (false negative), truly dangerous offenders will be placed in the community with increased possibility of future violent behavior. Because of the difficulty in predicting a relatively rare future event such as sexual recidivism or murder, the chances of false alarms are greatly enhanced.

Perhaps one of the more important studies in the field of risk prediction was undertaken by Hanson and Bussiere (1998). These researchers conducted a meta-analysis of 61 studies involving 28,972 sex offenders who were followed over a period of four to five years. This study demonstrated the following recidivism rates: 13.4 (sexual recidivism), 12.2 (nonsexual violent recidivism), and 36.3 percent (any type of offense). Their research showed that the strongest predictors of sexual recidivism were factors related to sexual deviance such as sexual arousal to children, prior sexual offenses, and having never been married.

Hanson and Bussiere’s work also challenged commonly held belief regarding factors associated with sexual recidivism such as histories of substance abuse, childhood sexual victimization, or general Axis I disorders such as depression or anxiety. The following factors were not found to have an insignificant correlation to sexual recidivism:

  • Legally classified mental disorder (.07)
  • Degree of sexual contact (-.03)
  • Empathy (.03)
  • Social skills (-.04)
  • Prior violence (.05)
  • Young versus older child victim (.05)
  • High plethysmograph scores on theme of rape (.05)
  • Alcohol abuse (.0)
  • Prior nonviolent offense (.0)
  • Use of force or injury during commission of crime (.01)
  • Psychological problems (-.01)
  • History of sexual abuse (.02)

L. Assessments of Dangerousness

Broadly speaking there are three predominant methods of assessing risk for violent recidivism (Melton, et al.): 1) clinical judgment, 2) structures clinical judgment, and 3) actuarial.

1. Clinical Judgment relies on unsystematic and widely differing approaches to data collection, generally based on a clinician’s personal instincts regarding a defendant and what he attributes as the most import factors related to future violence. Within this approach, empirical research may be considered but is secondary to the evaluator’s personal impressions and ideas. The primary strength of clinical judgment is its flexibility in considering individual characteristics of a specific defendant. This approach is criticized for its lack of specificity in delineating reasoning for the clinical opinion. It should be noted that criticism of clinical judgment has likely been overstated. There are multiple ways of utilizing clinical judgment. However, clinical judgment is often pigeonholed as “gut feelings.”

Clinical judgment is often difficult to challenge, support, or defend. Unlike the actuarial or guided clinical judgment, clinical judgment does not have a readily identifiable methodology or inter-rater reliability data. Clinical judgment also does not provide the forensic examiner with a protocol of relevant factors. In such cases, the evaluator may consciously or unconsciously be persuaded on variables which do not necessarily correlate with the fundamental question of recidivism risk. To the reviewer, clinical judgment may be evident when an evaluator’s points are supported by his level of experience at the expense of research data on topics such as frequency and nature of future violence. A standard quote may be, “In my twenty years of professional experience…” Unfortunately, experience does not always correlate with competence or judgmental accuracy. In research conducted by Dawes (1989 cited in Campbell, 2004), clinical experience and the validity of clinical accuracy were unrelated. Often in mental health practice, as one gains more experience and respect, less opportunities are available for supervision and objective feedback. Clinical judgment may also place undue influence on psychological testing which has not been designed to directly address the referral question. Finally, clinical judgment is vulnerable to dramatic factors which may compromise objectivity. For example, a shocking detail or visual evidence of abuse may strike the evaluator at a visceral level and, thus, color his impressions of the defendant’s level of future risk. In the opposite direction, a particularly charming and socially gifted defendant may create a subterfuge away from a lengthy history of past assaults.

Clinical judgment may also be impacted by a clinician’s relative ignorance of the research involving the inaccuracy of clinical judgment. The research of Rock (1994 cited in Campbell, 2004) surveyed 106 practicing, doctoral-level psychologists to assess their awareness of the state of clinical judgment research. Rock concluded that the surveyed clinicians “have no direct knowledge of clinical judgment research.” Research on the prediction of future violence (Jackson, 2002; Rogers et al., 2005) has not demonstrated predictive superiority between doctoral-level practitioners and graduate students in predicting future violence.

2. Structured Clinical Judgment or Empirically Guided Clinical Approach: Clinicians using this approach use an a priori set of variables to be considered and attribute great influence of some factors and lesser influence to other factors. The SVR-20, discussed later in this chapter provides an example of this methodology. However, the HCR, also mentioned later, is considered the preeminent model of this approach to risk assessment (Doren, 2002). A major advantage of structured clinical judgment is that evaluators can attribute various weights to characteristics which have particular relevance to the defendant’s history and factors related to the instant offense. This approach should not be confused with actuarial methods, discussed below. Actuarial implies that factors have been researched to a point of statistical significance.

3. Actuarial follows a rigid set of characteristics which necessitate a comparison of a defendant’s characteristics to factors identified in the professional literature to be associated with a given trait. In general, a rigorously actuarial approach allows the clinician to consider a set of characteristics and derive a statistical probability of the individual’s likelihood to act out based on similarities to group trends. This approach is plagued by the absence of consideration of a defendant’s unique set of circumstances. Unlike guided clinical judgment, actuarial measurements identify factors associated with a phenomenon and attribute a statistical weight associated with the highest correlation with known sexual and/or violent recidivism. Actuarial tools are distinguished by their methods of combining data in an explicit and objective manner. Such instruments are developed with the express purpose of comparing an individual to a sample of individuals with similar traits.

Actuarial methods have gained popularity due to their ease of use, demonstrated accuracy, and increasing acceptance by state and federal courts. These instruments also shield the evaluator from extrinsic and under-researched variables. Actuarial methods are criticized by some as mental health profiling. They raise questions about generalizing the characteristics of a group to a unique individual. Actuarial data also provides limited insight into the dynamic variables of the individual or management strategies. An evaluator runs a risk of under- or overprediction of violence if the actuarial tool that is used does not closely represent characteristics relevant to the defendant. Thus, attorneys should query the clinician as to the appropriateness of an instrument as it relates to their clients. As noted, actuarial methodology does not consider dynamic (changeable) factors or protective (defendant strengths) factors which may provide data not addressed by actuarial tools.

In choosing an actuarial tool there are several considerations. Dennis Doren (2002) illustrated the following criteria:

  • Inter-rater reliability
  • Number and proportion of replicated validity studies of the given instrument.
  • The degree of similarity between the sample of the instrument and the relevant examinee.
  • The degree to which the instruments’ outcome measures are in keeping with the relevant statutory definition of sexual violence.

Actuarial measurement relies on standardization. A test or measurement is considered standardized when a large group of individuals who share common characteristics are compared with one another on a specific outcome (e.g., sexual recidivism). Based on characteristics or factors found within this sample, standardized scores are produced which allow comparison of future individuals who share similar traits to the original sample. The process is similar to actuarial specialists who work in the life insurance industry. Instead of looking at traits such as family history of heart disease, use of tobacco, or the presence of obesity, mental health actuaries have developed methodologies to aid in the determination of future risk of violence. These predictors are coded with values ranging from low to high. In principle, subjects with high levels of correlation to factors such as “past conviction for sexual violence” or “psychopathy” are believed to present higher levels of risk for sexual recidivism. In general, it can be interpreted as the probability that a randomly selected sexual recidivist would have a higher score on the instrument than a randomly selected nonrecidivist. For such methodologies to be effective, prediction has to be above chance or .5. Perfect predictive accuracy would be represented by a score of 1.0. This statistical formulation is referred to as “area under the curve.”

It should be noted that an actuarial instrument allows its user to make probabilistic statements such as, “This examinee possesses traits of individuals who were convicted of sexually related crimes at a rate of ten percent over a period of five years post-release.” These instruments can not identify sexual recidivists in advance. Instead, they offer statements on probability of reoffense.

M. Risk Assessment Tests and Actuarial Measures

The work of John Monahan has been central to the field of the assessment of dangerousness and violent recidivism. In 2001, Monahan assessed a large number of men and women who were civilly committed to psychiatric settings. This research is referred to as the MacArthur Violence Risk Assessment Study. The goal of the research was to identify characteristics which could predict future engagement in violence after release from a secured setting. He collected data on his subjects 20 weeks after discharge and one year after discharge. Monahan identified four prominent domains of risk:

  • Dispositional variables (demographic characteristics such as race and gender and personality variables such as impulsivity and problematic anger)
  • Historical variables (significant life history events such as history of violence and past psychiatric hospitalization)
  • Contextual variables (evidence of high stress environments and quality and degree of social support)
  • Clinical variables (types and symptoms of mental disorders, drug use history, and general level of functioning)

The MacArthur Violence Risk Assessment Study made the following conclusions:

  • Gender: Prevalence of violence was found to be similar between men and women during the follow-up evaluations. Men’s violence related more to substance abuse and noncompliance with medication regimen. Their acts of violence were considered more injurious. Women, on the other hand, were more likely to act out against their own family members.
  • Prior violence and criminality: History of violence and criminal behavior were found to be strong correlates of future violence.
  • Childhood experiences and violence: History of childhood physical abuse as well as parental substance abuse were associated with post-discharge violence. Sexual abuse was not found to be a strong correlate.
  • Neighborhood context: Discharge into high-crime areas was found to be predictive of future risk for violence. In other words, individuals who returned to high-crime areas were more likely to be accused of future criminal and violent behavior. Race was not found to be a strong correlate.
  • DSM-IV diagnosis: Diagnosis of a major mental disorder was associated with a lower rate of violent recidivism. A diagnosis of schizophrenia was associated with lower rates of future violence than a diagnosis of Major Depression or Bipolar Disorder. Similarly, the presence of delusions and hallucinations (per se) were not strong correlates of future violence. Comorbidity with substance abuse or substance dependence was shown to be predictive of future allegations of violent behavior.
  • Violent thoughts: Violent thoughts were associated with greater likelihood of violence during the first 20 weeks post-discharge.
  • Anger: Individuals with higher scores on the Novaco Anger Scale (a commonly used cognition scale measuring anger) were twice as likely to engage in post-discharge violence than individuals with lower scores on the same measure.

Monahan’s research was instrumental in setting the stage for other research designed to predict future violence and sexual violence. His research also challenged what had been intuitive beliefs about the role gender and major mental illness play in future risk of violence.

Discussion

In general, psychological testing is administered to help provide reasoning for incompetence, detection of malingering, and instructions for adaptive means of communication between attorney and client. It should be noted that psychological testing is not as commonly used in aid and assist assessments as in other types of forensic referrals. The specific nature of the referral question and the data being measured does not necessarily lend itself to a comprehensive psychological testing battery. As noted previously, knowledge of the exact level of intellectual functioning or nature of thought disturbance are secondary to an examinee’s ability to meet the Dusky prongs. (For more on the Dusky prongs, see the Chapter 2, “Fitness to Proceed.”)

Psychological testing may help elucidate the underlying reasons for incompetence. Intellectual testing helps to uncover profound levels of mental retardation. Neuropsychological testing may help to identify the presence of brain injury, which can explain deficits in competence. The Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS) is an instrument commonly used to assess competence. The Wechsler scale provides data on factors believed to be central to a defendant’s ability to participate in the legal process. Research conducted by Nestor, Daggett, Haycock, and Price (1999 cited in Stafford) showed significant differences in WAIS measuring verbal reasoning, episodic memory, and social judgment.

The professional literature strongly discourages the isolated use of objective personality assessment tools such as the MMPI-2 and PAI in the determination of forensic issues such as competency. They are most effectively used as adjuncts to help explain functioning.

I. Common Deficits in the Use of Psychological Testing Inventories

  • Reading comprehension: Reading abilities vary on objective personality tests. The MMPI-2 and MCMI-3 manuals recommend that test-takers have at least an 8th grade reading level. However, research has shown that the average difficulty of MMPI-2 items requires a 6th grade reading level. The PAI requires a 4th grade reading level. Reading levels may be assessed through brief achievement tests such as the Wide Ranging Achievement Test series. Clinicians often ask would be test-takers to read aloud test questions for an instrument to ensure that the individual is able to demonstrate basic reading ability.
  • Confirmatory bias: A common error found in test interpretation is “cherry picking” interpretations which most closely resemble the clinician’s preconceived notions regarding the examinee. Examiners should be able to provide explanation as to how conclusions were drawn and alternative hypotheses which were considered.
  • Linguistic equivalence: While a test may be translated into another language, it does not necessarily mean that cultural considerations have been fully addressed. Simple comparisons of vocabulary are often insufficient to establish clinical validity.

II. Ethical Considerations

A. Informed Consent

An examinee must understand that he is not the client in this clinical interaction. The only privilege that exists in these circumstances is between the attorney and the client.

According to professional guidelines stated in “Ethical Guidelines for Forensic Psychologists” (APA, 1991), psychologists are not permitted to utilize work product (e.g., psychological testing) from one forensic assessment to address a different forensically related referral question unless the examiner has clearly obtained permission from the examinee. For example, data compiled in an aid and assist evaluation may not be used to gauge factors relevant to waiver of miranda rights, GEI, or assessment of dangerousness without verbal or preferably written consent from the examinee. Psychologists are encouraged to pursue written authorization from relevant parties as a part of the initial evaluation, as the attorney may alter the referral question during the evaluation process.

In forensic issues such as aid and assist, GEI, and mental state, the examinee may not be able to provide informed consent. In such cases, it is incumbent upon the examiner to provide his rationale to proceed with the assessment despite the lack of a signed informed consent form.

B. Sufficiency of Data

Goldstein et al. (2004) discuss ethical considerations regarding the sufficiency of forensic evaluations for the purpose of insanity defense. As specified in the APA ethical guidelines, forensic examiners must be careful to include all materially relevant sources of data before reaching an opinion. A psychologist who bases her conclusions solely on responses from a clinical interview or a single psychological instrument may be vulnerable to pointed cross-examination as to the basis of her opinion. These guidelines also direct forensic practitioners to avoid reporting disclosures that exceed the original referral question. Given the specific nature and timeline of a GEI assessment, the examiner is encouraged to not ask questions or memorialize issues related to unrelated past criminal behavior.

III. Evaluating the Credibility of Scientific Assertions and Instruments

A. Validity

Internal Validity refers to the soundness of experimental design with the goal of excluding any factors other than the design variables from influencing experimental results.

Threats to Internal Validity: Among other possible concerns, research participants may respond differently than normal because they are being watched (Hawthorne Effect) or even specifically in reaction to subtle cues given by the observers (demand characteristics). Also, if variables are not objectively and clearly defined, the results will be subject to possible rater and observer error through differing interpretations. When experimenters measure concepts related to values such as political conservatism, unless the test is greatly disguised, participants are likely to react to the fact that these values are being observed (Hawthorne Effect). If an experimental design involves experimenter observation, it is highly likely that the experimenters would have difficulty remaining neutral in their scoring based on their political orientations and the perceived orientations of their participants (experimenter bias). Along the same lines, it is possible that participants could respond to the measure depending on their perception of what was desired or expected of them from the observers (demand characteristics).

External Validity explains how well experimental results can be generalized to populations and situations other than those studied. For example, if an experiment was done using college freshmen who are participating in a research project for a grade in a class, it would be difficult to make any assumptions about married couples from the results. Most freshmen aren’t married and the sample is less than random. The more people involved, the more contexts sampled, and the more times the results were replicated, the more externally valid the study would become.

Measurement Validity describes how well a tool actually measures what it claims to measure. For example, if an inventory claims to measure depression, it should include such elements as amount of sleep, weight gain or loss, motivation, mood etc. There are four different aspects of measurement validity:

1. Criterion-related validity (CRV)refers to the relationship between the test results and an external factor which is meant to represent what the test claims to measure. For example, court reports of recidivism could be the external factor used in conjunction with a predictive measure of recidivism. CRV is either predictive (when the test is administered and then the external factor is later assessed) or concurrent (when both measures are evaluated at the same time). CRV is demonstrated when the test and the external factor are highly correlated.

2. Content validity describes whether the items on an instrument accurately represent the field of content they purport to measure. For example, a driver’s test should include items referring to the driver’s handbook (the field of content), but not items which cover piloting an airplane or operating a forklift.

3. Construct validity answers the question: Is the theoretical construct (attraction, aggression, hyperactivity, etc.) we are attempting to assess being accurately measured? Construct validity can be measured in many ways including by comparing the measure to previously proven measures of the same construct, by comparing the test results to external professional evaluations for the same construct, or by running factor analyses. A factor analysis examines patterns of correlations between items on a measure. When items which should show a positive relationship with one another do, these results are called convergent. When items which should not demonstrate a strong relationship don’t, these results are called discriminate. Highly convergent or highly discriminate data from a factor analysis can strengthen construct validity.

4 Factual and face validity both refer to the experience of the test-taker. A test is factually valid if it draws out truthful responses from test-takers. A test has face validity if its takers believe that it measures what it should. For example, a lawyer sitting for the bar would probably be suspicious of essay questions regarding their interest in scuba diving.

B. Reliability

Reliability is a gauge of how consistently a measure produces the same results when employed repeatedly in the same manner. Reliability sets the upper limit on validity and is measured by a coefficient between 0 and 1, 1being a measure of perfect reliability.

C. Correlations

Correlations examine relationships between variables and whether or not variances in one factor are associated with variances with other factors. If so, the strength and direction of these relationships are expressed with a correlation coefficient. It is important when studying such relationships to remember that a relationship does not necessarily imply or explain causation. For example, one could collect the number of houseplants in an individual’s home and a score of their overall health for a year to see if there is a relationship between owning plants and maintaining health. If a relationship is found, the researcher would not know if owning houseplants causes people to be healthier, the reverse, or if there are other factors which can explain the relationship (people who own plants have more free time and are more relaxed, people who are healthier tend to have more energy to spend on hobbies, etc.). Although correlational research cannot offer explanations of relationships, it often leads the way for specific, causational experimental designs.

In statistics analyzing experimental results, when only two sets of scores are being compared for significance, a t-test is used. When more than two sets of numbers need to be analyzed, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) computes the probability that two or more mean scores are drawn from populations having the same mean. For example, if a researcher obtained the results that students receiving five days of after school tutoring obtained an average grade point level of 3.2, students who only received two days of tutoring earned a 2.5, and students in a control group who were not tutored at all averaged a 2.0, an ANOVA could be run to determine the probability that these differences could be accounted for by reasons other than the experiment.

Once numerical results have been collected from an experiment, the first method of examining them is by plotting them on a scatterplot, bar graph, pie graph, linear graph or other visual representation. Although graphical analysis can almost never definitively state whether or not experimental results are statistically significant, looking at results plotted on a graph can be very helpful in conceptualizing the effect of an experiment. A graph can immediately point out input errors if one result is somehow way off the chart. It can also quickly give a researcher an idea of the distribution of scores, leading to an educated guess regarding potential experimental effect.

When data is nominal, a chi-square test can be used to analyze the proportional repre-sentation of categorical data. Chi-squares are not used with scores or measures other than simple counts. An example of the use of a chi-square with only one area of interest would be if a researcher wanted to know whether or not a sample of the political affiliation of Portland was equal across parties (Republican, Democrat and Green). The number of people surveyed who fit into each category would be analyzed against an even distribution of numbers in each category (representing the null hypothesis). Chi-squares can also be utilized when a study has more than one dimension, in which case data is presented in a crosstab or contingency table. For example, if a doctor divided her patients into two categories and gave one half flu vaccinations but not the other and then counted how many in each group got the flu over the next season, the differences in the numbers could be analyzed with a chi-square.

D. Base Rates

In attempting to evaluate the accuracy of a psychological instrument it is crucial to know and understand the applicable base rate. A base rate is the proportion of a population that performs a certain behavior over a specified period of time. For example, if 50 convicted sex offenders in a population of 1000 sex offenders were ultimately convicted of another sexually related crime, the accurate prediction that none of the original population would commit future sex crimes would be 95 percent.

The less common an event (e.g., winning the lottery or being struck by lightning), the more difficult it is to predict. Actuarial measurement is utilized to bolster predictive accuracy. During shorter periods (less than five years), sex offender recidivism has been shown to be approximately 9 percent (Barbaree et al. 2001). Accurately identifying recidivism with such a small base rate is very difficult. A well-known sex offender researcher, Robert Prentky, once suggested that over the short run it is safest to predict that no sex offender will commit another sex crime in order to be correct over 90 percent of the time. However, research has demonstrated that sex offender recidivism base rates increase over extended periods of time.

To demonstrate this proposition, assume a base population of one million violent offenders in which the recidivism rate is nine percent (90,000 out of 1,000,000). Further assume a highly valid violence prediction tool which shows that 80 percent of people with the profile of a “very violent offender” will commit another crime of violence in the future. This means that of the 90,000 recidivating violent offenders, the prediction tool will give us a correct prediction of future violence in 72,000 of the 90,000 cases (90,000 x .8). However, the prediction tool will also give us an incorrect prediction that there will be future violence in 20 percent of the 910,000 nonrecidivating violent offenders, or 182,000 cases (910,000 x .2). To get the probability of a correct prediction in a particular case, we divide the true positives (correct predictions of violence: 90,000) by the total number of positives (predictions of violence: 90,000 + 182,000 = 272,000) to get .330 or a little less than 1 in 3. Intuitively, it seems like a test which is correct 80 percent of the time would yield a predictive probability of 80 percent in a particular case. Unfortunately, the answer is completely dependant on the base rate. In our hypothetical, the probability that the prediction would be wrong is much higher (67 percent) than the probability that the prediction would be correct (33 percent).
