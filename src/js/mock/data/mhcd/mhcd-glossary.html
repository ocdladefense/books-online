Mental Health and Criminal Defense

Mental Health and Criminal Defense Manual

Active Phase – The phase of schizophrenia in which prominent or florid symptoms of psychosis are present. The prodromal phase precedes and the residual phase succeeds the active phase.

Acute – A term used to describe a disorder or a phase or episode of a disorder which has a sudden onset, relatively short duration and extremely intense symptoms.

Addiction – A state of physiological or psychological dependence on a substance or activity.

A.D.L.s – Activities of Daily Living – e.g., showering, dressing, preparing meals.

Affect – The outward display of emotions or mood.

Agnosia – The failure to recognize or identify objects despite intact sensory function. (DSM-IV-TR p. 168)

Akathisia – Extreme restlessness and discomfort of the limbs. A side effect of neuroleptic medications, especially for Parkinsonian symptoms. See EPS, below.

Akinisia – Loss of muscle movement. A side effect of some medications, most typically the Neuroleptics.

Alogia – An impoverishment in thinking that is inferred from observing speech and language behavior. There may be brief and concrete replies to questions and restriction in the amount of spontaneous speech (poverty of speech). Sometimes the speech is adequate in amount but conveys little information because it is over-concrete, over-abstract, repetitive, or stereotyped (poverty of content). (DSM-IV-TR p. 820)

AMIP – An acronym which stands for allegedly mentally ill person. “Mentally ill” under ORS 426.005 means that a person has a mental disorder which is causing them to be dangerous or dangerously unable to care for themselves. Mental illness is merely alleged until it is proven by clear and convincing evidence.

Anhedonia – A negative symptom of schizophrenia in which there is a lack of pleasure in normally pleasurable activities.

Anterograde Amnesia – The inability to remember new information, often the result of shock or trauma. It can be a side effect of some psychiatric medications.

Antisocial personality disorder – An Axis II personality disorder which is characterized by a pattern of disregard for, and violation of, the rights of other people. (DSM-IV-TR, p.701)

Aphasia – An impairment in the understanding or transmission of ideas by reading, writing or speaking due to an injury to the language center of the brain.

Apraxia – Despite full motor functions, the loss of ability to carry out specific motor activities.

AFH – Oregon agency lingo for an Adult Foster Home.

ATS – Adult Treatment Services. This is the name used by the Oregon State Hospital system and state agencies to refer to civilly committed patients. ATS accounts for approximately 25 percent of the total beds in the state hospital system.

Autonomic effect – Refers to the body’s ability to keep a constant internal environment. There are two types of autonomic effects: anticholinergic and antiadrenergic. They are both normal bodily functions. The anticholinergic effect may result in dry mouth, blurred vision, flushing, headaches, constipation, urinary retention, dizziness or palpitations. Drugs which cue the anticholinergic effect are often used as a side effect medication for the older generation of antipsychotic medications. The tricyclic antidepressants also cue anticholinergic symptoms. The antiadrenergic effect may result in postural hypotension, lightheadedness, fainting, ringing in the ears, sweating, depression, shortness of breath, or irregular heartbeats.

Avolition – A symptom of schizophrenia in which there is an apathy about goals and decisions resulting in an inability to initiate or complete anything but the most basic activities.

Back-to-back holds – Also called a double hold. Refers to a second five-day period of custody being placed by a hospital at the end of the first five-day period of custody, leading a client to be held for more than the five judicial days allowed by ORS 426.232. When the hearing does not occur within the required period, the trial court should dismiss the case. State v. Bradsteen, 196 Or App 634 (2004). A writ of habeas corpus may be required to get a person into court so a dismissal can be requested.

Baseline – Refers to the best possible state of mental health for a particular individual, with or without medication. (It can refer to the client’s state after being completely stabilized on the best possible medications).

Basic Needs – The legislature’s “basic needs” commitment standard focuses on the capacity of the individual to survive, either through his own resources or with the help of family and friends. The state must establish by clear and convincing evidence that the individual, due to a mental disorder, is unable to obtain some commodity (e.g., food and water) or service (e.g., lifesaving medical care) without which he cannot sustain life.” State v. Jayne, 174 Or App 74 (2001).

Behavior Therapy – A method of treatment that focuses on changing observable behavior by altering environmental variables. For example, a person with a phobia of flying might be slowly flooded with flight simulation and finally actual flying (systematic desensitization).

Bipolar Disorder – A mood disorder which includes at least one manic episode. More commonly, bipolar disorder includes intermittent manic and depressive episodes. Bipolar II Disorder is where there are recurrent major depressive episodes with cyclical hypomania.

Bizarre Delusion – A firmly held belief in something that is truly impossible. For example, a belief that one is being followed all the time would not be bizarre. A belief that one is being followed by aliens would be bizarre.

Blunted Affect – The outward display of significantly less emotion than most people. Also called restricted or constricted affect.

Borderline Intellectual Functioning – An IQ range from 71 to 84.

Borderline personality disorder – An Axis II personality disorder which is characterized by a pattern of instability in interpersonal relationships, self-image and mood as well as impulsivity. Borderline personality disorder requires five or more of the following requirements: 1) frantic efforts to avoid real or imagined abandonment; 2) a pattern of unstable and intense interpersonal relationships characterized by alternating between extremes of idealization and devaluation; 3) persistently unstable self-image or sense of self; 4) self-damaging impulsivity; 5) recurrent suicidality (defined below) or self-mutilating behavior; 6) unstable moods; 7) chronic feelings of emptiness; 8) inappropriate, intense anger or difficulty controlling anger; or 9) transient stress-related paranoid ideation or severe dissociative symptoms. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 710)

Boundary – Emotional or social markers setting limits in any social system like a family or group.

Brokerages - County and regional organizations set up to help people with mental retardation or developmental disabilities find services and support. Their creation was an attempt to solve some of the problems associated with extremely long waiting lists and therefore waiting periods, for services in Oregon. The brokerage will help a person create a plan, apply for money and set up services.

Catalepsy – Rigid maintenance of a body position over an extended period of time. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 820)

Cataplexy – Episodes of sudden bilateral loss of muscle tone resulting in the individual collapsing, often in association with intense emotions such as laughter, anger, fear, or surprise. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 820)

Catatonic behavior/Catatonia – Marked motor abnormalities including motor immobility (i.e. catalepsy or stupor), certain types of excessive motor activity (apparently purposeless agitation not influenced by external stimuli), extreme negativism (apparent motiveless resistance to instructions or attempts to be moved) or mutism (social anxiety disorder in which a person who is normally capable of speech is unable to speak in certain situations or to certain people), posturing, or stereotyped movements, and echolalia or echopraxia.

Chronic – A descriptive term for a disorder meaning long-term.

Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) – A method of treatment which focuses on altering thoughts and behaviors in practical, direct ways. The underlying premise is that negative thinking patterns and behaviors are learned and because they are learned they can be unlearned.

Cognitive Disability – Both a general and politically correct term encompassing mental retardation, serious learning disabilities, traumatic brain injuries and neurological impairments. It is meant to exclude anything which might be considered a mental illness or disorder and include stable, organic causes of significantly less than average measured intelligence, adaptive functioning or academic functioning. It is not a legal term used in Oregon.

Comorbidity – Co-existing diagnoses. Also called “dual diagnosis.”

Compulsions – Repeated behaviors or thinking exercises that a person feels driven to act out.

Concrete Thinking – Thinking which relies on immediate experience as opposed to abstract thought. Such an absence of abstract thought is a common symptom of schizophrenia.

Conditioning – A term used in behavior modification. The process of acquiring or reproducing a specific behavior under a specified set of conditions. Classical (also called respondent) conditioning is the pairing of a stimulus and a reflective response that aren’t normally associated. For example, Pavlov trained a dog to associate the ring of a bell with food and thus the dog was trained to salivate upon hearing the bell even in the absence of food. Operant conditioning pairs a voluntary behavior with a reward or punishment until the behavior seems intuitively good or bad in and of itself. For example, teachers often give stickers or points for good behavior so that students will associate the desired behavior with something good.

Conscious – The content of mind of which one is aware. This is to be contrasted with the much larger portion of mental processes which take place below the level of awareness.

Consumer – A person who is engaged in mental health treatment. This word is preferred by many over “mentally ill person” because it does not imply anything except current treatment and it implies the power and ability to choose whether or not to consume (like any other consumer of economic goods).

Counter Transference – A common problem in individual therapy in which the therapist reacts to the patient based on the desires and fears of the therapist rather than an objective assessment of the patient.

Continuum of Care – Oregon agency lingo for the idea that the various parts of the mental health system should not be conceptualized as separate “silos” but rather as mutually dependent parts of a single system that ought to be working together and sharing information to the maximum extent possible. Stated another way, one ought to think of mental health services from the perspective of the consumer bouncing between the parts, rather than from the perspective of any single part.

Course – The age of onset and development of a particular disorder.

Cyclothymic Disorder – A mood disorder characterized by numerous hypomanic episodes and mild depressive symptoms. See mood disorders.

CMHP – Oregon agency lingo for a Community Mental Health Program, as opposed to a state-run program.

Decompensation – Short term deterioration of a person’s mental health. Usually a person decompensates because of either a specific stressor or a cessation of psychiatric medication.

Defense Mechanisms – A set of unconscious strategies designed to protect one’s concept of self from real or imagined threats. For example, repression is an unconscious method of banishing unpleasant ideas from the mind in order to keep a consistent and positive sense of self.

Delirium – A disorder characterized by a reduced clarity of awareness, a reduced ability to focus, shift or sustain attention and an impairment of memory, orientation, language or perception. Deliria develop over a short period of time, fluctuate during the course of the day and are caused by a medical condition or a substance intoxication or withdrawal. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 136)

Delusion – A false belief that is firmly sustained despite incontrovertible and obvious evidence to the contrary. See psychotic disorders section above for more detail.

Delusion of Control – A delusion that one’s mind is being controlled by other people.

Delusion of Grandeur – A delusion of being a specially empowered person.

Delusion of Persecution – A delusion of being deliberately victimized.

Delusion of Reference – A delusion that ordinary events or objects are speaking directly to the deluded person.

Dementia – A disorder characterized by multiple, stable cognitive deficits that include the impairment of memory which causes significant impairment in functioning and is caused by a medical condition or a substance. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 135)

Depersonalization – An alteration in the perception or experience of the self so that one feels detached from, and as if one is an outside observer of, one’s mind or body.

Derailment – A common symptom of schizophrenia in which there are rapid, barely logical shifts from one topic to another, also called “loose associations.”

Derealization – A symptom of schizophrenia in which everything which should seem normal instead seems unfamiliar and disconcerting.

DHS – Department of Human Resources is Oregon’s child welfare agency, the agency in charge of child protective services.

Dialectical Behavioral Therapy – Dialectical Behavioral Therapy is a relatively successful treatment primarily used for treating Borderline Personality Disorder, a notoriously difficult disorder to treat. It primarily focuses on teaching specific, practical methods for dealing with the surges of emotion and panic characteristic of people with Borderline Personality Disorder.

Differential Diagnosis – An explanation of why one disorder is a more appropriate diagnosis than other ones with similar symptoms.

Diplomate – An individual who has been recognized by a professional board for having established competence in a specialized area of practice.

Disorientation – Confusion about day, date, time, place, or self.

Dissociation – Any disruption in the usually integrated functions of consciousness, memory, identity or perception. Dissociative symptoms can arise in many different illnesses but is most often seen in the dissociative disorders such as dissociative identity disorders (formerly multiple personality disorder). (DSM-IV-TR, p. 519).

Disorganized Speech – Also called tangential speech, tangentiality, word salad or schizophasia, where speech may move quickly through multiple topics, be confused, repetitive or string together unrelated words or sounds without meaning.

Dopamine – A neurotransmitter whose inhibition reduces the symptoms of psychosis.

Double Hold – See Back-to-back Holds.

Dysarthria – The impaired ability to articulate.

Dysgraphia – The impaired ability to write.

Dysnomia – The impaired ability to name objects.

Dysphoria – A normal feeling of unhappiness or sadness, sometimes confused with depression.

Disorder – See Mental Disorder.

Dissimulation – A general term that includes malingering, factitious disorders and feigning. It implies that there is an unlikely set of responses on a psychological instrument such that there may be intentional faking of symptoms.

Dysphoria/Dysphoric mood – An unpleasant mood such as sadness, anxiety or irritability.

Dyssomnia – Any disorder of the amount or quality of sleep.

Dysthymic Disorder – A chronic but less disabling form of depression.

Dystonia – facial grimacing or spasms, stiffness, as a side effect of some medications, most typically the Neuroleptics. See EPS below.

Echolalia – A symptom of schizophrenia in which sounds or words are heard and then repeated in an uncontrollable manner.

Echopraxia – Repetition by imitation of the movements of another. The action is not a willed or voluntary one and has a semiautomatic and uncontrollable quality. (DSM-IV-TR p. 823)

ECMU – (pron. Eck-Moo) – the Extended Care Management Unit is the unit of the state mental health agency (OMHAS) which is responsible for screening and placement of long-term care for people with Severe and Persistent Mental Illness (SPMI). For example, when a social worker at the Oregon State Hospital is attempting to place a patient in a group home, he or she will usually have to make an application to ECMU in order to acquire approval for such services.

Electroconvulsive Therapy (ECT) – A treatment for severe depression in which electrodes are attached to the patient’s head and electrical currents are passed through the brain. The civil commitment statutes explicitly prohibit ECT during the prehearing period of detention. 426.005(1)(f). However, it is allowed during a period of involuntary commitment.

Elevated mood – An exaggerated feeling of well-being, or euphoria or elation. A person with elevated mood may describe feeling “high,” “ecstatic,” “on top of the world,” or “up in the clouds.” (DSM-IV-TR, p. 825)

ECOS – Oregon agency lingo for Enhanced Care Outreach Services. EC is agency lingo for Enhanced Care.

Effect – Result or consequence, sometimes confused with affect, which refers to a person’s observable mood.

Enuresis – An inability to control one’s bladder, not due to a medical condition.

Etiology – The cause or source of a disorder or illness.

Euthymic mood – Normal mood, neither elevated nor dysphoric.

Executive Functioning – “Higher” functions of the brain like planning, organizing, inductive reasoning.

Expanded Criteria – Refers to ORS 426.005(e)(C) which creates an alternative definition of mental illness that does not require current dangerousness or inability to care for basic needs. Instead, it requires chronic mental illness, multiple previous hospitalizations, symptoms similar to those which preceded the previous hospitalizations and a reasonable medical probability that further decompensation will result in dangerousness or inability to care for basic needs in the near future.

Expansive mood – Lack of restraint in expressing one’s feelings, frequently with an overvaluation of one’s significance or importance. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 825)

Extra-Pyramidal Symptoms (EPS) –The Extra-Pyramidal System helps maintain equilibrium and motor function. Extra-pyramidal side effects include Parkinsonian traits (muscle rigidity, motor retardation, drooling, shuffling gait), Akathisia (extreme restlessness), Dystonia (facial spasms, grimacing, stiffness), Akinesia (loss of muscle movement), Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome and Tardive Dyskinesia (involuntary muscle spasms/twitches in face and body). All EPS can persist despite cessation of medication. EPS is most commonly a side effect of the older generation of anti-psychotics.

Factitious Disorder – A physical or psychological illness with no organic cause in which the person is intentionally producing the symptoms in order to be placed in the role of patient; to be contrasted with malingering which is a similar feigning for the purpose of avoiding something bad or gaining something good. Munchausen syndrome is severe form.

Feigning – The intentional faking of psychological or physical symptoms, whether for an external goal or as a result of a factitious disorder.

Flat Affect – The outward display of almost no emotion at all.

Flight of Ideas – A nearly continuous flow of accelerated speech with abrupt changes from topic to topic that are usually based on understandable associations, distracting stimuli or plays on words. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 823)

Front End Services– Oregon agency lingo for services provided in the community: case management, crisis intervention, prevention, housing, employment and respite care.

Geropsychiatric – aka geropsych – The treatment of frail elderly people with mental disorders, dementia or head trauma.

Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF)– Axis V of the DSM’s multiaxial diagnostic system is used for assessing the person’s overall level of functioning on a scale of 1 to 100. It works by evaluating each range from top down and deciding whether the person’s symptom severity or overall functioning is worse than the description of that range. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 34)

Grandiosity – An inflated assessment of oneself.

Group Therapy – A method of treatment involving a therapist and a group of approximately 4 to 12 clients in which the interactions of the clients are used to modify behavior and relieve distress.

Habeas Corpus – See writ of habeas corpus.

Hallucinations – The experience of an imagined sensory experience as if it were real. Can include, sight, sound, smell, touch, or taste but sound is by far the most common.

Hebephrenic – The old name for Disorganized Schizophrenia.

HIPAA – The Health Insurance Portability Accountability Act of 2003 is a federal act which attempts to standardize the privacy regulations for medical records and other health insurance information.

Histrionic personality disorder – An Axis II personality disorder which is characterized by a pattern of excessive emotionality and attention seeking. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 711)

Hypersomnia – Excessive sleepiness.

Hypertension – Chronic high blood pressure, a side effect of some psychiatric medications.

Hypochondriasis – A preoccupation by a person that he or she has an illness or infirmity despite strong evidence to the contrary.

Iatrogenic Disorders – Those disorders which are caused by the treatment itself.

Ideas of Reference – The feeling that everyday events or objects have a particular meaning specific to the person. Not held with delusional conviction.

Ideation – The lesser form of delusions: a verifiably false feeling or idea which is not held firmly enough to be a delusion.

Illusions – The lesser form of hallucinations: a misperception of an external event like hearing the rustling of leaves as the sound of voices.

Inappropriate Affect – The outward display of emotions which are incongruent with the context.

Inapsine – The trade name for Droperidol, a drug often used in hospitals to calm an agitated or violent patient because of its strong sedative effects which dissipate within two hours.

Insanity – A state of having a sufficiently severe mental disorder at the time of a crime such that the criminal behavior is excused. In Oregon, the insanity defense requires the defendant to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that as a result of a mental disease or defect at the time of engaging in conduct, the defendant lacked substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality of the conduct or to conform the conduct to the requirements of the law.

Insight – The ability to understand that one has a mental illness and the difficulties that may arise from it, usually posed in the negative, as in: “Patient has a complete lack of insight as to his mental illness.”

Insomnia – Difficulties initiating or maintaining sleep. Initial insomnia is where the person has difficulty initiating sleep. Middle insomnia is where the person repeatedly wakes up. Terminal insomnia is where the person wakes up before fully rested and cannot fall back asleep. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 825)

IQ/Intelligence Quotient– An approximation of a person’s overall level of intelligence based on an intelligence test like the Wechsler scales.

Irritability/Irritable mood – Easily annoyed or provoked to anger.

Koro – A pattern of anxiety found in Southeast Asia in which a man suddenly becomes intensely afraid that his penis will withdraw into his abdomen causing immediate death. This disorder is unlikely ever to be seen at a civil commitment hearing. However, it is interesting that there are mental disorders which are specific to certain parts of the world. There are also thinking patterns, such as the belief in magic, that might be considered delusional in this country but not symptomatic at all in a place where magic is a commonly believed entity.

Korsakoff’s Syndrome – A disorder created by chronic alcohol abuse which manifests in severe neurological symptoms like extreme confusion or impaired memory, occasionally seen in commitment hearings.

Labile/Lability – The rapid shifting of one intense emotion to another.

Latencies of Speech – Long pauses before a person appears able to speak.

Magical Thinking – The erroneous belief that one’s thoughts words or actions will cause or prevent a specific outcome in some way that defies commonly understood laws of cause and effect. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 825)

Malignant Neuroleptic Syndrome – See Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome.

Malingering – The intentional faking or gross exaggeration of psychological or physical symptoms with the intent of achieving an external goal. Contrast with factitious disorders.

MAOI – Refers to an older generation of antidepressants which are rarely used unless both Atypical antidepressants and Tricyclics have failed. They have severe dietary restrictions and the potential for hypertensive crisis. MAOIs inhibit the enzyme MonoAmine Oxidase from destroying the neurotransmitter Norepinephrine, thereby increasing the Norepinephrine in the brain.

Medicaid – U. S. health program for low-income individuals and families.

Medicare– Social insurance program administered by the federal government for people who are 65 and older or who meet other criteria.

Mental Disease or Defect – Oregon statutory language for any Axis I or II disorder which is not solely a personality disorder or manifested by antisocial behavior. The phrase is used primarily for the purpose of the GEI, mental state and fitness to proceed statutes.

Mental Disorder – Should not be confused with mental illness, which has a specific legal definition. According to the DSM-IV-TR, a mental disorder is a clinically significant behavioral or psychological syndrome or pattern that occurs in an individual and is associated with present distress (e.g., a painful symptom) or disability (i.e., impairment in one or more important areas of functioning) or with a significantly increased risk of suffering death, pain, disability, or an important loss of freedom. In addition, this syndrome or pattern must not be merely an expectable and culturally sanctioned response to a particular event, for example, the death of a loved one. (DSM-IV-TR, p. xxxi)

Mental Illness – Per ORS 426.005, means that a person has a mental disorder which is causing a person to be dangerous or dangerously unable to care for themselves. Alternatively, a person can be mentally ill if they’re chronically mentally ill, have been hospitalized multiple times previously, are experiencing similar symptoms to those which preceded the previous hospitalizations and there is a reasonable medical certainty that without immediate hospitalization they will become dangerous to self, others or unable to care for their basic needs. The definition of mental illness is also called “the criteria.” And the chronic mental illness alternative is called “expanded criteria.”

Mentally Retarded Person – This is a phrase which is used solely for the purpose of the MRDD commitment statutes in ORS 427.235 to 427.290. It means a person who, because of mental retardation, is or is alleged to be either:

(1) Dangerous to self or others; or

(2) Unable to provide for basic personal needs and not receiving care as is necessary for the health, safety or habilitation of the person.

Mental Retardation – “Mental retardation” means significantly subaverage general intellectual functioning existing concurrently with deficits in adaptive behavior and manifested during the developmental period. Persons of borderline intelligence may be considered mentally retarded if there is also serious impairment of adaptive behavior. Definitions and classifications shall be consistent with the Manual on Terminology and Classification in Mental Retardation of the American Association on Mental Deficiency, 1977 Revision. Mental retardation is synonymous with mental deficiency. ORS 427.005(11). To be eligible for services in Oregon, the deficits must have arisen prior to the person’s 18th birthday.

Mood – A pervasive and sustained emotion that colors the perception of the person’s world. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 825)

Mood Congruent Psychosis – Delusions or hallucinations which are consistent with the typical themes of the person’s mood. For example, if the mood is depressed, the themes would involve death, disease, guilt, worthlessness, nihilism or deserved punishment. If the mood is manic, the themes might involve power, knowledge or special relationships to famous people. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 825)

Mood Incongruent Psychosis – Delusions or hallucinations which are inconsistent with the typical themes of the person’s mood.

MRDD – The acronym stands for Mental Retardation/Developmental Disability but it is generally used to refer to either ORS chapter 427 — “Persons With Mental Retardation; Persons With Developmental Disabilities” or to the county mental health agency in charge of such cases, including civil commitment proceedings.

MRT – Moral Reconation Therapy is a particularly effective and researched form of cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), particularly for reducing recidivism and dealing with substance abuse.

Multiaxial System – A classification system, like the DSM, in which different categories or “axes” are used for different types of diagnostic information. Thus, for example, in the DSM Axis I is used for major mental disorders, Axis II is used for personality disorders and developmental disabilities, Axis III is used for medical problems, Axis IV is used for social crises, and Axis V is used for an overall assessment of functioning.

Narcissistic Personality Disorder – An Axis II personality disorder which is characterized by a pattern of grandiosity, need for admiration and lack of empathy.

Negative Symptoms – In the jargon of Schizophrenia, symptoms are divided into negative and positive. Negative symptoms are those in which a normal function is absent, such as normal emotions, normal speech or ability to control one’s actions (flat affect, poverty of speech and loss of volition). Schizophrenia which is dominated by negative symptoms is called Type II Schizophrenia.

Negativism – An apparently motiveless resistance to instructions or maintenance of a rigid posture against attempts to be moved. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 316)

Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome – A potentially lethal side effect of antipsychotic medications in which there are severe reactions of the autonomic system.

Neuroleptics – Another name for antipsychotic medication. These drugs alleviate symptoms of psychosis such as cognitive perceptual disorders and disorganization as well as affective symptoms and arousal symptoms.

Neurologist – A medical doctor who specializes in organic diseases of the nervous system (which includes the brain).

Neuropsychologist – A clinical psychologist who has post-graduate training in the nervous system (which includes the brain).

Neurosis – An obsolete Freudian term used to refer to any abnormal emotional pain or disorder which was not psychosis.

Neurotransmitters – A chemical of the brain exchanged between neurons via the synapses. Dopamine, seratonin and norepinephrine are all neurotransmitters whose decrease or increase are the target of psychiatric medications.

NIMH – The National Institute of Mental Health is the primary funding source for mental health research.

Notice of Mental Illness (NMI)– A Notice of Mental Illness is the paperwork which must be filed and given to county mental health department in order to start the civil commitment process.

Objective Personality Test – Personality tests are a category of psychological tests which attempts to measure a person’s ingrained patterns of thought and behavior. Objective tests are distinguished by being highly structured and reliably scored. The foremost example of an objective personality test is the MMPI-2.

Obsessions – Persistent ideas, thoughts, impulses, or images that are experienced as intrusive and inappropriate, and that cause marked anxiety or distress. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 457)

OMHAS – (pron. O-Moss) Oregon Mental Health and Addiction Services. OMHAS is the state agency in charge of all mental health programs. It is the new name for what used to be called the Mental Health and Developmental Disability Services Division (MHDDSD).

Orientation – Awareness of oneself in the correct time, place and situation.

OSH – The Oregon State Hospital. The main campus and administrative core is in Salem. This is where One Flew Over the Cuckoo’s Nest was filmed. However, there is an additional OSH campus in Portland, near the Rose Garden arena. There is also the Blue Mountain Recovery Center, also known as the Eastern Oregon Psychiatric Center in Pendleton, which serves the same population of both forensic and committed patients.

Organic Mental Disorder – Disorders which have a clear physical cause.

Outpatient – Community-based treatment as opposed to treatment which occurs in a hospital.

Overvalued Idea – An unreasonable and sustained belief that is maintained with less than delusional intensity (i.e., the person is able to acknowledge the possibility that the belief may not be true). The belief is not one that is ordinarily accepted by other members of the person’s culture or subculture. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 826)

PAITS – Oregon agency lingo for Post Acute Intermediate Treatment Services. Post-Acute services are programs that transition patients from a hospital setting (usually the state hospital) into a community setting.

Paranoid Ideation – Suspicions or beliefs of less than delusional proportions (i.e. the person is able to acknowledge the possibility that the belief may not be true) that one is being harassed, persecuted or unfairly treated. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 826)

Paranoid Personality Disorder – An Axis II personality disorder which is characterized by a pervasive distrust and suspiciousness of others such that their motives are interpreted as malevolent.

Paraphilias – Mental disorders involving the obsession with or inability to control sexual urges. Pedophilia is a paraphilia involving recurrent sexual urges or fantasies involving prepubescent children. Paraphilias can be mental disorders under the civil commitment statutes assuming they cause a serious difficulty controlling one’s dangerous behavior. State v. Gibson, 196 Or App 57 (2004).

Parens Patriae – The legal authority of the State to limit people’s liberty in order to protect them from themselves. Examples include motorcycle helmet laws and civil commitments based on the inability of the individual to meet their basic needs.

Parkinsonian Traits – Symptoms like tremors, rigidity or unsteadiness that are similar to those which occur in Parkinson’s disease. Parkinsonian traits or symptoms are a common side effect of antipsychotic medications, particularly the older generation or typical antipsychotics.

PCP – A primary care practioner is the family or general practice doctor who is often responsible for making a referral to a mental health professional under many health plans.

Pedophilia – See Paraphilias.

Perseveration – Persistent repetition of a thought beyond logic.

Personality – Enduring patterns of perceiving, relating to, and thinking about the environment and oneself. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 826)

Phobia – A persistent and irrational fear of a thing or circumstance such that the person feels compelled to avoid it.

POSH – The Portland branch of the Oregon State Hospital located next to the Rose Garden arena.

Positive Symptoms – In the lingo of schizophrenia, the symptoms are usually divided into positive and negative. Positive symptoms include delusions, hallucinations and disorganization. Schizophrenia that is dominated by positive symptoms is called Type I Schizophrenia.

Posturing – A voluntary assumption of inappropriate or bizarre postures. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 316)

Potency – When used with medications, it refers to the power of the drug in relation to others of its type. Higher potency drugs require lower doses and are usually less sedating but they often incur more side effects. Lower potency drugs require higher doses and are usually more sedating but they often incur fewer side effects.

Poverty of Content – A description of a person’s speaking patter in which there is little information being conveyed because it is overly concrete, overly abstract, repetitive or stereotyped.

Poverty of Speech – A description of a person’s speaking pattern in which there are only brief and concrete replies to questions and restriction in the amount of spontaneous speech.

Premorbid – Indicates a period of time prior to the onset of illness.

Prodrome/Prodromal – An early or foreshadowing sign or symptom of a disorder.

Prognosis – A prediction of the course or outcome of a disorder.

Projective Tests – Psychological tests that use ambiguous stimuli (like inkblots) which require the test-taker to impose meaning, ostensibly revealing otherwise hidden aspects of personality in doing so.

Provider – Any agency, group or individual authorized by law to provide mental health treatment.

PSRB – The Psychiatric Security Review Board is a novel monitoring program for defendants who have been adjudicated Guilty Except Insane.

Psychiatric Survivor – Often used in consumer circles to refer to someone who has been treated in a psychiatric ward.

Psychogenic – A physical symptom caused by psychological processes. The symptoms could be genuine (for example with tension headaches) or imagined.

Psychomotor Agitation – Repetitive, meaningless movements resulting from tension or anxiety, such as pacing, fidgeting or wringing of the hands. (DSM-IV-TR, p. 819)

Psychomotor Retardation – Visible, generalized slowing of movements and speech.

Psychosis – The loss of a particularized point of contact with reality. Diagnostically, the presence of one or more of the following symptoms: delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized behavior or catatonic behavior.

Psychosomatic – From the Greek, psyche for mind and soma for body, a disorder with physical symptoms that originate from mental or emotional causes.

QMHA/Qualified Mental Health Associate– A person serving under the direct supervision of a QMHP who usually has some background or training in mental health – for example, a bachelors degree in psychology or three years training in the relevant field.

QMHP/Qualified Mental Health Professional– A licensed medical practitioner (LMP), licensed nurse or person with a graduate degree who has competency in diagnosis, assessment, therapy and written treatment plans.

Rapid Cycling – Four or more episodes of a mood disturbance in a year where each episode transitions immediately to an episode of the opposite polarity.

Reality Testing – A fundamental human tendency to confirm or test one’s understanding of the world.

Recidivism – Repeating of criminal behavior. Usually counted by new, similar convictions.

Recovery Model of Care – A model for treating mental health which is intended to stand in opposition to the traditional model which views schizophrenia as a chronic and permanent condition with a pessimistic outlook. The recovery model assumes that a person suffering from schizophrenia can recover from their temporary symptoms. It’s supported by various studies indicating that as many as 50 percent of people diagnosed with schizophrenia have good outcomes.

Regression – A change in thought or behavior suggestive of an earlier stage of development.

Residual Phase – The phase of an illness that occurs after remission of the florid or active-phase symptoms.

Respite Care – A place for a person with a decompensating mental illness to stabilize short of a hospital setting. Respite care beds are much cheaper than hospital beds yet there is astonishingly little respite care in Oregon.

RTF – Oregon agency lingo for a residential treatment facility. An authorized RTF may provide 24-hour supervised care for any amount of people.

RTH – Residential Treatment Home. An authorized RTH may provide 24-hour supervised care for 5 or less people.

SCF – a shortened acronym for SOSCF. The State Office for Services to Children and Families was Oregon’s child welfare agency, now known as DHS.

Schizoid Personality Disorder – A personality disorder characterized by a pattern of detachment from social relationships and a blunted affect.

Schizophreniform Disorder – An Axis I disorder in which all the criteria are met for schizophrenia except the time period. Must be more than one month but less than six.

Schizotypal Disorder – A personality disorder characterized by a pattern of acute discomfort in close relationships, distortions of reality which are short of delusions, hallucinations and eccentricities of behavior.

Secondary Gain – An external reward derived from having an illness, for example, extra attention or disability benefits.

Self-Determination – A phrase used to indicate that a person with mental retardation or developmental disabilities should be provided with support with the goal of maximizing the ability of the person to be responsible for his or her own money, choices and life.

Somatoform Disorder – A physical illness which cannot be explained by a medical condition, the effects of a substance or another mental disorder. The person has no sense of controlling the illness.

Somatic Hallucination – A hallucination involving the perception of a physical experience localized within the body (such as a feeling of electricity). (DSM-IV-TR, p. 824)

Somatization – Psychological distress expressed as physical symptoms, such as a tension headache. Tension is converted to a headache.

SPMI – Severe and persistent mental illness. This is a relatively new term, used often in non-profit and government agencies (but not courts) to describe the mental health of a person who has a serious and long-term mental disorder.

SRTF – Oregon agency lingo for a Secure Residential Treatment Facility.

SRTH – Oregon agency lingo for a Secure Residential Treatment Home.

SSD – Social Security Disability.

SSI – Supplemental Security Income.

SSRIs – The group of second-generation antidepressants which work by selectively inhibiting the neurotransmitter seratonin, whose overproduction has been linked to depression, eating disorders and obsessive-compulsive disorder.

Stereotyped Movement – Repetitive, seemingly driven, and nonfunctional motor behavior (e.g., hand shaking or waving, body rocking, head banging, mouthing of objects, self-biting, picking at the skin or body orifices or hitting one’s body). (DSM-IV-TR, p. 827)

Sub-acute beds –Sub-acute means just short of the hospital. More specifically it either means respite beds, a place where a person can stabilize instead of the hospital, or post-acute placement, a transitional bed from the hospital to the community.

Suicidality – Includes any desire or thoughts of ending one’s life. Suicidal intent is the affirmative desire to end one’s life. Suicidal ideations are thoughts about committing suicide, regardless of plan or intent. A suicidal plan is where a specific method of committing suicide has been considered. “Approximately 10 percent of individuals with schizophrenia commit suicide – and between 20 percent and 40 percent make at least one attempt over the course of the illness. Although the risk remains high over the whole lifespan, specific risk factors for suicide include male gender, being under 45 years of age, depressive symptoms, feelings of hopelessness, unemployment, and recent hospital discharge. Suicide risk is also elevated during post-psychotic periods.” (DSM-IV-TR, p. 304)

Stupor – A state of unresponsiveness with the apparent inability to move or speak.

Symptom – An objective sign of illness.

Syndrome – A group of symptoms that usually occur together.

Synesthesia – A condition in which one type of sensory experience is stimulated by another; for example, a sound produces the sensation of a particular color.

Tangential Thinking – Thought process which involuntarily segues from the topic of focus to a related but irrelevant topic. Tangential thinking is a classic symptom of schizophrenia.

Tardive Dyskinesia – Side effect of antipsychotics (particularly the older generation antipsychotics) in which extrapyramidal effects, such as a facial twitch, are obvious and consistent.

Thought Broadcasting – A positive symptom of schizophrenia where people report hearing the thoughts of others, broadcasting their own thoughts and claiming to hear voices or thoughts where none exist.

Thought Insertion – The delusion that thoughts are being inserted into one’s brain by someone else.

Tic – An involuntary, sudden, rapid, recurrent, non-rhythmic, stereotyped movement or sound.

Transference – The unconscious assignment to another person of the attitude or intent of a significant figure from the past, like a parent. In Freudian therapy, this tendency is used in the therapist-patient relationship to demonstrate to the patient otherwise unconscious behaviors and feelings.

Tricyclics – Tricyclic antidepressants (or simply tricyclics) is a newer generation of antidepressants, so called because it has three rings in its molecular structure.

Validity – Accuracy of prediction. High validity indicates high accuracy, usually used to refer to the correlation of a test score and a predicted behavior.

Wechsler Intelligence Scales – Also known as the WAIS (for adults) and the WISC (for children, the Wechsler Intelligence Scales are widely recognized as being the most valid and reliable IQ test. On the Wechsler Scales, intelligence is divided into performance, verbal and full-scale IQ. One hundred is normed to be the average.

Word Salad – Incomprehensible speech or thinking in which seemingly random words are joined together in what seem like clauses and sentences.

Writ of Habeas Corpus– Latin for “you have the body”; a writ of habeas corpus is used to bring a person before a judge to question the basis of their confinement. They are used in civil commitment proceedings when a person is held for more than five judicial days without a hearing (e.g. where there is a back-to-back hold). Once in front of a judge, the defense attorney may move to dismiss the case.
