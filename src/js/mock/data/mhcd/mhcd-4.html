Guilty Except for Insanity

Thirteen Elements You Should Know About the GEI Defense

1. The Standard

“…as a result of a mental disease or defect at the time of engaging in conduct, the person lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality of the conduct or to conform the conduct to the requirements of the law.”

2. "[A]s a result of a mental disease or defect”

a mental disease or defect is any mental abnormality (including mental retardation) so long as it is not:

  • Solely a personality disorder. ORS 161.295(2).
  • Manifested only by antisocial or criminal conduct. ORS 161.295(2). For example, the DSM diagnostic criterion for Intermittent Explosive Disorder is “several discrete episodes of failure to resist aggressive impulses that result in serious assaultive acts or destruction of property.” It is, therefore, not a mental disease or defect for GEI purposes. Sandgathe v. Maass, 314 F3d 371 (2000).
  • Controlled by substance or alcohol dependency. Tharp v. PSRB,338 Or 413 (2005), Ashcroft v. PSRB,338 Or 448 (2005).
  • A combination of mental condition and intoxication where the mental condition alone would be insufficient. State v. Peverier,192 Or App 229 (2004).
  • A sexual conduct disorder, which the courts will consider a personality disorder. Beiswenger v. PSRB, 192 Or App 38 (2004). Pedophilia, however, is a mental disease or defect because it requires more than a manifestation of criminal or antisocial conduct. Rios v. PSRB, 135 Or App 265 (1995); Osborn v. PSRB, 135 Or App 94 (1995).

3. “[A]t the time of engaging in conduct”

The insanity defense zeroes in on the defendant’s state of mind at the time of the incident. Counsel should document carefully any observations of the client’s behavior. This could become the best evidence of defendant’s state of mind at the time of the alleged crime. Counsel should also hire an expert as quickly as possible so that the clinical interview is conducted prior to the time that the defendant has become stabilized on medication so that the expert can see the client in as similar a state as possible to the time of the incident.

4.“[A]ppreciate the criminality of the conduct”

The essence of this prong of the insanity defense is that the defendant did not understand that what he was doing at the time of the incident was criminal in nature. For example, the defendant may have been motivated by delusional or nonsensical considerations without a logical sense of consequence.

5.“[C]onform the conduct to the requirements of the law”

Often called “the control test,” this prong of the insanity defense requires proof that, despite defendant’s understanding, he was unable to control his behavior. The question is one of volitional conduct. For example, persons experiencing strong command hallucinations often have the feeling that the conscious self has faded into the background, and, as a result, feel powerless to make the body resist the demands of the voices.

6. Notice

Filing a notice raises the defense. ORS 161.315. This should be done as soon as counsel anticipates that a GEI defense will be raised. The notice can always be withdrawn.

7. Affirmative Defense

GEI is an affirmative defense that must be proven by a preponderance of the evidence. ORS 161.305.

8. State Evaluation

Once defense notice has been filed, the State has the right to have the defendant examined. The State must file written notice of its request for evaluation. The defendant may object to the State’s evaluator and, assuming good cause, the State must choose a different one. The court can, in its discretion, commit the defendant for up to thirty days for observation and examination. ORS 161.315.

9. Disposition

The court must discharge the defendant after GEI unless all of the following findings under ORS 161.327 are made:

  • The criminal charge was either:

– (a) a felony or

– (b) a misdemeanor which involved physical injury or a risk of physical injury

  • The person has a mental disease or defect
  • The person presents a substantial danger to others requiring commitment.

10. Conditional Release

Even if the court makes the findings for commitment, it may release the defendant to the Psychiatric Security Review Board (PSRB) under a conditional release. The court must find that “the person can be adequately controlled with supervision and treatment” and “that necessary supervision and treatment are available.” ORS 161.327(2).

11. Maximum Sentence

The maximum term of a GEI commitment is the statutory maximum sentence the defendant could have received had he or she been convicted and sentenced to jail. If the defendant pleads GEI to multiple crimes or counts that could be run consecutive under the statute, the maximum GEI term is calculated as if the defendant had been sentenced to consecutive, as opposed to concurrent, time. The sentencing guidelines are irrelevant for determining the maximum term of a GEI commitment. ORS 161.327. State v. Brooks, 187 Or App 388 (2003).

12. The Psychiatric Security Review Board (PSRB)

The PSRB is a probation-like monitoring program that supervises the defendant after a GEI commitment. If the defendant is initially sent to the hospital, she will likely spend a considerable amount of time at the Hospital. Defense attorneys often significantly underestimate the amount of time it takes a defendant to get out of the hospital after a GEI adjudication. After release from the hospital the defendant will continue to be supervised by the PSRB and can be brought back to the hospital for any violation of the conditions of release.

13. A GEI Adjudication Is Not a Conviction

It does not trigger mandatory fines and it does not count as a prior conviction for the sentencing guidelines. State v. Saunders, 195 Or App 357 (2004). OAR 253-09-002. However, a GEI adjudication will trigger sex offender registration because ORS 181.594(5)(b) specifically includes within the definition of "sex offender" a person who "has been found guilty except for insanity of a sex crime."

Discussion

I. A Readable Version of the Statutes Related to GEI

My intent with this section is merely to translate statutory language into readable, plain English. I have tried not to alter any legal standards or phrases. The main reason for doing this is that, on the one hand, very few people read these statutes because they are thick with the language of statutory repetition and overkill and they mostly deal with hearings that occur after the criminal defense attorney’s representation is complete. On the other hand, there are crucial aspects of the statutory scheme that must be understood before advocating for or pleading to a GEI adjudication. For example, buried deep within ORS 161.336, a boring, repetitive statute regarding conditional release, section 5 makes it clear that the chair of the PSRB can order a person returned to the hospital if it “appears” that any condition of the release has been violated or even if it appears that the person’s “mental health has changed.” People have been known to be returned to the hospital for minor violations such as curfew or verbal arguments. The person is entitled to a hearing within 20 days but that’s often far too late to put the person back in the position they were in.

A. ORS 161.295 – GEI standard

A person is GEI if:

  • as a result of a mental disease or defect
  • at the time of engaging in criminal conduct,
  • the person lacks substantial capacity
  • to appreciate the criminality of the conduct or
  • to conform the conduct to the requirements of law.

An abnormality is not a “mental disease or defect” if it is:

  • manifested only by repeated criminal or otherwise antisocial conduct; or
  • solely a personality disorder.

B. ORS 161.305 – GEI Is an Affirmative Defense

C. ORS 161.309 – Notice

(1) No evidence may be introduced for a GEI defense unless proper notice is given.

(2) Disordered mental state evidence also requires proper notice if an expert will be called.

(3) Proper notice means “a written notice of purpose at the time the defendant pleads not guilty.” Notice may be filed up until trial (or never filed) if the court is satisfied there is “just cause” for not filing at the time of plea (or not filing at all).

D. ORS 161.313 – Jury Instructions: A GEI jury instruction must be given in accordance with 161.327.

E. ORS 161.315 – State’s Right to an Evaluation

  • Once notice is given, the state has the right to have its own evaluation of the defendant.
  • The state must file notice of its intention to have the defendant examined.
  • The court may order the defendant committed for up to 30 days for observation and examination.
  • If the defendant objects to the state’s examiner, the court for good cause may direct the state to choose another examiner.

F. ORS 161.319 – Judgment: When a defendant is found GEI, “the verdict and judgment shall so state.”

G. ORS 161.325 – Order of Commitment or Discharge

(1) After a judgment of GEI, the judge must either order a commitment to the PSRB or discharge. A separate hearing may be requested by either party.

(2) If the court orders commitment to the PSRB, it shall also state:

  • The offense for which the person would have been convicted
  • The specific mental disease or defect
  • Whether there is a victim
  • Whether the victim wants to be notified of PSRB hearings and release, discharge or escape of defendant.

(3) The court shall include any such findings in its order.

(4) The court shall order a blood or saliva sample for a GEI adjudication as if the person were convicted of the underlying offense.

H. ORS 161.326 – Victim Notification

(1) Whenever a person already under the board’s jurisdiction commits a new crime, the court or the board shall make the findings described in ORS 161.325 (2).

(2) If the victim requests it, the board shall make a reasonable effort to notify the victim of board hearings, conditional release, discharge or escape.

I. ORS 161.327 – When the Court May Order Commitment to the PSRB

(1)(a) The court shall order supervision by the PSRB if:

  • The adjudication was either for a felony or for a misdemeanor “during a criminal episode in the course of which the person caused physical injury or risk of physical injury to another.”
  • The court is in receipt of a psychological evaluation (if not it should order one.)
  • The person is affected by a mental disease or defect
  • The person presents a substantial danger to others requiring commitment to OSH (or an inpatient facility if the person is a juvenile.)

(b) The period of jurisdiction of the board is equal to the maximum sentence provided by statute for the underlying crime.

(c) The court can order PDSC to pay for an evaluation under this section.

(2) The court shall either commit the person to the State Hospital (or an inpatient facility for juveniles) or conditionally release the person:

(a) If the person presents a substantial danger to others and is not a proper subject for conditional release, the court shall commit the person to the State Hospital (or inpatient facility if the person is under 18) pending a hearing before the PSRB in accordance with ORS 161.341 to 161.351

(b) The judge may order conditional release if:

  • the person is a substantial danger to others
  • but can be adequately controlled with supervision and treatment
  • necessary supervision and treatment are available.

The judge may order all conditions of release which are in the best interests of justice, the protection of society and the welfare of the person. The court shall designate a person or agency to supervise the defendant upon release after giving the potential supervisor a chance to be heard. The supervisor shall be under the direction of the PSRB and required to report in writing no less than once per month to the board regarding compliance with the conditions of release.

(3) A mental illness in remission is still a “disease or defect requiring supervision” when the illness is likely, by a reasonable medical certainty, to occasionally become active and render the person dangerous.

(4) In determining whether a person should be conditionally released, the court may order evaluations, examinations and compliance as provided in ORS 161.336(4) and 161.346(2).

(5) When deciding whether a person should be committed or conditionally released, “the court shall have as its primary concern the protection of society.”

(6) The court shall notify the PSRB in writing of:

  • the court’s conditional release order,
  • the supervisor appointed, and
  • all other conditions of release.

Upon completion of the court order and notification, the court’s jurisdiction is terminated and the PSRB assumes jurisdiction.

(7) A dispositional GEI order is a final order for appeal purposes and entitles the person to suitable counsel in the usual manner. Notice of an appeal shall be filed within 90 days after entry of the order (“notwithstanding ORS 19.255”).

(8) Upon placing a person under the jurisdiction of the board, the court shall notify the person of the right to appeal and the right to a hearing before the board in accordance with ORS 161.336 (7) and 161.341 (4).

J. ORS 161.328 – Civil Commitment: If the court discharges the person, it may always initiate civil commitment proceedings in the usual course.

K. ORS 161.329 – Discharge: If the court finds that the person is no longer affected by mental disease or defect, or no longer presents a substantial danger to others and is not in need of care, supervision or treatment, the court shall order the person discharged from custody.

L. ORS 161.332 – “Conditional release” includes, but is not limited to, the monitoring of mental and physical health treatment.

M. ORS 161.336 – Conditional Release

(1) The PSRB may order conditional release if it finds that:

  • the person presents a substantial danger to others
  • but can be adequately controlled with supervision and treatment if released
  • such supervision and treatment are available.

The board may order conditions of release as are in the best interests of justice, the protection of society and the welfare of the person. The PSRB may order any person or agency to supervise the conditional release, after giving them an opportunity to be heard.

Conditional release may be terminated (see ORS 161.351) or the conditions modified by the PSRB.

(3) A mental disorder in remission is a “mental disease or defect requiring supervision” when it may occasionally become active and render the person a danger to others.

(4)(a) As a condition of release, the board may require the person to be evaluated and cooperate with treatment from any mental health facility.

(b) The evaluator shall submit a written report to the PSRB which should include recommendations for treatment.

(c) The treatment provider shall furnish regular reports to the PSRB.

(d) All reports submitted to the PSRB shall be furnished to the person and their counsel. The confidentiality of these reports is determined pursuant to ORS 192.501 to 192.505.

(e) The facility shall comply with all conditions of release.

(5) The PSRB or its chair may order a person returned to OSH (or a facility for juveniles) if “it appears . . . that the person has violated the terms of the conditional release or that the mental health of the person has changed.” A written order of the chair will function as an arrest warrant and cause an officer to execute the order. Within 20 days, the person must be given a PSRB hearing with an appointed attorney and all rights under 161.346. The PSRB must continue the person on conditional release unless it finds, by a preponderance of the evidence, that the person:

  • is affected by a mental disease or defect
  • presents a substantial danger to others and
  • cannot be adequately controlled if conditional release is continued.

(6) Any peace officer, county mental health designee or supervising person may take a person on conditional release into custody if there is reasonable cause to believe:

  • the person is a substantial danger to others
  • because of a mental disease or defect and
  • the person is in need of immediate custody and treatment.

A person taken into custody must be taken as soon as practicable to the appropriate facility. The person retains all rights under 161.346.

(7)(a) A person on conditional release may apply to the PSRB for discharge or modification of conditions once every 6 months. Notice must be given to the Attorney General. The person must prove, by a preponderance of the evidence, that he or she:

  • is no longer affected by mental disease or defect or
  • no longer presents a substantial danger to others and
  • no longer requires supervision, medication, care or treatment.

(b) Any supervising person or agency may apply for modification or termination of conditional release. The application must be accompanied by a report setting forth supporting facts and will cause a PSRB hearing to be held.

(8) Conditional release counts toward the maximum period of time the person may be supervised by the PSRB.

(9) The PSRB shall keep current the medical, social and criminal history of all PSRB clients. The confidentiality of such records shall be determined under ORS 192.501 to 192.505.

(10) In all decisions regarding commitment, release or discharge the board shall have as its primary concern the protection of society.

N. ORS 161.341 – Applications to the PSRB for Discharge or Conditional Release

(1) If the PSRB decides not to conditionally release the committed person at the initial hearing, it must commit him or her to the state hospital.

(2) The hospital may apply to the board for a discharge or conditional release at any time if it feels the person meets the relevant standards. The board must hold a hearing within sixty days of the application. If the application is for conditional release there must be a “verified conditional release plan.”

Standard for Discharge: “The person is no longer affected by mental disease or defect, or, if so affected, no longer presents a substantial danger to others.”

Standard for Conditional Release: “The person can be controlled with proper care, medication, supervision and treatment if conditionally released.”

(3) The state’s attorney may choose an expert to conduct an evaluation and file a report as to the committed person’s mental condition, level of dangerousness and potential for being adequately controlled with treatment on conditional release.

(4) The committed person or any other person may apply for discharge or conditional release if:

  • the person is no longer affected by mental disease or defect
  • the person no longer presents a substantial danger to others or
  • the person can be adequately controlled and given proper care and treatment if placed on conditional release.

(5) Under a section (4) application, the facility must submit a report discussing whether the standards for discharge or conditional release are met. The committed person must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the standards for discharge or conditional release are met unless “more than two years have passed since the state had the burden of proof on that issue, in which case the state shall have the burden of proving by a preponderance” the person’s “lack of fitness for discharge or conditional release.” There may be no more than one application made every six months, from any source.

(6) A hearing on a committed person’s application must be held within ninety days of the application. Any subsequent hearings must be held within sixty days of the initial hearing.

(7) In any case, a person on a court commitment must have an initial hearing within ninety days of the court’s commitment order.

O. ORS 161.346 – PSRB Hearings

(1) At PSRB hearings findings shall be made as follows:

(a) If the board finds that the person no longer has a mental disease or defect or is not a substantial danger to others, it shall discharge.

(b) If the board finds that the person’s mental disease or defect and dangerousness can be adequately controlled on conditional release, it shall order the person conditionally released.

(c) If neither (a) nor (b) the board shall order continued commitment.

(2) At any time, the board may appoint a expert to perform an evaluation and submit a report on the issues related to discharge or conditional release. The board may order the person placed in the temporary custody of a facility to accomplish the evaluation.

(3) The board may make a decision based on the written reports. Any board member may summon the evaluator to testify. The board shall consider all evidence available to it which is material, relevant and reliable, including the trial record, the person’s criminal record and any information submitted by any “interested party.” Testimony must be under oath. “All evidence of a type commonly relied upon by reasonably prudent persons in the conduct of their serious affairs shall be admissible at hearings.”

(4) The board shall give notice to the committed person, Attorney General, district attorney, and court of:

(a) The time, place and location of the hearing.

(b) The nature of the hearing, issues to be considered, and statutes and rules involved.

(c) The authority and jurisdiction of the board.

(d) A statement of rights under subsection (6).

(5) Prior to the commencement of a hearing, the board or presiding officer shall inform each party as provided in ORS 183.413 (2).

(6) At the hearing, the person shall have the right:

(a) To appear at all proceedings held pursuant to this section, except board deliberations.

(b) To cross-examine all witnesses.

(c) To subpoena witnesses and documents as provided in ORS 161.395.

(d) To be represented by suitable legal counsel possessing skills and experience commensurate with the nature and complexity of the case, to consult with counsel prior to the hearing and, if financially eligible, to have suitable counsel appointed at state expense.

(e) To examine all information, documents and reports considered by the board. If available to the board, the information, documents and reports shall be disclosed to the committed person so as to allow examination prior to the hearing.

(7) A record shall be kept of all hearings before the board, except board deliberations.

(8) Upon request of any party before the board, or on its own motion, the board may continue a hearing for a reasonable period not to exceed sixty days to obtain additional information or testimony or for other good cause shown.

(9) Within fifteen days following the conclusion of the hearing, the board shall provide to the person, the attorney representing the person, the Attorney General or other attorney representing the state, if any, written notice of the board’s decision.

(10) The burden of proof on all issues at hearings of the board shall be by a preponderance of the evidence.

(11) If the board determines that the person is financially eligible, then a PDSC qualified attorney shall be appointed and fairly compensated by the PDSC.

(12) The district attorney may choose whether or not to represent the state, but must notify timely the Attorney General, the board and the committed person’s attorney. Otherwise, the Attorney General will represent the state.

P. ORS 161.351 – PSRB Discharge

(1) The PSRB shall discharge when it finds that the person is no longer affected by mental disease or is no longer dangerous.

(2) A person still has a mental disease for discharge purposes if the mental disease is in remission but occasionally becomes active and when active renders the person a danger to others.

(3) After five years of conditional release, a person must have a hearing within thirty days to determine discharge.

Q. ORS 161.375 – Escape of PSRB Client

(1) When a PSRB client has escaped from OSH, the superintendent of OSH (or a designee) may order the arrest and detention of the patient.

(2) When a PSRB client has escaped from an inpatient facility, the director of the facility shall notify DHS. The director of DHS may order the arrest and detention of the patient.

(3) The order to arrest may be issued upon a reasonable belief there has been an escape.

(4) All laws applicable to warrant or arrest apply to an arrest order under this section. The

order will expire 72 hours after being signed.

R. ORS 161.385 – The PSRB

(1) The PSRB shall consist of 10 members appointed by the Governor and confirmed by the Senate.

(2) None of the 10 members may include a district attorney, deputy district attorney or a public defender. It shall include:

(a,b) A psychiatrist and a licensed psychologist who have criminal justice experience and are not full-time employees of DHS or a community mental health/DD program.

(c) A member with substantial experience in parole and probation;

(d) A criminal defense attorney;

(e,f) An Oregon certified child psychiatrist and a licensed child psychologist who have juvenile justice experience and are not full-time employees of DHS or a community mental health/DD program.

(g) A member with substantial experience in juvenile parole and probation;

(h) A juvenile attorney and

(i) Two members of the general public.

(3) The members are appointed for four years but may be removed for inefficiency, neglect of duty or malfeasance. There are no term limits.

(4) The members are paid $289.22 a day plus travel and other expenses.

(5) The board may hire employees to aid it in performing its duties.

(6) The board is split into an adult panel and a juvenile panel, each with 5 members.

(7)(a) Each panel shall choose a chair and determine its duties.

(b) A majority of the voting members of a panel constitutes a quorum

(8) Each panel shall meet at least twice every month, unless there is insufficient business.

(9)(a) When a PSRB client is aggrieved by a PSRB order, the client is entitled to judicial review and appointment of suitable counsel.

(b) The order and the proceedings are subject to review by the Court of Appeals if a petition is filed within 60 days.

(c) The court may affirm, reverse or remand the order as provided in ORS 183.482.

(8)(d) The filing of the petition does not stay the panel’s order, but the panel or the Court of Appeals may order a stay.

S. ORS 161.387 – PSRB Rulemaking and Open Meeting Requirements (OAR 859-010)

(1) The PSRB may create interpretative rules necessary to carry out its responsibilities.

(2) Neither PSRB administrative meetings nor the evidentiary phase of PSRB hearings are exempt from the public notice and recording laws in ORS 162.110 to 162.190.

T. ORS 161.390 – Rule Creation and Discharge Planning

(1) DHS shall create rules and standards for evaluation, treatment and placement decisions.

(2) DHS is responsible for preparing conditional release and discharge plans for people in OSH (or an inpatient facility for juveniles). I.e., the PSRB is not responsible for discharge planning.

(3) DHS may contract with anyone to provide supervision and treatment under a conditional release plan.

U. ORS 161.400 – Passes

A hospital or other facility in charge of a PSRB client may grant passes of up to 48 hours if:

  • an absence from the facility would be therapeutic for the person
  • it would pose no substantial danger to others
  • notice is given to the PSRB before the pass is granted.

II.A Brief History and Context

A. History

Oregon is one of the only states in the nation with the unusual guilty except insanity structure and one of two states with a psychiatric security review board (PSRB). Many states still acquit and then civilly commit criminal defendants found to be insane and dangerous. Several states have abolished the insanity defense altogether.

Generally speaking, Oregon’s insanity defense statute is based on the American Law Institute (ALI) standard developed as part of the Model Penal Code. The ALI standard reads:

A person is not responsible for criminal conduct if at the time of such conduct as a result of mental disease or defect he lacks substantial capacity to either appreciate the criminality of his conduct or to conform his conduct to the requirements of law.

The primary distinction between the ALI standard and the Oregon standard is that in 1983 Oregon changed the wording from “not responsible for criminal conduct at the time of such conduct” to “guilty except for insanity.” ORS 161.295 now states:

A person is guilty except for insanity if, as a result of mental disease or defect at the time of engaging in criminal conduct, the person lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality of the conduct or to conform the conduct to the requirements of law.

Traditionally and under the ALI standard, a person was not responsible for his conduct if he was insane. The test for insanity has changed since the inception of the insanity defense, but until the 1980’s (when Hinckley successfully asserted the insanity defense after shooting President Reagan) the lack of responsibility was an essential aspect of insanity. Within four years of the Hinckley verdict more than half the states as well as the federal government constricted the scope of their insanity statutes.

Prior to its adoption of the ALI standard, Oregon’s insanity statute was based on the legendary M’Naghten insanity test with the irresistible impulse test addendum. The traditional formulation of this rule is that:

In all cases of this kind the jurors ought to be told that a man is presumed sane . . . until the contrary be proved to their satisfaction. It must be clearly proved that at the time of committing the act, the party accused was laboring under such a defect of reason, from disease of the mind, as not to know the nature and quality of the act he was doing, or as not to know that what he was doing was wrong. If he did have such knowledge, he may nevertheless not be responsible if by reason of the duress of such mental disease, he had so far lost the power to choose between right and wrong, and to avoid doing the act in question as that his free agency was at the time destroyed.

See Commentary, 1971 Or. Crim. Code. In State v. Brooks, the Oregon Court of Appeals discussed the history and present state of the insanity defense:

Although the insanity defense itself evolved from English common law, the disposition of those who successfully assert such a defense under Oregon law appears to have been based solely on statutory law rather than common law. Oregon’s first law concerning the insanity defense was enacted in 1864, shortly after Oregon became a state. It provided that if a person was found not guilty by reason of insanity, and the court deemed the person “dangerous to the public peace or safety,” the court was “to order him committed to any lunatic asylum.” Such a commitment was to continue “until he becomes sane, or otherwise discharged therefrom by authority of law.” General Laws of Oregon, Crim Code, ch XVII, § 170, p 469 (Deady 1845–1864). The law in that respect remained essentially unchanged until the creation of the PSRB in 1977.

State v. Brooks, 187 Or App 388 (2003).

Oregon is one of only two states that has a psychiatric security review board (PSRB), a monitoring board for those who have been found GEI and are a substantial danger to others. The PSRB is composed of a multi-disciplinary group which includes, by statute, a psychiatrist, a psychologist, an attorney experienced in criminal practice, a parole officer and a member of the general public. The primary purpose of the PSRB, again by statute, is to protect the public. As a result, the PSRB tends to make more conservative decisions regarding release to the community than one would get from, say, a hospital risk review board which weighs the risk of release with the monetary costs and therapeutic benefits of keeping a person in the hospital as well as any other factors which may seem relevant. It’s not that the PSRB doesn’t consider other factors but it has only one primary purpose – safety of the community. Thus, even a tiny risk of harm may be too great if the degree of potential harm is severe. The PSRB decides when a person should be released from the state hospital on conditional release but it also monitors people while they’re on conditional release and can revoke a person off of conditional release (sending the person back to the hospital).

Oregon has one of the highest proportions in the nation of state hospital patients with criminal court commitments as opposed to civil commitments. Criminal court commitments are either defendants being treated for competency issues or defendants who have been adjudicated guilty except insane. Sixty-seven percent of the beds at Oregon State Hospital are used by patients who have been committed to the hospital through one of these two paths. Unfortunately, the numbers are trending in the direction of even more criminal commitments (with correlatively less non-criminal beds). The new state hospital is being built with an underlying assumption that vastly more criminal beds will be needed in the future. In contrast, Connecticut, which has a similar population size and is the only other state with a psychiatric security review board, fills thirty-four percent of their state hospital beds with criminal commitments and sixty-six percent with civil commitments (OMHAS submission to Oregon legislature, 2005). Among other things, such statistics reflect the fact that Oregon, whether by default or design, uses its criminal justice system as a substitute for a strong, connected community mental health system.

One reflection of the criminal to civil bed ratio is that people tend to stay in the hospital on a GEI adjudication for a longer time than most defense attorneys would intuit and much longer than in other states. For example, in Tharp v. PSRB, 338 Or 413 (2005), Mr. Tharp was in the state hospital for over five years for a mental illness he did not have. He was mistakenly diagnosed with schizophrenia in 2000, but five separate treatment teams at the state hospital found that, while he had some drug-induced psychosis at the time of the crime, he did not have any major mental disorder. Nonetheless, the PSRB refused to release him, even into a secure nonhospital setting. As Mr. Tharp’s case demonstrates, an individual who improves with treatment will stay in the hospital until the PSRB is satisfied that the community will be safe.

On the other hand, it is worth noting that the PSRB has had unparalleled success with reducing recidivism to as close to zero as it is possible to get. The PSRB will say that their average yearly recidivism rate for those who are on conditional release is approximately 2.3% (as opposed to more than 30% for those who are on parole to the Department of Corrections.) However, it is only because of an accounting quirk needed to standardize the data with the DOC that the recidivism rate is as high as it is. Were it to be counted by a more traditional method, the recidivism rate would be less than .1%. For example, in 2008 there were 744 people committed to the PSRB with 367 people on conditional release and exactly one person was alleged to have committed a new felony. It is also worth noting that the number of conditional releases has been sharply rising over the past five years as more group homes have been sited and built. Such additional releases have not resulted in more recidivism.

Here is the takeaway point: If your client is committed to the PSRB, he or she will likely stay in custody for much longer than you think and the probationlike supervision they will receive will be more onerous than the supervision provided by Community Corrections. However, the chance of your client’s short term treatment success and long term transition to being a productive member of the community will be much greater under the PSRB than through the normal criminal justice route.

B. Insanity Is Not the Same as Psychosis or Psychopathy

1.Insanity is a legal word that means whatever the legislature says it means. In Oregon, the legislature has defined insanity as a state of mental disease or defect such that the person lacks the capacity to control behavior or recognize its criminality. Other states define insanity differently. The importance of insanity is that it excuses otherwise criminal behavior and ostensibly leads to treatment rather than punishment or incarceration.

2.Psychopathy is a word used primarily in the forensic arena. While it is not an official diagnosis in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders(DSM-IV-TR) by the American Psychiatry Association, it refers to a particular constellation of personality traits which have been strongly linked to criminality, violence and recidivism. There is a test called the PCL-R, created by Robert Hare, which can be used to assess psychopathy. According to Dr. Hare, psychopaths lack conscience and feeling for others. Cold-bloodedly taking what they want and doing what they please, they are unencumbered by even the slightest sense of remorse or regret. While psychopathy has some overlap with the DSM-IV-TR diagnosis of Antisocial Personality Disorder, psychopathy is intended to catch a much smaller and more dangerous population. Most psychopaths are antisocial, but most persons with Antisocial Personality Disorder are not psychopaths. For example, while many criminal defendants may be accurately diagnosed with Antisocial Personality Disorder, the vast majority are not psychopaths. The colloquial term “psycho” is short for psychopath, not a person who is psychotic or has psychosis. Sociopath is another term for psychopath.

3.Psychosis is an official psychological/psychiatric term that encompasses a variety of psychotic disorders defined in the DSM-IV-TR. The general definition of psychosis from the DSM-IV-TR is the presence of delusions, prominent hallucinations, disorganized speech, or catatonic or disorganized behavior. Note that a person may be psychotic but not insane and a person may be insane but not psychotic. Psychosis, however, is the diagnosis most likely to result in a successful insanity defense.

C. Clark v. Arizona, 5448 US 23 (2006)

The recent U.S. Supreme Court case of Clark v. Arizona, 548 US 23 (2006) does not affect Oregon’s insanity defense. It does, however, indicate the high Court’s willingness to permit an intensifying national trend to strictly limit the insanity defense and, as such, merits a brief summary.

Mr. Clark was convicted of murdering a police officer in Arizona. He suffered from schizophrenia and was strongly symptomatic at the time of the crime. The defense attempted to present both an insanity defense and evidence of a disordered mental state. Unfortunately, the defendant was barred from presenting evidence of his mental illness because Arizona has severely limited the admissibility of mental health evidence in criminal trials.

Arizona’s current insanity defense consists only of the moral capacity prong of the old M’Naghten defense: that at the time of the crime the defendant’s mental disease or defect was so severe he did not know his actions were wrong. Mr. Clark’s attorneys argued that the traditional M’Naghten formulation is the minimum required by due process. The traditional formulation, in existence in almost every state until the middle of the twentieth century, included both the moral capacity prong (capacity to know that actions are “wrong”) as well as a cognitive capacity prong: that the defendant understood the nature and quality of his actions.

The Supreme Court, however, ruled that states are largely free to define the insanity defense as they see fit. The M’Naghten formulation held sway for a couple hundred years but that doesn’t make it constitutionally required. Definitions of insanity vary greatly from state to state and there at least four separate common formulations. Given this, it would be odd to set M’Naghten as the minimum requirement.

Additionally, the Court wasn’t convinced that there were situations in which a person would be able to understand that his actions were wrong (and therefore be sane under Arizona law) but unable to understand the nature and quality of his actions (therefore insane under the traditional formulation). In fact, Mr. Clark could point to no evidence that was excluded; nor was he able to set forth a logical argument that he lacked cognitive capacity but possessed moral capacity.

III. The Statutory Scheme

A. The Basic Standard

A person may successfully assert the defense of guilty except for insanity if:

  • “...as a result of a mental disease or defect
  • at the time of engaging in conduct,
  • the person lacks substantial capacity either to
  • appreciate the criminality of the conduct or to
  • conform the conduct to the requirements of the law.” ORS 161.295.

B. “[A]s a result of a mental disease or defect”

A mental disease or defect is any mental abnormality (including mental retardation) so long as it is not:

  • Solely a personality disorder. ORS 161.295(2).
  • Manifested only by antisocial or criminal conduct. ORS 161.295(2). For example, the DSM-IV-TR diagnostic criterion for Intermittent Explosive Disorder is “several discrete episodes of failure to resist aggressive impulses that result in serious assaultive acts or destruction of property.” It is, therefore, not a mental disease or defect for GEI purposes. Sandgathe v. Maass, 314 F.3d 371 (2000).
  • Controlled by substance or alcohol dependency. Tharp v. PSRB,338 Or 413 (2005), Ashcroft v. PSRB,338 Or 448 (2005).
  • A combination of mental condition and intoxication where the mental condition alone would be insufficient. State v. Peverieri, 192 Or App 229 (2004).
  • A sexual conduct disorder, which the courts will consider a personality disorder. Beiswenger v. PSRB, 192 Or App 38 (2004). Pedophilia, however, is a mental disease or defect because it requires more than a manifestation of criminal or antisocial conduct. Rios v. PSRB, 135 Or App 265 (1995); Osborn v. PSRB, 135 Or App 94 (1995).

Neither the term “personality disorder” nor the phrase “mental disease and defect” is defined in the ORS. The Oregon Supreme Court has implied that the term “personality disorder” refers to the Axis II Personality Disorder section of the primary diagnostic manual, the DSM-IV-TR. In Mueller v. PSRB, 325 Or 332 (1997), the court relied on the DSM-III in determining that organic personality syndrome is a mental disease rather than a personality disorder. The court later noted that phrases such as “mental disease and defect” and “personality disorder” “are terms of art that are used in the context of professional disciplines such as psychiatry and psychology.” Tharp v. PSRB, 338 Or 413 (2005). There is no definitional scheme that would create a term of art for a mental disorder in psychiatry or psychology except for the DSM (or its very similar European counterpart, the ICD).

However, the Tharpcourt also reviewed lengthy legislative history, which indicated that the legislature intended the phrase “personality disorder” to “include such diagnoses as sexual conduct disorders, drug dependent and alcohol dependent.” While this is inconsistent with the DSM, the legislative history is abundantly clear. So, in Oregon, a controlled substance dependency is a personality disorder, despite the DSM’s inclusion of alcohol or drug addiction as an Axis I major mental disorder. Tharp v. PSRB, 338 Or 413 (2005). The Oregon Supreme Court has also ruled specifically that an alcohol addiction is a personality disorder. Ashcroft v. PSRB, 338 Or 448 (2005).

A mental disease or defect arising from a combination of a mental condition and voluntary intoxication is not a sufficient mental disease or defect for the purposes of the GEI defense. State v. Peverieri, 192 Or App 229 (2004). In other words, if the mental condition is insufficient to stand on its own as a mental disease or defect, it cannot be made sufficient by the addition of voluntary intoxication or the use of controlled substances.

The Oregon Administrative Rules governing the PSRB define mental disease and mental defect as follows:

(4) “Mental Disease”: Mental disease is defined as any diagnosis of mental disorder which is a significant behavioral or psychological syndrome or pattern that is associated with distress or disability causing symptoms or impairment in at least one important area of an individual’s functioning and is defined in the current Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders(DSM IV) of the American Psychiatric Association.

(5) “Mental Defect”: Mental defect is defined as mental retardation, brain damage or other biological dysfunction that is associated with distress or disability causing symptoms or impairment in at least one important area of an individual’s functioning and is defined in the current Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders(DSM IV) of the American Psychiatric Association.

(6) Proof of Mental Disease or Defect. The Board shall not discharge a person whose mental disease or defect may, with reasonable medical probability, occasionally become active, and when active, render the person a danger to others:

(a) The term “mental disease or defect” does not include an abnormality manifested solely by repeated or criminal or otherwise antisocial conduct;

(b) For offenses committed on or after January 1, 1984, the term “mental disease or defect” does not include any abnormality constituting solely a personality disorder.

OAR 859-010-0005.

C.“[A]t the time of engaging in conduct”

The insanity defense focuses on the defendant’s mental capacities at the time of the incident. Thus, counsel should observe and document carefully the client’s behavior at the first interview. Such observations may be the closest an evaluator or fact finder can get to the state of the defendant’s mind at the time of the incident. Counsel should also hire a expert as quickly as possible so that the clinical evaluation is conducted while the client is still symptomatic.

D.“[T]he person lacks substantial capacity”

“Substantial” capacity does not mean “all” capacity. The addition of the word “substantial” was intended as a rejection of some older cases that required a “complete” or “total” destruction of the normal cognitive capacity. According to the commentary to the ALI Model Penal Code standard (from which the Oregon statute was taken):

To identify the degree of impairment with precision is, of course, impossible both verbally and logically. The recommended formulation is content to rest upon the term ‘substantial’ to support the weight of judgment; if capacity is greatly impaired, that presumably should be sufficient. An expert witness, called upon to assess a defendant’s capacity at a prior time (which, of course, the witness probably did not observe), can hardly be asked for a more definitive statement – even in the case of extreme conditions.

Commentary to ALI, 1962. In Oregon, requiring such a determination was meant to “liberalize the kind of expert evidence which is necessary for the jury to have a more complete understanding of the defendant’s mental life before it makes its decision.” Commentary to the Proposed Oregon Criminal Code of 1971.

E.“[A]ppreciate the criminality of the conduct”

The essence of this prong of the insanity defense is that the defendant did not understand that what he was doing at the time of the incident was legally wrong. For example, the defendant may have been motivated by delusional or nonsensical considerations without a logical sense of consequence. The defendant’s lack of understanding has to have been part of a larger incapacity for understanding, not an isolated ignorance of the law.

It was not an accident that the word “appreciate” was used instead of the more traditional “know.” The idea was to “allow a full examination of the mental condition of a defendant on not only the intellectual awareness of his acts but also the emotional awareness. The word ‘know’ in the psychiatric sense is understood to be not limited to intellectual awareness. Psychiatrists uniformly insist that it is possible for a person to ‘know’ intellectually what he is doing but not to ‘know’ it emotionally, and if either of the two levels of ‘knowledge’ is missing, a person qualifies as insane under the test.” Commentary to the Proposed Oregon Criminal Code of 1971.

The Model Penal Code, on which Oregon’s law was based, gives the option of using either the word “criminality” or the word “wrongfulness.” “Wrongfulness” appears to have a moral component that “criminality” does not. Thus, in Oregon, the question is not whether the defendant understood that his actions were “wrong,” but whether he understood them to be “criminal.” Remember, however, that ignorance of the law is no excuse, thus the question is also not whether the defendant was aware that he was violating the law. An appreciation of criminality under Oregon law seems to arise where a person would normally know that his actions were a violation of the law, but because of a mental illness or cognitive disability the defendant, at the time of the crime, was largely incapable of appreciating that his actions might be illegal.

F.“[C]onform the conduct to the requirements of the law”

Often called “the control test,” this prong of the insanity defense requires proof that, despite understanding his actions to be criminal, the defendant was unable to control himself. For example, persons experiencing strong command hallucinations often have the feeling that the conscious self has faded into the background and, as a result, are powerless to make the body resist the demands of the voices. The control prong has some overlap with the statutory element of actus reus or volitional conduct. After all, if the conscious you isn’t controlling your conduct then how is it different than sleepwalking, the classic non-volitional act?

Capacity to conform conduct was specifically excluded as a path to the insanity defense by Oregon statute prior to the 1971 reformulation. That statute read: “A morbid propensity to commit a prohibited act, existing in the mind of a person who is not shown to have been capable of knowing the wrongfulness of the act, forms no defense to a prosecution for committing the act.” Former ORS 136.410, as quoted in the Oregon Commentary to the 1971 revisions.

IV. Procedure

A. Notice: Filing a notice raises the defense. ORS 161.315 This should be done as soon as counsel anticipates that a GEI defense will be raised. Notice can always be withdrawn later if need be.

Though ORS 161.315 requires that notice be filed at the time of the not guilty plea (unless just cause is established), counsel should file the notice at the earliest time that GEI becomes a realistic possibility and argue just cause at a later date. The DA will rarely argue a lack of sufficient notice unless he or she has truly been caught off guard by an eleventh-hour filing.

The downside to filing notice is that it triggers the state’s right to have its own evaluation. It also may give away the fact that the defense has hired an expert and require certain discovery be provided to the state.

B. Affirmative Defense:

GEI is an affirmative defense that must be proven by a preponderance of the evidence. ORS 161.305.

C. State Evaluation:

Once defense notice has been filed, the state has the right to have the defendant examined. The state must file written notice of its request for evaluation. The defendant may object to the state’s evaluator and, assuming good cause, the state must choose a different one. The court can, in its discretion, commit the defendant for up to thirty days for observation and examination. ORS 161.315.

D.Disposition:

The court must discharge the defendant after GEI unless all of the following findings are made:

  • The criminal charge was either:

(a)a felony, or

(b)a misdemeanor which involved physical injury or a risk of physical injury.

  • The person has a mental disease or defect.
  • The person presents a substantial danger to others requiring commitment.

V. GEI Disposition

A. Dispositional Options

Once a person has been found GEI, the judge has three dispositional options:

  • Discharge the defendant.
  • Commit the defendant to the state hospital under the jurisdiction of the PSRB.
  • Release the defendant to the supervision of a person or agency appointed by the court who, in turn, would be under the direction of the PSRB.

B. Discharge

The court must discharge the person unless all of the following findings are made:

(1) The criminal charge was either:

(a) a felony or

(b) a misdemeanor which involved physical injury or a risk of physical injury. ORS 161.327(1).

(2) The court finds by a preponderance of the evidence that the person is suffering from a mental disease or defect (Sec.1). A person who is currently in a state of remission is considered to be suffering from a mental disease or defect if the mental disease occasionally becomes active and, when active, renders the person a danger to others. ORS 161.327(3).

(3) The court finds by a preponderance of the evidence that the person presents a substantial danger to others requiring commitment or conditional release. ORS 161.327(1). There is little in the way of case law defining dangerousness specifically in the context of a GEI commitment. However, there is a substantial body of law defining dangerousness in the context of civil commitment. In that both situations involve a mental health commitment, there is little reason why the definition of dangerousness should be substantially different between the two arenas. If anything, a GEI commitment should require a higher level of dangerousness since the statutory term is “a substantial danger to others” rather than simply “danger to others”. The word substantial is presumably not superfluous.

The most important concept that should be transported from civil commitments is that evidence is not relevant to the issue of dangerousness unless it is predictive of actual harm in the future. Just because there were threats or even actual harm in the past does not mean that we can predict future harm. For example, the threats could be words upon which the person doesn’t intend to act. Or the person may not have the means or capability to act upon such threats. Recent harm in the past may have been an isolated occurrence. If there’s no basis on which to conclude that it was not an isolated occurrence then one should presume it is.

Unfortunately there is also an OAR defining dangerousness for the purpose of PSRB hearings which is unhelpful:

(7) “Dangerousness”: A person is a substantial danger to others if the person is demonstrating or previously has demonstrated intentional, knowing, reckless or criminally negligent behavior which places others at risk of physical injury.

(8) “Proof of Dangerousness”: In considering the issue of dangerousness, the Board may hear testimony on whether the person’s mental disease or defect may, with reasonable medical probability, occasionally become active, and when active, render the person a danger to others.

OAR 859-010-0005. This definition seems to say that (1) a person is dangerous if they have ever been dangerous, and (2) dangerousness is any non-accidental behavior which poses a risk of physical injury. However, it is important to keep in mind that this definition only applies to PSRB hearings. The reason it is written this way for the PSRB is that it is a threshold discharge question for a person who has already been found by a court to be a substantial danger to others. Imagine a person with schizophrenia who does really well in the structure of the hospital while taking medications. If such a person were considered to no longer be dangerous they would have to be released without supervision because a person who isn’t a danger must be discharged from a PSRB commitment. However, where the person committed a violent crime or has a violent past it is good policy to make sure the person continues to follow the treatment plan once they leave the hospital. Applying the same logic to GEI dispositional hearings before the court, it makes sense to commit a person to the PSRB if the crime was violent, the person has a violent past or there have been credible, threats that were likely to have led to violence but for an intervening factor. But it does not make sense to search for attenuated risky behavior where there is no obvious violence in the past. This logic circles back to the parameters of dangerousness in the commitment scheme. If there is neither actual violence nor credible threats in the person’s past (or if the incidents are remote in time) then there is little basis on which to predict actual violence in the future.

C. Commitment or Conditional Release

If the court makes all three above findings, the court must commit the person to the state hospital unless it makes the additional findings allowing for conditional release:

(1) The person can be adequately controlled with supervision and treatment ORS 161.327(2)(b).

(2) Necessary supervision and treatment are available. ORS 161.327(2)(b).

(3) Any concerns for the protection of society are satisfied. ORS 161.327(5).

If a conditional release is ordered, the court may require any supervisory condition which is in the best interests of justice. The supervising person or agency must report to the PSRB in writing no less than once a month concerning the person’s compliance. ORS 161.327(2)(b).

D. Jurisdiction and Appeal

Once the court notifies the PSRB of either commitment or a conditional release, the court’s jurisdiction is terminated and the board assumes jurisdiction. ORS 161.327(6).

Despite this shift of jurisdiction, the defendant does not lose the right to appeal. The defendant may appeal within ninety days of the dispositional order. ORS 161.327(7).

E. The Maximum Period of PSRB Jurisdiction

1. Single Criminal Episode

The maximum period for any one crime is calculated using the statutory maximum for the crime committed, not the maximum sentence under the sentencing guidelines. State v. Brooks, 187 Or App 388 (2003), reviews the relevant legislative history:

In 1989, the legislature amended the key language of ORS 161.327(1) as follows:

The period of jurisdiction of the board shall be equal to the maximum sentence [the court finds the person could have received had the person been found guilty not subject to exception]provided by statute for the crime for which the person was found guilty exceptfor insanity.

Or Laws 1989, ch 790, § 48. The bracketed language was removed from the statute in 1989 and replaced by the boldfaced language.

“The amendment to ORS 161.327(1) was part of omnibus legislation designed to implement the sentencing guidelines, which changed felony sentencing in Oregon, for the most part, from an indeterminate system to a determinate system. Or Laws 1989, ch 790. The legislative history demonstrates that the change in ORS 161.327(1) was meant to make it clear that the length of PSRB jurisdiction was to be measured by the statutory indeterminate maximum sentence rather than by the guidelines presumptive sentence. (Tape Recording, Senate Judiciary Committee, SB 1073, Apr 3, 1989, Tape 91, Side B [statement of Bob Durston, Criminal Justice Council]). The change to ORS 161.327(1) was not intended to change “the terms of the Psychiatric Security Review Board’s custody because of the guidelines.” Tape Recording, Senate Judiciary Committee, SB 1073, June 6, 1989, Tape 221, Side B (statement of Kathleen Bogan, Criminal Justice Council).

“The legislative history thus discloses that when the legislature changed the wording of ORS 161.327(1) in 1989, it did not intend to change how the maximum PSRB commitment term was to be calculated. Rather, the 1989 amendment was intended to preserve the status quo and ensure that the implementation of the sentencing guidelines did not alter how the commitment term was to be calculated. In light of the legislative history, we conclude that the legislature did not intend to legislatively overrule our holding in Norman concerning consecutive commitments to PSRB’s jurisdiction. ORS 161.327(1) continues to authorize the imposition of consecutive PSRB terms.” State v. Brooks, 187 Or App 388 (2003).

2. Multiple Criminal Episodes

Multiple crimes may be run consecutively.

ORS 161.327 requires the court to fix the length of PSRB’s jurisdiction by reference to the maximum sentence that a defendant could have received if he or she had been criminally sentenced. That, in turn, requires the court to determine if the defendant could have received consecutive sentences under the standards prescribed in ORS 137.123.

Thus, to determine the total maximum sentence that can be imposed for multiple GEI adjudications, the court must first determine if the offenses arose “from the same continuous and uninterrupted course of conduct.” ORS 137.123(2), (4). If not, then the maximum possible sentences would be consecutive to one another, ORS 137.123(2), and consecutive PSRB commitment terms must be imposed. If, however, some or all of the offenses arose from a “continuous and uninterrupted course of conduct,” then the maximum sentences for those crimes can be imposed consecutively to one another only if the court makes findings pursuant to ORS 137.123(5). If the court is able to make the factual findings necessary under ORS 137.123(5)(a) or (b) with respect to some or all of the offenses, then the PSRB commitment terms for those offenses must be made consecutive. If, however, the court finds that the factual predicates for consecutive sentences under ORS 137.123(5)(a) or (b) are not present, then the PSRB commitment terms stemming from those offenses arising out of a continuous and uninterrupted course of conduct must be concurrent. State v. Brooks, 187 Or App 388 (2003).

Practice Tips

Defense counsel should argue for the lowest possible maximum sentence and request an order clearly stating the trial court’s findings. If there is no specific finding, the PSRB will assume that the counts are to run consecutively. The stakes are high because the defendant may stay in the hospital for the maximum term or the defendant may stay under the onerous supervision of the PSRB for the maximum term.

In plea negotiations, counsel should seek dismissal of counts or reduction of the primary count. Prosecutors often deliberately overcharge in order to make plea-bargaining easier. Counsel may argue to the DA that it is likely the defendant will be in the state hospital for a longer period of time than she would be in jail or prison if she took a plea.

3. Not Facing Substantial Prison

If the defendant is not facing a significant prison sentence, defense counsel should consider carefully whether to negotiate a GEI plea or employ a GEI defense at trial.

Counsel should discuss with the client whether he or she would prefer a longer time in the state hospital to jail or prison. Counsel should estimate the maximum amount of time with consecutive sentences and explain what supervision under the PSRB is like. The PSRB has sole responsibility for deciding whether and when to release and rehospitalize its patients. Its primary purpose is the protection of the community. For this reason they are often quite cautious in their decision-making, erring on the side of ensuring the safety of the community even if it means deferring a stabilized patient’s release. They hold public hearings, but also are given broad legal standards which place high legal hurdles in the path of a person who wants to be released from the hospital. Also, a person on conditional release may be revoked and placed back in the hospital for seemingly minor violations, a change in mental health or even disappearing community resources (due to no fault of the defendant). For example, one common problem is that employees of the post-acute group homes are less well trained than their counterparts at the state hospital. It is not unusual for patient and staff members to have disagreements for which they are mutually responsible. A patient may be rehospitalized for months or even years as a result of such a verbal disagreement.

Moreover, counsel should not promise that there will be a correlation between the ability to function and the speed with which the patient is released. The state hospital has a Risk Review Board (RRB) which grants privileges to patients as they complete tasks and steps and become more stable and trustworthy. Such privileges may include passes to leave campus or additional freedoms within the state hospital walls. Such RRB determinations are relevant to, but certainly not determinative of PSRB action. It is not unusual for a person to progress all the way through the hospital’s steps but continue to be rejected for conditional release by the PSRB. There are patients at the state hospital right now who have been granted full unsupervised off-campus privileges by the RRB and the treatment team but are still not eligible for conditional release under the PSRB’s criteria. Once conditional release has been authorized, the patient still must wait for a bed to open at a PSRB-approved secure residence. Of course, the main holdup is usually that there is no community program that will take the person. If the crime was a violent one and the person has not been in the hospital for very long, it is doubtful that a community program will accept the person, even if the PSRB believed the person to otherwise be ready for conditional release.

F. GEI Is an Adjudication

1. GEI Is Not a Conviction

  • Generally speaking, a GEI adjudication is not a conviction. State v. Saunders, 195 Or App 357 (2004).
  • A GEI adjudication is not a prior conviction under the Oregon Felony Sentencing Guidelines. OAR 253-09-002.
  • A GEI adjudication does not trigger any of the statutes authorizing fines or unitary assessments. State v. Gile, 161 Or App 146 (1999).

2. May Result in Collateral Consequences As If It Were a Conviction

  • A GEI adjudication may trigger certain conviction-based driver’s license revocation and suspension statutes. State v. Counts, 311 Or 616 (1991), State v. Olmstead, 310 Or 455 (1990), Muller v. Benning, 314 Or 615 (1992).
  • Possession of firearms: It is an A misdemeanor for a person with a GEI adjudication for a felony to possess a firearm. ORS 166.250(c)(C).
  • Concealed handgun license: A person who has been adjudicated GEI for a felony or who has been adjudicated GEI for a misdemeanor within the previous four years is not eligible for a concealed handgun license. ORS 166.291(g)(h)
  • Sex offender registration: AGEI adjudication will trigger sex offender registration because ORS 181.594(5)(b) specifically includes within the definition of “sex offender” a person who “has been found guilty except for insanity of a sex crime.”
  • A guilty verdict in a GEI adjudication is a finding of guilt. Therefore, just as in any other criminal case, the state must prove all the elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt and disprove all raised defenses before a finding of GEI is appropriate. If the defendant proves insanity by a preponderance of the evidence but the state fails to disprove a raised defense or an element of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt, the appropriate verdict is not guilty. State v. Counts, 311 Or 616 (1991).

3. Not Eligible for Expungement or Set Aside

While there is no case on point, the expungement statute, ORS 137.225, limits the authority of a judge to set aside a criminal case to situations that resulted in a conviction, an acquittal, a dismissal or where an accusatory instrument was never filed. See, for example, ORS 137.225(1)(a) and (b).

G. Civil Commitment

If the charge is for a misdemeanor that did not cause or risk physical injury, the court must discharge the defendant. However, the court may always initiate civil commitment proceedings pursuant to ORS 426.070 if it believes the defendant is a danger to self or others. ORS 161.328 This mechanism for civil commitment which is buried within the GEI statutes does not add anything to either the GEI or the civil commitment scheme. It merely stands as a reminder to the judge and the parties that initiating the civil commitment process is always an option.

VI. Trial Issues

A. Evidentiary Issues

1. Mental health history upon which an expert relies is admissible.

The history upon which an expert relies in coming to a conclusion is admissible, including both hearsay and prior bad acts. State v. Goss, 33 Or App 507 (1978).

State v. Massey, 34 Or App 85 (1978). Such evidence is not admissible if the expert is not relying upon it in coming to a conclusion. The admissible history rule applies to both favorable and prejudicial evidence. Id.

2. A broad range of evidence is admissible on the issue of insanity.

Because “insanity is an elusive concept,” any psychological finding on any aspect of the defendant’s mental condition is relevant and admissible in a GEI trial. State v. Matthews, 20 Or App 466 (1975).

3. A defendant’s silence may not be used as evidence of sanity.

The state may not use a defendant’s assertion of the right to remain silent as evidence of sanity. Due process prohibits the introduction of such evidence just as it would in a criminal trial. Wainwright v. Greenfield, 474 US 284 (1976). State v. Wall, 78 Or App 81 (1986). However, other statements made around the time of the assertion may be used. For example, in Wall, the state was able to use a refusal to be photographed before legal consultation to prove sanity. Id.

4. Evidence that civil commitment proceedings did not result in a commitment is not admissible.

The state may not introduce evidence that a Notice of Mental Illness was filed and withdrawn. State v. Peacock, 75 Or App 217 (1985); State v. Hickham, 71 Or App 471 (1984).

5. DA may not suggest GEI is illegitimate or the easy way out.

The prosecutor may not suggest that GEI is a way to avoid incarceration or in any way imply that the insanity defense is illegitimate. State v. Wall, 78 Or App 81 (1986). See also State v. McDonald, 472 A2d 424 (Me. 1984).

6. The Ultimate Issue Rule

Experts in Oregon are allowed to express an opinion about the ultimate issues in a GEI trial.

7. Voir Dire

Assuming that the GEI defense has been raised, the parties have the right to question the jurors in voir dire about their ability to fairly and objectively evaluate evidence of insanity and lack of mental capacity. State v. Wallace, 170 Or 60 (1942); U.S. v. Allsup, 566 F2d 68 (9th Cir. 1977).

B. Jury Instructions

Before a defendant may be found GEI there must be a finding that the defendant is actually guilty. That is, the state must prove all the elements of the crime beyond a reasonable doubt and disprove any raised defenses beyond a reasonable doubt, and the defendant must fail to prove any affirmative defense that he or she raised. The defendant is entitled to a jury instruction setting forth the state’s burden. State v. Counts, 311 Or 616 (1991).

A defendant who has properly raised the GEI defense is entitled to a comprehensive jury instruction which will tell the jury both the elements they must find and that GEI is not an acquittal. State v. Amini, 331 Or 384 (2000).

UCRJI No. 1121. Affirmative Defense – Mental Disease or Defect – GEI

The defendant has raised the defense of guilty except for insanity due to mental disease or defect.

The defendant is guilty except for insanity due to mental disease or defect if:

(1) At the time of engaging in the criminal conduct, the defendant suffered from a mental disease or defect; and

(2) As a result of that mental disease or defect the defendant either:

(a) lacked substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality of conduct; or

(b) lacked substantial capacity to conform conduct to the requirements of law.

The terms mental disease and mental defect do not include an abnormality manifested only by repeated criminal or otherwise antisocial conduct, nor do they include any abnormality constituting solely a personality disorder.

The burden of proof is on the defendant to prove this affirmative defense by a preponderance of the evidence.

UCRJI No. 1122. Affirmative Defense – Insanity – Means of Disposition

If the defendant is found guilty except for insanity, the defendant is subject to the following dispositions:

(1) By the court.

(a) If the court determines that the defendant is presently affected by a mental disease or defect and presents a substantial danger to others requiring commitment to a state mental hospital, the court will order the defendant placed under the jurisdiction of the Psychiatric Security Review Board, and order the defendant committed to a state mental hospital pending further disposition by the Psychiatric Security Review Board.

(b) If the court finds that the defendant is affected by mental disease or defect but either that it is in remission or that the defendant is not presently a substantial danger to others requiring commitment to a state mental hospital, the court will order the defendant placed under the jurisdiction of the Psychiatric Security Review Board and may order that the defendant be conditionally released. A defendant who is conditionally released is subject to such supervisory orders of the court as are in the best interests of justice, the protection of society, and the welfare of the defendant.

(2) By the Psychiatric Security Review Board. The Psychiatric Security Review Board is a state agency that by statute has as its primary concern the protection of society. After the court places the defendant under the jurisdiction of the Psychiatric Security Review Board, the board will have jurisdiction over the defendant for a length of time equal to the maximum period of incarceration to which the defendant could have been sentenced had the defendant been found guilty of the charged crime.

(a) If the board determines that the defendant continues to be affected by a mental disease or defect and presents a substantial danger to others and is not a proper subject for conditional release, the board will order the defendant committed to a state mental hospital for custody, care, and treatment.

(b) The Psychiatric Security Review Board will order that the defendant be discharged from its jurisdiction if at its first hearing or at some later date the board determines that either:

(i) the defendant is no longer affected by mental disease or defect, or

(ii) the defendant is no longer affected by mental disease or defect but no longer presents a substantial danger to others.

(c) If the board, either at its first hearing or at some later date, determines that the defendant is still affected by a mental disease or defect and is a substantial danger to others, but can be controlled adequately if conditionally released with treatment as a condition of release, the board will order the defendant to be conditionally released. A defendant who is conditionally released is subject to such supervisory orders of the board as are in the best interest of justice, the protection of society, and the welfare of the person.

A person is considered to have a mental disease or defect requiring supervision even when that disease or defect is in a state of remission when the disease may, with reasonable medical probability, occasionally become active and render the person a danger to others.

Using the second, explanatory, jury instruction does not violate a defendant’s Constitutional rights. State v. Amini, 331 Or 384 (2000).

C. Burden of Proof

“Mental disease or defect constituting insanity under ORS 161.295 is an affirmative defense.” ORS 161.305.

Placing the burden of persuasion on the defendant is not a violation of due process. State v. Dodson, 25 Or App 859 (1976).

D. Framing Issues for the Jury

The language used in a trial can make or break the case, especially where mental illness is concerned.

1. Naming the parties.

The Accused: It is always best to refer to the accused by his or her first name. If the court balks and insists on Mr. or Ms., that is second best. However, there may be moments where, if the client is in treatment, defense counsel may want to refer to the accused as a “patient” instead of a “defendant” or a “client.”

The “Victim”: Counsel should not call the named criminal complainant “the victim.” Anything else is preferable. If there is a dispute over whether the crime has been committed, the defense attorney should move to prevent the district attorney from using the word “victim.” “Accuser,” “complainant,” “alleged victim,” and “primary witness” are all better because they do not assume what the prosecutor is trying to prove. If the defense is admitting that the individual in question is actually a victim (because, for example, the defense argument is that the crime occurred but this defendant is not responsible because of his mental illness), counsel should still refer to her as something like “the state’s witness, Ms. Jones.” Elevating a witness whose credibility and perception are yet to be judged to the status of victim grants a level of trustworthiness that should not be voluntarily given by the defense. The defense attorney is similarly encouraged to impress upon his/her expert witnesses to refrain from use of the word “victim” in written reports or during testimony.

2. Mental illness as a character in your client’s story of innocence.

The defendant is a hollow vessel through which the outside force of the mental illness acted. The mental illness must appear to be independently responsible for the bad act. You may occasionally want to refer to the mental illness directly, anthropomorphizing and externalizing it. For example, “It grabbed his mind and wouldn’t let go, squeezing harder and harder on his brain until there was nothing there except what It demanded.” Or, “It created a fog and draped it over the defendant’s mind so that she couldn’t think straight.” The key to successful externalizing is the use of active verbs like “grabbed,” “squeezing” and “created,” which imply that the defendant was unable to resist the force of the mental illness.

In cases where the crime is more serious, counsel may even want to consider implying that the mental illness had some sort of criminal intent toward the defendant by use of phrases such as “hijacking” or “kidnapping” the defendant’s mind, or “deviously manipulating” the defendant’s perception.

3. Neutralize the notion that the mental illness is an excuse.

Counsel must also work against the public perception that intelligent people will manipulate the insanity defense in order to get away with crimes. Do this by:

  • Addressing it in voir dire; assume the perception is correct for the purposes of the trial. It will be much easier to convince the jury that your client is very different from those who have faked mental illness than it will be to erase this public perception.
  • Call a family member or long-term friend of the defendant’s to testify regarding the history of defendant’s illness.
  • Ask any expert witnesses to comment on how commonly the insanity defense is used. Assuming there is no indication of malingering in your client’s testing, ask the expert about the lack of signs of malingering.

4. Humanize the defendant and bring his or her story to life.

Create sympathy and understanding for the defendant’s plight. To win an insanity defense, the jury must feel just as much sympathy for the defendant as for the complainant. Emphasize and dwell on the sympathetic details of the defendant’s life. If there is a mother or kindly neighbor to be called, she should almost surely be asked to testify because there is no greater way to create sympathy than to allow the jury to see the client’s story through the lens of a mother’s hopes and dreams dashed by mental illness.

GEI and disordered mental state cases can become a “battle of the experts.” But the testimony of “mom” can change the whole dynamic. Imagine that both experts have testified and disagreed on the clinical aspects of the case. Then imagine that the defendant’s mother takes the stand and brings into focus the bits and pieces of the defendant’s life that make it real. Instead of test scores on the MMPI, she will talk about the time the defendant sat catatonic in a chair for two days straight. She will bring the jury into the defendant’s home and allow the jury to see the side of the defendant’s illness to which only his loved ones are normally privy. If you’re lucky, on cross-examination, the prosecutor will ask the mother some variation of: “You weren’t there at the time of the crime, right? So you can’t know whether he had the capacity to appreciate that he was committing a crime?” The defendant’s mother can respond to the effect of “The heck I don’t, I’m his mom. I know what he’s like when he’s not taking his medications and he doesn’t understand what he’s doing at all.” Once the prosecutor asks such a question, he or she has opened the door for the defendant’s mother to give opinion evidence, and there is no more instinctively trustworthy expert to a jury than a mom. If defendant’s mother is unavailable or unwilling to testify, another family member can also be effective.

E. Anticipating and Refuting the State’s Arguments

1.Argument:The defendant acts “normal,” therefore he or she is not mentally ill enough.

The prosecutor will often imply (or even state outright) that a defendant is not really mentally ill or, alternatively, not mentally ill enough, because the defendant’s behavior was not entirely bizarre. If the defendant made himself a sandwich, went to a movie or walked to the grocery store on the day of the crime, the prosecutor will use this “normal” behavior to make it seem that the defendant cannot be mentally ill in the way the law requires. In opening argument, the prosecutor will likely fail to mention the mental illness at all, instead slowly reciting and dwelling on the conscious, normal decisions the defendant made in the hours and days leading up to the crime.

Response: The defense, of course, will want to counter with a recitation of all the abnormal and objectively “crazy” behaviors the defendant exhibited in the same time period (“I’ll raise your ‘went to a movie’ with two episodes of ‘yelling at a shrub’). But this tactic only works to a point. The underlying prosecutorial assumption must also be attacked.

A person’s behavior need not be objectively bizarre all the time for the person to be profoundly impaired. A person suffering from an acute episode of psychosis may be able to perform a wide variety of habitual tasks yet be completely stymied by a simple but new decision. Habitual tasks and movements utilize a different part of the brain from novel events. Moreover, a particular form of mental illness on a particular day may shut down one type of thinking process and not another. It is entirely possible for a person to lack the capacity to process the criminality of an action, but to be quite capable of making a sandwich or going to the grocery store. Making a sandwich is a concrete task. Thinking about the potential criminality of an action is quite abstract. For example, a defendant given a forged check to cash would need to think through the possibility of the check being forged, the implications of that possibility, where the money comes from, and why it might be illegal.

Second, the defense should question the comparative “normality” of the same action for different people. The prosecutor’s argument of “normal” behavior is powerful because we rely on mental pictures from our own experience to make sense of words and phrases. If I hear that a defendant “went to the store,” I picture my own experience of looking at price and quality as I pick out products at my usual grocery store, and it is difficult to imagine a profoundly mentally ill person doing the same thing. It is quite possible, however, that when the defendant said he “went to the store,” he meant that he picked up a pack of cigarettes at the local convenience store, a pattern that has become completely automated. Or, perhaps, he did, indeed, go to the store but was mostly just staring at the muffins. Without additional information, the fact that defendant “went to the store” turns out to be utterly irrelevant.

Third, if the defendant has a psychotic disorder like schizophrenia, counsel should warn the jury repeatedly—preferably with the assistance of an expert—against relying on the specific accuracy of words or phrases used by the defendant during a psychotic episode. Just because the defendant told the police that he made a sandwich does not mean that he or she put meat, cheese and condiments between two slices of bread. Perhaps what the defendant calls a sandwich is a folded-over piece of bologna or a pencil between two slices of bread, or maybe the defendant meant to say “bologna” instead of “sandwich.” The process of communication is complicated and can become blocked at a number of different junctures in the brain. Most people have experienced being unable to sing or hum a tune which seems so clear in their mind. People with psychosis frequently have this experience when trying to communicate everyday words and thoughts.

Defense counsel must also make the jury (or the judge) understand the chaos and disorganization of the defendant’s mind. The jury must see and feel how difficult it is for this defendant to undertake a seemingly normal activity or interaction. The disorganization, the paranoia, and the hallucinations must come alive for the jury so that each juror understands how different the defendant’s experience is from his or her own.

Fourth, if the defendant suffers from traumatic brain injury (TBI) or substantial cognitive deficits, there may be few bizarre or obvious symptoms despite a profound impairment of executive functioning and impulse control. For such folks, strong emotions may be like a roaring river with the conscious mind like a man without a boat or paddle, swept along with the current. Without the equipment supplied by the frontal lobe, the capacity to consciously control behavior may be substantially lost to unconscious currents. Ultimately though the general defense strategy is no different in these cases than where the defendant has psychosis. Ideally we want an expert who can explain the psychological symptoms and a series of family members who can talk about how it has played out for the defendant on a day to day basis and from a historical perspective.

2.Argument:The absence of other behavioral problems in the defendant’s life proves that he had the capacity to conform his behavior at the time of the crime.

The prosecutor may argue that a lack of evidence that the defendant got into trouble in school, jail, or a hospital indicates that the defendant has the capacity to control his or her own behavior and conform his or her conduct to the requirements of the law. Alternatively, the prosecutor may argue that because the psychologist did not take any special precautions when visiting with the defendant, the psychologist must believe that the defendant can control his behavior.

Response:The best response to this argument is to use it against the DA to establish that when the defendant’s mental illness is stabilized he is more than able to control his behavior and, therefore, the jury should not worry about the possibility that he will not be incarcerated in jail or prison. It is only when the mental health system fails that decompensation of the defendant follows, which can lead to psychosis and a correlative inability to control behavior under some circumstances.

The defense should identify a precipitating factor that caused the defendant to be at a low point at the time of a crime. Typically this will have something to do with his or her medication regimen (e.g., not taking enough, taking too much, taking the wrong kind, taking it at the wrong time, or is in the process of changing it).

Turn the prosecutor’s argument around and use it as a chance to allay the jury’s fears that if they acquit or find GEI, the defendant will just commit the same crime again.

3.Argument:If the defendant can talk about the crime in some detail, it means he was aware enough to know exactly what he was doing and in control enough to have been able to adjust his behavior.

One very effective prosecution tactic is to carefully go through the details of the crime, noting all the little things to which the defendant was paying attention at the time of the crime. Detailed awareness can be a powerful argument to show organized and rational behavior.

Response:The problem with the argument is twofold. First, there is no necessary link between awareness and rational motivation. Quite often, for example, persons with strong command hallucinations are aware of what they are doing but feel compelled to obey the voices. The individual perceives but cannot control.

Second, memory does not work like a camera. The human mind unconsciously fills in missing pieces and rewrites parts of history in order to avoid inconsistency in memory. The memories of those with psychosis have been little studied, and the memories of stabilized individuals looking back on a psychotic episode have barely been studied at all. Many people with schizophrenia are quite adept at filling in large, foggy patches of memory with enough specifics to create a consistent whole. So, just because a person looking back on a psychotic episode says that she walked up the stairs, turned a corner and picked up a knife does not mean that it actually happened that way. And, even if it did, it doesn’t mean the person was organized and rationally motivated.

4.Argument:The defendant is making up or exaggerating his symptoms in order to obtain a defense to which he is not otherwise entitled.

Response:Educate the jury on malingering and prepare your expert to testify on the subject.

When a person exaggerates or feigns mental illness for some gain, it is called malingering. Malingering is easily uncovered by a trained psychologist. Malingerers almost invariably exaggerate the severity of their symptoms or add a symptom that does not fit with the profile of the mental illness they are trying to imitate. There are specific assessments that can be used to flesh out the possibility of malingering. It is very difficult to fool these assessments because it is not at all intuitively obvious which symptoms align and which do not and it is extremely difficult to anticipate which questions a person with the faked symptoms would answer correctly.

F. Anticipating Prosecution Strategies

A 1980 book written by three people who worked for the Lane County District Attorney’s office set out four basic strategies for beating back the insanity defense. The four strategies are named, with no apparent irony, The Bullet, The Double Cross, One-on-One, and The Ambush. (David Nissman, Beating the Insanity Defense, Heath and Company (1980)). More descriptively (and with less brio) the four strategies might be described as:

  • No prosecution expert.
  • Prosecution calls the defense expert.
  • Battle of the experts.
  • Experts have no place in criminal cases.

1. No Prosecution Expert –The Bullet

There are generally two keys to the DA strategy when they are not calling an expert. First, they will hammer the inconsistencies between the tests the defense expert performed and between the defense expert’s opinion and her tests. Then, they will equate the expert’s opinion with the tests and discredit the tests and with them, the expert herself. Second, they might call lay witnesses to testify to all the obviously sane actions performed by the defendant around the time of the incident (e.g., he waited for the signal before crossing the street therefore he knew what he was doing and was consciously in control). The underlying theme of the prosecution strategy is that what a person can see with their own eyes is far more trustworthy than a bunch of psychological mumbo-jumbo.

The key to defeating such a strategy is to establish the essential absurdity of the notion that outward behavior tells you everything you need to know about the human mind. This starts in voir dire by talking about how difficult it is to know for sure what’s in a person’s mind and what it means to be have a substantial incapacity to understand wrongness or control behavior. What will quickly become obvious in any such discussion is that it is complicated. The defense expert should be trained not to soft sell the difficulty and complexity of figuring out what was in a person’s mind. The defense expert should try to completely excise jargon and labels from any discussion and use primarily descriptive terms to get the jury to understand, as best as possible, what it is like to be the defendant. And some portion of both opening and closing should be devoted to what it feels like to have the symptoms that the defendant had on the day of the incident. The idea is both to engender sympathy for the defendant and a sense of awe for the complexity of the human mind.

The psychologist/psychiatrist and the attorney should have a frank discussion about the attorney’s impressions of his or her client and aspects of the case which suggest GEI.

The attorney should ensure that the expert has access to all relevant medical and psychological history of the client, before the evaluation if possible. The examiner should receive the following documents: past medical and mental health reports, police reports with eyewitness statements of the instant offense, names and telephone numbers of witnesses to the alleged incident, names of individuals associated with the examinee near the time of the alleged incident, and jail and booking records. One of the worst possible things that can happen is for the district attorney to provide new information to the expert on cross-examination. Avoid such a possibility assiduously.

2. Prosecution Calls the Defense Expert – The Double Cross

There are times when the prosecutor will figure out that you hired an expert that will not be called as a witness. The prosecutor will then assume that the expert did not have favorable things to say and call the defense expert as a prosecution witness.

Fortunately, this is a strategy that should easily be defeated. First, the fact of hiring should remain confidential until the defense no longer wants it to be confidential. Any application for indigent defense funds through Oregon Public Defense Services should be sealed. It should not be filed with the court. Nor should notice be given to the DA.

Second, there is no discovery statute that requires defense counsel to turn over information regarding an expert who will not, in any way, be used at trial. Counsel should always talk to the expert prior to a report being written. If it does not sound like a report would be helpful then don’t have the expert write a report.

Third, even if defense counsel accidentally sends to the DA a copy of a horrifyingly bad report that never should have been written, it is still doubtful that the State could call a defense expert in the GEI context. The relevant standard is provided by State v. Riddle, 330 Or 471 (2000): while there is no absolute privilege arising out of the attorney-client privilege, the work product doctrine or Oregon caselaw, the State may only call a defense expert if the testimony is not bound up with confidential information. For example, in Riddle, the court allowed an accident reconstruction expert hired by the defense to testify for the prosecution because the expert’s opinions and observations “were not derived from any communication between” the expert and the defendant. The expert’s opinions and observations were, instead, derived from an examination and analysis of the scene of the accident and the police reports which were made at that time. Conversely, it’s hard to imagine a situation involving the insanity defense where one could unwind the observations and opinions of a mental health expert from confidential defense communications such as the mental health examination, medical records, mental health records, observations of the attorney and her staff, statements made by the attorney to the expert, etc. It’s a very different situation than that of an accident reconstruction expert.

3. Battle of the Experts – One on One

This strategy is relatively straightforward. Their expert will often have the imprimatur of the state hospital (for good and bad). But mostly it will all come down to who the jury trusts more.

The insanity defense focuses on the defendant’s capacity for control and awareness at a time in the past. Thus, the psychologist becomes a detective. She must piece together the extent to which the allegations are true with the defendant’s emotional functioning right before the crime and past diagnostic considerations in order to form a complete picture of the defendant’s mental state.

Inevitably, this process involves guesswork and logic. Yet in the end, the expert will be asked to make a confident statement as to the nature of the defendant’s mind at the time of the alleged crime. There is no easy answer for the expert except to adhere to one’s training and be candid in conversations and written reports about the areas in which she does not have a high degree of confidence. If assumptions must be made or gaps in knowledge must be glossed over, the expert should flesh them out and alert the attorney to their presence as soon as possible. The worst possible scenario is for the attorney to be surprised by such assumptions and gaps at trial.

The great majority of the time, the experts will agree on most of the clinical issues but argue over the psycho-legal questions, like whether the defendant had the capacity to control his behavior at the time of the crime. This difference is often the natural result of having heard the story of the criminal case first from one side or the other. In any event, it is extremely important that the expert not take any differences personally. Any sense of personal affront on the witness stand will make that expert seem less authoritative and more biased.

In the end, the battle of the experts at trial is usually won by the expert who can best boil down complex concepts into understandable metaphors, examples, or bite-size chunks for the jury. Jurors are already overwhelmed with information by the time the defense expert testifies. Even extremely intelligent jurors will find it impossible to absorb an entire field’s concepts and language in the time it takes the expert to testify. If the expert can give an engaging metaphor or example, the jurors will be relieved and grateful that they can follow along without having to work to hold together so many tenuously understood concepts.

4. Psychologists Have No Place in Criminal Cases – The Ambush

This strategy is basically a more extreme version of the first strategy in which the psychologist or other expert will be made to seem silly. The prosecutor will emphasize the infant state of the science of psychology and the difficulty of scientifically deducing a person’s capacity or intent rather than just using common sense. The expert should be counseled to be honest but with the understanding that a courtroom has a different standard than a science journal where evolution and gravity are still theories. While it is true that the science of psychology is still new and has much to learn, it is also true that there has been an enormous amount of research in the past couple decades and there is a growing consensus around a core group of concepts. In the same way, it is true that the DSM was built with haggling and compromise but it is also true that most of the haggling was and still would be done around the edges not at the core disorders.

The most effective way to beat back the accusation that the state of psychology is too precarious for a firm psychological opinion is to follow the advice of the U.S. Supreme Court in Clark. Experts should be urged not to hide behind the jargon of diagnosis. Whether a person has schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, psychosis NOS or even bipolar disorder is often exceedingly difficult to figure out and, ultimately, isn’t very important compared to the symptoms. Diagnoses are simultaneously difficult to understand and vulnerable to attack while symptoms are easy to understand and relatively invulnerable to attack. Anyone can, with a little experience or training, tell when someone is responding to voices that aren’t present. But we need to trust an expert and her science to tell us if someone has schizophrenia. Most important of all, symptoms are details that form the basis for a story that can be told to a jury. A story of a client who wakes up every morning and lives every minute of the day with two voices yelling that he’s a bad person and he failed, is much more powerful than a diagnosis of schizophrenia with auditory hallucinations. If it can’t be felt by the judge or jury then it ought to be decentralized or dropped altogether.

VII. The Psychiatric Security Review Board

A. Composition

The board is made up of a psychologist, a psychiatrist, an attorney with a criminal practice, a parole or probation officer and a member of the public. Quorum for a hearing is three out of the five members. Thus, most board hearings have only three board members present. OAR 859-010-0005(13).

The clients are overwhelmingly male whites with a psychotic disorder who are under the PSRB for commission of a felony. Mary Claire Buckley, executive director of the PSRB, gives the following information:

  • 83 percent of PSRB clients are male, 17% are female.
  • 83.8 percent of PSRB clients are white, 7.8 percent are African American, 4.1 percent are Hispanic, 2.4 percent are Native American and 1.6 percent are Asian.
  • The primary diagnoses are schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. Many of the clients also have a secondary diagnosis of drug or alcohol addiction issues.
  • 98.7 percent of PSRB clients, at any given time, are being supervised for a felony adjudication. About half of the PSRB clients are being supervised for an A felony.
  • About half of the PSRB clients are on conditional release. For example, in 2008, there were a total of 744 clients under the supervision of the PSRB. 367 clients were on conditional release.

B. Hearings

1. Determinations

a.Does the person continue to suffer from a mental disease or defect? If not, the board must discharge the person.

b.Does the person continue to be a substantial danger to others when the mental illness is active? If not, the board must discharge the person.

c.Could the client be adequately controlled with supervision and treatment in the community? If so, proceed to d.

d.Is the necessary supervision and treatment available at this time? If so, then a conditional release should be granted.

2. Mandatory Hearings

a.Initial: A person committed to the PSRB must have a hearing within 90 days of commitment.

b.Revocation: A person who is revoked from a conditional release is entitled to a hearing within 20 days of re-admission to the hospital.

c.Two-year: A person is entitled to an automatic hearing if the person has been in the hospital for two years without a hearing.

d.Five-year: A person is entitled to an automatic hearing if the person has been on conditional release for five years without a hearing.

3. Hearings upon Application

a.Hospital Request: The state hospital may request a hearing at any time and the hearing will be set as soon as possible.

b.Patient Request: A person who is either in the hospital or on a conditional release may request a hearing no more than once every six months. The hearing must be held within 60 days.

c.Outpatient Supervisor Request: The person in charge of supervising a person’s conditional release may request a hearing at any time. The hearing will be set as soon as possible.

d.Status Review Hearings: The board may always set a hearing sua sponte.

4. Procedure

a.Due Process: The person under the supervision of the PSRB is entitled to be represented by an attorney of one’s choosing or by an appointed attorney. At a hearing the person, through their attorney, has the right to examine all evidence, subpoena witnesses and documents, cross-examine all witnesses the state calls and call witnesses to provide evidence for the petitioner. The person also has the right to appeal a decision of the board.

b.Procedurally, the PSRB hearing is not unlike a PV hearing: relatively informal, the rules of evidence don’t apply, the trier of fact may ask a lot of questions, and the person has all the significant due process rights but, generally speaking, has a pretty difficult row to hoe.

c.Burden of Proof: The burden of proof is on the state unless the committed person or someone representing the committed person is moving for a conditional release or discharge. The state is almost always represented by the Attorney General. However, occasionally, the district attorney that prosecuted the case will go to Salem to conduct the hearing. More often, the district attorney will go to the hearing to present testimony or evidence.

5. Possible Board Decisions Resulting from a PSRB Hearing

a.Continue the commitment with no action.

b.Order a conditional release evaluation as a first step in the process.

c.Find the person appropriate for conditional release but for a plan.

d.Order a conditional release.

e.Discharge a person who does not meet legal criteria for jurisdiction.

6. Evidence Considered When Determining Conditional Release

a.Any reports and evaluations, especially those prepared by OSH staff.

b.Testimony of OSH professional staff and others.

c.Any evidence of the risk to the community posed by the supervised person.

d.Recommendation of the OSH Risk Review Board

e.Evidence regarding the availability of resources in the community

C. PSRB Monitoring (Supervision on Conditional Release)

1. The following conditional release placements are possible:

a.Secure Residential Treatment Facility (SRTF)

b.Residential Treatment Facility (RTF)

c.Residential Treatment Home (RTH)

d.Adult Foster Home (AFH)

e.Intensive Case Management (ICM)

f.Independent Living

2. The Monitoring Itself

a.The client is only released pursuant to a specific plan with specific rules.

b.A person in the community, usually a case manager, makes sure the specific treatment, housing and resources are provided and supervises the client for the PSRB.

c.The board monitors progress through calls and correspondence from the case manager and treatment providers to PSRB staff, monthly progress reports submitted to the PSRB and LEDS checks.

See Appendix A for an example of a progress report.

D. Revocation

1. Revocation: The legal process by which the board orders the return of an individual on conditional release to the state hospital. Note that the revocation almost invariably occurs prior to the hearing for the revocation.

2. Grounds for Revocation

a.Violation of the terms of the conditional release plan (including something as big as absconding from supervision or as small as a curfew violation).

b.A change in mental health status.

c.The treatment or housing resources have become unavailable.

3. Possible Decisions from a Revocation Hearing

The board may make any number of different decisions. The conditional release could be revoked and the whole process could start over. The conditional release could be continued and the person returned to the community. The conditional release could be continued with new or different terms. Or any other result that makes sense to the board. Fault need not be found with the person. A change in mental health status or disappearing resources may very well have nothing to do with the supervised person’s compliance with the plan but revocation may result nonetheless. Because of this, a relatively high percentage of people are revoked from conditional release in a given year. For example, in 2008, a low year for revocations, approximately one in every nine clients on conditional release was revoked (41 revocations out of 367 clients on conditional release). In a high year the rate of revocations can rise to almost one in every four clients (72 revocations out of 294 clients on conditional release). It is this broad power and quick trigger on revocation that has led to the astoundingly low recidivism rates of less than one percent (when counted properly).
