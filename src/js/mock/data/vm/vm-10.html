Chapter 10. The “Good Soldier” Defense

By Amy Lammers*

2023 Update

In Pike v. Cain, 303 Or App 624, 465 P3d 277, rev den, 367 Or 75 (2020), the Court of Appeals, without explicitly citing it, relied on the "good soldier defense" (as well as Mitigating Factor J) to hold that trial counsel failed to provide constitutionally adequate and effective legal assistance.

There, the petitioner, Adrian Leslie Pike (now deceased), was an active duty soldier stationed at Joint Base Lewis/McChord in Washington State, but he was off duty and spending time in Oregon. He got into an altercation, which ended with him pleading guilty to second-degree assault. He faced a 70-month "Measure 11" prison sentence. But he was eligible for application of the "exit door," see ORS 137.712, which would allow him to be sentenced to a lesser, Guidelines term, including to probation. At the sentencing hearing, trial counsel called petitioner's platoon sergeant who "testified in support of petitioner’s case for probation. The sergeant stated that petitioner was a 'good soldier,' who 'excelled above his peers,' and who, prior to the assault, was up for a promotion to sergeant." 303 Or App at 629 (emphasis added).

Unfortunately, trial counsel otherwise failed to investigate and present evidence of petitioner's "good soldier" service in the military. Relatedly, counsel failed to rely on Mitigating Factor J to seek a mitigated departure from Measure 11 sentencing. The sentencing court denied the defense request for "exit door" treatment, and imposed the 70-month sentence.

Owing to those failures, the petitioner sought post-conviction relief, which the post-conviction court denied. But the Court of Appeals reversed, explaining that it

"conclude[d] that * * * petitioner is entitled to a new sentencing hearing because his trial counsel’s decision not to further develop the evidence of petitioner’s military service, and his failure to argue for a [mitigated] departure under * * * OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(J) based on petitioner’s servicemember status, did not comport with constitutional standards."

Pike, 303 Or App at 627.

The court then "remand[ed] with instructions to grant post-conviction relief on petitioner’s claim that trial counsel was inadequate and ineffective for failing to investigate and develop further evidence pertaining to petitioner’s military service." Id.

I. Background

The Military Rules of Evidence (MRE) 404 on character evidence for crimes or other acts states,

(a) Character Evidence.

(1) Prohibited Uses. Evidence of a person’s character or character trait is not admissible to prove that on a particular occasion the person acted in accordance with the character or trait.

(2) The accused may offer evidence of the accused’s pertinent trial and, if the evidence is admitted, the prosecution may offer evidence to rebut it.

(Emphasis added.) “Good military character” is that character which makes one a good soldier, seaman, airman, or marine. It can include any number of qualities, including, but not limited to: integrity, sense of duty, conformity to standards of military behavior, skill and efficiency in performing assigned duties, acceptance of authority, leadership, military appearance.1

Until 2015, “good military character [was] a character trait within the meaning of Mil R Evid 404 (a)(1).”2 The defense had broad applicability, allowing defendants to introduce evidence of general good military character in defense of virtually any charged offense. Rather than limiting the defendant’s argument to specific, pertinent character traits—for peacefulness in an assault case, or truthfulness in a case involving dishonesty—this enabled defendants to argue that because they had good military character, and sometimes for this reason alone, the factfinder should reasonably doubt that they did or could have committed the offense alleged.

With Section 536 of the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2015, Congress rewrote and severely restricted the applicability of MRE 404. Congress did so by prohibiting use of the good military character defense (also commonly known as the “good soldier” defense) in cases alleging rape, sexual assault, stalking, larceny and wrongful appropriation, robbery, forgery, making checks with insufficient funds, sodomy, arson, extortion, burglary, housebreaking, perjury, frauds against the United States and “[a]ny other offense in which evidence of general military character of the accused is not relevant to any element of an offense for which the accused has been charged.”

The change in the defense is one of many that began with the politicization of the military’s response to sexual assault in 2012, following a commanding officer’s essentially overturning a jury’s conviction for rape of a high-ranking officer in United States v. Wilkerson, and geared in large part toward amelioration of a perceived sexual assault crisis in the military.

Despite the 2015 rewriting of MRE 404(a), the defense may still be used where it is truly a “pertinent” character trait. This puts the defense more on a footing with most civilian character-evidence laws, while still allowing its use in purely military type crimes—missing movements, contempt toward officials, disrespect toward a superior commissioned officer, assaulting or willfully disobeying a superior commissioned officer, insubordinate conduct toward officers, failure to obey an order or a regulation, cruelty and maltreatment, non-compliance with procedural rules, or being drunk on duty.

II. How is the “good soldier” defense used?

The good soldier defense is simple and was often effective. As with any character-witness foundation, the character witness is first asked about their relationship to and knowledge of the defendant. They then are asked if they have an opinion of the defendant’s military character. Immediately before this question, the witness often is asked to define what, in their opinion, constitutes “good military character.” The military judge ultimately determines which specific characteristics are included in the definition of “good military character,” though in practice the defense is often able to introduce a myriad of good character traits through this step. The witness then is asked their opinion of the defendant’s military character.

The effectiveness of the defense varies depending on the rank of character witness. For example, the endorsement of a very high-ranking individual is usually more valuable, although an opinion from a variety of ranks is also telling. The number of witnesses called, the type of case, and the relationship between the accused and character witnesses also play a role.

Part of what makes good military character testimony so effective is military culture. Military members not only work together, they also often live in close quarters, meaning the same witness could testify that they observe the defendant to exhibit the relevant character trait both at their everyday job and in social circles; under enemy fire and at the community pool. Moreover, the defendant, lawyers, witnesses, and juries generally share a common set of values and lexicon to express them, which means the defense is easily communicated to its audience.

III. Application: What can Oregon defense attorneys learn from the “good soldier” defense?

While the good soldier defense cannot be used in civilian courts per se, many of the strengths of the defense can still be applied in civilian courts when introducing evidence under OEC 404(2)(a) (allows evidence of a pertinent trait of a person’s character offered by an accused to prove that the person acted in conformity therewith) or in mitigation. A military member’s current or recent supervisor and colleagues often make excellent character witnesses, even in civilian courts. They often know the defendant on a more intimate level than a typical co-worker or supervisor, thus rendering them qualified to issue an opinion on the defendant’s character and reputation for qualities such as law-abidingness, truthfulness, and peacefulness. They often present well as witnesses: they have training in “military bearing,” which translates into proper decorum; they are usually difficult to impeach; and many military positions involve presenting information to audiences and authority figures.

Likewise, the principles behind the good soldier defense can be used effectively in presenting mitigating evidence. ORS 137.090(2) states: “In determining mitigation, the court may consider evidence regarding the defendant’s status as a servicemember as defined in ORS 135.881.” (Emphasis added.)3 Any military member or veteran client should have records containing a plethora of favorable information concerning their contributions and accomplishments in the military. Ask your client for performance reports and citations of awards and medals. Also consider asking colleagues and former supervisors to testify or provide affidavits to the court. Again, for many of the same reasons they make effective “good soldier” defense witnesses, these individuals are often in an excellent position to testify to the client’s character and ability to learn from their mistakes and bad decisions.

Example: A senior non-commissioned officer (NCO), who is a member of a National Guard unit scheduled for imminent deployment to a combat zone, is facing criminal prosecution. A sentence of incarceration, but not probation, would bar his deployment. The NCO’s superiors attest that his deployment is critical to his unit’s cohesion and performance, and that preventing the NCO’s deployment could cause the unit to fail in its mission—including by suffering additional casualties owing to the substitute deployment of someone who lacks the training and experience, and the unit loyalty, as the NCO. Defense counsel should propose these military community matters, including the good soldier defense, as grounds for imposing a sentence of probation, instead of incarceration. Cf. United States v. Milikowsky, 65 F3d 4, 8 (2d Cir 1995) (“[a]mong the permissible justifications for downward departure … is the need, given appropriate circumstances, to reduce the destructive effects that incarceration of a defendant may have on innocent third parties”); United States v. Kloda, 133 F Supp2d 345 (SDNY 2001) (in business tax fraud case, mitigated departure granted in part because of “the needs of [defendant’s] business and employees”).

IV. Conclusion

The “good soldier” defense created by the Military Rules of Evidence allows a servicemember’s positive character traits be considered in defense of charges. For the same reasons that make this defense so valuable in military courts, it can be used to a servicemember’s advantage in Oregon courts—both in presentation of character evidence and as a mitigation tactic.
