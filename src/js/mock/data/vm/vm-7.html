Chapter 7. Impact of Warzone Deployment

By Suzanne Best, PhD*

[2023 update fixes the link to Porter v. McCollum under "Suicide by Cop."]

“He was a good, honest officer. In fact, I would be very shocked if there were any truth to it…it would be unfathomable.” This was the response from an Army Captain after being told by an investigator that his former platoon commander had embezzled hundreds of thousands of dollars. During a psychological evaluation, the accused West Point graduate appeared equally astonished by his own criminal behavior.
“It’s not who I am, who I was raised to be…. I’m just different now.”

I. Impact of War

Every veteran would agree that war is a life-changing experience. Because many who enlist are in the throes of young adulthood (close to 40 percent of post 9/11 veterans were under the age of 24 when they deployed), the experience of war significantly impacts their beliefs about themselves and the world, causing abrupt shifts in identity and goals, relationships, and basic values. Simply the reality of having survived leads many young veterans to experience a supercharged sense of invulnerability, whereas others return home bearing the heavy burden of survivor guilt and related self-blame.

A. Reintegration Issues

Regardless of age, the process of post-deployment reintegration can be difficult for veterans, particularly in regions such as Oregon where our veterans return not to military bases but to communities where they are expected to instantly adapt to civilian society. After serving in an overseas warzone, where rules of engagement and even daily survival strategies defy U.S. domestic laws, it should not be surprising when veterans struggle to, for instance, obey basic traffic laws. Moreover, after repeatedly responding to and, at times, themselves giving orders carrying life or death consequences, veterans may have difficulty adhering to what they see as meaningless laws or respecting local representations of domestic authority who they deem weak and even contemptable.

B. Impact on Stress Response System

Ultimately, while some return from warzone deployments with a new set of skills and a matured and strengthened sense of self, others bring home a gunnysack full of physical and psychological injuries. The most widely known combat-related psychiatric condition is of course Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD1) (see Chapter 6 for an in-depth discussion), an injury that is both physical and psychological. While reexperiencing the trauma through nightmares and distressing images is considered to be the hallmark of PTSD, it is the neurophysiological impact on the stress response system, otherwise known as the HPA Axis, which causes veterans chronic distress (Ben-Zur & Zeidner, 2009; Sherin & Nemeroff, 2011). Simply put, the experience of combat trauma may cause disruptions in the fight or flight arousal system, leading to symptoms such as sleep disturbance, panic attacks, rage reactions, hypervigilance to the point of paranoia, and exaggerated startle.

II. Reactions to War

Since the return of our first wave of veterans of our nation’s current wars, there has been a concerted effort to develop, disseminate, and implement meaningful assessments and effective treatments. Yet, military culture has continued to perpetuate a sense of weakness and stigma for those who admit to having problems for which they need help. In fact, in one study only half of those who had been identified as needing treatment after returning from deployment actually followed through with their referral (Rand, 2008). Thus, rather than seek assistance, many veterans develop their own strategies for symptom management—strategies which run the gamut from self-medication to self-destruction.

A. Self-medication and Self-modulation

“It’s like all my nerve endings are on the outside,” an Army Captain said in an effort to explain his post-deployment abuse of prescription pain medications. Paradoxically, some turn to stimulants such as cocaine as a means of feeling more comfortable and confident in crowds or social situations in which they now feel anxious. Still others rely on marijuana or increasing amounts of alcohol to get to sleep and effectively drown out the nighttime images and nightmares that plague them.

But it is the drive to engage in behaviors that are risky and dangerous that most confounds veterans and their loved ones. “It’s like there’s a switch in my head that suddenly turns on and it takes everything I’ve got to fight the impulse to do something crazy,” one veteran told me. Though often observed, this phenomenon of “reckless and self-destructive behavior” was only recently added to the diagnostic criteria for PTSD (DSM-V; American Psychiatric Association, 2013). The fact that this symptom was included under the category of Marked Alterations in Arousal reflects the tendency of those with PTSD to engage in risky behaviors that give them a “rush,” thereby serving as a means of adaptation to a disturbed and unmodulated arousal system.

But it is not just PTSD that drives reckless behavior in veterans. Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI), often referred to as the “signature injury” of the current wars, also can lead to impulsivity, aggression, and impaired decision-making. Because TBI often occurs in combination with PTSD, recent veterans are particularly at risk for violent outbursts (Sherin & Nemeroff, 2011). Add to that the high co-occurrence of substance abuse in both veterans with PTSD (Pietzrak, et al., 2011) and TBI (Ommaya, et al., 1996), and the probability of impulsive behavior in Veterans is further multiplied.

B. Rage Reactions

PTSD and TBI increase agitation and irritability and thus the likelihood of aggression in veterans. Some veterans may use violence as a means of releasing pent-up anxiety, whereas others may feel the impulse to aggressively express anger forged in the combat zone. While many relied on anger to fuel their strength and confidence under intense threat, other veterans developed a “righteous rage” in reaction to perceived betrayals by superiors, comrades, or even loved ones at home. After returning stateside, veterans may find themselves “addicted” to anger and the artificial sense of power that it brings (Armstrong, Best & Domenici, 2006).

C. Self-Destruction

Finally, many veterans return from war with a pervading sense of guilt, shame, and self-blame (Sherman, 2011). Perhaps they feel guilty for having survived when their combat buddies did not or for decisions or actions which lead to the loss of life, whether “ours,” “theirs,” or civilians. It is important to note that while PTSD is most often thought of as a fear-based condition driven by personal life threat, it may also develop in reaction to traumatic events for which we feel responsible. Because guilt reactions negatively impact mood, veterans with PTSD are at significant risk for associated major depression, a combination which results in more severe symptoms and resistance to treatment. Moreover, research has shown that veterans with PTSD are more likely to engage in risky and impulsive behaviors when they are in a depressed state. It is also important to note that while most consider depression to be a withdrawn and passive state, veterans suffering from a combination of PTSD and depression have been found to have an intensification of anger (Gonzalez, et al., 2016).

Ultimately, veterans with PTSD have been found to be four times more likely to have suicidal thoughts (ideation) than veterans without PTSD, and are at an increased risk of suicide compared to the general population (Jakupcak, et al., 2009). When combined with substance abuse as well as TBI, suicidal veterans may also be more likely to act impulsively and violently.

D. Criminal Behavior Among Veterans

In reaction to the traumas of warzone deployment, veterans may behave in ways that are not only out of character but undeniably criminal. The relationship between PTSD and criminal behavior was initially hypothesized and supported by Wilson and Zigelbaum (1983) in their research with Vietnam veterans, just three years after the diagnosis of PTSD was introduced as a psychiatric disorder. In the past few years there has been an increase in studies of such behaviors in our recently returning veterans. For instance, Strom, et al. (2012) found a relationship between PTSD symptoms and elevated rates of substance abuse, aggressive driving, thrill seeking, violence, risky sexual practices, and firearm possession.

III. Case Examples

What follows is a sampling of illegal behaviors and activities engaged in by veterans whom I have evaluated or treated. Names, anecdotes and quotes have been altered in order to protect the identities of these veterans. The exception to this is the final case which is presented with the consent of the veteran.

A. Reckless Driving and Road Rage

“Something snapped inside me and I didn’t care anymore.” During the course of his forensic psychological evaluation, Matt reflected that following his deployment, he was highly reactive to any potential threat and went into “combat mode” quite frequently. This was particularly true when driving, as he had been a gunner with a transportation unit and was constantly on the lookout for snipers and roadside bombs. In his initial year of post-deployment reintegration, Matt found himself speeding under overpasses that reminded him of staged Iraqi snipers and wildly veering around potholes and road debris. On one occasion, he was pulled over for reckless driving but received only a warning after explaining his behavior to the sympathetic trooper. Matt recalled feeling intensely relieved that the trooper hadn’t detected the fact that he was driving under the influence of intoxicants (DUII).

In addition to being highly reactive, Matt recognized that since his deployment he had used anger to keep people away and that he enjoyed the “rush” of going into a rage. Thus, on one particular evening when his agitation was high, Matt was cut off in traffic while driving home, leading to an assault charge. He recalls little of the incident, beyond the intensity of his anger and his single-minded drive to seek retribution.

B. DUII/Illegal Drug Use

Jason reported that while still in the military, he saw an Army physician because he had been experiencing nightmares and was feeling depressed. Although he tried to explain to the physician
what he was experiencing, he did not fully divulge the extent of his problems because he was concerned it might damage his military career. He recalled, however, that he was given a prescription for Prozac which helped to a certain extent, though he continued to increase his alcohol use to manage intrusive images and nightmares of his combat experience. Jason knew that he was “going crazy” but didn’t know why. “I was running with PTSD and didn’t know I had it. All I knew was that alcohol took care of it.”

After returning home, Jason continued to drink throughout the day and night. He was charged twice with DUII’s, once while riding a bicycle. He eventually was evaluated through the VA and was determined to be one hundred percent disabled due to PTSD. With the compensation he received, Jason relocated to a rural area of Oregon in order to “get away.” There he met a group of people who introduced him to methamphetamine and took advantage of his monthly VA check.

Jason was eventually arrested for methamphetamine possession and delivery. At the time of his evaluation he was anxious about the outcome of his case but grateful to have been arrested because it provided him with the opportunity to receive drug and alcohol treatment. “I was trapped in a cycle that I couldn’t get out of.” In addition, in response to my treatment recommendations his PTSD symptoms were better managed through medication initially prescribed during his incarceration.

C. Theft/Robbery

“It’s like something comes over me and I suddenly have this irresistible urge to steal the weirdest things. It’s not like I even need any of it. And then I leave the store and get out to my car and I can’t believe the stuff that I’ve jammed in my pockets. Dental floss, candy, a pair of socks. One time I even stole a cat toy—I hate cats!”

Fortunately for Angie, she recognized that she was “losing it” and needed to seek treatment before she was arrested. But for some veterans, it is necessary to “hit bottom” before they can ask for help.

After turning to alcohol to self-medicate his sleep disturbance, Brian was unable to curb his anger to the point that his mother became fearful and barred him from her home. Unable to trust anyone else and without any means of support, Brian became homeless. One bitterly cold winter night, Brian simply “snapped.” “The best way I can describe it is that I was desperate. I figured I had two choices: jump off the nearest bridge or rob the credit union. Funny—I had never actually stolen anything before in my life and there I was holding up a bank.” Being a pacifist who had served as a medic, Brian was clear that he did not want to harm anyone. “I slipped the teller a note and said, ‘I’m sorry.’ After she handed me the money I felt bad and so I said, ‘Thank you, have a nice day.’ When I think about that now it just seems crazy.”

D. Illegal Pornography/Sex Crimes

James began viewing pornography while deployed to Iraq simply because it was passed around the unit as something to ease the intermittent boredom. He then increased his viewing following deployment because he was unable to sleep due to PTSD-related nightmares. James also reported that he had begun experiencing erectile dysfunction and that viewing adult pornography increased his arousal.2 He naively reported that although he did not feel sexually aroused when viewing child pornography, he did experience a “rush” similar to the feeling he had when parachuting or rock repelling. Knowing that it was “wrong,” he nevertheless experienced the same rush when engaging in sexual acts with his brother’s 14-year-old daughter. At the same time, he recognized that what he was doing constituted what he called a “train wreck” that was not in his control.

E. Intimate Partner Violence/Domestic Violence

Veterans with PTSD as well as depression have been found to engage in domestic violence at significantly higher rates than veterans who do not suffer these conditions, as well as higher rates than the general population as a whole (Sherman, et al., 2006). Most interestingly, shame has been found to be an even stronger predictor than PTSD of intimate partner violence in veterans (Hunt & Holohan, 2012).

While serving as a cook in Kabul, Afghanistan, Eric was often teased for jumping each time he heard gunfire or bomb explosions. In order to “toughen him up,” some men from his unit took him out on patrol on three occasions, with the final patrol ending in his lying frozen with fear on the floor of the vehicle. After returning home, Eric vacillated between lying immobilized on the couch and yelling at the top of his lungs. Once a calm and gentle husband, he found it particularly difficult to control his anger with his wife. One night when his wife asked him why he had not stayed in touch with men from his unit, Eric flew into a rage. To this day, he recalls nothing of what happened until the police arrived.

F. Suicide by Cop

According to a 2015 epidemiological study Iraq and Afghanistan era service members were found to be at a 41–61 percent higher risk of suicide than the general population. The suicide risk, however, was actually found to be higher for those who had not deployed than for those who deployed to a warzone (Kang, et al., 2015). Yet the accuracy of these statistics has been questioned, given that certain types of veterans—such as those who were dishonorably discharged—as well as certain types of suicides such as vehicular and other lethal accidents and “suicide by cop” were not included. According to a summary of the research presented in a 2014 FBI bulletin, “individuals who feel trapped, ashamed, hopeless, desperate, revengeful, or enraged and those who are seeking notoriety, assuring lethality, saving face, sending a message, or evading moral responsibility” are more likely to attempt suicide by cop (Salvatore, 2014).

Over the past decade, numerous veteran suicide by cop incidents have made the news in cities across the nation. One example close to home is Nikkolas Lookabill, an Iraq War veteran suffering from PTSD who in 2010 told Vancouver, Wash., officers about his combat experience as he repeatedly reached toward his gun saying, “Just kill me.” Following his death it was learned that he had returned home from Iraq only four months prior (Lookabill v City of Vancouver, Clark Case No. 13-5461 RJB).3 It has also been widely speculated that LaVoy Finicum, spokesman for the Malheur National Wildlife Refuge occupation and Vietnam veteran, instigated a suicide by cop situation because he repeatedly told FBI agents and state troopers, “You’re going to have to shoot me.”

United States v Jonathan Whitecloud-Courtney (Case No. 315-cr-360-JO)

On September 14, 2015, retired Army Ranger Captain Jonathan Whitecloud Courtney pointed a loaded handgun at Warm Springs Indian Reservation Police Chief and officers while yelling, “Just shoot me!.”

Courtney’s military records indicate that he consistently received outstanding performance ratings from his superiors and was strongly recommended for promotions. During his Iraq War deployment in 2006, he assumed command of a battalion after his company commander was wounded in action. Then a first lieutenant, Courtney was touted as having excellent skills as well as “unstoppable drive.” He was recommended for promotion ahead of his peers and placed in the “most demanding assignments.” Courtney participated in over 550 combat patrols and received the Bronze Star in April 2006 for exceptional valor exhibited in a particular battle in which he saved the lives of numerous Iraqi police officers and ensured the defeat of an insurgent attack. He was promoted to captain in July 2006, and was awarded a second Bronze Star for his service as the executive officer and assistant operations officer. Courtney went on to receive other awards for meritorious service and outstanding achievement before retiring from the Army in 2010.

Courtney recalled that in 2007, after returning from his deployment, he underwent a VA PTSD screening. Although he was at that time experiencing significant sleep disturbance, depression, and what he now knows to be PTSD, he denied experiencing any symptoms because as a Ranger and superior he was “not supposed to have problems.” Jonathan instead stayed as busy as possible during the day and increased his alcohol use when home in an effort to “drink away” the memories that plagued him and interfered with his ability to sleep.

Asked about the combat memories that most disturbed him, Courtney tearfully recalled one incident in which he and his battalion engaged Iraqi insurgents in a 12-hour firefight that resulted in their killing 40 and capturing three. Courtney recalled several horrific incidents such as having to carry a decapitated body and a burned baby, and finding the body of a boy who had waved at him drilled to a post. While these incidents were highly distressing, Jonathan reported that his most traumatic experience during deployment occurred when he was battle captain, because he was charged with sending men into danger but was not allowed to go himself. He then became very tearful as he described the day that he sent a platoon on a mission and eight were killed. “I sent them out to die,” Courtney said.

Eventually Courtney’s PTSD, depression, and associated alcohol abuse caused the break-up of his marriage. After returning to Oregon and obtaining work at the Warm Springs Reservation, he remarried and relied upon his wife as his primary support. The day before the incident, his wife left in anger after an argument about his excessive alcohol use. He then continued to drink, keeping his pistol by his side as he did not feel safe when alone. Although his memory of the incident is spotty, Courtney is certain he did not at any time intend to harm anyone, particularly the police chief whom he liked and respected. “I just wanted to be left alone.” When later told that he had asked the officers to shoot him, Courtney responded with surprise. Upon further reflection, however, he acknowledged that while he would never shoot himself, he had had thoughts in the past of wanting to be killed by someone else, and had on one occasion even handed his gun to his mother and begged her to shoot him. “I guess I was looking for a way out.”

In my initial assessment of Courtney conducted at the Multnomah County Inverness Jail, he appeared seriously fatigued, depressed, and anxious. He acknowledged that although he was glad to be sober since being placed in custody, he was now plagued on a nightly basis by PTSD-related nightmares and night terrors that caused him to wake up punching the wall and to be distressed throughout the day. When asked what he would do if he were released tomorrow, Courtney responded that although he was eager to return to his wife and home, his priority was to stay sober by seeking intensive substance abuse treatment followed by PTSD treatment. “I want to really get help, and I want to know that I’m not alone.” I therefore recommended that Courtney be released to a VA residential intensive substance abuse treatment program, followed by intensive outpatient PTSD treatment.

Courtney entered the residential treatment program at the Vancouver VA and was reported to be actively engaged in his substance abuse recovery and invested in developing skills for managing his PTSD symptoms. He was discharged to his parents’ home in December 2015, where he lived with his wife. He continued outpatient PTSD treatment, maintained his sobriety, began building furniture, and eventually obtained full-time employment.

At Courtney’s sentencing his attorney, Assistant Federal Public Defender, Chris Schatz, sought treatment as an alternative to imprisonment. On the basis of a medical record review and follow-up evaluation of Courtney I testified that he would, based on a reasonable degree of medical probability, experience a significant resurgence of symptoms and disruption of his heretofore steady treatment progress should he be imprisoned. In support of his recommendation, Schatz relied on [Porter v. McCollum, 558 US 30 (2009), which declares: “Our Nation has a long tradition of according leniency to veterans in recognition of their service, especially for those who fought on the front lines[.]” Schatz also relied on United States v. Oldani (SD W Va June 16, 2009), which recognizes that the Federal Bureau of Prisons “is not uniquely situated, as is the VA, to treat the signature injuries from the United States’ current military engagements.” [The link to Porter v. McCollum was fixed in the 2020 Update.]

Rather than the 21-month prison term the United States Attorney sought, the court gave Courtney a two-year probationary sentence in accordance with defense recommendations.

IV. Conclusion

In a foreword to Courage After Fire (Armstrong, Best, and Domenici, 2006), former senator and wounded World War II Veteran Bob Dole wrote,

I have seen too many brave men and women—selfless, smart, and strong to the core—lose their way after fighting a war, and I confess that unnecessary suffering is difficult to stomach. Some veterans become delayed casualties of a war from which they returned home, in large part due to a misplaced selflessness that prevents them from putting the resources into their own physical and mental health.

I believe that it is for us all—especially those providing social, mental health, or legal services—to advance our understanding of the impact of warzone deployments on our nation’s veterans and to support them, by whatever means possible, when their injuries lead them to impulsive and at times desperate actions that go beyond the limits of the law. Jonathan Courtney’s case serves as proof that it is never too late to help these lost heroes regain the honor and respect that they deserve.
