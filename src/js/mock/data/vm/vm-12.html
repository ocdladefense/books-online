Chapter 12. Mastering the Challenges of Representing Veterans

Strategies for Successful Resolutions

By Terri Wood* & James Gardner*

Includes 2023 Update

Veteran defendants1 require special consideration in developing the best strategies for resolving their cases. While each client is unique, veteran defendants often present with common issues that can be used to their advantage in navigating the criminal justice system through plea negotiations and sentencing. Those same issues impact trial strategy, including whether a case should not or cannot be resolved by plea.

Armed with a basic knowledge of those issues—and how to build them into negotiations, trial, and sentencing—enables defense counsel to achieve outcomes often superior to what can be achieved for a similarly-situated defendant with no military service. The converse is equally true: attorneys ignorant of these issues do a great disservice to their veteran clients, and risk malpractice. This chapter addresses defense strategies for successfully resolving a veteran’s case.

The core principle—and primary difference in defending a client with military service—is identifying and proving veteran-specific issues for mitigation of guilt or punishment. Persuading the “decider,” be it the prosecutor, judge or jury, that your evidence is worthy of a better deal, an acquittal, or a lesser sentence, relies on the same skills defense counsel employ on behalf of any other client. But to be effective, counsel must become conversant in the specialized knowledge that underlies veteran-specific issues, using a process analogous to learning how to defend a driving under the influence of intoxicants case, mastering methods to attack complex scientific evidence, or how to try a guilty-except-insane defense to a jury.

Simply put, practitioners must immerse themselves in the topic—read, study, borrow, and learn from more experienced attorneys, then transform that knowledge into practice. The time spent will be worth it as there is, unfortunately, no shortage of veteran defendants in need of counsel who have made that immersion effort.

Readers are referred to the other chapters of this manual that provide comprehensive summaries of this specialized knowledge and links to additional resources. This chapter is focused on how that knowledge can be put to practice to present the best possible defense. It seeks to open the eyes of any defense attorney who thinks that there is no cause to deal with “veterans issues”—and the time and effort that would entail—so long as the prosecutor is making the same plea offer usually extended to non-veteran defendants, or that there is no defense because there is no dispute that the veteran did the acts.

I. Do No Harm: Building the Attorney-Client Relationship

For most veteran defendants, the criminal justice system is something unforeseen and foreign. For many attorneys, the specialized knowledge needed to represent veterans is also unforeseen and foreign. This section addresses building client trust and solving the majority of veteran-centered roadblocks to an effective attorney-client relationship. For example, many of the common problems associated with veteran clients are denial, distrust, the unwillingness to discuss “invisible injuries,” and resistance to seeking treatment.

A. Identifying Veteran Clients

The first issue in dealing with any veteran defendant is identifying that status. This may seem obvious, but many veterans are in denial about their current situation. For example, multiple veteran clients when asked if they are veterans indicate they are not. But family members disclose that they are in fact veterans.

One reason this occurs is that the veteran is embarrassed. The mere fact he is charged tarnishes the perception of the military and his service.2 Not every veteran client will think this way. But it helps to understand how a veteran might think about their service and how asking the question does not always identify who is a veteran. Most important is for the client to understand how his or her veteran status can assist in their representation. It is also important for the client to understand that military service—even if less than distinguished—can still assist in the representation. For more information this area, see The “Not So Good Soldier” Defense, below.

After determining whether your client is a veteran, counsel must determine the nature and extent of their military service. All military service is important, but the nature and extent will determine possible defenses and mitigation. For those unfamiliar with veterans or the military, use the Veteran Intake Form found on the web site of the Veterans Defense Resource Center.3 Using this form during intake will enable counsel to determine the client’s branch of service, military occupation, periods of service, potential witnesses, overseas deployment, potential combat related injuries, and if he or she has service records or records from the federal Department of Veteran Affairs (VA).

B. Building Trust

Building trust with veteran clients is not easy. This is especially true for the majority of attorneys who know little about the military and the many military concepts and terminology which seem to be part of a foreign language and culture. Understanding these concepts is vitally important to help build credibility with your client. Lack of understanding may give rise to a communication barrier that could disrupt the attorney-client relationship and lead to claims of inadequate and ineffective assistance of counsel. See Lichau v. Baldwin, 333 Or 350, 359-61, 39 P3d 851 (2002) (counsel’s erroneous withdrawal of alibi defense owing to his misunderstanding of military terminology constituted inadequate assistance). Therefore, it is important to understand military terminology and culture in conjunction with your client’s individual military service.

For example, consider a veteran defendant who tells his lawyer that he “was deployed as part of Operation New Dawn and operated a 240B,” reports that his “MOS” was “13-B,” and he was an “E-4.” The lawyer most likely would be clueless about the meaning of those terms or could potentially miss pivotal information. Many resources are available to help assist the attorney in understanding the military and many military concepts and terminology. One resource is Chapter 3 of this manual, which provides an extensive glossary. Another resource is the Veterans Defense Resource Center (VDRC). Yet another is the Department of Defense’s free online resource to translate military acronyms and terminology.

In the above example, translators could explain that the veteran defendant’s statement that he “was deployed to Operation New Dawn” means he served in Iraq on or after September 1, 2010.4 A “240B” is a type of fully automatic weapon, so explains the veteran was a machine gunner. “M.O.S.” stands for “military occupational specialty,” which is the term the army and the Marine Corps use for job description.5 In the army, the number “13” means the veteran was an artilleryman, and the letter “B” means he was an enlisted soldier. Therefore, the veteran served on patrols as a machine gunner in an artillery unit. Finally, “E-4” is a pay grade associated with the veteran defendant’s rank. The letter “E” establishes that he served in the enlisted ranks, and the number “4” shows that his rank placed him on the fourth step of the military’s nine-step pay scale for servicemembers.6

Understanding this information could be essential in building possible defense and mitigation strategies. Moreover, a lawyer’s ability to use terminology could help build trust with the client.

C. Addressing “Invisible Injuries” for Combat and Non-Combat Veterans

By virtue of their situation, veteran clients often fight a war within, affected by both the visible and invisible injuries of war. Those who suffer from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), traumatic brain injury (TBI), military sexual trauma (MST), moral injury, or other service-related injuries may be devastated by the additional stress of litigation. Evan R. Seamone, “Attorneys as First-Responders: Recognizing Counselors: Iraq War Veterans with Traumatic Brain Injury and Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder on the Combat Veteran’s Legal Decision-Making Process,” 202 Mil L Rev 144 (2009).7

Bear in mind that a veteran need not have experienced direct combat to have invisible injuries. For example, medical personnel experience the trauma of war, death, and battle wounds without being exposed to significant personal danger. Likewise, drone pilots watch the war live on a television thousands of miles away but have been known to suffer from PTSD. Veterans who have never seen combat but are victims of military sexual assault8 may suffer from PTSD. See Brockton D. Hunter and Ryan C. Else, The Attorney’s Guide to Defending Veterans in Criminal Court 448-452 (2014) (hereafter Hunter & Ellis, Defending Veterans).9 These invisible injuries must be addressed for access to services, counseling, and communication with your client, and may provide substantial support for defense and mitigation strategies.

For veterans who indicate they are diagnosed with PTSD or TBI, the assessment of defense and mitigation available is easy. If the client reports having sought medical care from an active-duty military installation, he or she may request their medical records from their last treatment facility. For information on submitting military record requests, see Chapter 3.

For clients who have sought medical care from the VA, a separate type of record request is used. For information on submitting VA record requests, again see Chapter 3. An example of a pre-filled form for obtaining VA records is available in Chapter 4 and here.

A military or VA medical professional’s diagnosis of PTSD or TBI will be greatly helpful. However, if a military or VA medical professional concluded that a veteran does not suffer from PTSD or TBI but that conclusion conflicts with the client’s intake screening for PTSD/TBI, defense counsel need not treat the negative conclusion as final. Defense counsel should seek an independent examination by medical professionals. If the client has court-appointed counsel or if counsel is retained but the client cannot afford the costs of such examinations, counsel may request indigent-based funding by filing a non-routine expense (NRE) request with the Contract & Business Services Division of the state Office of Public Defense Services (OPDS). Most critically, an effort to convince the trier of fact of the legitimacy of a claim for special consideration should not stop with an examination for such things as PTSD and TBI.

It is not uncommon for veterans to avoid disclosing the nature of the injuries while on active duty, or to have undiagnosed injuries. Unfortunately, the military has created a culture where injuries are seen as weaknesses. For example, if a soldier has to seek counseling, he is seen as weak because in essence he cannot “hack it” or handle the stress of combat, making him second-rate in the eyes of his fellow servicemembers and supervisors. Another likely cause of a veteran going undiagnosed is that the injury or injuries did not manifest until after he or she veteran left active service.

This is why all veteran clients should be screened for invisible injuries such as PTSD and TBI. Screening questionnaires will assist in determining whether clients should be evaluated by medical professionals, despite their rationale to not disclose potential invisible injuries. Two screening tools—the PTSD Screening Checklist (PCL) prepared by the federal Department of Veteran Affairs, and Screening Questionnaire: Readjustment Counseling Service: Pacific Western Region Traumatic Brain Injury—are available at the VDRC. These will help address whether a veteran client needs further evaluation.

D. Motivating the Client for Evaluation and Treatment

Many veteran clients will be told for the first time that they are experiencing a mental illness only after being charged with a criminal offense. This can be shocking. Other clients, including those who suffer from delayed onset of the mental illness, will remain in denial, even when confronted with criminal charges. The question becomes: how to motivate the client to submit to an evaluation and treatment for his or her mental illness.

The first step: suggest a knowledgeable service provider who can provide effective assistance for veteran clients, or one who specializes in service-related mental illnesses. Carol M. Suzuki, “When Something is Not Quite Right: Considerations for Advising a Client to Seek Mental Health Treatment,” 6 Hasting Race & Poverty LJ 208, 217 (2009). Then be sure to explain why treatment can help.

Making only a general recommendation for referral to a mental health provider is ineffective and leaves too much to chance. The key to motivating the client and ensuring they get evaluated and treated is making an effective referral. The components for an effective referral are: (1) make contact with the prospective referral source before making a recommending to the client, (2) determine whether the provider meets the client’s specific needs regarding mental illness and case strategy, (3) identify potential conflicts, such as differing views and objectives of the legal representation, and (4) trial availability. Hunter & Else, Defending Veterans at 310. In Oregon, practitioners should contact the VDRC for specialized service providers to meet your client’s needs near your location. A list of treatment providers and free treatment resources is available here.

After obtaining a service provider, counsel must motivate the client for treatment. The best way to do that is to explain how or why it might be effective for representation. For example, many clients ask, “How will this help?” Depending on the client and the case, the answer may be for a defense, for mitigation, or both. For clients in denial or who still do not believe they have a mental illness and thus do not want treatment, explain there may be a viable defense to explore and see if it is available. For clients who acknowledge they may have a mental illness, it is easier to explain how treatment can help the defense, mitigate the client’s actions for plea negotiations, and obtain access to services or disability benefits after the case is over.

II. Directing the Investigation

A client’s description of military service, as well as professional diagnoses, should be corroborated with service records, witness interviews, and other evidence. This is necessary for many reasons, including the possibility that the client has fabricated claims about his or her service or is malingering. If relying on the client to obtain records, which is the most expedient means to get them, be sure the client understands and agrees that defense counsel needs all of the records, including those the client may not want to provide.

A short cautionary tale may help make that point. One veteran defendant elected to withhold disciplinary records—records that did not result in any loss of rank or affect discharge status but which contained negative facts and opinions reflecting on the veteran’s credibility in a particular situation. The state obtained the records via subpoena shortly before trial. Because defense counsel was unaware of the records, the defense experts who were going to testify were equally unaware. Defense counsel could not ethically reveal the reason the records had been withheld from the experts. The end result was counsel lost credibility with the prosecutor and judge for apparently “hiding the ball,” and the defense experts could not testify without being subjected to impeachment, all of which certainly cast doubt on the integrity of the defense theory. These disastrous consequences could have been avoided if the client had not made the unilateral decision regarding what records would be helpful to his or her defense.

A. Supervising the Investigator

When resources are available, attorneys should use an investigator with military-specific expertise.10 Usually, investigators who are veterans are the most beneficial. This expertise can be invaluable in conducting the investigation, understanding military concepts discussed in the previous sections, and interviewing potential witnesses. However, when a military-savvy investigator is not available, the attorney must supervise the investigator.

B. Areas of Investigation

There are five essential areas of investigation for all military clients:

  • service records
  • VA records
  • other medical records
  • military witnesses
  • family member or other witnesses

1. Obtaining Service Records

Start your investigation by seeking the client’s military service records. The client presently on active duty, in the reserves or the National Guard can be documented easily with a current copy of their military orders and a complete copy of their Official Military Personnel File (OMPF). Obtaining records is much easier with your client’s cooperation. A client’s OMPF should contain all orders, prior discharges, promotion orders, awards, and evaluation reports. This information is instrumental in determining witnesses you need to interview and potential sentencing material. For example, see Draft DA Form 2166, NCO Evaluation Report. This evaluation report will contain a description of the client’s duty performance, rating, and military character witnesses with their contact information. Likewise, an Army Enlisted Record Brief (ERB), which is essentially a one-page resume, lists a soldier’s duty assignments, military and civilian education, overseas service, awards, test scores, and security clearance.

If the client is not presently on active duty, this information can still be obtained. The client can access his or her information online through the VA at https://www.ebenefits.va.gov (last visited on March 17, 2017). All clients with prior active-duty service will have been issued a Certificate of Release or Discharge from active duty, called a DD-214. The DD-214 documents the character of the veteran’s service, overseas tours, military specific, and awards. This will be true even for those who served in the National Guard or reserves but were called onto active duty. After they are released from active duty, they will be issued a DD-214.11 Clients with prior service in the National Guard (but only that) will have been issued a National Guard Bureau (NGB) Form 22 instead of a DD-214.

For clients who cannot access their military records, because they are in jail or their records are inaccessible, their records may be obtained online. For information on obtaining records in that manner, including on means to obtain them expeditiously, see Chapter 3.

2. Obtaining VA and Other Health Records

VA records and military medical records are as important as service records in determining defense strategies and mitigation evidence. These records include mental health and treatment records, as well as ratings and determinations for service-connected disabilities. These can help draw the connection between your client’s military service, criminal history, mental illness, or disability.

Generally, if the client was released from active duty with a DD-214 and honorably discharged, his or her disability or health records will not be part of the service records. To specifically request the client’s medical records while on active duty, obtain them directly from the medical treatment facility using a form called DD Form 2870, Authorization for Disclosure of Medical and Dental Information.

For the majority of clients, VA records are the key to proving service-connected disabilities and mental health diagnoses. Most clients do not claim or document injury or illness until after discharge because of embarrassment, denial, or other reasons. If the client reports having sought medical care from the VA, a form called VA Form 5345, Request for and Authorization to Release Medical Records or Health Information, can be used to obtain the client’s VA records (again, see a sample form in Chapter 4 and here). The VA Form 5345 must be sent to the client’s treatment facility. Click here for a list of Oregon treatment facilities.

3. Finding and Interviewing “Good Soldier” Witnesses

The best evidence to convince the trier of fact of the legitimacy of a claim for special consideration or a defense is the client’s “good soldier” witnesses: the men and women who served with your client, including superiors and subordinates, who can attest to the client’s performance and character. Defense counsel and investigators should contact the client’s current or former commanders, supervisors, peers, and subordinates to determine whether they are willing to vouch or testify to the defendant’s good military character and service. If the client kept in good contact with his friends and colleagues, this should be easy. But most clients do not keep in contact with their fellow veterans. Witnesses still in the military can be located online using the military
locator.

When interviewing military witnesses, keep in mind that given the nature of the charge and the individual defendant, they may not be willing to testify. Therefore, it is important to educate each witness as to why his or her testimony is essential to help the trier of fact understand the nature and extent of the client’s military service. For example, explain that no one is justifying what happened or blaming the military, but that their testimony is needed in order for the trier of fact to understand what kind of servicemember the client was and what they experienced during service.

If despite best efforts the military witness is unwilling to testify, the witness may be subpoenaed. For information about doing so, see Chapter 3.

4. Friends and Family Members Interviews

Interviewing close family members and friends is also essential to building an effective defense. Family members and close friends can help define your client’s character and behavior prior to military service, which will then help you prove how he or she has changed. For example, in explaining the military culture and total institution to the trier of fact in the case of a client charged with assault, it is important to understand how peaceful or aggressive your client was before being conditioned and trained to kill without question and to follow orders. A parent could testify that prior to her son attending basic training and deploying to Afghanistan, he was soft spoken, nerdy, studying to be a chemist, and had never been violent. But after returning from the deployment he turned into a different person—angry, short-tempered, and drank a lot. This information can demonstrate how your client’s self-defense claim could be reasonable based on an objective and subjective standard.

III. Constructing Mitigation Strategies for Plea, Trial or Sentencing

Defense counsel know how to negotiate a plea, try a case, and conduct a sentencing hearing. But only by identifying veteran-specific issues and developing corresponding mitigating evidence as part of the defense investigation will counsel be armed with the necessary information to evaluate the best avenue for resolution in the case at hand. Importantly, attorneys who have acquired this knowledge and information can pursue many of these strategies in the absence of retaining a defense expert or in addition to a defense expert.

A. Obtaining Mitigating Evidence During Pretrial Incarceration and Obtaining Pretrial Release

From the outset, counsel should consider directing the incarcerated client to seek medical and mental health services from the custodial provider. As discussed previously, the client may not be initially receptive to that advice for a variety of reasons, including lack of rapport with counsel. But if the client was receiving such services prior to incarceration, he or she will likely agree. In addition, have the client sign Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPPA) releases to jail health services staff so that counsel can be proactive in obtaining services on the veteran’s behalf for later use in the client’s defense. The client’s requests for services, as well as services provided while in custody, may also assist in persuading the release authority to grant conditional release.

Furthermore, the records generated by requests for services and services provided may prove valuable in other ways, such as:

  • Documentation of post-offense rehabilitative efforts.
  • Support for seeking funds from OPDS for expert assistance.
  • Evidence for mitigation arguments to the prosecutor in plea negotiations and to the court at sentencing in the absence of retaining a defense expert or in addition to a defense expert.
  • “Objective” evidence of medical or mental health issues, i.e., evidence less subject to impeachment than the opinions of retained defense experts.

Counsel should also obtain the client’s non-medical jail records including intake, custody classification reviews, and disciplinary actions. A general release of information form should suffice. Those records may provide mitigating evidence of good conduct and/or may document the adverse impact of incarceration on the veteran defendant. For example, both anecdotal reports and research show that incarceration exacerbates the symptoms of PTSD.12 A veteran defendant with PTSD may request solitary confinement to avoid interaction with other inmates, knowing such interaction could trigger anger outbursts or increased anxiety and hypervigilance resulting in greater stress and insomnia. On the other hand, it is well established that solitary confinement can both cause mental illness, and exacerbate preexisting mental health conditions. Joshua Manson, “New Report Documents Devastating Effects of Solitary Confinement on Mental Illness,” Solitary Watch (Sept. 2016) (with links to related articles).

Such evidence and supporting literature may persuade the release authority to grant a conditional release with a treatment component. It may also help convince a prosecutor or judge to favor a resolution that does not involve extended incarceration, given that extended incarceration could cause the client to return to society more damaged and more dangerous.

This avenue of defense is also available in the absence of retaining a defense expert or in addition to a defense expert. This is important for three primary reasons:

  1. Veterans not granted pretrial release may be promptly offered a misdemeanor resolution or a presumptive probationary sentence in exchange for pleading guilty, which makes obtaining a defense expert impracticable—i.e., the client would serve far more time than the negotiated sentence waiting for a defense expert to come on board, perform an evaluation, and provide assistance.
  2. Indigent veterans charged with misdemeanors or presumptive probation offenses, even if released pretrial, may not be granted thousands of dollars to fund a defense expert when all counsel can represent is that the assistance could be helpful in seeking mitigation of sentence, particularly if the sentence the prosecution is seeking involves no or minimal jail time.
  3. The veteran may not qualify for indigent defense, but may still lack the means to hire an expert in addition to paying for counsel, or may decide the financial hardship that would result from funding an expert is not worth it.

Veterans Administration treatment services and programs should be explored as part of a defense release plan for veterans in pretrial confinement. Outpatient services are most readily accessible. Intensive, inpatient treatment programs for substance abuse as well as PTSD are available but generally have waiting lists that can be weeks and even months long; however, the veteran may be able to enter such programs sooner if allowed to travel to VA facilities in adjoining states.

The VA does not offer secure inpatient treatment; in other words, the defendant is able to walk away from the facility, unless placed in an acute psychiatric lockdown ward due to posing an immediate danger to self or others. Although all VA medical centers have these lockdown wards, veterans are typically held for only a few days, until stabilized or civilly committed to a state hospital. However, the VA does have its own police force at each facility. With the proper releases of information in place, those officers may temporarily detain a veteran defendant who attempts to leave the treatment center contrary to his or her release conditions, or may immediately contact the court so a warrant can be issued and local law enforcement dispatched. Hunter & Else, Defending Veterans at 443.

The VA will not treat a veteran on “furlough” from a custodial authority, nor will it provide treatment to an incarcerated veteran. But the VA will provide services and programs to veteran defendants released pre-trial or post-sentence.

Even if the defendant has not previously accessed VA services, counsel may solicit assistance from staff with the local Veterans Justice Outreach Program (VJOP) to obtain services for the client. See Chapter 3 for more information about VJOP.

B. Developing “Pre-Sentence Rehabilitation” Mitigating Evidence

Pre-sentence rehabilitative efforts reflect favorably toward the defendant’s acceptance of personal responsibility and accountability, two of the three principles expressed in the Oregon Constitution for arriving at just punishment.13 Participation in treatment services, whether on an outpatient or inpatient basis, or even the limited services available in pretrial detention, is a “win-win” proposition in most cases. Treatment helps the veteran cope with substance abuse and/or mental health issues that have caused problems reintegrating to civilian society. And progress in treatment provides counsel with mitigating evidence of pre-sentence rehabilitation.

Unlike the non-veteran client, the veteran may obtain treatment services at no cost through the VA. In recent years, and in the wake of scandals and escalating suicide rates, the VA has increased its efforts to provide better restorative care to veterans, including providing more inpatient centers for intensive PTSD treatment. The local VJOP specialist will meet with clients who are out of custody—as well as those in custody—to assist in making treatment referrals.

Counsel should obtain the treatment records for use as mitigating evidence in the absence of retaining a defense expert or in addition to a defense expert. The client can obtain a copy of the records at no charge and provide them to counsel. However, have the client sign the VA release of information forms (called ROIs) authorizing the VJOP as well as VA treatment providers to share all information and speak freely with defense counsel. This is necessary because, for example, email communications may be important records not furnished to the client as part of his or her medical service records.

Counsel may find that veteran clients who have resisted treatment in the past—including those who have denied substance abuse or mental health issues in the face of contrary evidence—may become open to seeking treatment once embroiled in the criminal justice system. To be productive at this endeavor, counsel must first become culturally competent to work with veterans and acquire the specialized knowledge to identify and address these veteran-specific issues.14 Only then will the attorney be equipped to speak genuinely with veteran clients about the criminal prosecution serving as an uninvited “intervention” that nonetheless presents the opportunity (and additional motivation) to meaningfully address long-standing problems. Conquering those problems will lead to a better life for the veterans and their families, and will provide counsel with evidence that could achieve a better outcome in the criminal case. Veterans have been indoctrinated to complete the mission. Counsel’s job is to enlist the client to work as part of the defense team to complete the mission of achieving the very best outcome, not only for the criminal case but for their life after the case is done.

C. Military Culture as Mitigating Evidence

The starting point for every attorney—including prosecutors and judges who seek to do justice—is to become competent in military culture and its indoctrination processes, to understand at least the basics of how military culture shapes and defines human behavior, and the stark differences between military and civilian culture in modern America. Those cultural conflicts, as well as the “invisible injuries” from military service that manifest in substance abuse, PTSD, and other maladies of the psyche, make post-service reintegration difficult for many veterans, and cause more than a few to need criminal defense counsel.

From the defense standpoint, a working knowledge of military culture is fundamental to analyzing the client’s culpability under the law. This specialized knowledge must be acquired before counsel can fully understand—and therefore be able to effectively explain—why, for example, a veteran with PTSD engaged in the behavior that resulted in arrest, and to effectively advocate for a just resolution. Even veteran defendants with no service-connected injuries, or those for whom the service connection is in dispute, are products of military culture. Their current behavior may have been influenced by military programming, and the absence of any structured deprogramming upon reentry to civilian society.

Military culture is largely explained and defined by the military total institution (MTI), and the terms are often used interchangeably in the literature. In Chapter 5, Dr. William Brown discusses in detail the military total institution and its influence on a veteran’s resulting behaviors and belief system. A brief overview of the MTI condensed from Dr. Brown’s work is included here, followed by examples of how a veteran’s indoctrination into military culture can be used to mitigate, challenge, or negate alleged criminal conduct.

Brief Overview of the Military Total Institution

The basic characteristics germane to any total institution include:

  • All components of an individual’s life occur in the same place or setting.
  • Large numbers of people are treated nearly or exactly the same.
  • All stages of the individual’s day and night are tightly scheduled and monitored.
  • All participants are required to accept and adapt to the total institutions’ cultural expectations and standards. 15

These characteristics are prevalent in all military institutions throughout the world. Military total institutions require complete control of the recruit’s entire being and replacement of the recruit’s civilian cultural beliefs and responses with those of the military. The recruit is first stripped of civilian values and habits and, through rigorous training, ingrained in military values and routines. This is necessary to produce soldiers who will respond to orders without question, carry out the mission, and kill the enemy.

The military operates on a completely different set of expectations than the civilian culture and subscribes to a different set of values and morals. Four core values are ingrained in the military total institution: obedience, discipline, survival, and sacrifice. Deployment, particularly to combat zones, further ingrains the core values which are then put to practice in life-or-death situations, rather than training exercises.

Those four indispensable factors maintain the foundation of the military total institution, as follows:

Obedience – requires military personnel to accept the command of authority without hesitation or question.

Discipline – is crucial to the perfection of the recruit’s mental faculties and moral character. High levels of discipline are obtained through repetitive training, whereby the trainee’s reaction to situations becomes second nature.

Survival – is the means and individual commitment to ensure the continuation of life—for fellow soldiers as well as self—to carry out the mission.

Sacrifice – requires the soldier to surrender something of value to ensure that something else of value is maintained or secured. As recruits are integrated into the military total institution, they are required to sacrifice their own individuality. During the training process, sacrificing one’s own life is portrayed as an honorable act. This segment of training and indoctrination prepares soldiers, who may later be confronted with life-or-death situations, to willingly make this ultimate sacrifice.

For many veterans, particularly those who participated in combat, their military total institution experiences are embedded for life. The experiences become part of the baggage many veterans carry as they navigate their transition back into civilian culture. Many veterans are not aware of that baggage until they become homeless, involved in domestic violence situation at home, or as a defendant in the criminal justice system.

Following release from the military the veteran does not experience extraction or deprogramming of the military-installed “mental software.” Some veterans are successful in deprogramming and reintegration to civilian culture. Others are not so successful.

Using Military Culture to Explain Defendant’s Conduct

David agreed that his experiences in the military culture played a role in the way he behaved post-deployment in civilian culture. He described the role of military culture as having a “significant” impact, and said it has resulted in feelings of irritation in the company of civilians, feeling comfortable only with veterans, being highly suspicious of strangers, and being quite hypervigilant.16

Indoctrination into military culture not only changes the veteran’s behavior, but changes the beliefs and values that motivate behavior. It also changes behavior through conditioned responses to situations the veteran is likely to encounter during service, including perceived threats. For example, veterans are conditioned through repetitive drills and exercises—as well as psychological indoctrination—to react immediately to any perceived threat by eliminating the threat. They are conditioned to be expendable in pursuit of the mission. Veterans are also conditioned to remain externally calm and regimented in the face of danger, by controlling strong natural emotions of fear and distress. But the suppression of emotions does not eliminate the internal psychological force of those emotions on the individual’s physical and mental health.

1. Military Culture as Part of the Defense to Charges

David has experienced recurrent or intrusive thoughts about his experiences in a war zone. The focus of these thoughts pertain to witnessing the death of another American, constant fear of the unknown, and the killing of another person. He has had dissociative reactions (flashbacks) to the experiences he encountered in combat. He has experienced periods of sudden acting out or feeling that a previous combat-related event was reoccurring. He often becomes hypervigilant, afraid, and angry when these things happen.

David has had experiences in civilian culture that remind him of traumatic experiences he encountered during his deployment. The typical triggering stimuli include sudden or loud noises, finding himself in crowded areas, when strangers approach him, and sometimes when he is driving. Often when he is confronted with these stimuli, he becomes hypervigilant, afraid, and then he becomes angry.

An understanding of military culture and how it likely influenced a veteran’s mental state and resultant conduct could be used to mitigate or negate a criminal charge. For example, it can help explain why the veteran experienced “road rage” in an assault or vehicular homicide case. Exactly contrary to civilian culture, where individuals are expected to embrace a “think first, act later” strategy, the military total institution “programs” servicemembers to “act first, think later” for survival. Additionally, VJOP coordinator Eric L. Dungan, LCSW, explains that servicemembers in combat zones always have the right of way on the road, so are able to shoot or run over any vehicle in their way. See Responding to the Needs of Justice-Involved Veterans (May 2014).

Understanding this “programming” may play an important role in explaining why a veteran defendant, in self-defense, instinctively shot to kill rather than disable or deter a perceived aggressor as a non-veteran might have done in the same situation. Since World War II the military has improved its combat training methods to produce troops who will kill the enemy without hesitation. Only 15 to 20 percent of soldiers in World War II would fire at the enemy, whereas up to 95 percent would do so by the Vietnam War. Modern training methods include simulated combat calculated to cause severe stress and thereby train the recruit to select fight as the instinctive reaction, as opposed to flight or freeze. Marching cadences resound with kill, kill, kill and blood will spill. Hunter & Else, Defending Veterans at 473-76 (frequently citing Dave Grossman, On Killing (1995).

Success in the military culture and total institution largely is measured by the servicemember’s proficiency in mortal combat. (Section IV of this chapter provides an overview of specific defenses and partial defenses, as well as relevant case law.)

2. Military Culture as Relevant to Involuntary or False Confessions

Military culture can be used to help explain why the veteran made an involuntary or even a false confession to a crime. Dr. Robert Stanulis17 has testified that one of the core military values—sacrifice of oneself to protect others, and resulting acceptance of one’s expendability—was a powerful influence on the defendant’s motivation to make a false confession to protect his sister. Military “muscle memory”—overtraining to respond immediately in assessing and solving the problem at hand—also influenced the defendant’s conduct. But this was to his grave disadvantage because “this obviously isn’t a military problem; this is a very intense personal family problem.” Further, the defendant’s calm and cool demeanor during the interrogation was consistent with his military training to control one’s outward emotional demeanor even when experiencing strong internal emotions of shock and distress, rather than showing he was not coerced by the interrogator.

3. Military Culture to Help Explain Veteran’s Opposition to Civil Authorities

Military culture can also help explain the conduct of clients whose post-discharge history involves opposition to authority. According to Dr. Brown, the development of what some characterize as “anti-government” views by military personnel and veterans is not uncommon. To start with, the government is largely run by civilians, who many veterans have little respect for due to differences in military versus civilian culture and values.

George was asked to list the “baggage” acquired during his military service that seemed to have the biggest impact on him in civilian culture. He said hypervigilance, suspicion of strangers, self-isolation, emotional numbness, and irritation in the company of civilians. He said many, if not most, civilians demonstrated very little commitment to loyalty and willingness to sacrifice for others. After serving more than 10 years in the military, George said he had difficulty understanding why civilians behaved this way.

4. Military Culture May Give Rise to Moral Injury

The motivation for becoming “anti-government” is often linked to feelings of betrayal, both by the government and by the military. Those feelings may arise from the indoctrination during military training that they will be deployed to defend democracy or to protect civilians in the combat area of operation. Many veterans, in the aftermath of their deployment experience, come to believe that this was not truthful. For those veterans who have taken human life, sustained injury themselves, or witnessed injury or the death of fellow Americans—for what they come to see as a false purpose—the sense of betrayal often runs deep and lasting. This collective sense of betrayal is a hallmark of “moral injury.” Moral injury can give rise to depression, self-sabotage, a sense of purposelessness, and other psychic maladies that may have driven the veteran’s behavior in the criminal case. For a detailed discussion of moral injury, see Chapter 8.

David deployed to defend America, defeat the enemy, and ensure that the United States Constitution would be protected. He was exposed to the horrors of war, sustained physical injuries including TBI, and suffers from PTSD. His military service altered his life’s course and his future—seemingly for the worse. He and the public in general have come to recognize that the motives behind the rationale for the nation to engage in this conflict have proven to be false. Feelings of betrayal invariably result.

5. Using Military Culture to Mitigate Culpability for Plea and Sentencing

An understanding of the military culture and total institution, and how it likely influenced a veteran defendant’s mental state and resultant conduct, also provides fertile grounds for brainstorming mitigation used in plea negotiations and sentencing. (Section IV of this chapter provides guidance for structuring negotiations and sentencing arguments, as well as case law.)

George experiences diminished interests in intimacy, previous hobbies, employment, and family. He feels uncomfortable around people who do not share his military culture experiences, uncomfortable around former friends, and sometimes feels uncomfortable around members of his family. He noted that if he doesn’t fit in anywhere, he doesn’t have much of a future anywhere.

He said that even though he was able to find employment, he felt that the job was beneath him when compared to the level of responsibility he held while in the military. He often feels that if he is doing something he considers worthless, it generally means that he too is worthless.

A large majority of veteran defendants share a distinct similarity with veterans who have not become criminal defendants: the absence of any criminal history prior to entering the military and during the period they served in the military. Their criminal history began with their struggles to reintegrate to civilian society and their failure to do so. See, e.g., William B. Brown, “From War Zones to Jail: Veteran Reintegration Problems,” 8 Justice Policy Journal (Spring 2011).

6. Methods of Proving Military Culture as Mitigation

Expert testimony by a forensic psychologist versed in military culture, such as Dr. Stanulis, will likely be necessary to support theories of defense or partial defense at pre-trial hearings and at trial. The reader is referred to Mental Health & Criminal Defense (OCDLA 2010) by Alexander Bassos (with Dr. Keith Linn) for instruction and case law helpful in analyzing and presenting mental health issues in criminal courts.

Dr. William Brown is the only expert who has conducted sociological evaluations of veteran defendants for use in court proceedings. Although Dr. Brown has been qualified to testify as an expert on military culture in various courts, including some in Oregon, defense counsel should expect the state to mount OEC 702–based challenges to the admissibility of his testimony at a trial. A sample Motion to Admit Expert Testimony on Military Culture & Total Institution is provided for defense counsel use in the appendices as Appendix A and in storage here.18 More recent appellate law in Oregon authorizing expert testimony by law enforcement officers on a variety of topics, based solely on their “training and experience,” should be consulted in updating the sample motion.

Should Dr. Brown be unavailable to assist in a given case, defense counsel may still use arguments grounded on the military culture and total institution in plea negotiations and sentencing hearings, where OEC 702 does not apply, by citing to numerous peer-reviewed articles by Brown and a variety of coauthors.19 To make convincing arguments, counsel will need to obtain and analyze not only the client’s military records, but also records documenting the client’s pre-military and post-military histories, supplemented by interviews of the client as well as individuals who have known the client over that time period, and individuals who served with the client.

In other words, counsel would be well served by employing a mitigation investigator experienced in defending veteran clients. The alternative is for counsel to become knowledgeable enough to direct an investigator in pursuit of the necessary information.

D. PTSD and Other “Invisible Injuries” as Mitigating Evidence

PTSD, TBI, and other invisible injuries sustained or aggravated during military service may be part of the theory for a complete or partial defense at trial, or for mitigation in plea bargaining and sentencing. This manual contains discrete chapters discussing each invisible injury in detail, along with examples of how each type of injury may be used in defense of the veteran. In addition to the information contained in those chapters, the reader is referred to Section IV of this chapter, which focuses on practice pointers for defense counsel, including case law and authorities. See also Terri Tanielian & Lisa H. Jaycox, eds., Invisible Wounds of War: Psychological and Cognitive Injuries, Their Consequences, and Service to Assist Recovery (Rand Corporation 2008).

E. The “Not So Good Soldier” Defense

Not every servicemember was a model citizen before enlisting. Some come from dysfunctional families or worse circumstances. Many have experienced some form of disciplinary action during their indoctrination into military culture or during deployment. Some will have been court-martialed and dishonorably discharged, or exited service under less than honorable conditions. Some will have post-discharge criminal arrests or convictions before the current case. But none of this means their military service should be abandoned as mitigating evidence for plea or trial without further exploration, just because their service was not that good—or even downright bad.

Indoctrination into military culture takes its toll on recruits from all walks of life, but may take a higher toll on those with preexisting mental health or substance abuse issues. The indoctrination process itself—and particularly combat training—will expose recruits to potentially traumatic events at the same time as they are being conditioned to show no weakness. The veteran may be experiencing symptoms of PTSD or TBI during boot camp as well as active duty, and years before diagnosis. Alcohol abuse is part of military culture and well documented.20 Moral injury may occur long before discharge from service.

Counsel knowledgeable in military culture and the invisible injuries of military service will be able to understand and explain why the “Not So Good Soldier” remains deserving of leniency. For example, in Porter v. McCollum, 558 US 30, 130 S Ct 447, 175 L Ed 2d 398 (2009) (per curiam), the defendant had a history of going absent without leave (AWOL) during combat, which prosecutors urged negated his military service as mitigation. That disparaging fact was explained through the efforts of post-conviction counsel who obtained military records contradicting several of the AWOL incidents and detailing the horrific battles where the defendant did fight. Counsel also presented corroborating testimony from the defendant’s commanding officer. The result was reversal of his death sentence and imposition of a life sentence.

F. Military Service as Mitigating Evidence

As explained in Chapter 1, both houses of the 2013 Oregon Legislative Assembly unanimously approved Senate Bill 124 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2013, chapter 331), which expressly authorizes courts to consider military service as a mitigating factor at sentencing.

Senate Bill 124 did this by amending ORS 137.090 to state in pertinent part, “In determining mitigation, the court may consider evidence regarding the defendant’s status as a servicemember as defined in ORS 135.881.” ORS 137.090(2) (emphasis added). Subsequently, the Criminal Justice Commission added servicemember status to the Felony Sentencing Guidelines, as enumerated Mitigating Factor J. See OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(J). In felony cases courts must make a “substantial and compelling” finding to rely on SB 124 to impose a mitigated (downward) departure. ORS 137.671(1); OAR 213-008-0001.

2023 Update

In Pike v. Cain, 303 Or App 624, 465 P3d 277, rev den, 367 Or 75 (2020), the Court of Appeals relied on Mitigating Factor J (as well as the "good soldier defense," although without explicitly citing it) to hold that at sentencing, trial counsel failed to provide constitutionally adequate and effective legal assistance.

There, the petitioner, Adrian Leslie Pike (now deceased), was an active duty soldier stationed at Joint Base Lewis/McChord in Washington State, but he was off duty and spending time in Oregon. He got into an altercation, which ended with him pleading guilty to second-degree assault. He faced a 70-month "Measure 11" prison sentence. He was eligible for application of the "exit door," see ORS 137.712, which would allow him to be sentenced to a lesser, Guidelines term, including to probation. At the sentencing hearing, trial counsel called petitioner's platoon sergeant who "testified in support of petitioner’s case for probation. The sergeant stated that petitioner was a 'good soldier,' who 'excelled above his peers,' and who, prior to the assault, was up for a promotion to sergeant." 303 Or App at 629 (emphasis added).

Unfortunately, trial counsel otherwise failed to investigate and present evidence of petitioner's "good soldier" service in the military. Relatedly, counsel failed to rely on Mitigating Factor J to seek a mitigated departure from Measure 11 sentencing. The sentencing court denied the defense request for "exit door" treatment, and imposed the 70-month sentence.

Owing to counsel's failures, the petitioner sought post-conviction relief, which the post-conviction court denied. But the Court of Appeals reversed, explaining that it

"conclude[d] that * * * petitioner is entitled to a new sentencing hearing because his trial counsel’s decision not to further develop the evidence of petitioner’s military service, and his failure to argue for a [mitigated] departure under * * * OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(J) based on petitioner’s servicemember status, did not comport with constitutional standards."

Pike, 303 Or App at 627.

The court then "remand[ed] with instructions to grant post-conviction relief on petitioner’s claim that trial counsel was inadequate and ineffective for failing to investigate and develop further evidence pertaining to petitioner’s military service." Id.

Practice Tip: The “substantial and compelling” finding requirements of ORS 137.671(1) and OAR 213-008-0001 do not apply in misdemeanor cases. But to enhance the chances of a court applying the mitigating factor in a misdemeanor case, defense counsel should assert claims designed to meet the substantial and compelling requirements, as they would in felony cases.

Moreover, defense counsel should consult and when appropriate cite case law that supports relying on a defendant’s military service to impose a mitigated sentence. Such case law includes:

  • Kimbrough v. United States, 552 US 85, 110, 128 S Ct 558, 169 L Ed 2d 481 (2007), which affirmed the trial court’s imposition of a sentence more lenient than the guidelines range, which trial court based in part on the fact that the defendant “served in combat during Operation Desert Storm and received an honorable discharge from the Marine Corps.”
  • Porter v. McCollum (discussed above) which holds that trial counsel provided constitutionally deficient assistance by failing to present evidence of the client’s military service during penalty phase of his capital trial.
  • United States v. Canova, 412 F3d 331 (2d Cir 2005), in which the defendant was convicted of offenses involving multimillion-dollar Medicare fraud; the imposition of a downward departure sentence based, in part, on a six-year military service was not error.
  • United States v. Pipich, 688 F Supp 191 (D Md 1988), where defendant was convicted of mail theft, his extraordinary military record warranted departure to probation.

Consistent with the decisions in cases such as Kimbrough, Porter, Canova, and Pipich, defense counsel may also seek to base mitigation on the “good soldier defense” found in the Uniform Code of Military Justice’s Military Rules of Evidence (MRE). Chapter 10 provides detailed information about this defense.

Example: A senior non-commissioned officer (NCO), who is a member of a National Guard unit scheduled for imminent deployment to a combat zone, is facing criminal prosecution. A sentence of incarceration, but not probation, would bar his deployment. The NCO’s superiors attest that his deployment is critical to his unit’s cohesion and performance, and that preventing the NCO’s deployment could cause the unit to fail in its mission—including by suffering additional casualties owing to the substitute deployment of someone who lacks the training and experience, and the unit loyalty, as the NCO. Defense counsel should propose these military community matters, including the good soldier defense, as grounds for imposing a sentence of probation, instead of incarceration. Cf. United States v. Milikowsky, 65 F3d 4, 8 (2d Cir 1995) (“[a]mong the permissible justifications for downward departure . . . is the need, given appropriate circumstances, to reduce the destructive effects that incarceration of a defendant may have on innocent third parties”); United States v. Kloda, 133 F Supp2d 345 (SDNY 2001) (in business tax fraud case, mitigated departure granted in part because of “the needs of [defendant’s] business and employees”).

Under appropriate circumstances, certain of the Felony Sentencing Guidelines’ enumerated factors that preexist Mitigating Factor J may be used. For example, consider a veteran defendant whose ostensibly criminal conduct could be explained by his training and experience in the military culture and total institution (Chapter 5), his suffering from service-connected post-traumatic stress disorder or traumatic brain injury (Chapter 6), and his suffering from moral injury (Chapter 8). For that defendant, the following enumerated factors would apply and would enhance efforts to secure a mitigated sentence:

  • OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(B): “The defendant acted under duress or compulsion (not sufficient as a complete defense).”

Example: As a consequence of using a pocket knife to fend off a large belligerent drunk man who threatened to assault combat veteran James Anthony Harrell, his friend, and their dates, the state brought major felony assault charges against Harrell. As explained in the Brief of Amicus Curiae The Bunker Project, State v. Harrell, No. S059513, [available as Appendix B and here] Harrell’s training and experience in the military culture and total institution compelled him to act in defense of himself and his friends in a way that his jury concluded was criminal. Fortunately for James, he was acquitted of the charges after his post-appeal proceedings.21 But if his conviction had stood, Mitigating Factor B would have applied at his sentencing.

  • OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(C): “The defendant’s mental capacity was diminished (excluding diminished capacity due to voluntary drug or alcohol abuse.)”

Example: Mitigating Factor C would apply to veterans whose criminal behavior is linked to a diminished mental capacity stemming from their affliction with post-traumatic stress disorder, traumatic brain injury, and moral injury. (See Chapters 6 and 8). But Mitigating Factor C also may apply to veterans whose criminal behavior is linked to a diminished mental capacity stemming from their drug or alcohol abuse if it can be established that the abuse was involuntary “self-medication,” to deal with, for example, PTSD. (See Chapter 6.)

  • OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(I): “The offender is amenable to treatment and an appropriate treatment program is available to which the offender can be admitted within a reasonable period of time; the treatment program is likely to be more effective than the presumptive prison term in reducing the risk of offender recidivism; and the probation sentence will serve community safety interests by promoting offender reformation.”

Mitigating Factor I may be the most dynamic of the enumerated factors. It requires defense counsel to construct for their clients individualized sentencing plans which include proposed treatment programs. Strategies on constructing such plans are described below.

Finally, defense counsel should be aware of the possibility of representing defendants who served in the militaries of other nations. Those veteran defendants could be eligible for all of the mitigating factors discussed above except ORS 137.090(2) and Mitigating Factor J. This is because they will not have “[s]erved as a member of the Armed Forces of the United States, the reserve components of the Armed Forces of the United States or the National Guard[.]” ORS 135.881(4)(b)(A).

Nevertheless, the case law discussed above—Kimbrough, Porter, Canova, and Pipich—does not limit its application to veterans of the United States military. Consequently, in cases involving defendants who are veterans of other nations’ militaries, defense counsel may rely on the case law to establish their client’s military service as a non-enumerated mitigating factor.22

IV. Strategies For Persuading the “Decider” to Mitigate the Resolution

When military service and resulting injury to the psyche lead to criminal charges, our government and public at large should acknowledge the role played: we created and maintain the armed forces, we urge our young people to enlist in service of our country and to repeatedly deploy to combat zones, and we depend on them to defend our lives and freedoms in the on-going war against terrorism and other global threats. See Youngjae Lee, “Military Veterans, Culpability, and Blame,” 7 Crim L Phil 285 (2013). Conveying that truth persuasively to the prosecutor, jury or judge, and convincing the “Decider” to do justice is the job of defense counsel. “As criminal defense attorneys, we are all that stands between these protectors and the nation they sacrificed to protect. We are the only ones who can hold the State, and society as a whole, accountable for its role,” Hunter & Else, Defending Veterans at 400.

This message should be delivered subtly. It can be conveyed without direct blame: tell the compelling story of the client’s service and sacrifice on behalf of our country, including the client’s indoctrination into the military culture and total institution and any injuries resulting from that service, followed by the government’s failure to provide a viable reintegration strategy for veterans, as well as its failure to adequately fund programs for necessary services.23 In that way you may enlist the Decider to help bring the veteran truly home.

A. Plea Negotiations: Educating the Prosecutor About Why the Veteran Defendant Deserves Leniency

Simply telling the prosecutor the defendant served in the military and has some mental health and/or substance abuse issues that would benefit from treatment is never enough. Defense counsel must tell the client’s story, as well as educate the prosecutor on the mitigation theory. This is best done through a defense plea proposal letter with supporting documentation. That method also allows counsel to present information to the prosecutor clearly denoted as protected by plea negotiations, rather than reciprocal discovery. The mitigation theory must explain the client’s behavior and clearly link that behavior to his or her military service. To do so convincingly requires counsel to have investigated the veteran’s history—from pre-military, military, and post-military—using all of the means outlined earlier in this chapter, to the extent there is time and resources to do so. The client’s DD-214 and other select military and VA records are necessary exhibits to the plea proposal letter in every case. Interviews of important witnesses to corroborate the veteran’s story should be obtained, depending on the circumstances of the case.

Letters from family, friends, past and present employers, and fellow veterans describing the client’s character and background are relatively easy to obtain in most cases and should be part of the negotiation package. Asking the client to work on a list of such individuals and as much of their contact information (phone, email, etc.) as possible should be done at an early stage of the defense investigation. If there are likely to be only 30 days or so between plea negotiations and a sentencing hearing, consider telling writers to address the letter to the prosecuting attorney; but to streamline the process, suggest they include a line stating, “I understand this letter may be given to the judge who will sentence the defendant.”

Photographs, copies of awards and commendations, and any other records that help tell the client’s story and portray him or her as a real person—not just a “defendant” the prosecutor has only seen in a mug shot or standing next to you at a routine appearance—should also be incorporated. These items should be gathered early in the case investigation as well.

Counsel may or may not need to retain expert assistance and evaluations to support the mitigation theory. In the absence of a retained expert’s report—or to corroborate a retained expert’s opinion—counsel should provide the prosecutor with copies of at least several articles and excerpts of studies that address invisible injuries common to the defendant and their established link to military culture and service. See, e.g., Brown, From War Zones To Jail, supra; Brockton D. Hunter, Echoes of War: The Combat Veteran in Criminal Court (evidencing the link between military service, PTSD, and resulting criminal behavior); Invisible Wounds of War (Rand Corp. 2008).

The plea proposal letter should include a specific treatment plan identifying available resources in the community or accessible through the VA outside the community that will serve to rehabilitate the client. Moreover, the proposal could recommend diversion from prosecution under authority of ORS 135.881, et seq., or entry into a veterans treatment court if one is available.24

If the client is not already in treatment or on a waiting list to start treatment, counsel should address how this will be accomplished and approximately how long it will take for treatment to commence. The VJOP specialist is an invaluable resource for obtaining that type of information. Sometimes other components of a non-incarcerative sentence—such as housing, employment, and transportation—must also be addressed.

Particularly in cases involving violent crimes, the prosecutor will have concerns about public safety if the defendant is not incarcerated, while the defense will be urging diversion or probation with intensive treatment. If the veteran suffers from a mental illness such as PTSD, a persuasive argument may be made that, as explained previously in this chapter, prison exacerbates mental illness and Oregon prisons offer no therapy for PTSD. The veteran is already trained to kill without hesitation. “PTSD is commonly associated with substance abuse, unregulated anger, aggressive behavior, and hazardous use of alcohol, all of which are themselves associated with legal problems,” i.e., criminal charges.25 The result of incarceration is thus likely to produce an individual even more damaged and dangerous to public safety. See also Hunter and Else, Defending Veterans at 462.

B. Trial Defenses: Educating the Jury About Why the Veteran Defendant Deserves to Win

As in any case, the first step towards persuading a jury is for counsel to become fully informed. Conducting a complete defense investigation, as outlined earlier in this chapter and in Chapter 3, is always essential if the case is likely headed for trial. Consequently, these are the appropriate steps for defense counsel to take are:

  • Retain a military-savvy investigator if possible, or one who is willing learn about veteran defense.
  • Obtain all potentially relevant records, which may include non-military related medical, mental health, education, pretrial detention, and criminal records.
  • Identify, locate, and interview all potentially viable witnesses.
  • Retain necessary forensic experts if your client suffers from any invisible injuries.
  • Conduct further legal and forensic research on case-specific theories of defense.26

Counsel should, of course, keep an open mind in deciding on a theory of defense for trial. Initial theories must be subject to modification or abandonment as the defense investigation progresses and the facts available for legal analysis increase, present internal conflicts or contradict initial assumptions. A defense expert in military culture and invisible injuries can provide a wealth of information to assist counsel in deciding on the defense theory to be pursued at trial. But in order to work effectively with the expert, as well as to present expert testimony persuasively at trial, counsel must become conversant in the subject matter of the expert’s testimony. Do not underestimate the time required to do that, particularly if working with a specialized subject matter for the first time. Expect to experience a learning curve that will extend far beyond the first few hours of reading literature in the specialized fields of military culture and veterans with PTSD or other invisible injuries.

Engaging the client to become part of the defense team in case preparation must not be overlooked, and is perhaps more vital to a successful defense when dealing with a veteran versus a non-veteran client. Trust issues, as discussed previously, do arise from the military culture’s disdain of civilians, further complicated by the symptoms of invisible injuries—anxiety, depression, isolation, etc.

To successfully educate the Decider, counsel must have earned the trust of the veteran client. Only if the veteran is willing to discuss with members of the defense team the traumatic experiences that injured his mental health will counsel be able to present the full picture to the jury. The veteran may also need to testify, particularly if the case involves self-defense.

Even if counsel happens to be a veteran, there will likely be non-veteran members of the defense team, including defense experts, whom the client must be willing to trust. A suggested approach is to speak with the client early on about the ground rules of representation. Those include (1) all defense team members making no promise to the client, no matter how small, that will not be kept if humanly possible, (2) keeping the client “in the loop” and asking the client to commit to assist and participate when called upon in order for the team to achieve the best result, and (3) telling the client that there are no “dumb questions” if the client has concerns or anything he may not understand.

C. Common Defense Theories

To begin, the reader is referred to Chapter 3, Defenses, of The Trial Notebook (OCDLA 2013) for practice skills and case law on conducting the defense at trial.27 The primary defenses utilizing veteran-specific mitigating evidence are summarized below.

1. The Guilty Except Insane (GEI) Defense

This affirmative defense negates criminal liability but can result in significant restraints on liberty through the Psychiatric Security Review Board (PSRB). ORS 161.295 provides “a person is guilty except for insanity if, as a result of mental disease or defect at the time of engaging in criminal conduct, the person lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality of the conduct or to conform the conduct to the requirements of law.”

To utilize this defense, the veteran must have PTSD, TBI, or another qualifying mental disease or defect. Although moral injury is not a mental disease or defect, it is associated with depression, which is. Insanity defenses based on veterans suffering from PTSD occasionally have been used successfully since the Vietnam War.28 Indeed, success with this defense is even more likely now, given that surveys show a “positive juror bias toward veterans with PTSD [that] represent a strong indicator of community support for this group,” Hunter & Else, Defending Veterans at 411.29

Most but not all successful service-related PTSD insanity defenses nationwide have involved dissociative flashbacks to combat experiences where violence was the conditioned response for survival. Those cases relied on the defendant’s inability to appreciate the wrongful nature of his conduct under those circumstances. It is also possible to contend the psychological conditioning to immediately eliminate the threat rendered the defendant unable to control his conduct. Defending Veterans at 414. In State v. Jessie Bratcher, Grant County Case No. 08-08-219CR, Oregon attorney Markku Sario obtained a GEI verdict for Bratcher, an Iraq war veteran, who killed the man accused of raping his girlfriend, by showing the jury his client had been trained to kill without hesitation and had not been deprogrammed.30 Bratcher was released from the state hospital in 2014 after the PSRB determined he no longer had a mental illness. Bratcher’s 2009 trial was the first in Oregon that successfully used this defense for an Iraq war veteran with service-related PTSD.31

PTSD may also explain nonviolent criminal conduct or acts of violence done recklessly. For example, the disorder may cause the veteran to engage in risky behavior or to act out of guilt. Hunter & Else, Defending Veterans at 419. Veterans with PTSD may seek out activities that offer a level of danger similar to their combat experiences as part of a compulsive desire for re-exposure to trauma. That theory was used successfully in the insanity defense of a veteran charged with drug trafficking, who viewed the smuggling trip aboard a boat from Morocco to the United States as “just another combat mission.”32

2. Self-Defense

This defense negates criminal liability and results in acquittal. It is not an affirmative defense, but written notice must be given in advance of trial if the defendant wants to rely on evidence presented during the state’s case. Once raised, self-defense becomes an element the state must disprove beyond a reasonable doubt.

ORS 161.209 justifies the use of physical force to defend against what the defendant “reasonably believes to be the use or imminent use of unlawful physical force,” and further provides that the defendant may use “a degree of force which the [defendant] reasonably believes to be necessary.” Thus, Oregon uses a subjective test to determine the reasonable use of force. See State v. Oliphant, 347 Or 175, 191, 218 P3d 1281 (2009) (the self-defense statute’s focus on the reasonable belief of “the person” who used physical force “establishes that, in general, a person’s right to use force in self-defense depends on the person’s own reasonable belief in the necessity for such action” (court’s emphasis)). Because of that, defense counsel may use an invisible injury like PTSD, the military conditioned response to eliminate the threat or both to argue that the veteran subjectively, reasonably believed that the degree of force used was necessary. See Hafemeister & Stockey at 126 n 15 (discussing veteran self-defense cases from other jurisdictions).33

3. The Disordered Mental State Defense

This is not an affirmative defense; rather, it is a statutorily prescribed method to raise reasonable doubt that the defendant lacked the mental state element of any given crime. ORS 161.300 makes admissible “evidence that the actor suffered from a mental disease or defect . . . whenever it is relevant to the issue of whether the actor did or did not have the intent which is an element of the crime.” Courts have construed the statute to apply to crimes based on knowing or reckless conduct, not solely intentional conduct. State v. Nebert, 244 Or App 80, 260 P3d 559 (2011); State v. Smith, 154 Or App 37, 960 P2d 877 (1998). ORS 161.309(2) refers to this defense using the terms “partial responsibility or diminished capacity,” and requires written notice in advance of trial if the defendant intends to offer expert testimony.

In Mental Health and Criminal Defense, Bassos writes authoritatively about this defense, suggesting it could be used whenever the defendant suffers “from a chaotic, disordered mind beset with . . .PTSD terror, cognitive impairment or any number of disorders” that are prevalent with veteran defendants. He advises that this defense allows presentation of historical information regarding how the client suffered the traumas that resulted in the disordered mental state, and it may be combined with an involuntary intoxication defense. Note also that OEC 404(4) authorizes the admission by either party of a defendant’s prior specific acts to prove character and propensity to act accordingly. Thus the defense may show the veteran’s specific good acts of sacrifice and protection of others while in service, to contrast with the criminal conduct, and argue that as further evidence of his disordered mental state.

If the veteran is adamant about not raising a GEI defense, much the same case can be presented—with or without defense expert testimony, and definitely without giving the state a right to examine the defendant—through a disordered mental state defense. That type of defense was tried in an aggravated-murder prosecution of a veteran defendant. State v. Supanchick, 245 Or App 651, 263 P3d 378 (2011), aff’d, 354 Or 737, 323 P3d 231 (2014). There, the jury convicted on all counts but acquitted the defendant of the death penalty.

4. Automatism or Non-Volitional Conduct Defense

This defense is based on the criminal law’s requirement of a voluntary act in that an individual who commits a crime when asleep or otherwise unconscious cannot be said to have acted voluntarily. See State v. Newman, 353 Or 632, 302 P3d 435 (2013) (“sleep driving” is an involuntary act). See also ORS 161.085(2); ORS 161.095(1). It is not an affirmative defense and if proven negates guilt. It is theoretically available to a veteran who committed a crime during a dissociative flashback and their conduct was a conditioned response from military culture.34

Traumatic brain injury has been linked to sleep disorders which during dreams can produce violent conduct that could injure a bed partner; PTSD nightmares have been known to result in violent acts during sleepwalking. Hunter & Else, Defending Veterans at 423-24. Editor Jesse Wm. Barton has blogged about the need for defense counsel to explore the actus reus line of defense for veteran defendants with PTSD and TBI, citing professor Melissa Hamilton’s article, “Reinvigorating Actus Reus: The Case for Involuntary Actions by Veterans with Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder,” 16 Berkeley J Crim L 340 (2011).

5. Partial Defenses that Mitigate Culpability

If less than perfect or persuasive enough to obtain a verdict of acquittal, any of the defenses discussed above may result in conviction on lesser-included offenses. For example, when the mental state required for conviction of the charged crime is intentional, military conditioned responses, invisible injuries, or both may result in the jury finding the veteran acted with a less-culpable mental state.

Presenting evidence of the veteran’s service and resulting invisible injuries to explain the allegedly criminal behavior is not only likely to have a mitigating impact on jurors but also on the judge, should a complete acquittal not be obtained. Furthermore, a veteran-specific defense could also be used in plea negotiations as a reason for the prosecution not to gamble on the outcome at a trial. Furthermore, with respect to any defense or partial defense, counsel should be alert to the documented overuse of antidepressants and psychotropic medications to treat veterans, and the documented impact of those medications causing individuals (including non-veterans) to engage in high-risk behaviors. Hunter & Else, Defending Veterans, supra at 420.

D. Practice Pointers for a Jury Trial

Below are the insights of Randy Vogt, who served as co-counsel in State v. Supanchick, for conducting a jury trial involving a veteran with PTSD, using a hypothetical veteran defendant client named Tom—

Announce and develop a theme. Refer to it at all stages of the trial. The basic theme should be, “If it weren’t for Tom’s service in the war, this incident would not have happened the way it did.”

Voir Dire

Include questions that track the basic theme: war, PTSD, and changes observed in Tom’s character.

  • Has anyone here ever gone to war?
  • Did your experiences in the XYZ war change your outlook on life?
  • Does anyone here know another person who was changed by war?
  • Tell us about what happened to that person.
  • Generally speaking, is war good for a soldier or not good for a soldier?
  • Do you know any veterans who have had problems adapting to civilian life?
  • Why do you think veterans have problems when they come home?
  • If you agree that war changes a soldier’s personality and character, do you agree that this is a legitimate issue in a case such as this?
  • Is PTSD real or just a media fabrication?
  • Tell us why you believe this.

Opening Statement

Tell the jury that your theory of the case is supported by experts as well as by common sense, and tell them what the experts will say.

The Defense Case

The reason Tom did what he did is that he has PTSD, which was caused by war.

1. One problem the attorney will have is proving that the veteran suffered the trauma that caused the PTSD. The lawyer should consider setting an omnibus hearing and ask the court to allow telephonic testimony and testimony of soldiers and veterans via audio or video depositions under the Rules of Civil Procedure. This is often the only way to get this kind of testimony before the jury because servicemembers are often stationed at locations remote from Oregon. But servicemembers are very inclined to support each other and often make excellent witnesses.

2. Should the veteran defendant testify? Base your advice to him on traditional criminal defense factors, but remember that the jury wants to know what he is like and to see whether he comes across as dangerous or visibly mentally troubled.

3. Defense experts should be used to do the following:

a. Experts can explain to the jury that Tom led a nonviolent life before he went to war, and the reason he did what he did is that he had a PTSD regression. For example: “When Bubba pulled the knife out of his jacket, Tom suddenly reverted back to his training and war experience. In his mind, for an instant, he was back in Iraq.” Testimony from Tom’s friends and family should be given to corroborate expert opinion.

b. Sociologists as well as psychologists can present admissible testimony to show that war impacts a great number of veterans, as shown by statistics, and Tom is consistent with that trend in society.

Closing argument

1. Stress the fact that Tom came by his mental health problems honestly—by serving his country, and by serving all of us in this room.

2. Stress the virtue and valor of patriotism, for example: “When his country needed volunteers, Tom was there. When his country needed combat medics, Tom was there. When his country needed soldiers who could make an amphibious landing under enemy fire, Tom was there. When his country needed a soldier who could fight the enemy hand-to-hand, Tom was there. I am asking you the jury to be there for Tom, because he’s a veteran, and now he needs you.”

E. Sentencing Hearings: Giving the Court What it Needs to Be Lenient

If counsel prepared a plea negotiation mitigation package for the prosecutor, that will provide the foundation for a sentencing memorandum and supporting exhibits to the court. Such a presentation is vitally necessary if the parties have not reached a joint recommendation as to the sentence. The sentencing memorandum should contain citations to as much case law and other authorities as counsel can find to support the arguments in favor of leniency. There is a dearth of Oregon case law regarding mitigating grounds for downward departures under the felony guidelines, and none that construes the most recent departure ground based on military service. Thus, counsel will need to look to other jurisdictions. Federal jurisprudence has fairly abundant case law on mitigating factors, including some involving military service. See Barton, ed., Felony Sentencing in Oregon § 7-4.2.10 (December 2016 update).

The supporting exhibits will generally be the same as those provided to the prosecutor during plea negotiations. However, counsel should consider including copies of authoritative articles from law and forensic journals, as well as anecdotal evidence from news articles on the problems common to veterans who go from war zones to jail. Of course, counsel may decide to present expert or other testimony, including “good soldier” witness testimony, depending on the case and available resources.

What follows are topical excerpts from a sentencing memorandum in a contested homicide case where the state sought a presumptive prison sentence and the defense sought a mitigated departure. These excerpts provide legal authorities as well as sample arguments on various grounds for departure—some enumerated in the guidelines and others not. This case was successfully resolved without retaining defense experts, given the availability of VA records and the client’s financial resources. An accompanying motion for downward departure is procedurally required pursuant to ORS 137.671 and OAR 213-008-0001.

1. The Court’s Authority to Depart

ORS 137.671 and OAR 213-008-0001 require the court to impose the presumptive sentence unless it finds substantial and compelling reasons to impose a departure. If the court departs, it must state those reasons on the record at the time of sentencing. Those reasons should support a finding that the presumptive sentence would not accomplish the purposes of sentencing as to the individual defendant. See generally State v. Wilson, 111 Or App 147, 149-151, 826 P2d 1010 (1992).

The Oregon Constitution as well as the guidelines address the purposes of sentencing. Article I, section 15, of the Oregon Constitution provides, “Laws for the punishment of crime shall be founded on these principles: protection of society, personal responsibility, and accountability for one’s actions and reformation.” The Felony Sentencing Guidelines’ “Statement of Purposes and Principles” similarly provides: “The primary objectives of sentencing are to punish each offender appropriately, and to insure the security of the people in person and property, within the limits of correctional resources[.]” OAR 213-002-0001(1). This rule also identifies “prevention of recidivism and reformation of the offender” as “basic principles which underlie these guidelines[.]” OAR 213-002-0001(3)(a). Thus the purposes of sentencing under the Oregon Constitution and the guidelines may be summarized as public safety, punishment appropriate to the individual offender based on personal responsibility and accountability, and offender reformation, all with due consideration of limited correctional resources.

Under the guidelines, the presumptive sentence is the maximum sentence the court may impose absent substantial and compelling reasons for upward departure. See State v. Woodin, 131 Or App 171, 175-76, 883 P2d 1332 (1994).

The decision to depart, based on valid departure factors, is discretionary with the court. See Wilson, 111 Or App at 149 (appellate court reviews the sentencing court’s factual basis for departing, not its decision whether to depart). However, valid departures are a two-step process: the reviewing court determines whether (i) the facts support the departure factors and (ii) substantially and compellingly justify the departure. See State v. Upton, 339 Or 673, 681, 125 P3d 713 (2005).

2. Procedural Requirements for Imposing Downward Departures

The evidence code does not apply in sentencing hearings. OEC 101(4)(d). However, ORS 137.090 provides in relevant part:

(1) In determining aggravation or mitigation, the court shall consider:

(a) Any evidence received during the proceeding;

(b) The presentence report, where one is available; and

(c) Any other evidence relevant to aggravation or mitigation that the court finds trustworthy and reliable.

V. Sentencing Memorandum

The remainder of this chapter is presented as a sentencing memorandum for a hypothetical veteran defendant named “Mr. Smith” who is charged with a homicide crime.

The defense has provided written documentation to establish the factual basis for a downward departure and requests that the court receive those documents as evidence for purposes of sentencing. The court may also rely on the written or oral statements of counsel in making its sentencing decision, if it finds the statements to be reliable. State v. Waage, 160 Or App 156, 165, 981 P2d 333 (1999).

OAR 213-008-0002 sets out a “non-exclusive list of mitigating and aggravating factors that may be considered in determining whether substantial and compelling reasons for a departure exist.” In the case at bar, the defense has given notice that five of the enumerated mitigating factors apply, along with five factors not contained in the non-exclusive list of OAR 213-008-0002. A court needs only one valid factor to depart. State v. Wilson, 111 Or App at 151 n 4, 826 P2d 1010 (1992). See also State v. Hays, 155 Or App 41, 49-51, 964 P2d 1042, rev den, 328 Or 40 (1998) (court relied on two factors, finding either one of which would support the downward departure).

Mitigating factors do not affect a defendant’s due process and jury trial rights. Therefore, the jury trial and pleading requirements required for aggravating factors are inapplicable to mitigation.

DEFENDANT’S MITIGATING FACTORS

The mitigating factors are discussed below, in the order that most coherently presents the underlying facts. [Note: Where “Appendix” is mentioned, be aware that this does not refer to this publication but is left in as an indicator for attorneys to know where and sometimes what records are needed to support the arguments.]

A. Defendant’s Pre-Sentence Rehabilitative Efforts

Since his release, Mr. Smith has accessed VA services for medications and counseling to help manage his chronic PTSD, which has a clear nexus to his history of alcohol abuse, as discussed in detail later. In recent months he has sought and been approved for intensive inpatient treatment for PTSD and related alcohol abuse. His pre-sentence conduct is further evidence that he is a good candidate for probation. Oregon law identifies “prevention of recidivism and reformation of the offender” as “basic principles which underlie these guidelines,” OAR 213-002-0001(3)(a). Thus pre-sentence rehabilitation that demonstrates pro-active reformation of the defendant is an important consideration in mitigation of sentence. See United States v. Thompson, 315 F3d 1071, 1077 (9th Cir 2002) (Burzon, J., c oncurring) (“[p]ost-offense rehabilitation—as distinguished from post-sentencing rehabilitation—can be a basis for downward departure.”).35

B. Defendant’s Extreme Remorse

Hays, 155 Or App at 50 n 4, notes that a defendant’s remorse may be a mitigating factor that would support downward departure in a proper case. Cf. State v. Reid, 140 Or App 293, 299, 915 P2d 453 (1996) (“[w]e agree that a defendant’s empathy—or lack thereof—is appropriate to a court’s consideration of what sentence should be imposed”). Appendix documents confirm that Mr. Smith is extremely remorseful.

C. Collateral Consequences of Conviction: Increased Severity of PTSD Symptoms Related to Criminal Prosecution

Mr. Smith receives disability benefits for combat-related PTSD. Post-offense, Mr. Smith’s chronic PTSD has been exacerbated to severe levels. He regularly experiences nightmares, flashbacks, irritability, impaired concentration, depression and feelings of failure, guilt and hopelessness. See Appendix (VJOP Intake Records). The exacerbation of Mr. Smith’s combat-related PTSD is unsurprising. “The act of killing another human being, even under circumstances that render the homicide a criminal offense, carries a high risk that the perpetrator will experience a severe case of PTSD.” J. Vincent Aprile II, PTSD: When the Crime Punishes the Perpetrator, 23 Crim Just 39 (2009). To engage in his defense, Mr. Smith is required to repeatedly recall and discuss this traumatic event, all of which trigger him to experience stress, anxiety, and the other symptoms of PTSD. “[T]he accused is forced throughout the pretrial and trial stages of a criminal prosecution to relive . . . the traumatic experience of the crime,” which has “the potential to stimulate and aggravate the accused’s PTSD.” Id. at 40.

Mr. Smith will continue to suffer daily from the debilitating symptoms of PTSD without intensive treatment that is unavailable in prison. See generally United States v. Clough, 360 F3d 967 (9th Cir. 2004) (court may impose mitigated departure where defendant was shot by police during arrest because his significant injuries constitute a continuing form of punishment). Indeed, the Oregon Department of Corrections has opposed legislation to provide PTSD therapy to inmates, claiming it would be cost prohibitive and counter-productive. Testimony, Senate Committee on Veterans and Emergency Preparedness, SB 780, Apr. 14, 2015 (statement of Heidi Steward, assistant director, Offender Management & Rehabilitation Division, Dept. of Corrections).

D. Defendant’s Prior Good Behavior

The guidelines recognize a downward departure may be warranted for a defendant who has generally behaved as a law-abiding citizen. OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(H) (defendant has lived conviction-free within the community for a significant period of time preceding the current crime of conviction). A mitigated departure on that ground is supported when incarceration is not necessary to protect the public, particularly given the need to consider limited correctional resources. “[T]he guidelines must conform corrections practices to available resources.” Guidelines Implementation Manual at 6 (Official Commentary). In the current biennium, the annual average per-inmate cost of imprisonment is $43,678. Jesse Wm. Barton, ed., Felony Sentencing in Oregon: Guidelines, Statutes, Cases at 1-65 (OCDLA Dec. 2016 Update ). Meanwhile, Oregon faces a $1.7 billion shortfall in the state’s budget for the 2017–19 biennium.

The state’s criminal history worksheet shows that Mr. Smith has a single misdemeanor in his distant past. Mr. Smith’s case for applying Mitigating Factor H is compelling because not only is his criminal history minimal and many years past, but he has been a valued member of his community for many years. See Hays, 155 Or App at 50-51 (explaining that Mitigating Factor H was applicable to the defendant, who had no criminal history, because his “criminal history is far superior to that of a petty shoplifter” who has racked up multiple Class B and C misdemeanors).

Many individuals from all walks of life who know Mr. Smith have written letters that attest to his good character. Collectively, these letters portray an honorable man who has contributed to the well-being of others in the community after leaving military service and who is the primary care giver for his son. He has led a life that demonstrates imprisonment is not necessary for public safety.

E. Defendant’s Status as a Servicemember

Mr. Smith joined the service at age 20 and engaged in combat. His DD-214, in the Appendix, shows that he received a General (Under Honorable Conditions) Discharge. This qualifies him as a “servicemember” as defined in ORS 135.881(4)(b)(B). During his military service he received a Good Conduct Medal, Letter of Appreciation, National Defense Service Medal, Combat Action Ribbon, Meritorious Unit Commendations, and other awards.

Oregon law recognizes that military service may serve as a mitigating factor if, as does Mr. Smith, the defendant qualifies as a servicemember. See ORS 137.090(2) (“[i]n determining mitigation, the court may consider evidence regarding the defendant’s status as a servicemember as defined in ORS 135.881”); OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(J) (enumerating military service as a mitigating factor).

Special treatment is appropriate for veteran defendants given their sacrifices for our country. This is particularly true given the exceedingly small number of American citizens who volunteer for active duty. For example, “[l]ess than 0.5 percent of the [American] population serves in the armed forces, compared with more than 12 percent during World War II.” Karl W. Eikenberry & David M. Kennedy, “Americans & Their Military, Drifting Apart,” New York Times, May 26, 2013.

Military service as a mitigating factor is particularly appropriate when the defendant suffers from a service-connected mental condition:

Our Nation has a long tradition of according leniency to veterans in recognition of their service, especially for those who fought on the front lines as [petitioner] did. Moreover, the relevance of [petitioner’s] extensive combat experience is not only that he served honorably under extreme hardship and gruesome conditions, but also that the jury might find mitigating the intense stress and mental and emotional toll that combat took on [petitioner].

Porter v. McCollum, 558 US 30, 550-51 130 S Ct 447, 175 L Ed 2d 398 (2009) (per curiam) (internal quotations and footnote omitted).

Legislative history to ORS 137.090(2) evinces a legislative intent to provide judges with a clear mechanism to help veterans suffering from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or Traumatic Brain Injury who “find themselves in the criminal justice system, convicted of a crime and facing incarceration, when a more appropriate sentence would be probation with a treatment regimen.” See Appendix (Oregon State Bar, Legislative Proposal). The legislation that created the mitigating factor passed unanimously.

F. Mr. Smith’s Mental Capacity Was Diminished at All Relevant Times Related to the Offense

As noted above, Mr. Smith suffers from service-connected PTSD and, prior to the homicide, had a 50 percent disability rating based on this disorder. PTSD is a stress and anxiety condition that results from exposure to an overwhelming traumatic event combined with feelings of utter helplessness. With this disorder the traumatic event “distressingly recurs” in various manifestations leading to impairment lasting more than a month. Anxiety is most apparent in the “chronic feeling of dread, apprehension, and hypervigilance” experienced by victims of PTSD. American Psychiatric Ass’n, Diagnostic & Statistical Manual Of Mental Disorders (DSM-IV). In general, between 15% and 40% of combat veterans develop PTSD. True PTSD figures are expected to be higher than the current estimates because many combat veterans intentionally mask their symptoms, live in denial, or remain unaware of their symptoms until long after experiencing the traumatic event. See Evan R. Seamone, “Attorneys As First-Responders: Recognizing the Destructive Nature of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder on the Combat Veteran’s Legal Decision-Making Process,” 202 Military Law Review 144, 153 (2009).

This disorder and the symptoms Mr. Smith experiences—sleep deprivation from recurring nightmares, impaired concentration, depression, and anxiety—necessarily result in impaired judgment and diminished mental capacity. OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(C) authorizes a departure when a defendant’s mental capacity was diminished by other than voluntary drug or alcohol abuse. In contrast, Mr. Smith’s mental capacity was further diminished on the day of the incident by consuming alcohol to excess. It is well established that alcohol abuse is a symptom of PTSD, see, e.g., William B. Brown, “From War Zones to Jail: Veteran Reintegration Problems,” 8 Justice Policy Journal, Spring 2011, at 2 (Abstract), and that Mr. Smith had a long history of self-medicating with alcohol. See Appendix (VJOP Intake Records). Thus, alcohol abuse is a component of his mental disorder and in that sense was involuntary, making Mitigating Factor C applicable.

G. Mr. Smith’s Military Service & Resultant PTSD Aggravated His Alcohol Dependency, Which Contributed to His Criminal Conduct

Researchers have documented the relationship between military service and such conditions as PTSD, anxiety and depressive disorders, and substance abuse. Inst. of Medicine of the Nat’l Acad., Gulf War & Health, vol. 6: Physiologic, Psychologic, & Psychosocial Effects Of Deployment-Related Stress 2 (2008). A study of nearly 3,000 Iraq War veterans found killing in combat “a significant predictor of PTSD symptoms and alcohol abuse . . . suggesting that taking a life in combat is a potent ingredient in the development of mental health difficulties.” Shira Maguen, et al., “The Impact of Reported Direct and Indirect Killing on Mental Health Symptoms in Iraq War Veterans,” 23 J of Traumatic Stress 86, 89 (Feb. 2010). The authors note that killing “was also a significant predictor of . . . anger and relationship difficulties.” Id. Another study suggests that service personnel with “higher levels of exposure to violent combat, who had killed another person,” were more likely to engage in risky behaviors, which may include excessive alcohol use, use of illicit drugs, and aggressive driving. William D.S. Killgore, et al., “Post-Combat Invincibility: Violent Combat Experiences Are Associated with Increased Risk-Taking Propensity Following Deployment,” 42 J of Psychiatric Research 1112 (Oct. 2008).

None of this is to say Mr. Smith has a good excuse for having drunk to excess on the day of the crime; rather, it serves to explain his conduct is related to two enumerated mitigating factors: military service and diminished capacity. Those factors tend to mitigate offender culpability and support a reasoned grant of leniency. See also State v. Esparza, 367 NW 2d 619 (Minn App 1985) (non-enumerated factors that tend to mitigate offender culpability provide a basis for downward departure).36

H. Mr. Smith Is Amenable to Treatment Which Is Readily Available & Likely to Be More Effective Than the Presumptive Prison Sentence in Reducing Recidivism & Protecting the Public

In recent months Mr. Smith has engaged with the local Veterans Justice Outreach Program to begin supportive therapy. He could continue to obtain further intensive services available through the VA, but only if he is not imprisoned.

Moreover, the “Good News” email in the Appendix shows that he has been accepted into the Substance Abuse Residential Rehabilitative Treatment Program. As explained by the VJOP specialist, successful completion of that 30-day program is a prerequisite for entering an inpatient VA treatment program for PTSD, which would last six to 10 weeks. Mr. Smith is also approved for that program. Clients are expected to enter the PTSD program within a few days of completing the substance abuse program. Although Mr. Smith has abstained from alcohol while on pretrial release, the VA evaluators concluded that he—like other clients who have a history of self-medication for PTSD—needs to be equipped with the tools for abstinence taught during the inpatient substance abuse program. This is because the PTSD program is so emotionally difficult to complete that veterans are prone to relapse and to dropping out of the program.

Counsel will work with the VA treatment center administrator once a sentencing date is set to secure a bed for Mr. Smith shortly after sentencing should the court exercise its discretion to grant the downward dispositional departure. This compares to State v. Waage, in which the Court of Appeals approved a downward dispositional departure to probation for a child sex offender to receive rehabilitative treatment with far less assurance of the defendant receiving treatment.

When left untreated, PTSD can lead veterans to behave irresponsibly, impulsively, violently, and self-destructively, which has created significant concern for their own well-being and the well-being of others. Seamone, 202 Military Law Review at 153. Thus, affording Mr. Smith this necessary and available treatment now, when he is most receptive to treatment, is likely to be more effective that the presumptive prison term in reducing recidivism and protecting the public.

I. Mr. Smith Cooperated With the State to Resolve This Case

Mr. Smith has accepted criminal responsibility for his conduct, demonstrated remorse, and will plead guilty. This suffices to establish an enumerated mitigating factor, OAR 213-008-002(1)(a)(F) (defendant cooperated with the state with respect to the current crime of conviction). See Hays, 155 Or App at 50 (affirming mitigated departure where defendant cooperated with authorities investigating his own culpability, and later stood trial).

J. Mr. Smith’s Family Will Experience Substantial Hardships If the Presumptive Sentence Is Imposed

Mr. Smith has a 50 percent VA disability rating, and those benefits help pay the mortgage on the family’s home. Those benefits will be suspended if he is imprisoned for more than 60 days, and not reinstated until his release.37 According to the VJOP specialist, he could continue to receive benefits if he were sentenced to jail and given alternatives, such as residing at the Community Corrections Center. Courts have approved downward departures in cases where incarceration would have a destructive impact on innocent family members. See, e.g., United States v. Galante, 111 F3d 1029 (2d Cir. 1997) (affirming departure when defendant was a caring father of two sons who would have faced severe financial hardships from his extended incarceration). See also United States v. Aguirre, 214 F3d 1122 (9th Cir. 2000).
