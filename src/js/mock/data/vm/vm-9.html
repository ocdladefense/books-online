Chapter 9. Unique Considerations in the Representation of Female Veterans

By Lori Daniels, PhD, LCSW,* & Emily De La Garza*

Introduction

A woman’s attitude toward her time in military service is often a mixture of pride and betrayal. Nearly all women veterans will admit with pride that their desire to join the military was to be part of a greater good, to learn a trade, to become a better person, to obtain higher education, and/or to serve their country. Often that is exactly what happened—women served in a wide variety of positions within all branches of the military and across many years and decades.

However, as will be seen, many women veterans also encounter disappointment and betrayal. Within the context of military service, whether serving in a warzone or stateside, incidents occur that change the course of a veteran’s motivation—incidents which may negatively impact her overall assessment of herself as a servicemember and, eventually, as a veteran.

Given that, there are three common situations women veterans face about which defense attorneys should be aware: 1) women veterans often hesitate to identify themselves as veterans unless prompted, 2) in most circumstances, a female defendant will not be initially asked if she ever served in the military, and 3) if a woman is identified as a veteran, her military background is rarely fully assessed by anyone representing her. As will be discussed throughout this chapter, lacking information about military history can be a problematic omission to a female veteran’s case; conversely, awareness of a veteran’s experiences assists with the development of a viable defense.

The first two situations listed above are based in a longstanding—and historically-based—struggle for women who have served in the military. The third situation may be explained by non-veteran attorneys (as well as health-care practitioners) hesitating to know more, or feeling uncomfortable delving into a military history when they personally do not have a military background.

The authors of this chapter therefore encourage non-veterans and veterans alike who serve as defense attorneys and on the defense team to proactively assess for past military history when beginning to work with female defendants in order to, at the very least, account for a wide array of experiences (positive and negative) of being a female servicemember. One’s military background is rarely insignificant given the intensity and level of commitment with which veterans have served. An attorney’s knowledge of a client’s military background—whether male or female—avails a more appropriate formulation of their client’s defense argument. It also provides a more accurate context to understand choices made by clients well after they’ve been discharged from active-duty service. In other words, a defendant’s military experiences could be a meaningful factor contributing to a legal case, and this is certainly true when the veteran defendant is a woman.

The unique issues of which military and veteran women have been dealing with are not unfounded. Women have traditionally been considered “secondary” servicemembers during their time in the military or when receiving services from federal agencies. Many veteran-centric service agencies are decorated with all-male photos or sentiments, often failing to recognize the importance of the roles women have served throughout the history of the armed services. Female veterans will often share numerous anecdotes about being asked if they were a spouse of a veteran, as opposed to being herself a veteran. These are but a few examples of scenarios within both military and veteran-centric institutions that contribute to a continued feeling of displacement for women veterans.

This chapter will only briefly introduce and review various issues involving female veteran clients. The goal of this chapter is to briefly explain some of the issues to guide defense attorneys toward proactive assessment of a client’s military service. Our hope is that legal representatives will consider past experiences, as well as a woman’s military background, as a contributing variable when arguing a client’s legal case. This chapter provides a brief history of women in the military, lists demographic and population healthcare access data of women veterans in Oregon, summarizes numerous challenges faced by women while on active duty (especially military sexual harassment and assault), provides national and Oregon state data for sexual harassment and assault, and shares information for both state and federal resources.

It is our belief that providing more context to any story lends perspective to how incidents from one’s past military life, as well as one’s current life, are influential. Thus, what we’ve compiled will illustrate a larger context for defense representatives to consider while they approach their work with veteran clients who are women.

I. A Brief History of Women in the Military

Women have served in the armed services since before the American Revolution, but their service is more accurately depicted as “serving with, not in the armed forces” (Devilbliss, 1990). Devilbliss provides a thorough historical description of women serving in the military, summarized below.

In the first few centuries leading to and beyond the nation’s establishment, women were not allowed to hold rank, were provided no benefits for serving, and were either not paid or not paid at the same level as their male counterparts. This differential treatment was in spite of the fact that women fought on the frontlines and played vital roles, in various occupations, during each battle and throughout each war in U.S. history.

When women served during wartime, their status was strictly alongside the military and they were promptly discharged after the war. It was not until 1942 that the armed forces gave women full military status.

Specific policies encouraging more women to join the military—addressing such things as the types of jobs women could work, training, and family planning during overseas deployments—did not emerge until 1973. In 1994, the Direct Ground Combat Definition and Assignment Rule was put in place to restrict women from performing in combat-related occupations and special operations.

During the post-9/11 era, military women’s roles have expanded immensely. The use of special operations during the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan enabled women to play a crucial part in gaining intelligence. In 2010, Female Engagement Teams (FET) utilized a policy loophole that restricted women in combat-related occupations, allowing FET-recruited women in the military to train in special operations and accompany special forces and Rangers on dangerous missions overseas with the purpose of engaging local Afghan and Iraqi women in consideration of cultural customs (Tzemach Lemmon, 2015).

Despite women serving in these roles, it was not until 2013 that the Direct Ground Combat Definition and Assignment Rule was overturned. In January 2016, all restrictions on combat-related jobs were removed and for the first time women were able to officially work in any military occupational specialty, as long as they were qualified and met specified standards.1 While women have been engaging in combat unofficially throughout history, the newest policies regarding women in combat could lead to an increase in women veterans with more complex military histories.

The historical and ongoing limitations that have excluded servicewomen from certain positions have sent a not so subtle message to numerous female veterans that they are not equal to their male colleagues. Because women’s traditional roles have been to support men in combat positions, women have been treated differently in their military occupations and duties. This exclusionary treatment has contributed in part to women veterans not seeing themselves as “veterans” because they do not match the stereotypical image of a male combatant.

II. Women Veterans in Oregon and Nationwide

Oregon is one of the few states where there are no active-duty military installations; it is therefore unlikely that clients needing legal assistance are currently on active duty. Veteran, reserve, and National Guard clients are more common in Oregon when seeking legal assistance. However, many resources are available to veteran clients throughout Oregon—see section XII near the end of this chapter.

Recent population statistics about active-duty servicemembers, male and female veterans for both the United States and in the State of Oregon are listed below.

National:

  • Total active-duty military members (male and female) in all branches: 1,326,273.
  • 15.1 percent of active-duty military are female.
  • Nearly half of all active-duty servicemembers are 25 years old or younger.
  • Most (92 percent) have a high school diploma or some college experience; seven percent have a bachelor’s degree or higher.
  • The number of female enlisted military members and officers has increased from 14.7 and 14.4 percent in 2000 to 14.8 and 16.7 percent, respectively, in 2014.2

Oregon:

  • Total number of female veterans: 28,483.3
  • Largest number of female veterans by county: Washington, Multnomah, and Clackamas—over 2,500 female veterans anticipated in each county by 2017.
  • 9 percent (2016) to 14 percent (2014) anticipated increase of female veterans out of all Oregon veterans (within the next 25 years).
  • Average age of female veterans is 43, as compared to average age of male veterans which is 60.4
  • Total number (male and female) National Guard and reserve servicemembers: 9,713.5

III. Gender Discrimination on Active Duty

In spite of the motivations and enthusiasm for enlisting, becoming a servicemember from civilian life can be a significant culture change for both men and women, often fraught with emotional, physical, interpersonal, occupational, and geographical challenges. Women on active duty face additional difficulties (situations which oftentimes are not anticipated), which can range from challenging collegial relationships to occupational and/or personal safety and social vicissitudes.

Every branch of the military has varying degrees of acceptance levels for women functioning in all positions within the ranks. Furthermore, military culture values physical strength, controlled aggression, repressed emotions, and hyper-masculinity—all of which is based upon the need for strong warriors, who historically are all male (Devilbliss, 1990; Turchik & Wilson, 2010).

A number of servicewomen and women veterans say they have not or did not encounter any gender discrimination while in the military—stating that they were included as an equal member of their unit while functioning within their work assignment, or they do not recall any problems as a result of gender. Unfortunately, and as the most recent data indicate, these experiences are not in the majority. More commonly, female veterans describe situations where their male counterparts focused on gender as an issue or they were subjected to harassment or sexual assault by male servicemembers.

Among those who did experience problems with sex discrimination, there is wide variation about the type and frequency of problems depending on what years they were in the military, the military branch they served in, their duty station, and how unusual it was for women to be full-time military. In some situations, due to the dangers posed toward women at certain duty stations or while overseas, female veterans have shared that they were not allowed to walk alone at night as a form of protection. The more recently women served, the less discrimination they faced.

All servicewomen who were subjected to discriminatory treatment by male peers are faced with a choice that male servicemembers rarely encounter: put up with uncomfortable comments and inappropriate behavior in order to maintain unit cohesion and camaraderie or attempt to minimize exposure to harassing comments and behaviors by withdrawing from others. Either option results in a version of social isolation, especially if the woman’s assignment results in her being one of the few women, or even the only woman, working in a particular job or duty station. The outcome is a servicewoman who is not availed the same level of social network and cohesiveness as her male counterparts—which for most anyone would mean a significant loss of a critical component of military service: servicemember friends.

IV. Sexual Violence in the Military

Unfortunately, when the topic of women veterans is discussed it is not possible to avoid the issue of sexual violence while on active duty. Although the Department of Defense continues to seek through various initiatives a considerable reduction of sexual harassment and assaults, the reality is that progress is not only gradual but reported incidents over the past few years have gone up. Whether the observed increases are due to servicemembers’ greater willingness to disclose being assaulted or an increase in the frequency of attacks is not clear.

Male victimization reports have steadily increased as well, to a point where the same number of men and women are being counted as military sexual trauma (MST) victims. Nonetheless, as long as sexual assault and harassment exists within the military, a significant proportion of active-duty women will continue to be victimized.

Sexual abuse and violence within the military was the compelling focus of the documentary The Invisible War, released in 2012. Directed by Kirby Dick and produced by Amy Ziering, the documentary received nationwide attention and 13 awards by 2014, including an Emmy, a Peabody, Best Documentary (Sundance Film Festival, 2012), and several Audience Awards. The documentary provides a wider, non-veteran audience a better understanding of discrimination and sexual violence in the military using interviews with survivors as well as statistics about the low conviction rate of perpetrators.

When assessing a veteran’s past history, be sure to query whether she experienced any incidents of sexual violence related to her military service. Incidents, whether reported or unreported, could influence a veteran’s later behavior that precipitates an attorney or judge to have contact with the veteran. An unreported sexual assault is critical to identify because the victim may never have received resources or support for recovery from the traumatic event. Below are some statistics and facts about sexual harassment and sexual assault in the military that may help underscore the prevalence of the problem.

A. Sexual Harassment

Unlike the non-veteran community, the VA includes sexual harassment in their definition of sexual assault, recognizing that non-contact incidents can also impair a servicemember’s functioning and have long-lasting ramifications. The definition supplements Title 38 (U.S. Code), and states that military sexual trauma (MST):

is defined by Title 38 U.S. Code 1720D as “psychological trauma resulting from a physical assault of a sexual nature, battery of a sexual nature, or sexual harassment which occurred while the Veteran was serving on active duty, active duty for training, or inactive duty training.” Sexual harassment is defined as “repeated, unsolicited verbal or physical contact of a sexual nature which is threatening in character.”

The following data below address sexual assault and harassment in the military. Like many data sources for sexually violent incidents, reporting levels are likely lower than the actual occurrences.

Military Sexual Harassment Statistics Nationwide:

  • In 2014, an estimated 116,600 members experienced sexual harassment in the past year (men and women).
  • 22 percent of all active-duty women experienced at least one incident of sexual harassment.6
  • In 2015, 657 incidents were reported alleging sexual harassment investigated or tried.7
  • One-third of those who were sexually assaulted say the offender sexually harassed them before the assault.8
  • 160,500 servicemembers (1 in 4 women, 1 in 14 men) faced severe and persistent sexual harassment or gender discrimination in 2014.
  • 60 percent of victims were harassed by someone in their chain of command.

According to the 2012 report from the National Sexual Violence Resource Center (NSVRC) Sexual Violence in the Military, sexual violence remains a significant issue. The NSVRC found that 47 percent of women endured sexist behaviors, 41 percent reported crude/offensive behaviors, and 23 percent reported sexual harassment. Given the frequency of harassment servicewomen endured, one can assume that nearly half of all women in the military are subjected to inappropriate behavior by their male colleagues, and subsequent emotional discomfort, while attempting to perform their military duties.

B. Sexual Assault and Rape

The U.S. Department of Justice definition of sexual assault states,

Sexual assault is any type of sexual contact or behavior that occurs without the explicit consent of the recipient. Falling under the definition of sexual assault are sexual activities as forced sexual intercourse, forcible sodomy, child molestation, incest, fondling, and attempted rape.

Sexual Assault/Rape Statistics — National:

  • In 2015, there were 5,240 reported incidents of sexual assault made by active-duty servicemembers (men and women). Of those, 413 were convicted of the crime they were accused of.
  • In 2012: an estimated 26,000 unreported sexual assaults were committed by active-duty servicemembers (men and women).
  • In 2014, an estimated 20,300 sexual assaults went unreported, indicating a decrease from previous reporting years.
  • Servicemen are sexually assaulted more often than servicewomen, with an estimated 10,600 unreported sexual assaults against servicemen.
  • Servicewomen are sexually assaulted at a proportionately higher rate than men, with an estimated 9,600 unreported sexual assaults against servicewomen.9 As high as 93 percent of female victims report that they knew their assailant.10
  • Protect Our Defenders has created a summary that consolidates reports from the RAND Corporation and the DoD into one downloadable factsheet which describes the ongoing problems with sexual violence in the military, including rape, assaults and harassment. Protect Our Defenders Factsheet.

Sexual Assault/Rape Statistics — Oregon Women Veterans:

  • Over half (59 percent) of Oregon women veterans report that they have experienced sexual assault, rape, or harassment while on active duty.
  • Of these, 65 percent are not receiving service-connected VA disability benefits for military sexual trauma or post-traumatic stress disorder.11

C. Sex Abuse Response Resources in the Department of Defense

There are numerous risk factors that contribute to sexual violence within the military, but the most noteworthy are: Alcohol use among active-duty servicemembers is greater than that of their same-aged civilian counterparts (Ames & Cunradi, 2004). Additionally, half of all servicemembers are 25 years or younger, which coincides with the maximum risk for committing sexual assault being those between the ages of 18 and 24 (US Commission on Civil Rights, 2013). The military utilizes a hierarchical structure of rank in which power and control are part of the military mindset, which contributes more heavily to incidents of sexual harassment.

In these ways, the military environment creates critical components that contribute to high rates of sexual assault. To make matters worse, there was a long history of inaction and a lack of a department-wide policy on the topic.

In 2005 the Sexual Assault Prevention and Response Office (SAPRO) was created within the DoD. Its main goals are in the areas of prevention, investigation, accountability, victim advocacy and assistance, and assessment.12 Within the SAPRO, a trained victim advocate called either a Sexual Assault Prevention and Response Victim Advocate or a Sexual Assault Response Coordinator (titles of responders are branch specific) will respond to a reported sexual assault immediately. Furthermore, the reporting system was improved to better address a lack of reporting due to professional and social retaliation. Victims can choose to report a sexual assault as unrestricted, which requires that law enforcement, command, and victim advocates be notified and undertake official measures to investigate the assault. Victims can speak with legal counsel and request forensic examinations.

The other option is a restricted report, which allows victims to have access to victim advocates, legal advocates, and necessary healthcare, but utilizing them remains confidential to their command and law enforcement. Victims can also switch their reporting from restricted to unrestricted at any time.

Trends in reporting since the inception of the SAPRO program have seen reduced frequency of sexual assaults (from 34,200 in 2006 to 20,300 in 2014) and increased percentages in restricted and unrestricted reports (from 7 percent in 2006 to 23 percent in 2014). Both of these changes are considered indicative of an improvement of sexual abuse reporting within the military.13

D. Military Sexual Assault Reporting Challenges

The goal of the SAPRO is to eliminate sexual assault and harassment from the military. Part of that mission includes making reporting an assault more commonplace and providing a supportive representative to guide a survivor to helpful resources. Although rates of reporting have increased, a significant number of victims still are not choosing to report being assaulted.

Prior to the implementation of the SAPRO, the Department of Defense Advisory Committee on Women in the Services (DACOWITS) investigated reasons for not reporting sexual assaults by servicemembers. DACOWITS was established in 1951 by the Secretary of Defense. It is composed of civilian women and men who are appointed by the Secretary of Defense to provide advice and recommendations on policies relating to numerous issues, ranging from recruitment and retention to the overall well-being of servicewomen. DACOWITS posts their recommendations online, and their ultimate purpose is toward relevant changes to policies pertaining to military women.

DACOWITS also provided a summary document in 2011 with information that emerged through 23 focus groups in seven different locations across the nation. The focus groups gathered information from 226 servicemembers, and included both women and men, enlisted personnel and officers. Some of the reasons for servicemembers’ hesitation to report appear related to lack of communication and lack of connection with those who need the information:

  • Lack of trust in the reporting system—“There is no confidentiality.”
  • Fear of stigma—“The stigma of reporting is still there. Some people are just lucky enough to have leadership to try to protect them from the stigma.”
  • Fear of reprimand for underage drinking—“People are scared because they have been drinking underage, and they are nervous to put themselves in trouble. So they won’t report because they don’t want to get in trouble.”
  • Victims’ conflicted feelings toward attackers.14

In spite of a decade of the DoD’s dedicated efforts and resource allocations to end sexual assault and harassment, victims still admit that they will not report their assault for the same reasons as before. In the DoD Annual Report on Sexual Assault in the Military, victims state that possible retaliation, the thought that nothing would be done, not wanting anyone to know about the incident, or feeling like they were responsible for the assault are the common reasons for not reporting (24 percent). Additionally, fear of repercussions (25 percent), victims did not believe the crime was serious enough to report (18 percent) or that they wanted to move on (17 percent) were described as deterrents.15

These beliefs are not unfounded. Victims who chose to report their assaults were surveyed about negative events they experienced afterwards; 52 percent of victims were further victimized by both retaliation and intimidation in their workplace.16 In a study focusing on personal accounts of retaliation when reporting a sexual assault, victims reported threats of violence, social retaliation, professional retaliation, disciplinary actions in reference to their assault, involuntary discharges from the military, and even retaliation against those that supported them.17

Another example of possible retaliatory action against women who opted to report their abuse is through their military discharge. Most veterans who served honorably in the military receive an honorable discharge, which is indicated on the bottom of their military discharge sheet, a DD-214 form. Less than honorable discharges suggest that a veteran had some difficulties or was disciplined while on active duty (e.g., disciplined for violating the Uniform Code of Military Justice).

In recent years women veterans have come forward indicating that they received less than honorable discharges based not on any of their own behavioral problems while on active duty but on their decision to report being sexually assaulted. These discharges are considered a form of retaliation against women who opted to pursue legal action against their perpetrators. The Protect Our Defenders website provides the following data as part of a larger diagram, and includes a segment entitled “Retaliation Is the Norm”:

  • 62 percent of women who reported a sexual assault face retaliation.
  • One-third of victims are discharged after reporting, typically within seven months of making their reports.
  • Victims received harsher discharges, with a majority of women incurring them as reprisal from superiors and commanders.
  • 24 percent received less than honorable, compared to 15 percent of all servicemembers.

Resources outside of the federal government have been made available for women veterans to get their discharges upgraded to honorable if it is deemed that lower discharge classifications were a result of retaliation. However, many women veterans say they would rather not pursue an upgrade of discharge because for them it is perceived as yet another fight against a federal establishment that did not support them while they were in the military during their time of need.18

E. Long Term Issues of Sexual Violence in the Military

The long-lasting consequences for female survivors of military sexual violence take many forms—manifested physically, mentally, and emotionally.

Physical health and medical problems have been studied and confirmed as a significant negative effect of military sexual trauma. Frayne and colleagues (2004) looked at the long term aftermath of military sexual assault, PTSD, and health problems when surveying over 3,600 female veterans who were receiving care from VA medical centers. The study’s authors found that 23 percent of women using VA services suffered military sexual assault; these assaults were significantly associated with the women’s physical symptoms and medical conditions.

Among women reporting a history of being sexually assaulted while in the military, 26 percent endorsed 12 of 24 symptoms or conditions, as compared to 11 percent of women with no reported military sexual assault.19

V. Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder

The incidences of significant military sexual harassment or any occurrence of sexual assault are considered traumatic events that can affect their current social and occupational functioning even decades after discharge from active duty. It is beyond the scope of this chapter to discuss the nuances and variations of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) among warzone and non-warzone survivors. It is important to note, however, that the type of traumatic stressor from any incident typically contributes to a survivor’s current trauma-based responses to various situations. Although PTSD is discussed at length in other chapters of this publication, it warrants a brief summary in this chapter as well.

The diagnosis was first officially recognized by the psychiatric community in 1980 through the third edition of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual (DSM-III). Originally, PTSD was considered a war veteran’s disorder—intermittently called shell shock, combat or battle fatigue, or “Soldier’s Heart”— specifically suggesting that a warrior’s memories and behaviors were adversely affected by combat exposure. Over the past 40 years, the diagnosis has expanded to include other life-threatening experiences. For survivors, the symptoms of intrusive memories, recurrent nightmares of traumatic events, hyper-arousal of the physiological emergency system, and trauma-based memories are continually “triggered.” These symptoms manifest similarly for male and female trauma survivors. Numerous women, as well as male veterans, who did not serve in combat zones may suffer from PTSD owing to other traumatizing experiences during military service.

The incidences of significant military sexual harassment or any occurrence of sexual assault are considered traumatic events which can affect their current social and occupational functioning even decades after discharge from active duty. It is beyond the scope of this chapter to discuss the nuances and variations of PTSD among warzone and non-warzone survivors. It is important to note, however, that the type of traumatic stressor from any incident typically contributes to a survivor’s current trauma-based responses to various situations.

Numerous studies have been conducted to better understand the prevalence of military sexual trauma and PTSD. Yaeger and colleagues (2006) conducted the first study that compared the impact of MST against other kinds of traumas, such as life-threatening illnesses, accidents, or other sexual trauma. Their findings include that victims of MST had higher rates of PTSD than those who suffered from other types of traumatic events, and that 60 percent of those with MST had a diagnosis of PTSD. In addition, the authors found that 43 percent of subjects with other traumas (with or without MST) had PTSD. They concluded that the rate of PTSD was higher for women in the MST group than for subjects who had a history of other types of traumas, and that among the 196 subjects who participated in the study with PTSD, military sexual trauma was identified as the most distressing trauma.

Using the same study subjects, another analysis was conducted by Himmelfarb, Yaeger, and Mintz (2006). This study examined the frequency of diagnosed PTSD among MST survivors, as compared with non-military sexual trauma. Among their sample of 196 female veterans, the authors found that the prevalence of military sexual trauma was higher than pre-military or post-military sexual trauma. They also found that women with MST had the greatest odds of developing PTSD—more so than those with pre-military or post-military trauma histories. Himmelfarb and colleagues contend that preventing MST may reduce cases of PTSD among female veterans.

PTSD includes other problematic symptoms, such as a susceptibility to strong reactions when faced with trauma-related environmental triggers. Due to the avoidance symptom of PTSD, untreated trauma survivors rarely fully understand the extent of their trauma-based triggers (for example, what kind of stimuli can trigger memories, and how often they are triggered), and are thus subjected to seemingly random panic attacks or anxious episodes when in otherwise benign situations. Typically it is only through comprehensive psychotherapy or insight-oriented guidance that survivors begin to understand how PTSD is operating within their bodies, minds, and emotions (Daniels, manuscript).

Relationship challenges often emerge as a result of untreated PTSD symptoms. These challenges for women veterans include multiple marriages, inability to maintain closeness to partners, mistrust of partners, fear of losing relationships with others, concerns of being assaulted again, and lack of attachment to others.

For veterans who have been harassed or assaulted while on active duty, ongoing struggles with low self-esteem or mistrust (of self and others) is not uncommon. Women veterans report not trusting their own ability to make decisions for fear of making “a wrong decision”—an anxiety that originated from their military traumatic events (i.e., a benign decision that resulted in their attack or someone else being harmed).

VI. Warzone Redeployments

War offers a major shift of women’s roles within the military. Within the last 20 years, more women have been deployed overseas to Iraq and Afghanistan than in any other conflict in the nation’s history. As a result, servicewomen have found themselves leaving their children for extended periods of time and thousands of miles away, adding stress to family engagement and involvement. As with any family member returning from warzone deployment, veterans are thrilled to be home safely but still may suffer from the aftermath of being in a combat area, with the same level of bittersweet readjustment issues.

Similar to male servicemembers returning from deployments, female veterans experience the “honeymoon” period of post-deployment reunions and, after settling back into their home environments, still have a warzone carry-over effect.

Family expectations may be that a woman will slip back into her role as mother just as before she deployed. However, the intensity of the deployment, as well as the length of time away from former family roles, may create a more challenging readjustment to being stateside, living with her family post-deployment.

VII. Eating Disorders

Recently the VA’s Advisory Committee on Women in the Military (Sept. 2016) recommended an expansion of identification and treatment of eating disorders within VA facilities. The committee is one of the more recent (2015) offshoots of the Center for Women Veterans established by Congress in November 1994 to monitor and coordinate VA programs for women veterans.

The committee cited a study by Forman-Hoffman and colleagues that interviewed 1,004 women veterans who used VA services over a three-year period. The study found that those with PTSD and sexual trauma histories (15.6 percent) were more likely to present an eating disorder. The study distinguished their sample into different sexual trauma experiences (e.g., those experiencing a completed rape during military service, an attempted rape, childhood sexual assault, or childhood completed rape). The study’s authors concluded that completed rape during military service was significantly associated with both current and lifetime eating disorders. The authors also concluded that sexual trauma during military service was more strongly associated with lifetime eating disorders than childhood sexual trauma.20

VIII. Homelessness

Of an estimated 2.3 to 3.5 million homeless people in a given year, an estimated 26 percent of homeless adults are veterans, with women veterans three to four times more likely to become homeless than non-veteran women. Gamache, Rosenheck, and Tessler (2003) conducted an analysis looking at the prevalence of homelessness among women veterans. They found that the highest risk of homelessness was within the 45-54–year age range, and women veterans were more than four times as likely to be homeless as their non-veteran counterparts.

Another study focused on comparing risk factors for homelessness among housed and unhoused women veterans. Using a case comparison research design, the authors investigated the risk factors of homelessness and found:

Homeless women veterans were significantly more likely than housed women veterans to be unemployed, disabled, and low income; to have experienced MST; to be in fair or poor health; to have diagnosed medical conditions; and to screen positive for an anxiety disorder, PTSD, or tobacco use…. Homeless women veterans were significantly less likely to be college graduates, employed, married, or have health insurance, and were more likely to have used mental health services, VA health care, or been hospitalized in the prior 12 months . . . In this study, we found that homeless women veterans had an MST prevalence of 53%. Its added effect on top of other risk factors may help explain women veterans’ higher risk for homelessness.”21

The Advisory Committee on Women Veterans also recommended expanding the definition of homelessness among women veterans. Numerous studies have noted that former servicewomen are prevalent among the overall homeless population. The committee sought an expansion of the VA’s definition of homelessness to include provisions from the Homeless Emergency Assistance and Rapid Transition to Housing Act (HEARTH) signed by President Barack Obama on May 20, 2009. The committee stated:

[T]he McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act’s definition was expanded to include individuals or families fleeing—attempting to flee—domestic violence, dating violence, sexual assault, stalking, or other dangerous or life-threatening conditions in the individual’s or family’s current housing situation, including where the health and safety of children are jeopardized, and who have no other residence and lack the resources or support networks to obtain other permanent housing (42 U.S.C. § 11302 b). Enhancing 38 U.S.C. § 2002 to include 42 U.S.C. § 11302 (b) of the McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act, would address this gap in eligibility. The Committee recommends that VA seek to legally expand its current definition of homeless, to meet this evolving need of women Veterans.

The VA declined the committee’s request but did offer to support women veterans attempting to flee their current domestic abuse situation.

The overall aspects of homelessness, the proportion of women veterans among the homeless population, risk factors for housed women to become homeless in the future, and attempts to generate gender-appropriate responses to circumstances resulting in homelessness such as domestic violence may be relevant in developing case arguments on a female veteran’s behalf. The reader is encouraged to access more information about homelessness among women veterans.

IX. Suicide Rates and Suicide Prevention Call Centers

In 2014 the VA published data of suicide rates among veterans that provided some startling findings and made national headlines. A major finding was that suicide rates among women veterans had increased by more than 85 percent since 2001. Below is a small excerpt of the report that compares suicide rates from 2001 to 2014.

  • In 2014, the rate of suicide among male veterans was 37 per 100,000. Since 2001, the age-adjusted rate of suicide among male veterans has increased by 30.5 percent.
  • In 2014, the rate of suicide among civilian adult females was 7.2 per 100,000. Since 2001, the age-adjusted rate of suicide among civilian adult females has increased by 39.7 percent.
  • In 2014, the rate of suicide among female veterans was 18.9 per 100,000. Since 2001, the age-adjusted rate of suicide among female veterans has increased by 85.2 percent.
  • After adjusting for differences in age, risk for suicide was 2.4 times greater among female veterans when compared to civilian females.

In response to the continued concerns of servicemember and active-duty suicide rates, several suicide prevention and crisis lines have been established at both the federal and state levels—

“1-877-WAR-VETS is an around the clock confidential call center where combat Veterans and their families can call to talk about their military experience or any other issue they are facing in their readjustment to civilian life. The staff is comprised of combat Veterans from several eras as well as families members of combat Veterans. This benefit is prepaid through the Veteran’s military service.”

  • Veterans Crisis Line — 1-800-273-8255 (Press 1), set up by VA. Formerly the National Suicide Prevention Hotline. The website includes “chat” option and call options, and self-assessment and educational pages to assist family members who have concerns about a veteran. From the website,

“Since its launch in 2007, the Veterans Crisis Line has answered nearly 2.8 million calls and initiated the dispatch of emergency services to callers in crisis nearly 74,000 times. The Veterans Crisis Line anonymous online chat service, added in 2009, has engaged in more than 332,000 chats. In November 2011, the Veterans Crisis Line introduced a text-messaging service to provide another way for Veterans to connect with confidential, round-the-clock support, and since then has responded to more than 67,000 texts.”

  • Oregon.gov Health Authority provides 24/7 crisis information across the state (not only for veterans).

X. Assessing Veteran Defendants’ Perceptions and Parallels to Military Experiences

There are outcomes of each veteran’s experiences worth understanding—even if it is many years after discharge from active-duty service. One outcome would be to determine if there is a carryover from the military into a veteran’s perceptions of how she is treated by others. For lawyers representing a female veteran, these perceptions may also be contributing factors in what manifests as legal problems. Yet, much of the carryover from active-duty service is not easily identified by the veteran until after an extensive interview, which includes specific questions about her military experiences and which illuminates whether past military service is influencing her perception of more current situations.

A full contextual assessment or psychosocial history is warranted, and this comprehensive understanding may help inform attorneys about aspects of a woman veteran’s personal history that could be relevant to her case. An added benefit is that the information may help guide lawyers with their conversations with their clients. Thus it is recommended that legal representatives either employ knowledgeable experts in their communities to conduct an assessment or conduct their own thorough assessment in some capacity to rule out or identify confounding past military experiences.

Components of a veteran’s military history should include information about: 1) whether she experienced any sexual harassment, sexual assault (including rape), or antagonistic interactions during her military service, 2) whether she was deployed to a combat area during her service, 3) whether she was deployed or not if she encountered any gender-specific restrictions or dangers, and 4) whether she recognizes any resemblance of warzone dangers, mistreatment, harassment, assault, stateside danger, or any form of threat which generates an automatic defensive response (i.e., behaviors she does to protect herself). This process may also determine whether a female veteran has unresolved traumatic events in her past or undiagnosed PTSD.

The following case description illustrates how a clearer understanding of a woman veteran’s military experience can better inform a defensive argument. For this case, the Metropolitan Public Defender’s Office accessed a content expert during the summer of 2016 to interview a female veteran defendant:

EXAMPLE

The defendant originally presented as a middle-aged female who wore sunglasses, a hat, and leather jacket during the first hour of the interview. She was needing defense representation due to charges of assault against her neighbor. Without knowing the specifics of the charges against the defendant, the interviewer conducted what would be a typical mental health assessment and psychosocial history (which provides information about childhood, military, deployment, post-military, current domestic, occupational, and access to health care history).

The interview lasted over four hours and, as the veteran felt more comfortable with the interviewer, she shed the sunglasses, hat, and jacket while providing more extensive information about her military experiences. She enlisted in the military to financially assist her family. She initially enjoyed being in the military, but at her permanent duty station she was subjected to significant sexual harassment by a male servicemember. The ongoing and random encounters with the male servicemember eventually negatively impacted on her perception of safety while she attempted to do her job.

As the veteran’s military story was disclosed, several unresolved issues emerged: that the client was “stuck” in her military duties, attempted to avoid unwanted sexual advances for months, and was unsupported by her peers and higher-ranking staff when she sought their help. In spite of being surrounded by other servicemembers with whom she had originally assumed would support her, she realized that she was on her own.

The incident that prompted her need for legal defense was then discussed. Although not apparent to the veteran as she described her neighbor’s ongoing threats against her and her family, the veteran described making the same decisions in her living situation with an aggressive neighbor as she had during her military service-time: she was stuck, on her own (her family did not believe that she was fearful), and attempted to avoid contact with someone whom she deemed a threat. When queried, the client admitted that she was hyper-aware of the neighbor for months, attempted to slip by the neighbor’s door without notice or was subjected to heckling by the neighbor as she passed by.

Prior to the day of the incident, the client had been successful in getting to her own apartment with minimal or no contact with the neighbor. The alleged assault occurred when the client found herself cornered by the aggressive neighbor one afternoon as she returned home with her family at her apartment building. The rest of the family had already gone ahead to their apartment, leaving the veteran to carry her youngest child from the car. On this particular occasion, the neighbor came out of her apartment and began taunting the veteran while aggressively approaching the client when no one else was around to protect her. Given the change of behavior, and one that the client deemed was an escalation of aggression, the client fought back until her husband separated them. The client was arrested a few hours later after the neighbor called and accused the veteran of assault.

Although during the interview the client endorsed many of the symptoms consistent with a PTSD diagnosis, the client had never been diagnosed with PTSD. She also had no prior history of physical violence. The determination by the mental health assessor was that the veteran acted in self-defense which stemmed from a reactivation of her prior military service, especially her hyper-arousal symptoms. In addition, the client stated she was protecting another more vulnerable family member, and she perceived the neighbor’s actions as a real threat. When suddenly confronted, the client was unable to flee or freeze, so as a last resort physically fought the neighbor. It was not until the client was interviewed by a mental health expert that the resemblance of the assault incident and past military sexual harassment was identified, which also provided some guidance toward future counseling for the client. In addition, after the mental health determination of the veteran’s state of mind prior to the assault, the prosecution declined to pursue the charges.

XI. Legal Incidents & Military Event Triggers

Knowledge of military history may inform attorneys of a female veteran’s “state of mind” during more current situations that may not be within a military context but still resemble the military experience in some capacity (i.e., emotionally, situationally, environmentally). Due to a “civilian” context, a threat similarity or “trigger” may not be recognizable—not even to a female veteran. But insight as to a possible parallel (between a more recent incident and past military experiences) can be identified with an experienced interviewer or listener who can better identify similarities.

In an incident involving a female veteran, triggers can occur just prior to the event, which may further explain how and why a veteran client responded to certain circumstances the way she did. Women veterans are often unaware of their incident reactions to current situations as trigger responses related to past experiences. Unfortunately for women, the most common triggers of military experiences are often related to sexual harassment while on active duty. Therefore, legal representatives should consider the possibility of past military events and incident triggers related to these events when assessing women veteran legal cases.

Attorney-Client Interactions: Considering Military Triggers

Another aspect of working with women veterans, especially those who have military sexual harassment or assault histories, involves how best to converse with these clients in a safe manner. An interaction between a male attorney and a female veteran client can inadvertently trigger a reaction stemming from a distressing military incident while the attorney is interviewing the client regarding legal charges. If attorneys lack knowledge of the client’s history (both military and pre-military), an attorney’s style of interviewing may reactivate a client’s memories of prior situations, which could generate a more defensive response by the client than is intended. In addition, if a legal representative has a “strong” (direct or demanding) interviewing style, this may unintentionally trigger memories of past emotional or physical damaging situations experienced by a female veteran client.

A client’s response may be avoidant or defensive and may not seem logical or rational, yet a veteran’s visceral response feels very real to her. Women veterans will attempt to make sense of their defensive reaction—i.e., “He’s my attorney, he’s trying to help me. I should not be getting upset when he raises his voice or loses his temper”—but these rationalizing thoughts do not automatically translate into feeling calm and clear-headed when an interaction feels threatening. Instead, they may bring to mind a past assault. Even decades after an incident, a past threat may impact on a woman veteran’s response (or for that matter, any trauma survivor’s response).

The combination of (a) not assessing female clients for trauma history (military-related sexual harassment or assault) and (b) attorneys or investigators being aggressive in their approach may give rise to resistance by the client (consistent with avoiding reminders of past traumatic events), little follow-up, or uncooperativeness. Therefore, not only are attorneys encouraged to assess for past incidents with female clients, but the process of interviewing a veteran must be thoughtfully considered.

XII. Barriers to Care and Resources for Women Veterans: Oregon

Most female veterans are not utilizing veteran-centric healthcare resources. In 2015 the VA published an extensive report indicating some of the barriers that limit women veterans’ access to services for which they are eligible. In some cases, women veterans have shared that their anxiety is triggered by walking into a large hospital area filled with men. Other women have admitted that they don’t feel “veteran enough,” or were not aware of their eligibility for services.

The 2015 Oregon Legislature’s House Bill 2539 instructed the Oregon Department of Veterans Affairs to undertake a study whose goal would be an improved understanding of healthcare barriers and demographics of Oregon women veterans. The Rede Group conducted the study, with its findings based on information from structured interviews of Veterans Health Administration (VHA) providers, focus groups of women veterans, questionnaire data from women veterans, and national data sets. The study provided major outcomes, one of which indicated that only 19 percent of female veterans access the VHA for their physical health or mental health needs. An excerpt from the study illustrates some of the reasons why so few women take advantage of VHA services:

Women often experience a sense of invisibility when interfacing with the VHA. Women report that they are frequently not recognized as veterans because VHA staff assumes that, because they are women, they are there to support a male veteran. Some women experience gender-specific hostility from VHA providers, including being chastised for taking a healthcare appointment or taking away a place in the system that a male veteran should have.

  • 28 percent of Oregon women veterans state that not using the VA is due to a complete lack of women mental health providers.
  • 47 percent do not like the services the VA offers.
  • 28 percent admit they don’t like the providers within the VA system.

Within the past decade, the Portland VA Medical Center, along with other VAMC’s nationwide, have attempted to provide a more woman-friendly option by opening a Women’s Healthcare Clinic located in a separate building on campus, or one very close to the main hospital building. In addition, Vet Centers in the community are located off campus from VA hospitals and are often staffed with veteran counselors who are women. In order to increase the comfort level of veterans seeking services, Vet Centers are purposely accessible and unassuming. In addition, Vet Centers were the first division of the VA to hire sexual trauma counselors over 20 years ago.

Federally Funded Counseling Resources

Below is a brief description of the Department of Veterans Affairs Counseling and Psychotherapy resources for military sexual trauma and war-related readjustment issues in Oregon:

Outpatient Community Resources

Through their mental health counseling Department of Veterans Affairs Medical Centers (VAMCs) utilize specific protocols, evidence-based interventions, and medication. Veterans need to self-identify as seeking MST services. Eligibility to receive MST treatment is different than other problem areas: a veteran does not have to be service connected for PTSD or MST, and the usual minimum of two years’ active-duty service is waived (service-time can be less than two years) so long as the veteran states that she is seeking services for MST when enrolling. Each VAMC also has an MST coordinator on staff whose role is to assist MST-afflicted veterans with connecting to VAMC services.

  • Click here for more information about VAMC MST services.
  • Main VA phone number (nationwide): 1-844-MyVA311 (1-844-698-2311).
  • Portland VAMC phone number: (503) 220-8262.
  • VA MST benefits counselor listing (nationwide, scroll down for Oregon).
  • Vet Centers (located in Portland, Salem, Eugene, Bend and Grants Pass) are storefront, community-based centers and are a division of the Department of VA’s Readjustment Counseling Service. All Vet Centers are primarily staffed by counselors who are veterans. Eligibility to receive Vet Center counseling includes having served in a warzone (as demonstrated by the DD-214) or self-reported history of military sexual assault. Vet Centers provide readjustment counseling (psychotherapy) for war-related issues and military sexual assault. Type of military discharge (per DD-214) does not have to be honorable. Every Vet Center is required to have at least one MST counselor on staff who has a specialized training background for treating sexual assault. Vet Centers are located away from the local VAMC and have separate record-keeping that is confidential (even from VA providers). No financial means test is utilized to determine the level of care, and all counseling is at no cost to veterans. Counseling is typically client-centered individual or group psychotherapy. Vet Centers have been assisting MST clients since the early 1990s.
  • VA Community Based Outpatient Clinics (CBOC) are mainly medical clinics in areas where a larger VAMC is not available or accessible. A CBOC may have a mental health counselor staffed who works with a wide range of issues; although not specifically for warzone issues or MST counseling, these counselors are available for counseling and support.

Residential Inpatient Resources

There is no inpatient treatment program specifically for treating military sexual trauma in Oregon. Women who need this type of treatment are typically referred from an outpatient counselor to a residential program outside of Oregon. VA residential MST programs throughout the country vary significantly in terms of program structure and length of stay. Each program will consider appropriately referred Oregon veterans with a full application by an outpatient therapist and veteran client.22

Nonfederal Resources for Counseling and Other Assistance

In addition to the above federally-funded veteran counseling options, information about other useful resources are provided in the following, not exhaustive list:

  • Veteran Service Offices: Disabled American Veterans (DAV), Veterans of Foreign Wars (VFW), American Legion. Each of these supportive organizations offers socialization and VA benefit application support. All branches of the military are welcome and multiple eras are included.
  • Oregon Department of Veterans Affairs: Assists with vocational rehabilitation and other benefits that the federal VA does not provide. Each state has its own state-funded VA, and levels of assistance vary from state to state.
  • The Returning Veterans Project: Unique to Oregon, the RVP coordinates private practice therapists, counselors, family therapists, and some alternative practitioners who are willing to donate some of their practice hours toward assisting veterans and their families.
  • Service Women’s Action Network: SWAN is an active political action committee which was featured in the documentary, The Invisible War. This organization provides support around legislative issues involving women veterans and lobbies congressional representatives toward larger policy changes.

Conclusion

Defense attorneys representing women veterans are sometimes the first point of contact due to legal circumstances, and thereby have the ability to identify underlying and carryover issues from military experiences.

It is vitally important to conduct a thorough process of discovery when working with veteran clients who are women. Likewise, be sure to gather the necessary contextual background information toward developing arguments for her defense. Be simultaneously aware of maintaining a neutral and professional demeanor to minimize reactivating military (or traumatic event) triggers while conducting your interviews.

Legal representatives are encouraged to access community providers and content experts to best determine the needs of women veteran clients. A plethora of information is available addressing the many complicated and unique challenges female veterans face.

Taking into consideration the unique circumstances that exist for women veterans may shed light on the veteran’s legal issues, could improve the interpersonal connection between you and your client, and generate an outcome that respects the challenges servicewomen have faced for generations.
