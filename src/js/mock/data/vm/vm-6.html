Still at War: A Guide for Defenders, Prosecutors and Judges Dealing with Oregon's Veteran Defendant Crisis

Still at War: A Guide for Defenders, Prosecutors & Judges Dealing with Oregon’s Veteran Defendant Crisis, 2017 edition

Chapter 6. Veterans, Combat Injuries, Military Training, and Criminal Justice

Robert G. Stanulis, PhD*

Veterans frequently become involved in the criminal justice system. In Oregon, about 6,000 veterans a year are booked into Oregon jails.1 Veterans are also overrepresented in Oregon prison. While they represent 8.4 percent of Oregon’s population, they represent 19.1 percent of Oregon prisoners in the DOC.2 Veterans also account for about 23 percent of Oregon’s suicides, interactions with law enforcement, with suicide the leading cause of death in veterans under 45.3

Homelessness, unemployment, mental health problems, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), traumatic brain injury (TBI) as the signature injury of the global war on terror, physical disabilities with neck, back, and knee injuries, and interaction with law enforcement traced to military service are common among veterans. Hence, a culturally competent justice system is required to ensure justice for veterans as it is, for them, literally a matter of life or death.

This chapter is divided into several sections. First, it outlines the basics of working with veterans. Then it addresses the most frequent combat and noncombat injuries veterans sustain that are relevant to the criminal justice system. It is important to note that each injury is a subspecialty unto itself and each section of this chapter is meant to be only a summary of an injury. Further, it must be emphasized that the goal is to explain, not excuse a veteran’s behavior, and that the goal is to ensure justice, not leniency.

I. The Basics

Veterans are a unique sub-population of prisoners that are the product of military culture and the military total institution (see Chapter 5 ). Working with veterans requires cultural competence, as veterans are the product of a unique culture with its own language, traditions, and values. It is necessary to know this culture and how it differs from civilian culture to provide minimally competent services.

Cultural competence also includes understanding bias towards and about veterans. Questions to ask include How do I perceive veterans? Are they killers? Are veterans in the criminal justice system the product of their military training and experience? Or are they a product of selection errors, because “real veterans” would not be involved in criminal acts? Another critical question is are veterans to be feared or respected? Frequently, civilians fear veterans, while veterans just want to be respected. Regardless of background (civilian or military), it is imperative to know one’s biases and beliefs about veterans.

In this same vein, it is critical to know what questions to ask and what not to ask. Asking a veteran if they killed anyone while deployed should be avoided. Cultural competency requires knowing what and how to discuss military training and experiences. It is also important to note that military experience can also be a source of bias about veterans in the criminal justice system.

Veterans are also unique in that they enter the criminal justice system typically at a later age than non-veterans and without any significant history of juvenile involvement in the system. Any significant juvenile history would prevent the veteran from having joined the military.

Establishing veteran status under Oregon law, that is, being identified as a “servicemember”4 requires obtaining the veteran’s Certificate of Release or Discharge from Active Duty, called a DD-214. A DD-214 is required to document the type of discharge and any awards. Having this form is the first step to determining eligibility for veterans treatment court, servicemember diversion,5 and servicemember mitigation.6

Besides the DD-214, other records that should be obtained include enlistment records, military health records, Military Evaluation Board (MEB), federal Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) disability evaluations and awards, and VA treatment records. Obtaining these records requires specialized knowledge and release forms. See Chapter 4.

Another source of valuable information is by interviewing the veterans’ “battle buddies.” They often can provide corroborating information including pictures that validate deployment situations and provide critical information about the veteran’s military experience overall. It is also critical to find those who can describe the veteran over time from the pre-military, military, and post-military periods. (See Chapter 3) Interviews with fellow veterans, family, and others who can identify and document changes in the veteran over time are essential. Changes in use of controlled substances and reasons for use need to be identified. The high rate of orthopedic injuries has resulted in veterans being prescribed opioids for pain. When prescription opioids are no longer made available, street opioids are often substituted. When this occurs, a downgraded discharge can occur.

It is important to establish veteran status as early as possible, including contacting staff from the local Veteran Justice Outreach Program (VJOP). (See Chapter 3.) Although VJOP staff may not testify in court, they can inform the court about programs the veteran needs, and whether and when they may be admitted. This can facilitate pretrial release and treatment, which allows the veteran to access needed treatment and help resolve the case. Eligibility for servicemember diversion, or a veterans court or docket should likewise be determined early on.

A VA disability rating determination verifies the types of disability and what symptoms exist to justify the type and severity of disability. VA disability evaluations are informed by the veteran’s entire military file. They are a forensic type of evaluation, where VA records are not as forensically rigorous.

VA records will document any post-military treatment for physical, psychiatric, or TBI injuries. It will sometimes reference toxic exposure, such as exposure to burn pits, Agent Orange, or jet propellant fuel, but not necessarily to all toxic exposures, for instance antimalarial and other medications given in theatre. It is important to remember that the VA has its own release form and does not accept a blanket release.

Evaluations should address three distinct time periods: pre-military issues, military training and experiences, and post-military issues. A thorough investigation of each of these three distinct time periods is required. An in-depth sociological evaluation provides critical information about the veteran’s social history across these periods. Many cases may require more than one expert to adequately evaluate a veteran’s case. The symptoms of PTSD are shaped by the military culture. Neuropsychological expertise is often required because of the high rate of TBI and blast TBI (bTBI).

As mentioned above, a significant percentage of veterans are also injured through toxic exposure. It is important to note that each generation of veterans was exposed to different toxins in the military. This ranges from Agent Orange, Gulf War Syndrome, burn pits, vaccinations, antimalarial medications, exposure to fuel neurotoxins, to bTBI, all depending on the war in which the veteran fought.

II. Common Issues and Injuries

The next portion of this chapter covers the most common issues seen in veterans in the criminal justice system. The most common diagnoses are PTSD, TBI and blast TBI.

A. PTSD

The symptoms of PTSD are well known to most, so only a brief description follows. It should be noted that there are some differences between the DSM IV TR, the DSM 5, and the ICD 10 formulation of PTSD, which are quite technical and beyond the scope of this chapter. It is important to note that Oregon administrative rules specify that the current DSM 5 must be used by certified forensic evaluators. But these rules apply only to fitness to proceed and insanity evaluations.

The expression of PTSD symptoms is influenced by the military culture, therefore cultural competence and knowledge is required to understand how PTSD is expressed in veterans differently than in a civilian context. A good example is how guns can be an expression of PTSD symptoms and used to monitor symptom severity. For some veterans, gun ownership and proximity are expressions of hypervigilance and anxiety levels. The number and proximity of guns reduces these veterans’ anxiety. For example, one veteran described his guns as being like “teddy bears.” As the proximity of the veteran to their guns decreases, their symptoms decrease.

The DSM 5’s description of PTSD explains that it occurs when an individual is faced with life-threatening trauma and then responds to that trauma through symptoms of reliving, avoidance, and physiological changes. It is important to note that PTSD symptoms are adaptive in combat but maladaptive in civilian life. There is also an emerging literature that symptoms from toxic exposure and blast TBI may mimic PTSD.

B. TBI and Blast TBI

TBI is a frequent diagnosis that co-occurs with PTSD, and there is a significant symptom overlap between the two. TBI has long been recognized as the disruption of brain function following a blow to the head. These disruptions can be temporary, as with a concussion, or of long duration, depending on severity and structural damage. The course of classic TBI is one of recovery, not necessarily complete, over time.

Lately, more attention is being devoted to blast TBI (bTBI). Blast TBI is different in that it is the result of exposure to blast waves and does not require a hit to the head or loss of consciousness. Importantly, bTBI is thought to have a different clinical course than standard TBI.

Blast TBI can result in progressive chronic traumatic encephalopathy (CTE), like the TBI experienced by football players. This means that symptoms of this type of injury, unlike classic TBI, may worsen over time. Recent autopsy studies have found what appear to be unique neuropathological changes in bTBI.

C. Moral Injury and PTSD

While historically moral injury has been subsumed as part of “survivors’ guilt” (or simply ignored), more recently it has been recognized as a substantial issue that requires specialized treatment. Indeed, moral injury is a concept that is now being addressed in some treatment programs. See Chapter 8.

Significantly, the Oregon Department of Corrections (DOC) will not provide imprisoned veterans treatment for moral injury or, for that matter, for PTSD. This lack of treatment is a policy decision. For example, on April 14, 2015, the DOC’s assistant director of the offender management and rehabilitation division testified before the Oregon Senate Committee on Veterans and Emergency Preparedness. In her testimony, she stated that the “DOC does have concerns with providing PTSD treatment, as the Department does not typically engage inmates in any process that would bring traumatic memories to the forefront.” She continued, “Inmates can become too vulnerable and may be victimized or extorted by other inmates. This particular type of work is best left for continuing care upon release.”

This means that imprisoned veterans’ life-threatening combat-related injuries will not be treated, resulting in a substantial increase in the probability of death from untreated injuries sustained while serving their country in a combat zone. For those who serve lengthy sentences, this means they will never receive treatment for their combat-related injuries.

Lack of treatment increases the risk of recidivism. Preliminary findings from past experience show that results from pretrial treatment have been quite good, with very little recidivism. This indicates an association between criminal behavior and mental health issues and that early intervention is effective.

PSTD is associated with a host of legal issues. First, it frequently co-occurs with TBI and bTBI, creating a significant symptom overlap between the two afflictions. There is also an emerging literature suggesting that PTSD symptoms may be physically caused by blast injuries, of which over 350,000 have occurred. Other literature discusses implications that Mefloquine, an antimalarial, causes symptoms that mimic PTSD.

PTSD hyperarousal symptoms can cause flashbacks or reactive actions that appear intentional but are in fact simply overtrained muscle memory to perceived threat. This is relevant to actus reus, as well as to mens rea. At least one court has recognized that if an individual is in a flashback state, then diminished capacity is clearly an issue.

D. Alcohol Abuse

The following articles discuss an epidemiological position that PTSD is considered to cause alcohol abuse:

“Post Traumatic Stress Disorder and Co-Occurring Substance Use Disorders: Advances in Assessment and Treatment” US National Library of Medicine, National Institutes of Health. 2012.

“Epidemiological evidence about the relationship between PTSD and alcohol abuse: the nature of the association” Addict Behav 1998.

The DSM-5 is by design silent on causation but considers alcohol abuse a co-occurring disorder. In exploring alcohol use among veterans, it is important to investigate both the amount consumed and the reasons for consumption. Data that Dr. Brown has published clearly show that while many veterans drank before entering the military, the reason for drinking changed markedly after their military service. While about 90 percent of survey respondents drank for enjoyment pre-military, 70 percent of respondents reported drinking post-military to alleviate symptoms, and drank alone rather than socially. Therefore, it is important to note that veterans are not self-medicating but, rather, have another diagnosis. This is because the term “self-medication” implies agency, which would suggest that somehow veterans are “choosing” a diagnosis.

E. Substance Abuse

Veterans suffer high rates of orthopedic injuries, with knee and back problems common. As a result, veterans are frequently prescribed opioids. This can result in abuse of other opioids once the prescription medications are no longer available. Like the pattern among civilians, this can lead to use of street drugs, such as heroin. The military culture has a relatively high tolerance for alcohol abuse but a low tolerance for substance abuse. If found to be abusing street drugs, a less-than-honorable discharge may occur. Investigating the reasons for a downgraded discharge requires obtaining military performance reviews.

F. Military Sexual Trauma

Military sexual trauma (MST) is unfortunately common and both men and woman are affected. In one study, of 2,925,615 male veterans 31,797 cases of MST were found (one percent). For women, 29,418 cases of MST were found out of 137, 006 screenings (21.5 percent). A positive screening for MST may double the likelihood of receiving a mental health diagnosis.

Depression and anxiety are common symptoms. The occurrence is underreported, however, owing to the military culture and because, especially in women, it is investigated by the chain of command that may be complicit, making a clear conflict of interest. (For a longer analysis on this topic, see Chapter 9.) When reported by women, it can kill careers or result in discharge. Male victims are also quite unlikely to report a homosexual rape.

G. PTSD/Fight Or Flight/Amygdala Hijack

PTSD results in a state of hypervigilance that has two critical components. First, hypervigilance is an adaptive response in combat but not in the civilian world. Second, hypervigilance changes the soldier’s physiology, placing him or her far closer to a state of fight or flight (described below). Last, military training is designed to overtrain an effective fight response so that while in a fight or flight situation, the soldier can effectively and without thinking fight effectively. This means that a veteran can engage in a set of behaviors that are without thought (muscle memory), whereas a civilian would likely require time to think in order to act in a similar way.

Fight or flight is an automatic instinctual response to a threat to survival. As such, the main focus is to identify the threat and eliminate it as soon as possible. When the amygdala identifies a threat to survival, the autonomic nervous system is activated and, as a result, cortisol, adrenaline, and noradrenalin are released into the bloodstream. Bodily changes are very quick and include an increase in breath rate. Blood is shunted from the digestive track and directed to muscles and limbs. Pupils dilate as the body automatically prepares to find the threat to survival and eliminate it. As a result, everything becomes the enemy.

The fight or flight response bypasses the cortex—the part of our brain that thinks—and the non-thinking part of our brain simply reacts to survive. This is sometimes referred to as an “amygdala hijack,” and it is an evolutionary response to threats to our very survival that simply do not allow time for a rational response. In low to moderate threat situations the prefrontal cortex where conscious decisions are made modulates the response of the amygdala. But when a life-threatening event is perceived, the conscious part of the brain is automatically turned off. This is because in situations of extreme threat thinking would result in response times that are too slow to ensure survival.

Several other factors occur, including tunnel vision and attending only to the threat. This results in a lack of attention to peripheral details and makes remembering these details impossible. Hence, recall can be fragmentary and later result in trouble sequencing events or remembering details that simply were not attended to at the time. Vision becomes our primary sense and auditory exclusion occurs.

These perceptual changes, in addition to attention focused just on the threat, are often the reason why trained police officers cannot recall how many shots they fired, may perceive innocuous items as lethal threats, and can misperceive both the situation and its details. Most notably, one study on officer shooting incidents demonstrates that even police officers who have been trained are often unable to avoid the prefrontal cortex bypass that occurs in a fight or flight response, with 70 percent reporting that they responded to the threat “not with conscious thought” but “on autopilot.”

This process is of course relevant to the analysis of a veteran’s seemingly violent criminal behavior during an alleged person crime. Without understanding the effect of military training on the behavior exhibited when a veteran is in fight or flight situation, one could reach erroneous conclusions about actus reus and mens rea.

H. Violence Among Veterans

While veterans as a group are not violent, increased violence since the 9/11 attacks is an important issue. One report states that during 1995–2001 there were 184 homicides in the United States committed by active-duty military or veterans. From 2001to 2007 there were 349 homicides, one-third of which were against immediate family, for an 89 percent increase. Furthermore, 30 percent of veterans with PTSD self-reported an act of aggression in the previous year, with 11 percent reporting at least “one serious violent act.”

This same report provides data that indicates that violence is not limited to American soldiers. A British study of returning Iraq/Afghanistan veterans under the age of 30 found that “20% had a conviction for violent offences compared to 6.7% of civilians the same age.” Another British study found that “soldiers in combat roles were 50% more likely than those in non-combat roles to commit assault or threaten violence on returning home.”

I. Domestic Violence

The same report cited above also studied domestic violence. The available data indicate that veterans’ rate of domestic violence incidents is higher than that of the general population. Combat veterans are responsible for almost 21 percent of domestic violence calls nationwide (and 20 percent of suicide calls). One third of the victims of homicides committed by global war on terror veterans were immediate family. Domestic abuse charges rose 250 percent at Fort Carson 2006–2009, and by 177 percent in the Army 2003–2010.

Patterns of domestic violence appear to be different for veterans than for civilians. Research recognizes three types of violence: violence committed in anger, dissociative violence following a trigger or during a flashback, and violence during sleep or upon awakening. This difference from civilian populations (usually conceptualized as issues around power and control) requires an exploration into the pattern in a veteran’s case and different treatment approaches.

Domestic violence is a major problem in the veteran community. The number of calls related to domestic violence, from individuals affiliated with the military, more than tripled from 2006 to 2011. According to a report of the National Center for PTSD, veterans with PTSD are “significantly more likely to perpetrate violence towards their partner.” Because PTSD-related symptoms are often the cause of domestic violence, traditional batterers programs are not only contra-indicated but cause harm and an increased risk of suicide.8

III. Legal Relevance

Forensic evaluations of veterans often requires a team approach. A complete sociological, psychological, and neuropsychological evaluation is often necessary. A complete investigation into a veteran’s pre-military, military, and post-military experiences is necessary to establish the correct diagnosis and to understand if the diagnoses are legally relevant to competency or mental defenses such as diminished capacity or insanity. This investigation is also required given that Oregon law now allows veteran status to serve as a mitigating factor. Only through a thorough team evaluation can evidence relevant to pretrial negotiations and/or sentencing be obtained.

The legal relevance of the issues presented above vary by case and at different stages of litigation. Early identification of veteran status and pretrial release and treatment is imperative. The impact of PTSD, TBI, and other injuries on trial competency, diminished capacity, actus reus, mens rea, and criminal responsibility must be systematically addressed.

Summary of Steps to Evaluating Veterans

1. Cultural competence

2. Obtain DD-214, VA disability award letter, other records

3. VJOP referral

4. Pretrial release for treatment

5. Sociological/psychological/neuropsychological evaluations for servicemember diversion, or entry onto a veterans docket or into veterans court. Exploration of defenses, mitigation, and sentencing.

IV. Conclusion

Veterans re-acculturating to civilian life face a multitude of challenges. First, there is an assumption that veterans are simply reintegrating, i.e., rejoining civilian life. In fact, they are attempting to re-acculturate into the civilian culture that was systematically stripped from them and replaced by the military culture and values. When re-acculturating, a veteran may no longer understand the values of the civilian culture, and civilians may not even recognize that military culture exists. Moral injury issues emerge as the veteran begins the process of re-acculturation to address the moral impact of combat.

Feeling isolated and morally injured, while also coping with PTSD, depression, and anxiety, often lead to alcohol abuse, isolation, and homelessness. With a broken moral compass and a strong sense of shame, the veteran with PTSD is set up to become involved in the criminal justice system. For most, this translates into risky driving and domestic violence. For some, it translates into reactive aggression that is perceived by civilian society to be the actions of an out-of-control vet. For a few, it translates into risk-taking activities. Solace is often found in substance abuse and in joining high-energy organizations such as motorcycle clubs. Unfortunately, involvement in illegal behavior is common.

But, by the veteran becoming involved in the criminal justice system, the process of re-acculturation and treatment for combat-related injuries can be aided. For most, this is a “wake-up” call that they use to regain their moral compass and get back on track to re-acculturation. Past experience has shown that if there is early intervention pretrial, and/or a well-planned treatment program as part of sentencing, the veteran will succeed at getting well and staying out of the criminal justice system. But for this type of outcome, all parties in the system must work together. This requires an understanding of the individual veteran and his or her sociological history, psychological and neuropsychological condition.

This multidisciplinary evaluation needs to be undertaken not to excuse criminal behavior nor to provide leniency but to ensure that the goals of justice are met, and that our contract with veterans, by which they agreed to risk their lives in return for treatment, is upheld by civilian society. When this occurs, justice is done.
