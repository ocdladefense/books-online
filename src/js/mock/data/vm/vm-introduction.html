Still at War A Guide for Defenders, Prosecutors & Judges Dealing with Oregon’s Veteran Defendant Crisis

This manual is the culmination of the work of 16 authors and co-authors on 15 topics. It provides basic information that is needed for the zealous defense of veterans facing criminal charges, i.e., “veteran defendants,” for the singular purpose of reducing the frequency with which veterans are charged with, convicted of and punished for crime, within the framework of maintaining public safety.

The manual’s chapters do not limit their information to that found in their texts. Instead, the chapters’ texts contain numerous links to literally thousands of pages worth of cases, articles, and the like, which provide information that supplements the chapters themselves.

A significant reason for this manual is that today the vast majority of Americans know little if anything about the military, rendering it virtually a foreign culture. This lack of understanding is unsurprising given that, as an L.A. Times article explains, “less than one-half of 1% of the U.S. population is in the armed services today—the lowest rate since World War II.”

A key to achieving that purpose is practitioners having a basic understanding of military concepts. A lack of understanding can have serious repercussions; for example, it can lead to defense lawyers providing constitutionally deficient representation for their veteran defendant clients.1 Various chapters in the manual provide basic information about military concepts, and Chapter 3 includes a helpful glossary of military terminology.

Problems stemming from this lack of understanding start at the most fundamental level. For example, in her article in the October 2013 issue of the Oregon State Bar Bulletin,2 Melody Finnemore described a 2013 survey conducted by the Pacific Policy and Research Institute which was designed to determine the quality of representation OCDLA member attorneys provide their veteran clients. “A snapshot of the survey responses showed that 74 percent of the veteran clients had been diagnosed with PTSD; [and] 33 percent had been diagnosed with a traumatic brain injury.” Moreover, Finnemore explained, “Nearly 70 percent of [survey] respondents said they believed military service was relevant during the pretrial/trial phases of a veteran’s case, and more than more than 81 percent said it was relevant during mitigation and sentencing.”

Finnemore explained that despite all that, the survey further disclosed,

Just over 43 percent of the defense attorneys who responded to the survey said they ask clients during intake whether they are veterans. Less than half of the respondents said they were familiar with the process of obtaining military records or V.A.3 records for a veteran client. Less than a quarter were familiar with codes for military or V.A. records.

This 43 percent figure is deeply troubling. A lawyer cannot possibly hope to provide proper representation without taking the initial steps of determining which clients are veterans and, when needed, obtaining their military and VA records.4

The troubling data played a crucial role in changing state policy to improve the quality of services the state’s defense bar provides its veteran clients. This includes the Board of Governors’ 2015 approval of a comprehensive revision of the bar’s “Specific Standards for Representation in Criminal & Juvenile Delinquency Cases.” This revision includes provisions that specifically address the proper representation of veteran defendants. Those provisions are detailed in Chapter 1.

After obtaining the client’s military records, the lawyer then faces an assortment of documents containing codes and terminology that most likely will be foreign to the lawyer. But just as defense counsel obtains expert translation services when representing a non-English-speaking client and dealing with foreign language documents, counsel should obtain the services of a military culture expert for help communicating with a veteran client and deciphering the client’s military records. For information about military culture expertise, see Chapter 5.

Moreover, to acquire basic knowledge about military topics, practitioners should consider consulting Veteranspeak, by Rep. Paul Evans, D-Monmouth.5 Amazon explains that Evans’s book “provides perspective and insight into military skills, training and the values that are central to all veterans.”

Finally, legal professionals should recognize that military service may be a causative factor of behavior that ends in legal proceedings other than criminal prosecutions.

Consider the findings of a 2011 California Healthy Kids Survey. They “suggest that military-connected adolescents have much higher rates of feeling sad or hopeless than found in other national studies,” and show that rates of suicidal ideation

were also higher than reported in other studies. Whereas the 2011 [Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System] data reported a rate of 15%, [the survey] found that 24.8% (parent in military) and 26.1% (sibling in the military) of youth reported seriously considered attempting suicide.6

The findings of the Healthy Kids Survey indicate that those who suffer casualties from military service are not limited to those who served in uniform. For example, consider a child who has spent years of his life with his mother or father repeatedly deployed to combat zones,7 worrying if his parent will come home in one piece. The survey findings indicate a likelihood that the child will suffer from depressive symptoms that lead in the extreme to suicidal ideations, or in a less extreme form, to conduct that results in juvenile delinquency allegations.

Or consider how the parent’s repeated deployments might take a toll on the “home front” spouse, causing his or her own depressive symptoms that lead to a dependency action grounded on allegations of child neglect or abuse.

The point is that just as much as legal professionals who work in the criminal justice field, legal professionals who deal with juvenile delinquency or dependency cases must take proactive steps to determine whether a parent or spouse served or is serving in the military, and whether that service was a causative factor in the events leading to delinquency or dependency actions.
