Still at War: A Guide for Defenders, Prosecutors and Judges Dealing with Oregon's Veteran Defendant Crisis

Still at War: A Guide for Defenders, Prosecutors & Judges Dealing with Oregon’s Veteran Defendant Crisis, 2017 edition, with 2023 Update

Chapter 2. Veterans Defense Resource Center

By James Gardner

I. What is the Veterans Defense Resource Center?

The Veterans Defense Resource Center (VDRC) is a publicly funded program specifically designed to train, advise, and assist public defense attorneys and court-appointed attorneys representing veteran defendants in the Oregon criminal justice system. This program was created in response to the nation’s and Oregon’s growing crisis whereby disproportionate numbers of veterans, many with untreated or undiagnosed service-related injuries and illnesses, are lost in the criminal justice system.

Unfortunately, veterans getting lost in the criminal justice system is not new. Every armed conflict had returning veterans who did not receive due care in the criminal justice process.

Often the hidden wounds of war and the ramifications of the injuries do not manifest until years later. Oregon has a veteran population estimated at 8.5 percent. Studies have reported epidemic numbers of Iraq and Afghanistan War veterans with mental health illnesses such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).1 Previous research also found significant numbers of Vietnam War veterans had undiagnosed PTSD.2 The federal Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) and the Institute of Medicine report such veterans are at significant risk of arrest as their trauma symptoms can lead them to criminal conduct.

Oregon has a more specific problem. Sadly, an analysis of Oregon’s prison population as of 2012 showed that veterans comprised 19 percent of that population.3 That imprisonment rate is almost seven times higher than California’s and four times higher than New York’s.

Not every veteran has a mental illness or combat-related injury. However, all veterans deserve competent representation. Competent representation and the ABA model standards require a court-appointed attorney to not only address relevant defenses but to understand the nature of a client’s military service and provide mitigation or other relevant facts as necessary.4 When the nature and quality of the veteran’s service is presented, a lighter sentence will be imposed. It is difficult for a civilian judicial system to understand what it means to serve, the culture of the military, and the stressors of combat.

To combat the growing crisis in Oregon, and with more veterans in prison and jail than necessary, the state Public Defense Services Commission funded the VDRC in late 2015. The VDRC is modeled after the New York State Defenders Association Veterans Defense Program.5 The VDRC exists to help any court-appointed attorney with a veteran client in Oregon. Simply stated the VDRC’s mission is to ensure that each veteran has an effective advocate, each veteran’s story is properly presented to the judge, jury, and prosecutor, and that veterans get the treatment they need and deserve. The VDRC website is www.lanepds.org. From there you will find links to resources and expert witnesses referenced later in this chapter.

II. What services can the VDRC provide?

The VDRC provides a wide variety of assistance to attorneys and veteran defendants including: building trust, determining the nature and quality of the military service, finding and obtaining service records, developing case strategies in defense and mitigation, assisting with motion practice, determining collateral consequences, and training.

A. Building Trust with a Veteran Client

The VDRC can go a long way in building trust and credibility with a veteran client. Very few individuals except veterans themselves understand what it means to serve. The VDRC is built on veterans, can give a client-centered approach to your representation, help you interview the client, and help you build a multidisciplinary team.

B. Determining the Nature and Extent of Military Service

Once a client is determined to be a veteran, the nature and extent of the client’s military service must be determined. We recommend using the Veteran Intake Form, available as an appendix and at the VDRC webpage here.

The majority of attorneys know little about the military, and many military concepts and terminology seem to be a foreign language and culture. This creates a communication barrier that may disrupt the attorney-client relationship and lead to claims of inadequate and ineffective assistance of counsel.6 VDRC can help explain terminology, military structure, and duty assignments.

For example, consider a veteran defendant who tells his lawyer that he “was deployed as part of Operation New Dawn and operated a 240B” or whose military records say that his “MOS” was “13-B” and his rank was “E-4.” The lawyer most likely would be clueless about the meaning of those terms. At the VDRC we can translate military terminology. We can tell you this soldier was deployed to Iraq from 2010 to 2011, was a junior enlisted soldier, artilleryman by occupation, and most likely was on patrol as a machine gunner who saw combat. Obviously, this essential information could help build a defense or substantial mitigation for the veteran defendant.

C. Service Records

All military service should be corroborated through service records. These records are the gateway to convincing the district attorney for a dismissal or advantageous plea offer. The VDRC can help explain the easiest and best way to find aclient’s service records.

If the client is presently on active duty, in the reserves or the National Guard, service can be documented with a current copy of their military orders. The VDRC has points of contact throughout Oregon and can attempt to obtain these records with a release from the client. Clients with prior active-duty service will have been issued a discharge document called a DD-214. This will be true even for those who served on active duty in the National Guard or reserves. For clients with prior reserve service in the National Guard they will be issued a NGB Form 22 (instead of a DD-214). Therefore the initial documents to obtain are the current military orders, DD-214, or NGB Form 22.

Other military records can be very helpful in determining the witnesses you need and sentencing material. Any awards, certificates, badges, and evaluation reports are instrumental. Typically listed on any award or evaluation report are specific details of your client’s meritorious services and potential witnesses to the events or good military character. If the client is still on active duty or in the National Guard or reserves, these records can easily be obtained by downloading them from the veterans Official Military File Online (often called the “OMPF” or “AMMHR”). If your client has prior service, hopefully they have their records. If not, a letter or Request Pertaining to Military Records, Standard Form 180 (SF-180) (available in Chapter 4 and here) can be sent via fax or mail or can be requested online in order to obtain the client’s military service records. Emergency and expedited requests can be made; however, the standard process usually takes six months or more. For information about how to expedite records requests, see Chapter 4.

D. VA or Other Medical/Treatment Records

The VDRC also has contact with local VA medical facilities and treatment centers through the Veterans Justice Outreach Program (VJOP) representatives. Local VJOP offices are available through the VA website here.

If the client reports having sought medical care from the VA, then VJOP staff can obtain the veteran’s medical records. A form called VA Form 10-5345, Request for and Authorization to Release Medical Records or Health Information, can be used to obtain copies of the client’s VA records. An example of VA Form 10-5345 is available in Chapter 4 and at the VDRC website here. The VA Form 10-5345 must be sent to the client’s treatment facility. The list and contact information of Oregon treatment facilities can be found at http://www.va.gov/directory. Obviously, a client’s VA medical records is the gateway to an appropriate defense and mitigation.

E. Defense and Mitigation Strategies

The VDRC can help explain effective defense and mitigation strategies based on your client’s individual military history and charges. Specifically, the VDRC can explain how the client’s PTSD, traumatic brain injury (TBI) or other mental illness and individual military service, may have affected the client’s behavior.

These matters will be specifically addressed in other chapters; however, a brief discussion of how the VDRC can be helpful is included here. For example, training for and service in the military total institution (see Chapter 5) may have profoundly affected a veteran’s behavior after discharge and reentry into civilian society. By itself, military training and service will contribute to what civilian society—but not military society—would consider antisocial and even criminal behavior. When the training and service is combined with PTSD, TBI or both, the chances of a veteran engaging in what civilian society considers antisocial or criminal behavior reach a depressingly high level. For an examination of how training and experience in the military total institution actually factored into a veteran’s civilian conduct that led to criminal charges, see Brief of Amicus Curiae, The Bunker Project, State v. James Anthony Harrell, 353 Or 247 (2013), which is available as Appendix B in Chapter 12 and online here.7

Additionally, for veteran defendants who recently served or are in the military (be it active duty, the National Guard, or the reserves), there is one last component of what the VDRC calls “good military character,” which is an attempt to convince the trier of fact of the legitimacy of a claim for special consideration (further discussed in Chapter 10). The VDRC can help interview and contact the veteran defendant’s military peers or colleagues to determine whether they are willing to vouch for the defendant. Bear in mind that they may not always be willing, given the nature of the charge and the individual defendant. However, this information can be essential to the client’s representation.

Finally, the VDRC can assist defense counsel by writing important case-specific legal documents, including in-depth mitigation memoranda, motions for expert witnesses, motions to postpone, motions to compel, or other legal memoranda.

F. Veteran Diversion

Veteran diversion is both underutilized and unknown by many defense counsel. By way of background, in February 2010 the legislature passed Senate Bill 999 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2010, chapter 25). This legislation enhanced the district attorney authority to divert servicemembers’ cases from criminal prosecution. Chapter 1 discusses the details of this diversion option.

The VDRC can advocate or negotiate with the district attorney and the court for a veteran defendant’s entry into diversion. For example, the diversion statutes direct district attorneys to consider several factors when determining whether to offer diversion, including “[t]he impact of diversion upon the community.”8 The VDRC can interview witnesses and obtain needed evidence to demonstrate the impact on the community, unit and mission. Additionally, where the district attorney refuses to consider an otherwise eligible servicemember defendant for diversion, the VDRC can help litigate motions to compel consideration or justification for denial.

G. Veterans Treatment Court

2023 Update:

There are currently six counties that offer Veterans Treatment Courts, those being Marion, Lane, Klamath, Malheur, Washington, and Columbia. [Information provided by Michael Hajarizadeh, the current director of the Veterans Defense Resource Center.]

[End Update]

The VDRC has points of contact for Veterans Court coordinators and VA representatives in every county which offers Veterans Court. The VDRC can advise what Veterans Treatment Court entails—the process, example petitions, and the benefits and drawbacks of participating in Veterans Court. More specifically, the VDRC can play an active role in advocating for the client’s best interest to enter and successfully complete Veterans Court.

Unfortunately, not all veterans are eligible for VTC. This is because each county has different standards on eligibility. For example, in Lane County a veteran’s eligibility for Veterans Court is initially up to the discretion of the assistant district attorney who charged the defendant. A veteran can have no prior criminal history and suffer from PTSD, but if the veteran is charged with fourth-degree assault, the assistant district attorney may deny eligibility. However, once Veterans Court eligibility is initially approved by the assistant district attorney, the court then has final approval for entry.

The VDRC can help advocate for entry into the VTC and assist in convincing the DA to allow initial eligibility. In the example above, through a variety of ways the VDRC can help explain the needed connection between the assault and the veteran’s military service. The VDRC can attempt to mitigate the client’s actions or explain why what may be “criminal” for a civilian is a normal learned behavior for a veteran needing treatment.

Chapter 15 addresses Veterans Treatment Court, and the Lane County court in particular, in more detail. However, bear in mind this can be a great benefit to your client to receive the medical and/or psychological care they require and any other assistance they need to stay out of trouble and lead productive lives. Veterans Courts have repeatedly demonstrated success in treating illness and reducing recidivism.9

H. Veteran Treatment Resources and Expert Witnesses

Another key goal of the VDRC is to provide the treatment resources and other services for veterans to stay out of the criminal justice system and lead productive lives. The VDRC has a list of available resources online at the website here.

Additionally, depending on the specific needs of an individual case, the VDRC has vetted expert witnesses to testify in veteran cases. The VDRC has found military specialized investigators and expert witnesses in PTSD, including that from military sexual assault, and experts in TBI and other military related mental health illnesses. A list of available experts can be found on the center’s website here.

I. Collateral Consequences

Special attention should be made to cases involving veteran defendants who are currently in the military or receiving VA benefits. Just as defense counsel must make an adequate and effective effort to protect defendant-clients from the immigration consequence of deportation,10 counsel must make an adequate and effective effort to protect veteran defendant clients from negative consequences to their military careers and VA benefits. The VDRC can help explain and assist defense counsel to understand collateral consequences of convictions and a lengthy incarceration.

For example, unless properly handled, a misdemeanor domestic violence charge may result in a client’s forfeiture of the right to possess firearms. This would likely end the career of a client currently serving in the military.11 The difference between pleading “recklessly” to fourth-degree assault as opposed to “knowingly” could save the defendant’s military career.12 The VDRC can talk to the superiors in the defendant’s chain of command and the local judge advocate, to assist in determining if a conviction will impact a client’s military career, and what might be done to avoid that impact.

The VDRC can also assist in determining if incarceration will terminate or suspend a veteran client’s VA benefits, and if so for how long. The VA can pay certain benefits to veterans who are incarcerated in federal, state, or local penal institutions; however, the amount depends on the type of benefit and applicable laws and regulations.

Detailed information about how incarceration may affect continuation of VA disability benefits is covered more fully in Chapter 11.

J. Continuing Legal Education (CLE) and Trainings

Throughout the year the VDRC conducts site visits to public defender offices and court-appointed attorneys to train, educate and provide advice on the most up-to-date issues surrounding veterans in criminal court and to help connect veteran clients with service programs. The VDRC provides Oregon-approved CLEs approximately once every quarter to counties in need of training and CLE. The most up-to-date CLE schedule is available at on the website here.

III. Summary

The VDRC is a free resource, at your disposal, providing expertise in all matters related to veteran defendants. The representation of a veteran defendant will be more effective with the VDRC. We must all ensure that each veteran has an effective advocate; each veteran’s story is properly presented to a judge, jury, and prosecutor; and veterans get the treatment they need and deserve.
