Chapter 4. Military Records and Witnesses: The Basics of the Privacy Act, FOIA, and Touhy regulations and their Application to Criminal Defense

By Bryan Boender

The purpose of this chapter is to provide criminal defense lawyers with a basic understanding of the laws governing the release of United States military and federal Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) information and material.

Defense attorneys should routinely seek out a client’s personnel, medical, and mental health records; records pertaining to military training and operations in which the client participated; and access to military or VA employee witnesses. The basic legal framework for accessing information held by the federal government is found in the following authorities: the Privacy Act of 1974, 5 USC § 552a; the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), 5 USC § 552; and “Touhy Regulations” based on the federal “Housekeeping Statute” found in 5 USC § 301 and the U.S. Supreme Court’s holding in United States ex rel. Touhy v. Ragen, 340 US 462 (1951). These authorities apply to the military and the VA.

The following sections provide an overview and application of each of these authorities in the context of representing servicemembers and veterans.

I. The Privacy Act of 1974

The Privacy Act of 1974, 5 USC §552a (2012) (hereafter Privacy Act), protects servicemembers’ personnel and medical records. The Privacy Act regulates the collection, maintenance, use, and dissemination of personal information held by federal executive branch agencies. The Privacy Act prohibits the disclosure of personally identifiable records maintained without the written request or consent of the individual to whom the record pertains.1 In other words, information protected by the Privacy Act is subjected to its “no disclosure without consent” rule. However, there are a number of exceptions to that general rule.

The Privacy Act provides 12 statutory exceptions that permit disclosure without consent of the person to whom the records pertain.2 Criminal defense lawyers should be concerned with three important exceptions to the “no disclosure without consent” rule. First, the FOIA exception. Second, the law enforcement exception, 5 USC § 552a(b)(7). Third, the “court order” exception, 5 USC § 552a(b)(11).

A. The Privacy Act’s FOIA Exception

The Privacy Act of 1974 never prohibits a disclosure that the Freedom of Information Act actually requires. See News-Press v. DHS, 489 F3d 1173, 1189 (11th Cir. 2007) (“The net effect of the interaction between the two statutes is that where the FOIA requires disclosure, the Privacy Act will not stand in its way, but where the FOIA would permit withholding under an exemption, the Privacy Act makes such withholding mandatory upon the agency.”); see also Sikes v. United States, [http://www.leagle.com/decision/In%20Adv%20FDCO%20140912-000376/SIKES%20v.%20U.S. 987 F Supp2d 1355], 1372 n 14 (SD Ga 2013) (finding that because the FOIA required disclosure of a list of names of individuals invited to a ceremony at which a Navy officer was sworn in as Chief of Naval Operations, the Privacy Act did not bar disclosure). As a result, an agency presented with a request that is not properly withheld under any FOIA exemption must disclose the information to the requestor. However, if a FOIA exemption applies to a given Privacy Act protected record, then the agency is barred from its disclosure. See e.g. Dept. of Defense v. Federal Labor Relations Auth’y, 510 US 487, 502 (1994).

B. The Privacy Act’s Law Enforcement Exception

The law enforcement exception permits disclosure to federal, state, or local law enforcement agencies for “civil or criminal law enforcement activity” upon written request “specifying the particular portion desired and the law enforcement activity for which the records is sought.”3 For state and local law enforcement or prosecutors, the Privacy Act’s law enforcement exception provides a streamlined method for obtaining a criminal defendant’s military or VA records. While that may be permissible under the Privacy Act, the Oregon Constitution could say otherwise.

Where the state obtains a defendant’s military or VA records without consent, it may implicate the defendant’s privacy interests under Article I, section 9, of the Oregon Constitution. In State v. Ghim, 360 Or 425, 437-38 (2016), the Oregon Supreme Court questioned whether a person has a constitutionally protected privacy interest in information held by third parties. In Ghim, a criminal case involving a spouse’s banking records, the court discussed whether an existing privacy interest may present a contextual question.4 The court referenced evolving Fourth Amendment jurisprudence.5 The above authority presents a novel issue in terms of whether a defendant could suppress the state’s use of his military or VA records. Regardless, a criminal defendant should litigate the suppression issues concerning any evidence obtained from his or her military records without consent.

C. The Privacy Act’s “Court Order” Exception

The Privacy Act’s “court order” exception permits disclosure of records pursuant to a judicially authorized subpoena. The “court order” exception works in the following way: Agencies may disclose Privacy Act–protected information pursuant to the order of a court of competent jurisdiction.6 The Privacy Act “cannot be used to block the normal course of court proceedings, including court-ordered discovery.”7 Subpoenas only fall within the meaning of a “court order” when they are “specifically approved” by a judge.8 Thus, the court itself must approve a subpoena.9 A state court order may not meet the act’s “court of competent jurisdiction” requirement, where a court orders disclosure of a nonparty federal agency’s records. This is because the doctrine of “sovereign immunity” may preclude state court jurisdiction over a federal agency or official.10

These issues are explored in greater detail in the section on Touhy requests below because disclosure of records into court may necessarily involve witness testimony.

D. The Privacy Act in Practice: The Standard Form 180 (SF-180) and VA 10-5345

Consent for the release of Privacy Act–protected records held by the military or VA is typically given through a standardized, form-driven release of information. The military branches and the National Archives utilize the Standard Form 180 (SF-180) (Request Pertaining to Military Records) to authorize the release of military personnel records (see Appendix A). In addition to the SF-180, the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs also utilizes the VA 10-5345 (Request for and Authorization to Release Medical Records or Health Information, a sample of this form is included in the appendices to this chapter and online here). The SF-180 and VA 10-5345 are the standardized release of information forms for requesting a client’s personnel and medical records from the military or VA.11

In order to complete the SF-180, the defense should include at least the client’s legal name, social security number, branch of service, dates of service, and locations of any hospitalizations. The SF-180 form includes detailed instructions and provides the addresses for where to send the form. In practice, a client’s own request or consent permits the release of personnel records held by federal agencies, including the military branches, the Department of Defense, and the VA. An authorized representative such as the attorney or the next-of-kin of a deceased veteran may also request his or her military records. In order for the records to be released to an authorized representative, the SF-180 must be accompanied by an authorization letter or power of attorney. A next-of-kin must submit proof of death in order to receive a deceased veteran’s records.

Best practices for requesting military personnel or medical records should include a detailed cover letter to supplement the SF-180 (also available in the appendices to this chapter) with a signature from the client in a section authorizing the records to be released to the attorney, and counsel should also include an unsworn declaration authenticating the client’s signature and consent for release of records pursuant to 28 USC § 1746. A certificate of counsel may be written directly into the cover letter in order to authenticate the client’s signature and authorization to collect records on his or her behalf. Under federal law, section 1746 permits unsworn declarations “[w]herever, under any law of the United States or under any rule, regulation, order, or requirement made pursuant to law, any matter is required or permitted to be supported, evidence, established, or proved by the sworn declaration, verification, certificate, statement, oath, or affidavit, in writing of the person making the same[.]” The text of the statute further provides the necessary language for making the declaration.

II. The Freedom of Information Act

The Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), 5 USC 552 (2006 & Supp. IV 2010), provides the public an enforceable right to access information held by the federal government, which includes the military and the VA. Under FOIA, the definition of “records” includes “any information that would be an agency record . . . when maintained by an agency in any format, including an electronic format.”12 An attorney making a FOIA request should be familiar with the basics of making a request and the relevant exceptions to FOIA.13

A. Making FOIA Requests

A proper FOIA request must meet two requirements: 1) the request must “reasonably describe” the records sought and 2) it must be “made in accordance with published rules stating the time, place, fees (if any), and procedures to be followed.”14 The first requirement is satisfied if agency staff can reasonably ascertain exactly which records are being requested and where to locate such records.15 However, a request must be written with specificity and may not be overly broad.16

Agencies have time limits to meet when replying to FOIA requests. When an agency receives a FOIA request it “must determine within twenty [working] days . . . whether to comply with such request.”17 The agency must at least indicate within the statutory time period the scope of the documents it will produce and any exemptions it will claim with respect to any documents to be withheld, but does not actually have to produce the documents within twenty working days.18 However, once an agency determines whether it will comply with a request, it must promptly make the records available to the requestor.19 A requestor may also request expedited processing of their FOIA request.

Pursuant to FOIA and U.S. Department of Defense policy, a requestor who demonstrates a “compelling need” may receive expedited processing of their FOIA requests.20 Under the DoD FOIA implementing regulations, a requestor demonstrates a “compelling need.”21 A “compelling need” means “failure to obtain the records on an expedited basis could reasonably be expected to pose an imminent threat to the life or physical safety of an individual.”22 Other reasons that merit expedited processing by DoD components are an imminent loss of substantial due process rights and humanitarian need.23 A request for expedited processing requires a statement certified by the requestor that demonstrates the compelling need.24

B. Information Protected by FOIA Exemptions

The FOIA has nine statutory exemptions to its presumptive mandatory disclosure rule, found at 5 USC § 552(b)(1)–(9). These exemptions are typically referred to by number (“exemption 1,” “exemption 2,” etc.). For purposes of this chapter, three of the nine exemptions are explained in greater detail: the national defense or foreign policy “classified” information (“exemption 1”); the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency (“exemption 2”); and the law enforcement purposes (“exemption 7”).25

1. FOIA Exemption 1: National Security

The government may invoke national security concerns in order to deny access to records that it deems “classified.” Under FOIA exemption 1, information that is properly classified pursuant to an Executive Order is exempt from disclosure under FOIA.26 The FOIA exempts from disclosure information that has been classified “under criteria established by an Executive Order to be kept secret in the interest of national defense or foreign policy” and is “in fact properly classified pursuant to such Executive Order.”27

As a result of exemption 1, when making FOIA requests on behalf of military servicemembers and veterans, practitioners should understand that national security interests may prohibit disclosure of classified material. However, this FOIA exemption does not protect information that is merely classifiable information that meets the substantive requirements of the current Executive Order but has not actually been classified under it. Schoenman v. FBI, 575 F Supp2d 136, 151-52 (D DC 2008); Assassination Archives & Research Ctr. v. CIA, 177 F Supp 2d 1, 7-8 (D DC 2001).

2. FOIA Exemption 2: Personnel Rules and Practices

The government does not need to disclose information that relates solely to internal personnel rules. FOIA exemption 2 protects the disclosure of trivial administrative matters that are of no genuine public interest. That is, material that is “related solely to the internal personnel rules and practices of an agency” are exempt from disclosure.28 In Dept. of the Air Force v. Rose, 425 US 352, 369 (1976), the Court construed this exemption to cover agency material that is so routine or trivial that it could not be “subject to . . . genuine and significant public interest.”

3. FOIA Exemption 7: Law Enforcement Purposes

The government may deny access to information that serves a law enforcement purpose. FOIA exemption 7 protects from disclosure material that is “records or information compiled for law enforcement purposes,” but only so far as one or more of six enumerated types of harm would result.29 Each of the six types of harm contemplated by FOIA are described below.

  • Exemption (7)(A) permits withholding of law enforcement information that would interfere with an ongoing enforcement investigation. See, e.g., National Labor Relations Bd. v. Robbins Tire & Rubber Co., 437 US 214, 224 (1978) (holding that the exemption applies where government demonstrates how records “would interfere with a pending enforcement proceeding”).

  • Exemption (7)(B) provides for withholding material that would interfere with a person’s right to a fair trial. A two-part test is used to determine the applicability of exemption (7)(B): “(1) that a trial or adjudication is pending or truly imminent; and (2) that it is more probable than not that disclosure of the material sought would seriously interfere with the fairness of those proceedings.” Washington Post Co. v. Dept. of Justice, 863 F2d 96, 101-02 (DC Cir. 1988).

  • Exemption (7)(C) implicates a person’s privacy interest in law enforcement records concerning them. See, e.g., Fiduccia v. Dept. of Justice, 185 F3d 1035, 1047-48 (9th Cir. 1999) (protecting records of FBI searches of two named persons’ home), Nation Magazine v. United States Customs Serv., 71 F3d 885, 896 (DC Cir. 1995) (material in investigatory records revealing subjects, witnesses, and informants in agency file are exempt from
    disclosure).

  • Exemption (7)(D) protects identities of confidential sources. The purpose of this exemption is to “protect confidential sources from retaliation that may result from the disclosure of their participation in law enforcement activities.” McDonnell v. United States, 4 F3d 1227, 1258 (3d Cir. 1993).

  • Exemption (7)(E) prohibits the disclosure of information that would reveal law enforcement tactics, techniques or procedures, if disclosure would reasonably risk circumvention of the law.

  • Exemption (7)(F) permits withholding of information that could reasonably be expected to endanger the life or physical safety of any person.

Practice Tip: Target your FOIA request appropriately. A military branch or the Department of Defense may have several different FOIA programs serving different organizations. These various military branches and federal agencies may also accept FOIA requests as both web-based forms and letters. Do your research on where to target your request before making it.

III. Touhy Requests

Attorneys seeking interviews or testimony from military personnel must develop a working knowledge of the appropriate agency or department’s “Touhy regulations” in order to properly request or even subpoena records and witnesses. These regulations are based on the federal Housekeeping Statute, 5 USC § 301, and the United States Supreme Court’s holding in United States ex rel. Touhy v. Ragen, 340 US 462 (1951). The military department or federal agency’s Touhy regulations will control how and from whom a request may be made. However, whether the client’s Touhy request comes in the form of a letter, a subpoena or both is best determined by a requestor’s strategy and purpose in seeking the records. In either case, substantial legal research is necessary to narrowly tailor an effective Touhy demand, especially when seeking the in-person testimony of a servicemember or federal employee.

In Touhy, an inmate at the Illinois State Penitentiary sought production of documents and testimony from an FBI agent for use in a federal habeas corpus case to show that his conviction had been obtained by fraud. 34 US at 463-65. Citing an order promulgated by the Department of Justice pursuant to the federal Housekeeping Statute, the agent refused to produce the requested material.30 The Court held that the statute was constitutional and the regulation was a proper exercise of agency authority.31 The federal Housekeeping Statute merely provides agency heads with the authority to make regulations for “the custody, use, and preservation” of agency records.32 It also explicitly states: “This section does not authorize withholding information from the public or limiting the availability of records to the public.” Since Touhy, however, federal agencies have enacted rather strict regulations governing their response to subpoenas and information requested in connection with litigation.

The U.S. Army, U.S. Navy, Air Force, Marine Corps, and the VA all maintain detailed Touhy regulations governing the release of official information in connection with litigation where the military or agency is not a party, such as state criminal matters. The army Touhy regulations are found at 32 CFR § 516 Subpart G (codifying Army Regulation (AR) 27-40 Chapter 7). The navy and Marine Corps Touhy regulations are found at 32 CFR part 725. Additional guidance on how the navy and Marine Corps implement Touhy regulations may be found in its Manual of the Judge Advocate General (JAGMAN) at JAGINST 5800.7F (June 26, 2012). The Air Force Touhy regulations are taken directly from the Department of Defense regulations, which are found at 32 CFR part 97. See Air Force Instruction 51-301. The VA maintains its Touhy regulations at 38 CFR §§ 14.800 – 14.810.

In practice, criminal defense lawyers should expect some resistance from military department or VA witnesses when seeking in-person testimony in a state trial court. While in some circumstances prosecutors may be able to obtain testimony from federal agents through a less formal request, defense counsel could work with military branch or federal agency counsel to help craft a Touhy request.

The best practice is to first use a demand letter applying the applicable agency regulations, and follow up with agency counsel to negotiate the limits of the witness’s testimony. If the defense believes it must seek to compel a military or federal government witness into state court, then the defense team should be prepared to bring a complaint into federal district court under the Administrative Procedures Act (APA), 5 USC §§ 551-59, 701-76, 1305, 3105, 3344, 5372, 7521. The APA expressly provides a private right of action to enforce federal rights against federal agencies.33 But before filing a complaint in federal district court, it may be helpful to have first issued a pretrial subpoena or subpoena duces tecum for the records and testimony sought pursuant to ORS 136.580(2) and State v. Cartwright, 336 Or 408 (2004).

The United States Attorneys’ Manual provides the government’s playbook for responding to Touhy requests. In some circumstances, the United States Attorneys’ Office may get involved when the state court subpoena cannot be resolved between counsel and the agency. If that occurs, practitioners should expect a federal court to recast the subpoena as an administrative demand under the Administrative Procedures Act.34 As a result, the military or a federal agency in receipt of a state court subpoena will treat it as a Touhy demand. The U.S. Attorneys’ Manual advises, “If a state court subpoena cannot be resolved amicably, including when a state court or litigant seeks to enforce the subpoena, the assigned attorney should timely remove the proceeding to federal court and seek to overturn the order on grounds of sovereign immunity.” Based on guidance found in the U.S. Attorneys’ Manual and from practical experience, when responding to state court subpoenas the military, VA, and other federal entities routinely take the position that sovereign immunity and the Supremacy Clause, US Const, Art VI, bars the enforcement of state court subpoenas against federal employees.

As explained above, a special problem arises for criminal defense lawyers in state court who attempt to use a state court subpoena to secure a military or federal agent. In that case, criminal defense counsel should also be prepared to litigate the issue in federal court because the interplay between the Sixth Amendment’s Compulsory Process Clause and the Supremacy Clause is still unclear. Where military service personnel or VA employees possess “official information” that is favorable to the accused in a criminal prosecution, however, the Sixth Amendment may prevail. In any case, criminal or civil, a subpoena should not be used unless counsel first complies with the applicable Touhy regulations because federal courts have quashed subpoenas, even in criminal cases, without reaching the constitutional arguments where the requestors failed to comply with Touhy regulations.

In United States v. Wallace, 32 F3d 921, 929 (5th Cir. 1994), the defendants “contend[ed] that the trial court’s quashing of the subpoenas [to government agents] denied them a fair trial and the right to present evidence on their behalf and compel witnesses to testify.” The court held that the Touhy regulations were valid and mandatory.35 The court declined to reach the constitutional question, because the defendants failed to comply with the regulations. The court also noted that the “necessity or value of the two agents’ testimony was questionable.”36 It then “h[e]ld in the alternative that the exclusion of the two witnesses may be upheld under the trial court’s power to control the trial and limit testimony that would be cumulative and marginally relevant.”37

In the criminal defense context, the take away from Wallace is that a defendant could still raise a constitutional challenge where he or she has attempted to comply with the Touhy regulations. It also seems likely that courts will favor a constitutional argument where the defendant’s rights are more clearly prejudiced by the exclusion of the testimony sought. Criminal defense counsel should consider these issues before using a subpoena to compel testimony from military personnel or VA employees. Considering the value of defense and mitigation evidence grounded in military service, a subpoena that includes a Touhy request for clearly favorable evidence from military personnel or VA employees may prevail. The best course of action, however, may be to negotiate with agency counsel to obtain the material sought.

Conclusion

Criminal defense lawyers must have a basic understanding of the federal laws regulating the release of military records. The basic legal framework for accessing information held by the federal government is found in the following authorities: the Privacy Act of 1974, 5 USC § 552A; the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA), 5 USC § 552; and “Touhy Regulations” based on the federal “Housekeeping Statute” found in 5 USC § 301 and the Supreme Court’s decision in United States ex rel. Touhy v. Ragen. These authorities apply to the military and the VA. These laws regulate the release of the defendant’s own personnel and medical records, records and information concerning military training and operations, and access to witnesses.
