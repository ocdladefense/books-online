Chapter 1. Legal Considerations, Including Performance Standards and Diversion Options

By Jesse Wm. Barton

Jennelle Meeks Barton edited the 2023 chapter update.

Introduction

This chapter analyzes the various laws that authorize special legal considerations in veteran defendant cases. The chapter also analyzes the Oregon State Bar’s performance standards, which effectively incorporate these special legal considerations.

I. The “Servicemember” Concept

Many of the state laws, as well as the performance standards, use the term “servicemember.” The term has a technical legal definition that is important to understand.

The term “servicemember” was created for use in Senate Bill 999 (2010). That bill, which is addressed below in greater detail, enhances district attorney authority to divert veteran defendant cases from prosecution. The definition states,

(4) “Servicemember” means a person who: (a) Is a member of the Armed Forces of the United States, the reserve components of the Armed Forces of the United States or the National Guard; or (b)(A) Served as a member of the Armed Forces of the United States, the reserve components of the Armed Forces of the United States or the National Guard; and (B) Received an honorable discharge, a general discharge under honorable conditions or a discharge under other than honorable conditions.

As seen, a key part of the definition is a veteran’s discharge level. Generally speaking, there are five discharge levels. Running from highest to lowest, they are:

1) Honorable 2) General (under honorable conditions) 3) Other than honorable conditions 4) Bad conduct 5) Dishonorable

The definition of servicemember excludes only the bottom two of these discharge levels.1 Both of these discharge types, bad conduct and dishonorable, require military courts-martial convictions.

The fact that the definition includes the first three levels is significant. Many jurisdictions limit the provision of benefits or special legal consideration to veterans who were honorably discharged.2 But far too frequently servicemembers are discharged less than honorably for demonstrably unfair reasons, which impairs those veterans’ ability to receive benefits or special legal consideration. Although veterans later may seek to upgrade their discharges, the process for doing so is complicated, time-consuming and uncertain. By including the top three discharge levels while excluding the bottom two (which require courts martial convictions), the “servicemember” definition minimizes the frequency of unfairly denying benefits or special legal consideration.

2023 Update

Page 1 of the original edition of this manual states that the criminal code has a single, technical legal definition of the term "servicemember." But owing to the passage of House Bill 2462 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2019, chapter 86), the term, discussed in greater detail in part V, now has two different definitions that apply in different situations.

The original definition of "servicemember," which is codified at ORS 135.881(4), excludes veterans who received bad conduct or dishonorable discharges. But the HB 2462 definition, which is codified at ORS 135.985(1), states: "As used in this section, 'servicemember' means a person who is a member, or who served as a member, of the Armed Forces of the United States, the reserve components of the Armed Forces of the United States or the National Guard."

As seen, ORS 135.985(1) definition's introductory phrase, "[a]s used in this section," limits the definition's application to HB 2462's other provisions. Moreover, ORS 135.985(1)'s definition of "servicemember" includes anyone who (i) is serving in the military; or (ii) has served in the military (a veteran), regardless of discharge type. Thus, ORS 135.985(1)'s definition is broader than ORS 135.881(4)'s definition. HB 2462's other provisions apply even to veterans who received bad conduct or dishonorable discharges.

II. Oregon State Bar Performance Standards

The Oregon State Bar’s “Specific Standards for Representation in Criminal & Juvenile Delinquency Cases” includes a series of standards pertaining to the representation of veteran defendants.3 These include:

  • During an initial interview with the client, a lawyer should…obtain information concerning…the client’s history of service in the military and the client’s physical and mental health, educational and military services records.

(Standard 2.2, items 6.a.2 and 6.a.3).4 Only by obtaining this information may defense counsel hope to provide a constitutionally proficient standard of representation. Chapter 3 explains how to obtain these records. Chapters 12 and 13 explain how to use those records to develop pretrial negotiation, trial, and sentence mitigation strategies.

  • Understand and advise the client concerning the availability of…diversion agreements (including servicemember status).

(Standard 8.1, item 1.c.) This standard is intended to ensure that whenever possible counsel will seek the specific types of diversions from prosecutions, legally available in servicemember cases, which are discussed later in this chapter.

These diversion programs include veteran treatment courts (in those counties that have such courts5). The original manual's Chapter 15 discusses veteran treatment courts in detail. Moreover, as discussed in part VI of this chapter's update, in order to enter into a veterans treatment court, the 2021 Legislature's Senate Bill 218 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2021, chapter 330) enacted procedures that require servicemembers to sacrifice their rights to fundamental fairness in the proceedings.

[End Update]

  • In understanding the sentence or disposition applicable to a client’s case, a lawyer should …

c. Obtain from the client and others information such as the client’s … current or prior military service.

(Standard 8.1, item 2.) This standard is intended to ensure that whenever possible counsel will seek sentence mitigation, legally available in servicemember cases, which is discussed later in this chapter.

  • Direct counsel to take steps to avoid collateral consequences that could prohibit further military service.

(Standard 1.2, item 4, and Standard 6.1, item 9.)

Example: A client who is in the military faces domestic violence charges. If convicted, the federal Lautenberg Act will bar the client from possessing firearms, which could end the client’s military career. Counsel should take steps to try to avoid application of the Lautenberg Act.6

It must be recognized that although the bar’s performance standards purportedly “are not intended, nor should they be used, to establish a legal standard of care,” they substantially mirror the Oregon Rules of Professional Conduct. To that extent, the standards have a mandatory characteristic.7 This is particularly pertinent to attorneys whose work is wholly or partially funded by the Public Defense Services Account (PDSA).8 The Public Defense Services Commission (PDSC) oversees the PDSA. ORS 151.225(1) and ORS 151.216(1) authorize the PDSC to

(f) Adopt policies, procedures, standards and guidelines regarding: …

(F) Professional qualifications for counsel appointed to represent public defense clients; (G) Performance for legal representation; (H) The contracting of public defense services.

Pursuant to that authority, the PDSC adopted “Qualification Standards for Court-Appointed Counsel to Represent Financially Eligible Persons at State Expense.” PDSC Standard III.5 provides:

Subject to the provisions of Standard V, the appointing authority shall appoint only those attorneys who: …Have read, understood and agree to observe applicable provisions of the current edition of the Oregon State Bar’s Performance Standards for Counsel in Criminal…Cases.

(Emphasis added.) As seen, in cases involving defendants represented by court-appointed counsel, PDSC Standard III.5 makes adherence to the bar’s performance standards contractually mandatory. This is true also of counsel who, although they themselves are privately retained, obtain PDSA funding for such things as expert witness services.9

Finally, although the bar’s performance standards and PDSC Standard III.5 impose no contractual obligations on attorneys who do not receive PDSA funding, court-appointed counsel represent the vast majority of criminal defendants.10 Consequently, court-appointed counsel’s contractually obliged adherence to the bar standards should play a primary role in setting the standard of care for the entire criminal defense community. For this reason it should be incumbent upon all criminal defense lawyers to treat the performance standards as mandatory.

A. District Attorney Diversion Authority

This is the first diversion authority that OSB Standard 8.1, implementation item 1.c addresses. The 2010 Legislative Assembly passed Senate Bill 999 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2010, chapter 25; codified at ORS 135.881, et seq.), which enhances district attorney authority to divert veteran defendant cases from prosecution.11 It declares “servicemembers” eligible for an SB 999 diversion, so long as they:

  • Pay a program fee of $100 (which may be waived if they are indigent, or may be paid “on an installment basis”).12
  • Are not facing a driving under the influence charge.13
  • Have not previously participated in a DA diversion.14
  • Are not charged with first-degree sexual abuse, or with first- or second-degree rape, sodomy, or sexual penetration.15
  • Are not charged with a Class A or B felony involving “physical injury,” or with any crime involving “serious physical injury.”16
  • Are not charged with a “domestic violence” crime “as defined in ORS 135.230,”17 involving an alleged victim who, at the time of the alleged crime, had a pending protective order against the servicemember.18

In situations where a servicemember is charged with a domestic violence crime but is diversion eligible because the alleged victim did not have a pending protective order, SB 999 requires the servicemember:

  • To plead guilty or no contest to the domestic-violence charge.19
  • To serve a two-year diversion period instead of the standard 180-day period for a misdemeanor, or 270-day period for a felony.20

Upon fulfillment of “the obligations of the [diversion] program…the criminal charges filed against the defendant will be dismissed with prejudice.”21

As seen, SB 999 expressly requires a guilty or no-contest plea for domestic violence charges only. Arguably, this express requirement establishes domestic violence charges as the only ones requiring guilty or no-contest pleas. The theory is that because SB 999 expressly requires such pleas only for domestic violence charges, the bill excludes all other charges from the requirement.22

ORS 135.886(2) directs prosecutors to consider several factors in determining whether to offer diversion. One factor of particular interest is “[t]he impact of diversion upon the community[.]”23

The statute does not define “community.” But nothing would prohibit—and logic supports—defining it to include the servicemember’s military community, if the servicemember currently is in the military (for example, in a National Guard or reserve unit).

Example: A senior non-commissioned officer (NCO) who is a member of a National Guard unit scheduled for imminent deployment to a combat zone is facing criminal prosecution. A conviction would bar his deployment, but he is eligible for SB 999 diversion. The NCO’s superiors attest that his deployment is critical to his unit’s cohesion and performance, and that preventing the NCO’s deployment could cause the unit to fail in its mission—including by suffering additional casualties owing to the substitute deployment of someone who lacks the training and experience and the unit loyalty as the NCO. Defense counsel should propose these military community matters as grounds for allowing SB 999 diversion.24

B. DUII Diversion Authority

This is the second diversion authority that OSB Standard 8.1, implementation item 1.c addresses.

ORS 813.200, et seq. authorizes defendants charged with their first offense of driving under the influence of intoxicants (DUII) to have their cases diverted from prosecution. The statutes allow defendants one year to complete diversion, with a single extension period of up to 180 days.25

In late 2010, servicemember advocates learned that owing to ORS 813.225(5)’s inflexible extension period, the statute inadvertently discriminated against diversion-eligible servicemembers who, for example, were on active duty and stationed outside of Oregon but home on leave when charged with DUII, or were members of National Guard or reserve units facing imminent active-duty deployment when charged with DUII. ORS 813.225(5)’s inflexible extension period facially prohibited such servicemembers from completing DUII diversion programs within the time allowed, so denied them the opportunity for diversion they would enjoy if they were not servicemembers.

The 2011 Legislative Assembly eliminated this discrimination by passing House Bill 2702 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2011, chapter 197; codified at ORS 813.220, 813.225, and 813.255).26 The bill:

  • Specifies as state policy that courts are prohibited from denying DUII diversion solely because active-duty military service would impair the servicemember’s ability to complete the conditions of a diversion agreement.27
  • Grants courts discretion to allow a servicemember however many extensions of time of whatever length are required to complete a DUII diversion agreement, if the servicemember shows that current or impending active-duty service would prohibit completing a diversion agreement within the time allowed by ORS 813.230(3) and (5).28
  • Grants courts discretion to allow a servicemember serving outside of Oregon to complete the conditions of a DUII diversion agreement in a comparable treatment program conducted or authorized by a government entity outside of Oregon.29

Moreover, if the prosecution moves to terminate the diversion agreement of an active-duty servicemember, HB 2702 grants the court discretion:

  • To allow the servicemember’s attorney to appear at the termination hearing on the servicemember’s behalf, if his or her military service prevents the servicemember’s personal appearance.30
  • To allow the servicemember to appear at the termination hearing by telephone or other communication device approved by the court, if the servicemember’s military service prevents a personal appearance, and appearance by telephone or other communication device can be arranged.31
  • To stay the termination proceeding if the servicemember’s military service prohibits appearance by telephone or other communication device, and prohibits the servicemember from aiding and assisting his or her attorney.32

Note that HB 2702 does not authorize delaying DUII prosecutions while a servicemember is on active duty. But implied consent license suspension actions which accompany DUII prosecutions are contested case proceedings under the Administrative Procedure Act; consequently, the federal Servicemembers Civil Relief Act (SCRA) provides authority to stay implied consent actions.

In pertinent part, the SCRA states,

The purposes of this Act are—

(1) to provide for, strengthen, and expedite the national defense through protection extended by this Act to servicemembers of the United States to enable such persons to devote their entire energy to the defense needs of the Nation; and

(2) to provide for the temporary suspension of judicial and administrative proceedings and transactions that may adversely affect the civil rights of servicemembers during their military service.”33

Before 2013, implied consent action notifications were silent about servicemembers’ rights under the SCRA. Advocates for servicemembers requested legislation that would require state agencies to provide notification of SCRA rights when bringing contested case proceedings. This legislation took the form of Senate Bill 125 (2013) (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2013, chapter 295; codified at ORS 183.413 and 183.415).

During the legislature’s deliberations over the bill, advocates for servicemembers faced state agency opposition. This opposition suggests that the state Driver and Motor Vehicle Services Division (MVD) may resist SCRA-based stays of implied consent actions. If it does, counsel for the servicemember may use House Bill 2303 (2009), which establishes SCRA violations as a private cause of action, to enforce the servicemember’s SCRA rights.

Counsel would do so by sending the MVD a written demand that it respect the servicemember’s SCRA right to a stay.34 If the MVD “fail[s] to remedy the [SCRA] violation…within 30 days,” counsel then may seek redress in the court of the client’s county of residence.35

A predecessor of the SCRA, the Soldiers and Sailors Civil Relief Act (SSCRA), was read “with an eye friendly to those who dropped their affairs to answer their country’s call” to military service.36 The SCRA serves the very same purpose as the former SSCRA, so the SCRA should be read in the same way.37 In addition, the Supremacy Clause, US Const, Art VI, demands that because the SCRA is federal law, it “takes precedence over [state] law[.]”38 For these reasons, unless the SCRA itself allows otherwise, a court should order a stay of the implied consent proceedings.

Assuming the court issues a stay over MVD opposition, the court then “shall award [the] servicemember reasonable attorney fees.”39 It further authorizes damages, as follows:

(a) The greater of $1,000 or actual damages, including damages for emotional distress; or

(b) If the court finds that the opposing party’s conduct was willful, as described in ORS 646.605, the court shall award the servicemember the greater of $5,000, or three times the amount of actual damages, including damages for emotional distress.40

As seen, the client ultimately should get a stay, a damage award, and attorney fees. Therefore, it would be to everyone but the MVD’s financial advantage for the MVD to oppose a stay and force the matter into court.41

III. Military Service as a Mitigating Factor

Both houses of the 2013 Legislative Assembly unanimously approved Senate Bill 124 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2013, chapter 331). It expressly authorizes courts to consider military service as a mitigating factor at sentencing.42

Senate Bill 124 did this by amending ORS 137.090. It now states in pertinent part, “In determining mitigation, the court may consider evidence regarding the defendant’s status as a servicemember as defined in ORS 135.881.”43 Subsequently, the Criminal Justice Commission added servicemember status to the Felony Sentencing Guidelines, as enumerated Mitigating Factor J.44

SB 124 took effect when the governor signed it on June 6, 2013 (an anniversary of the D-Day invasion of Normandy in World War II). The bill applies to felony and misdemeanor cases alike. Moreover, it applies in the sentencing of “crimes committed before, on or after the effective date of this 2013 Act.”45

For detailed information about seeking sentence mitigation in veteran defendant cases, see Chapter 12.

IV. Constitutional Considerations

In recent history, advocates for veteran defendants faced opposition to legal systems that provide special legal considerations to veteran defendants. This opposition tends to be grounded on the notion that veterans deserve nothing special, making special legal considerations for veteran defendants unconstitutional. The following case law answers this opposition.

Moreover, in Pike v. Cain, 303 Or App 624, 465 P3d 277, rev den, 367 Or 75 (2020), the Court of Appeals addressed trial counsel's failure to secure a mitigated sentence. The court ruled that counsel failed to provide constitutionally adequate and effective legal assistance. Part V and the Chapter 10 and 12 Updates discuss Pike in detail.

In MacPherson v. Dept. of Admin. Services, 340 Or 117, 130 P3d 308 (2006), the court rejected the plaintiffs’ claim that in violation of the Equal Privileges and Immunities Clause of Article I, section 20, of the Oregon Constitution, a property rights ballot measure created a “closed class” that benefited some property owners but not others (depending on when they acquired their property). The court explained that its

refutation of [the] plaintiff’s theory [of impermissible discrimination] must be correct. Were it not, plaintiffs’ theory would mean that the legislature would be precluded from enacting a law benefiting, for example, Vietnam veterans or Gulf War veterans, both closed classes.46

Thus the court acknowledged that laws which create closed classes, benefiting veterans only, comport with state constitutional principles.

Regan v. Taxation with Representation, 461 US 540, 103 S Ct 1997, 76 L Ed 2d 129 (1983), involved an IRS denial of a corporation’s application for 26 USC § 501(c)(3) status on the ground that “it appeared that a substantial part of [the corporation’s] activities would consist of attempting to influence legislation, which is not permitted by § 501(c)(3).”47 The corporation asserted that this denial violated “the equal protection component of the Fifth Amendment’s Due Process Clause,”48 because veteran organizations that obtain tax-exempt status under 26 USC § 501(c)(19) are permitted to engage in political activities.

The Court rejected this claim, explaining,

Veterans have been obliged to drop their own affairs to take up the burdens of the nation, subjecting themselves to the mental and physical hazards as well as the economic and family detriments which are peculiar to military service and which do not exist in normal civil life. Our country has a longstanding policy of compensating veterans for their past contributions by providing them with numerous advantages. This policy has always been deemed to be legitimate.49

Porter v. McCollum, 558 US 30, 130 S Ct 447, 175 L Ed 2d 398 (2009) (per curiam) involved a veteran defendant who was sentenced to death. On habeas corpus review, the Supreme Court concluded that trial counsel provided constitutionally deficient assistance in his client’s capital trial when he failed to present mitigating evidence grounded on his client’s military service. Echoing the Regan Court’s reasoning for rejecting a claim against a tax law that benefits veterans organizations, the Court explained,

Our Nation has a long tradition of according leniency to veterans in recognition of their service, especially for those who fought on the front lines as [petitioner] did. Moreover, the relevance of [petitioner’s] extensive combat experience is not only that he served honorably under extreme hardship and gruesome conditions, but also that the jury might find mitigating the intense stress and mental and emotional toll that combat took on [petitioner].50

As seen, the case law establishes that the purported legal foundation of the opposition to legal systems that provide special considerations to veteran defendants is groundless. To the contrary, laws that provide special treatment to veterans are constitutional.51

V. House Bill 2462 (2019)

At the request of the Oregon State Bar's Military & Veterans Law Section, the 2019 Legislature considered and unanimously enacted House Bill 2462 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2019, chapter 86). The bill has one section containing four subsections. It is codified at ORS 135.985, and states:

"(1) As used in this section, 'servicemember' means a person who is a member, or who served as a member, of the Armed Forces of the United States, the reserve components of the Armed Forces of the United States or the National Guard.

"(2) At the time of arraignment on a criminal charge, the court shall inform the defendant that the defendant’s status as a servicemember may make the defendant eligible for treatment programs, diversion, specialty courts or mitigated sentencing, and that the defendant may obtain information about these options by consulting with the defendant’s attorney.

"(3) In a criminal proceeding the defendant’s attorney may, with the permission of the defendant, notify the court that the defendant is a servicemember.

"(4) The fact that a defendant is a servicemember may not be used as an aggravating factor in determining the defendant’s sentence."

The bill has no special effective date, so it took effect on January 1, 2020. ORS 171.022.

As previously explained in this Update, ORS 135.985(1)'s definition of "servicemember" applies exclusively to HB 2462's provisions. Moreover, ORS 135.985(1)'s definition is broader than ORS 135.881(4)'s definition, which is narrower in that it excludes veterans who received bad conduct or dishonorable discharges. ORS 135.985(1)'s broader definition includes anyone who (i) is serving in the military; or (ii) has served in the military (a veteran), regardless of discharge type. Thus, even veterans who received bad conduct or dishonorable discharges are entitled to treatment under HB 2462's other provisions.

ORS 135.985(2) requires courts, at arraignment, to notify all defendants that treatment programs, diversion programs, specialty courts, or sentence mitigation are available to servicemember defendants. These notification requirements encompass the policies and programs discussed in the main chapters of this manual, which are applicable in servicemember cases.

At least arguably, the policies and programs that ORS 135.985(2) encompass also include the bar performance standards discussed in Chapter 1 of this manual, which defense counsel should meet. These include Standard 2.2, items 6.a.2 and 6.a.3), which state: "During an initial interview with the client, a lawyer should . . . obtain information concerning . . . the client’s history of service in the military and the client’s physical and mental health, educational and military services records." If defense counsel complies with this standard, counsel will know that their clients are servicemembers.

This leads to ORS 135.985(3). It permits trial counsel to notify courts of the client's servicemember status, but only if the client consents. Id. If the client consents, counsel will notify the court of the client's servicemember status, and counsel may use the client's servicemember status as part of a defense or mitigation strategy (or both). But if the client declines to consent, counsel may not notify the court of the client's servicemember status.

HB 2462's last provision, ORS 135.985(4), prohibits courts from using a defendant's servicemember status as an aggravating factor at sentencing. This subsection is important for two reasons.

First, in its original form, the legislation intended to create a military service mitigating factor, Senate Bill 124 (Introduced), could have been construed as allowing a court to treat any defendant's military service as an aggravating factor. House amendments to the bill largely eliminated this possibility. See Owens v. Maass, 323 Or 430, 442-44, 918 P2d 808 (1996) (history of bill drafts are persuasive in determining legislative intent). As amended, SB 124 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2013, chapter 331), codified at ORS 137.090(2)(a) and OAR 213-008-0002(1)(a)(J)), prohibited using a servicemember defendant's military service as an aggravating factor if they are a "servicemember" under ORS 135.881(4)'s narrower definition of the term. On the other hand, if a servicemember defendant's discharge type, bad conduct or dishonorable, excluded them from the ORS 135.881(4) definition, even SB 124 as amended could use military service as an aggravating factor.

But that allowance ended when HB 2462 took effect on January 1, 2020. Owing to the bill's broader definition of "servicemember," ORS 135.985(1), which includes veterans of all discharge type, ORS 135.985(4) universally prohibits using a servicemember defendant's military service as an aggravating factor.

The second reason of ORS 135.985(4)'s importance stems from the fact that although some prosecutors oppose using servicemember status of an aggravating factor,52 other prosecutors have used servicemember status of an aggravating factor.

For example, in petitioner Adrian Leslie Pike's criminal prosecution, State v. Pike, Marion County Case No. 14C4403502 (the precursor case to Pike v. Cain), defense counsel sought mitigation. But the prosecutor—without defense counsel's objection—used aggravation to oppose mitigation. The prosecutor relied on a military sociological concept that the late William Brown, PhD, discussed in great detail in Chapter 5—training and experience in the military culture and total institution. The prosecutor relied on the military sociological concept as an aggravating factor, by arguing:

"What is of concern to the State is [Pike's] also not a regular civilian. He's been trained and, based on his actions against the victim, used that training to assault and almost kill a civilian at a public family function.

" * * * * *

" * * * What I'm saying is that in the situation he found himself in, he used that training and experience that he has to almost hurt someone to the point where they could have been killed. And to me that is significant."

(Emphasis added.)53

ORS 135.985(4) makes clear that in cases sentenced on or after HB 2462's effective date, this type of prosecution argument is forbidden.

VI. Senate Bill 218 (2021)

On behalf of the Oregon District Attorneys Association, the Senate Interim Committee on Judiciary requested the pre-session filing of Senate Bill 218 (2021) to establish procedures for specialty courts. The legislature passed the bill, enrolled as Oregon Laws 2021, chapter 330. The bill took effect on January 1, 2022. ORS 171.022.

"'[S]pecialty court' has the meaning given that term in ORS 137.680." ORS 137.532(6). "'[S]pecialty courts' means drug court programs as defined in ORS 3.450, veterans courts, mental health courts or any other similar court or docketing system." ORS 137.680(1) (emphasis added). Thus, SB 218's new procedures apply to veteran treatment courts.

SB 218 incorporated provisions from the conditional discharge statute, which applies to certain controlled substances offenses. ORS 475.245. But SB 218's incorporated provisions apply a bit more broadly. It states:

"Whenever a person is charged with a misdemeanor or a Class C felony, other than driving while under the influence of intoxicants, and has been formally accepted into a specialty court [such as a veteran treatment court], the court, with the consent of the district attorney and the person, may defer further proceedings and place the person on probation. The terms of the probation shall be defined by a probation agreement."

ORS 137.532(1)(a) (emphasis added).

As in conditional discharge proceedings, "[a] probation agreement carries the understanding that if the defendant fulfills the terms of the agreement, the criminal charges filed against the defendant will be dismissed with prejudice." ORS 137.532(1)(b). "Upon fulfillment of the terms and conditions of the probation agreement, the court shall discharge the person and dismiss the proceedings against the person." ORS 137.532(3). But if

"[u]pon violation of a term or condition of the probation agreement, the [veteran treatment] court may resume the criminal proceedings and may find the defendant guilty of the offenses in the accusatory instrument in accordance with the waiver of rights in the probation agreement. The defendant may not contest the sufficiency of the evidence establishing the defendant’s guilt of the offenses in the accusatory instrument."

ORS 137.532(2).

"Nothing in [the bill] is intended to restrict a person's participation in a specialty court," ORS 137.532(5), including in the various counties' veteran treatment courts. A probation "agreement may not contain a requirement that the defendant enter a plea of guilty or no contest on any charge in the accusatory instrument." ORS 137.532(1)(e). But as a practical matter, the probation agreements imitate guilty or no contest pleas, because the servicemember defendant must acquiesce to waiving various "rights * * * with respect to each criminal charge[.]" ORS 137.532(1)(c). Specifically, the servicemember defendant must waive:

  • "The right to a speedy trial and trial by jury," ORS 137.532(1)(c)(A);
  • "The right to present evidence on the defendant's behalf," ORS 137.532(1)(c)(B);
  • "The right to confront and cross-examine witnesses against the defendant," ORS 137.532(1)(c)(C);
  • "The right to contest evidence presented against the defendant, including the right to object to hearsay evidence," ORS 137.532(1)(c)(D); and
  • "The right to appeal from a judgment of conviction resulting from an adjudication of guilt entered under subsection (2) of this section[.]" ORS 137.532(1)(c)(E).

In addition, the bill prohibits the servicemember defendant from "contest[ing] the sufficiency of the evidence establishing the defendant's guilt of the offenses in the accusatory instrument." ORS 137.532(2).

As seen, if the veteran treatment court resumes criminal proceedings consequent to a probation violation, the servicemember defendant will be tried to the bench and may suffer delay. Even if the prosecution evidence is insufficient to support a conviction, the servicemember defendant will be barred both from moving for judgment of acquittal, and from appealing the conviction on grounds of insufficient evidence.54

In addition, the servicemember defendant will be barred from (i) offering relevant defense evidence, (ii) confronting and cross-examining unreliable and untrustworthy prosecution witnesses, and (iii) challenging hearsay and irrelevant evidence offered by the prosecution. And just as the servicemember defendant will be barred from appealing the conviction on grounds of insufficient evidence, the servicemember defendant will be barred from appealing convictions on these additional grounds just mentioned.

PRACTICE TIP: The bill's prohibitions, on challenging veteran treatment court adjudications at trial and on appeal, deny servicemember defendants the judicial due process principle of fundamental fairness. Because the prohibitions transform probation agreements into imitations of guilty or no contest pleas, defense counsel should negotiate provisions dismissing or reducing charges, or provisions setting sentencing terms, or both.

Finally, before SB 218, well established case law denied jurisdiction over probation violations if no bench warrant or show-cause order was issued before expiration of the probationary period, State v. O’Neal, 24 Or App 423, 545 P2d 910 (1976),55 but with one exception. The conditional discharge system provided the sole exception, which allows courts to issue show cause orders after expiration of the probationary period. ORS 475.245(4).

SB 218 created the second exception to the established case law, by incorporating ORS 475.245(4). The bill's provision states:

"In the event that the period of probation under this section expires, but the terms and conditions of the probation agreement have not been fulfilled and no probation violation proceeding was initiated prior to the expiration of the period of probation, the court may not discharge the person and dismiss the proceedings against the person. The court shall instead issue an order requiring the person to appear and to show cause why the court should not enter an adjudication of guilt as described in subsection (2) of this section due to the failure of the person to fulfill the terms and conditions of the probation agreement prior to expiration of the period of probation. At the hearing on the order to show cause, after considering any evidence or argument from the district attorney and the person, the court may:

"(a) Order a new period of probation to allow the person to fulfill the terms and conditions of the probation agreement; or

"(b) Enter an adjudication of guilt as described in subsection (2) of this section."

ORS 137.532(4).

As seen, the servicemember defendant may present "evidence and argument" to contest claims of failing to complete the probation agreement, id., which should include relevant defense evidence. But if the servicemember defendant is found in violation and is facing an adjudication of guilt, the servicemember defendant will be deprived of challenging the adjudication of guilt. ORS 137.532(1)(c) and (2).

PRACTICE TIP: The veteran treatment court is free to consider even a minor failure to complete the agreement as a legitimate ground to order the servicemember defendant to show cause after the probationary period has expired. Moreover, there is no statute of limitations on a show cause order. But if there were a significant lapse of time between expiration of the probationary period and issuance of the show cause order, defense counsel could assert a laches defense (and could assert the statute of repose, ORS 12.140, if the lapse of time were 10 years or longer).
