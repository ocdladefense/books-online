Still at War: A Guide for Defenders, Prosecutors and Judges Dealing with Oregon's Veteran Defendant Crisis

This manual is dedicated to Walter W. Waters, whose work on behalf of his fellow World War I veterans gave birth to the nation’s modern veterans rights movement.

Mr. Waters, who was born in Burns, Oregon, served in France with the 41st Infantry Division (now designated the 41st
Infantry Brigade Combat Team headquartered at Camp Withycombe, Clackamas, Oregon) during World War I. His service entitled him and thousands of other World War I veterans to deferred compensation, colloquially called a “bonus,” authorized by a 1924 act of Congress, but not payable until 1945.1

By 1931 the Great Depression was raging, and the nation’s need for an expanded money supply was at its historical zenith. Texas Congressman Wright Patman sponsored legislation that would accelerate the bonus payments. Congress passed Patman’s legislative bill, but President Hoover vetoed it as too costly.

Patman’s idea didn’t die. In 1932 Walter W. Waters conceived and voiced a political strategy for enacting the bonus legislation: World War I veterans should exercise their First Amendment rights by marching on Washington and petitioning Congress to reconsider and enact Patman’s legislation.2

Even without benefit of the social media platforms available today, around 14,000 of Mr. Waters’ fellow veterans—including 300 Oregonians—heard his call.3 As members of the American Expeditionary Force that served in France just 14 years before, they called themselves the Bonus Expeditionary Force. Informally they were called the “Bonus Marchers” or the “Bonus Army.”

Countless veterans brought their families, swelling their ranks to more than 40,000 people. They built an enormous “Hooverville” community at Anacostia Flats near the Capitol. They established their own newspaper and schools for their children. Babies were born. And all the while, Mr. Waters and his lieutenants lobbied members of Congress to approve Patman’s renewed legislation authorizing immediate bonus payments. The House passed Patman’s bill in June 1932, but the Senate defeated it on a 62–18 vote.

Meanwhile, President Hoover—a Quaker who suffered such discomfort from seeing military personnel wearing uniforms in the nation’s capital that he asked them to wear civilian clothes—became concerned that the Bonus Army threatened some sort of communist-inspired coup d’etat.4 Hoover instructed attorney general William D. Mitchell to evict the Bonus Army.

On July 28, 1932—“Black Thursday”—Mitchell complied, using capital police. That effort failed, but in the process police shot two veterans, William Hushka and Eric Carlson. Both men later died of their wounds and are buried at the Arlington National Cemetery.

Army Chief of Staff Douglas MacArthur then took charge. Himself a World War I combat veteran, MacArthur ordered infantry and cavalry units, commanded by yet another World War I combat veteran, George S. Patton, Jr., to evict the Bonus Army. The units were armed with tear gas, machine guns and fixed bayonets, and were supported by a tank column.

This eviction effort succeeded, with the Bonus Army routed and its village in flames. The death toll reached four after a veteran’s pregnant wife miscarried and another veteran’s baby, Bernard Myer, already suffering from respiratory problems and exposed to tear gas while held in his father’s arms, later died from that exposure. Some veterans were herded to local rail yards where they were loaded onto rail cars and shipped elsewhere. These horrendous events prompted then presidential candidate Franklin Delano Roosevelt to question whether he even needed to campaign to beat Hoover in the fall.

But the veterans movement didn’t die with the Bonus Army’s eviction. The veterans’ work inspired a renewed national commitment to Lincoln’s pledge in his Second Inaugural Address: “To care for him who shall have borne the battle and for his widow and his orphan.” In 1934 the nation elected 91 pro-bonus candidates to Congress. In 1935 the new Congress again approved bonus legislation. Despite the political boost the Bonus Army gave him just three years before, Roosevelt—like Hoover before him—vetoed the act as unaffordable. The House voted to override Roosevelt’s veto, but the Senate effort failed. In 1936, Congress again passed the legislation, and Roosevelt again vetoed it. But with 1936 an election year, this time, both the House and the Senate voted to override the veto.

Certainly Mr. Waters had his flaws (not the least of which were his efforts to secure dictatorial authority over the Bonus Army). But the fact remains that the movement he conceived and led accelerated World War I veterans’ bonus payments by nine years, and laid the foundation for a renewed national commitment to Lincoln’s pledge. This commitment culminated in Congress’s passage of the “G.I Bill of Rights” (formally called the Servicemen’s Readjustment Act), which President Roosevelt signed into law on June 22, 1944.

These are the reasons this manual is dedicated to Walter W. Waters, Oregonian and World War I veteran, and the man who should be recognized as the “father” of the nation’s modern veterans’ rights movement.

2020 Update

Dedication

In addition to the father of the modern veterans rights movement (that being World War I veteran and Oregonian Walter W. Waters), this manual is now also dedicated to U.S. Navy Senior Chief Petty Officer (SCPO) Shannon Mary Kent and her family for their contributions to servicemembers and veterans.

Born Shannon Mary Smith in Oswego, New York, to Stephen and Mary Smith, SCPO Kent followed her family’s legacy of service by enlisting in the U.S. Navy in 2003. Her father and uncle were first responders at the twin towers on 9/11. Her brother is a Marine.

SCPO Kent worked as a high-level cryptologist. She was fluent in seven languages, including six dialects of Arabic, so she also served as a part-time linguist. In 2010, she received the Department of Defense Linguist of the Year Award.

SCPO Kent served multiple combat deployments, providing necessary intelligence to special operation forces (SOF) units such as Navy SEAL teams. After completing a “physically and mentally arduous [application] process,” SCPO Kent was selected to serve with a joint, special operations unit.

Following her fourth deployment in 2012, again in support of SOF units, SCPO Kent received the Defense Intelligence Agency Human Intelligence Collector of the Year Award. U.S. Senator Chuck Schumer of New York explained that SCPO Kent “was a pioneer in the [SOF] community. She was one of the first, if not the first, women to pass the course required to join Navy SEALS on missions.” As her husband, Oregonian Joe Kent explained, SCPO Kent “was a woman in SOF before there were women in SOF.”5

Shannon met Joe Kent in 2013, during a special operations training course. They married on Christmas Eve of that year, and later had two sons, Josh and Colt. During her Navy career, SCPO Kent obtained her bachelor’s and master’s degrees in psychology. She planned to become a Navy psychiatrist to help servicemembers with combat-related psychiatric conditions. She applied to a PhD program at the Uniformed Services University of Health Sciences. But because she was a thyroid cancer survivor,6 the Navy did not allow her to matriculate.

SCPO Kent fought the Navy’s rejection of her application. Ironically, the same week she received her rejection, she also received orders to deploy—this time to Syria as part of Operation Inherent Resolve in which U.S. military units, allied with Kurdish forces, sought to defeat the Islamic State of Iraq and Syria. Because she served in an all-volunteer unit, she could have declined the orders. But that was never an option SCPO Kent considered. Despite her ongoing dispute with the Navy, she prepared for her new mission, and deployed with her unit with the same dedication and zeal she had shown throughout her career.

On January 16, 2019, SCPO Kent and three colleagues were conducting operations near what was considered to be a relatively safe place in the Syrian city of Manbij. An Islamic State suicide bomber detonated a blast near SCPO Kent and her colleagues. The blast killed all four of them,7 along with 11 civilians. SCPO Kent was the first female sailor to be killed in Operation Inherent Resolve.

In recognition of her outstanding work and achievements as a cryptologist and linguist, the Navy honored SCPO Kent in unprecedented fashion. She became the first enlisted female sailor in U.S. Navy history to have a memorial service at the Naval Academy’s chapel in Annapolis, Maryland. About 2,000 people attended. At the service, the Navy presented SCPO Kent’s family the following posthumous awards: the Bronze Star, the Purple Heart, the Defense Meritorious Service Medal, the Meritorious Service Medal, and the Combat Action Ribbon. In addition, her name was added to the National Security Agency/Central Security Service Cryptologic Memorial Wall in February 2019—marking her as only the third woman to receive this recognition.8 That same year, Senator Schumer introduced an amendment to the defense authorization bill, urging the Navy to name a ship after SCPO Kent. On August 29, 2019, the Information War Training Command-Monterey dedicated its command training stage and surrounding buildings in honor of alumna SCPO Kent. The Pines Place Post Office in New York was renamed for SCPO Kent.

After SCPO Kent’s death, her family continued to fight the regulation that, for health reasons, prohibited her from attending the doctoral program—even though the Navy deemed her healthy enough for deployment to a war zone. The Navy ultimately changed its policy and named the amendments after SCPO Kent.9 But if those amendments had been in place at the time of her application to the PhD program, there is no question she would have been accepted.

While SCPO Kent’s career was remarkable, her Oregon connection prompts her addition to this manual’s dedication.

SCPO Kent was the daughter-in-law of Portland attorney Chris Kent and his wife Mary. In 1998, Chris and Mary Kent became actively involved in veteran and servicemember issues when their son Joe enlisted in the U.S. Army. He joined the Special Forces. He spent 17 years of his 20-year career on 11 combat deployments; for all those years, Chris and Mary Kent served as “blue star” parents—parents with a child serving in the military during wartime. Joe Kent retired at the rank of chief warrant officer.

In an effort to enhance its services to veterans, in 2011 the Oregon State Bar created the Lawyers for Veterans Steering Committee. The committee assumed its functions starting in 2012. The bar tapped blue star father Chris Kent as steering committee chairman. Among its various achievements, Chris Kent’s committee saw to the 2013 reconstitution of the bar’s Military & Veterans Law Section (which had been deactivated in 1999). From 2013 to2014, Chris Kent served as the first chairman of the section’s executive committee. Most crucially to criminal law practitioners, without Chris Kent’s leadership—first as steering committee chairman, then as executive committee chairman—none of the servicemember-related legislative concepts described in Chapter 1 and enacted in 2013 and 2014 ever would have seen the light of day.

SCPO Kent’s death transformed an Oregon family from blue star family into “gold star” family—one with a family member who was killed in war.10 In a small park just north of the Oregon Department of Veterans Affairs in Salem, a memorial honors the 142 Oregonians who gave their lives in the now nearly 19-year-old Global War on Terror. SCPO Kent was not an Oregonian, so her name is not on the memorial. But because of her connection to the Kent family—a family that has displayed such dedication to military service and veteran’s rights—and in acknowledgment that the Global War on Terror is truly “global,” Oregon should claim SCPO Kent as one of its own and place her name on the memorial.

Until then, in Oregon her recognition is in this publication. Along with Walter W. Waters, this manual is dedicated to Senior Chief Petty Officer Shannon Mary Kent and to her family.

OCTOBER 2023 UPDATE

Dedication

This manual is dedicated to Oregon’s “Father of Veteran Defense,” the late William Bud Brown, Ph.D.12—a husband and father, an academic sociologist, a military culture legal expert, and a decorated Vietnam combat veteran who passed away on April 9, 2022 due to long-term health issues stemming from Agent Orange exposure.

Bud was a long-standing tenured professor of criminal justice at Western Oregon University. In 2008, he became among the few modern scholars willing to discuss war as a catalyst for increased crime rates when he published his critical article, Another Emerging “Storm”: Iraq & Afghanistan Veterans with PTSD in the Criminal Justice System. There, Bud predicted that “problems faced by Afghanistan and Iraq veterans * * * who currently find themselves, or may find themselves in the future, entwined within the criminal justice system processes.”13 In preparation for the “emerging storm,” Bud founded The Bunker Project, a 501(c)(3) corporation, to provide information and support to justice-involved and at-risk veterans and servicemembers.14

Criminal justice data soon corroborated Bud’s predicted “emerging storm.” In 2012, the Department of Corrections conducted an inmate survey, which established “that veterans were overrepresented in Oregon prisons by a factor of 125%.”15

Bud’s prediction encouraged Oregon bar members—particularly veterans—to join with him to take decisive action in support of veterans and servicemembers facing criminal prosecution. The seed that Bud planted grew exponentially: articles, seminars, manuals, books, and expert services in veteran criminal defense in courtrooms in Oregon and nationwide. Until his untimely death, Bud worked to help juries, judges, and attorneys understand the pressures and processes of military life. This is why, without question, Bud is Oregon’s Father of Veteran Defense.

Exemplary Military & Academic Backgrounds

Bud’s standing, as a military culture legal expert, was based on his exemplary military and academic background. He began as a trained infantryman. He earned his jump wings from airborne training and later completed Army Ranger School. He served two combat tours in Vietnam. He was awarded the Combat Infantryman’s Badge and the military’s third highest decoration, the Silver Star. After his second combat tour, Bud served as a drill sergeant, leading a basic infantry training platoon. He then entered Officer Candidate School, where he graduated at the top of his class. Afterwards he served as an instructor at the Army Ranger School, and then as a platoon leader with the historical 75th Ranger Regiment.

After U.S. military operations ended in Vietnam, Bud chose to resign his commission and reenter the civilian world. For some years he worked as a logger, but then decided to enter college. He enrolled in the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, where he received his B.A. in social work (with distinction), his M.A. in sociology, and his Ph.D. in sociology. As his daughter Toni Dadkhah explained, Bud’s “collegial relationship with Richard Quinney,” a criminologist and philosopher who established social inequities as the root of crime, propelled Bud’s academic career and his interest in criminal justice issues.

Bud worked in various positions in various colleges and universities. He was a lecturer at (reunited) Vietnam’s Open University of Ho Chi Minh. Later, Bud became a tenured sociology professor at the University of Michigan-Flint. Later still, he joined Western Oregon’s criminal justice faculty.

As an academic, Bud published numerous articles and textbooks on sociological and criminological subjects. Another Emerging “Storm” was his first article on military culture. In it, Bud applied sociologist Erving Goffman’s “total institution” concept, which encompasses mental hospitals, state prisons, and county jails—i.e., institutions that socialize inmates and control their activity “24/7.”16 Established through socialization, the total institution concept encompasses the military.

Moreover, Bud explained, veterans “who have experienced traumatizing events, PTSD [(post-traumatic stress disorder)] and the remnants of their military socialization process . . . [may] find themselves as a defendant in the criminal justice system.”17 Bud expanded his expert services to include military issues and their impact on charges and sentencing.

Expert Services In Multiple Cases & Jurisdictions

Bud worked on numerous other cases, providing expert services “multiple times in multiple jurisdictions on behalf of veteran-defendants.”18 Those cases include:

  • James Anthony Harrell was convicted (non-unanimously) of second-degree assault charges, when he used a pocket knife in defense of his friends.19 On appeal, senior deputy public defender Anne Fujita Munsey retained Bud through The Bunker Project, to prepare Harrell’s social history for a clemency petition if the appeal failed. Bud’s report concluded that Harrell’s background in the military culture and total institution “conditioned [him] to select the fight option, as opposed to the flight option, when confronted with dangerous or stressful circumstances.”20 Although Munsey could not use the report in her appellate briefs, The Bunker Project filed an amicus curiae brief on Harrell’s behalf.21 The Supreme Court ultimately reversed. Justice pro tempore Paul De Muniz, a Vietnam combat veteran, wrote for the court. His opinion ordered remand,22 and on remand, the bench trial acquitted Harrell.
  • A 2009 murder prosecution involved PTSD-afflicted veteran Jessie Bratcher. On behalf of Bratcher’s legal counsel, Markku Sario, Bud, and his frequent colleague, psychologist Robert Stanulis, served on the multi-disciplinary team. The jury found Bratcher guilty but insane. He was committed to the state hospital and was released years ago.
  • Florida and Oregon attorney Elizabeth White explained that her husband and legal partner, Bill Sheppard, who practiced in Florida,23 “successfully used Bud on several state and federal [veteran defendant] cases.” White said Bud “was instrumental in helping us explain the concept of ‘total military institution’ to courts throughout the state. Bill and Bud also taught lawyers all over the country how to effectively represent returning combat veterans.” Bill too was a veteran. Bud and Bill both passed away on April 9, 2022.
  • Among Bud’s most recent work was the murder case of PTSD-afflicted veteran Peter Zielinski. The jury convicted Zielinski, but the Court of Appeals reversed.24 It held that the trial court erroneously allowed speculative bias aimed at Stanulis’s testimony regarding Zielinski’s service-connected PTSD.25

Oregon’s Father of Veteran Defense

Bud could have ignored the “emerging storm.” He was a loving husband and father of two daughters. He had other interests besides military culture. Indeed, after his discharge from the Army, he never again possessed a firearm. His friend and colleague, Randall Shelden, Ph.D., also revealed that on a return to Vietnam in 1990, Bud “donated his medals to the War Remnants Museum in [Ho Chi Minh City]. What he wrote on the plaque is very revealing: ‘I was wrong. I am sorry.’”26

Yet when he saw a new generation of veterans in peril, Bud used his knowledge, skills, and experience to help them. His organization, The Bunker Project, provided expert legal services for fellow veterans facing criminal prosecution in multiple cases in multiple jurisdictions. And even as his health was failing, Bud continued to travel around the country to help veterans in trouble.

Bud’s 2008 article Another Emerging “Storm” inspired the development of veteran defense in Oregon and across the nation. He cared about people and tried to improve the criminal justice system. For his dedication, his scholarship, his

compassion, and his willingness to act, we dedicate this edition of the manual to William Bud Brown, Ph.D., Oregon’s Father of Veteran Defense.
