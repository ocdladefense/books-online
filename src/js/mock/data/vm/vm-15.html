Chapter 15. Best Practices in Veterans Courts: Lane County’s Approach

By The Honorable Ilisa Rooke-Ley*

Original chapter

The perspective expressed in this chapter is the result of the two and a half years I have spent presiding over Lane County’s Veterans Treatment Court (LCVTC).2 It has been an honor to be part of the healing process for participants. I meet my veterans when they are broken, hunched and defeated, and have the honor of watching them graduate standing tall and regaining the lives they had before their trauma. This chapter is intended to give readers insights, information, and anecdotal evidence regarding the importance of ensuring justice for veterans within the criminal justice system.3

Veterans suffer for a variety of reasons. Vietnam-era veterans have a unique set of issues resulting from their service overseas and the way in which they were treated upon returning home. Younger veterans are traumatized by repeated deployments and the difficulty of reintegrating into society.

Many suffering veterans commit acts of domestic violence and have concurring issues, such as substance abuse or mental illness. Others struggle with agoraphobia or cannot be around crowds or loud venues. Families are often severely impacted by the untreated issues which have afflicted these veterans. One Vietnam-era participant recently reported that for the past forty years before his engagement in the LCVTC, all he was able to do was go to work or stay home. But since his involvement with the LCVTC, he now can take his wife out for dinner.

I currently preside over all treatment courts in Lane County, which in addition to the LCVTC includes an adult treatment court and a newly implemented mental health court. Creation of the LCVTC started in 2011, and it officially deployed in 2012. Since its inception, 35 veterans have graduated, and 20 are currently in the program. The participants tend to already have service-connected ratings, will establish a rating or, minimally, begin to get support services from the federal Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). The plight of this population differs from that of participants in other treatment courts. The success of veterans can be attributed to their conditioning and propensity to “follow orders,” to be timely, and their deference to the hierarchical nature of court proceedings and the implementation of best practices of VTCs. The camaraderie among servicemembers helps to cultivate a supportive and healing environment.

What is effective? Compassion, empathy, faith, radical acceptance, patience, boundaries, rules, individualized treatment, psychotherapy, drug and alcohol treatment, housing support, appropriate medication, family therapy, and trust. There is no room for a cookie-cutter approach. For example, a recently graduated veteran had suffered, over a decade, from both a substance abuse issue, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) as a result of his military service. This gentleman suffered abuse while in the military and has a 100 percent service-connected disability rating. The court team worked diligently to help this veteran. The VA sent him to in-patient drug and alcohol treatment numerous times, but he was terminated from each facility. He was arrested multiple times due to his ongoing substance abuse issues. The VA protocol was to treat the substance issue and then treat the PTSD issue. This did not work. (For more about treating mental health factors with substance abuse issues in veterans, see Chapter 6.)

Finally, prior to terminating this veteran from LCVTC and condemning him to prison, he was connected with a private psychologist who is also a veteran. Suddenly everything shifted. The veteran became sober and was able to engage in his treatment modules. He allowed himself to trust the team members. He became the person he was before the trauma. Before graduating, each veteran must perform community service with a “give back” project. This gentleman worked at each community agency where he had received help when he was homeless and without resources. There was hardly a dry eye in the courtroom when he presented his project. Today, this veteran is employed, seeking an advanced degree in his field, purchasing a home, and parenting his beautiful son. He is clean and sober and a proud graduate of the LCVTC.

Lane County is not unique in our approach to veterans courts. We implemented the best practices as defined by the experiences of veterans courts around the country. Veterans need a coordinated effort to address housing and food security, medical, dental and eye care, trauma counseling, vocational training, résumé support, VA benefit counseling, anger and domestic violence issues, and reintegration support. Our primary criterion for vetting a potential participant is that the person has a DD-214, which is explored in previous chapters. Once established, the veteran must either have a substance abuse or a mental health issue related to time in service. All veterans are served without regard to discharge status.4

The first VTC was formed by Judge Robert Russell in Buffalo, New York, eight years ago. Now there are more than 350 courts across the country, and countless more are expected to form in the next few years. The nonprofit group Justice for Vets is the most contemporary and comprehensive resource for information regarding VTCs, and offers technical support in the implementation of VTCs.

These courts provide wrap-around services for veterans who are criminal-justice involved. The purpose is to provide treatment for disorders such as PTSD, traumatic brain injury (TBI), major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and substance abuse, while reducing recidivism. Veterans face unique and difficult circumstances post-deployment that include, but are not limited to, trauma related to combat, difficulty in reintegrating into society, and stigmatization surrounding mental health issues within the backdrop of seemingly oppressive military culture.

The Lane County Veterans Treatment Court began as an offshoot of Lane County Adult Treatment Court (LCATC) when the treatment team realized several participants were veterans who needed assistance from the VA and were eligible for VA benefits and healthcare but unable to access services. In 2011 as more participants self-identified as veterans and also were diagnosed with co-occurring disorders, the treatment team decided to move the veterans to a separate docket and requested a Veterans Justice Outreach Program (VJOP) specialist to attend staffing and court, to report on compliance within the VA (i.e., medical appointments, medication compliance, diagnoses, and residential treatment referrals, etc.). The VJOP is a component of the VA’s Homeless Veterans Project, the mission of which is to end homelessness among veterans. (See Chapter 3.)

The LCATC coordinator applied for VTC Planning Initiative (VTCPI) training through the National Drug Court Institute (NCDI) and to Justice for Vets to receive formal training on the process of implementing a VTC. Lane County was selected to receive the training in December 2012. Unfortunately, funding was cut and therefore training was postponed. Two years later, trainers traveled to Lane County for four days of training. Prior to receiving the training, the team had to provide information about the demographics of veterans in Lane County, as well as commit 10 team members to attend two, two-day VTCPI trainings. The training resulted in a mission statement, operations manual, a phase/level system to ensure accomplishment of milestones, and a task plan timeline to continue implementing best practices. The final phase of training was having the team attend the nationally recognized VTC in Orange County, California.

After the extensive training, there was much work to be done to have a successful VTC. This included community outreach, coordinating services and referrals with the local Vet Center and Lane County Veterans Services, creating a separate veterans group at our community substance use treatment provider, creating a Memorandum of Understanding with all community partners, and trainings to better understand veterans suffering from TBI and PTSD.

Lane County received our first VTC grant in the summer of 2013, which created the ability to operate solely as a veterans court. Funding from the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission helped support a portion of a prosecutor’s salary to staff veteran’s court full time, PTSD training, part of a mental health therapist for the veterans not able to access VA services, incentives to acknowledge program compliance, and partial support for a primary care coordinator to assist in wrap-around services and coordinate with the VA. The funding was continued for the 2015–17 biennium, and we currently have an application pending with the commission for funding during the 2017–19 biennium. LCVTC’s current budget continues to fund a percentage of the prosecutor’s salary, treatment “slots” at our substance abuse disorder (SUD) treatment provider for veterans group therapy, and a percentage of a probation officer’s salary to supervise VTC participants only. Our first evaluation will be conducted by the Lane Council of Governments at the end of this grant cycle.

Since implementation in 2011 there have been 76 participants in the program. Twenty veterans remain in LCVTC. Thirty-five have graduated, which represents a 63 percent graduation rate as of December 2016.

Best practices for veterans courts were developed by Judge Robert Russell when he began the first VTC in the country. The summary that follows lists the ten key components and Lane County’s approach.

Key Components

1. Veterans Treatment Courts integrate alcohol, drug treatment, and mental health services with justice system case processing.

The LCVTC team consists of a judge, a coordinator, a probation officer, an assistant district attorney, an assistant public defender, a VJOP social worker, a police sergeant, a clinician and case manager from the substance abuse disorder (SUD) treatment provider. Once a month, a representative from the Lane County Veterans Services staffs cases and provides support for participants who wish to become VA-connected or upgrade their discharge status from the military.5

The team receives compliance reports from our treatment provider before each participant’s court appearance outlining urinalysis (UA) results, group treatment, and individual session attendance. The team also receives status reports directly from the VJOP, which outline diagnoses, medications, upcoming medical or mental health appointments, and particulars regarding any service-connected disabilities veteran may suffer. Information from the police sergeant and/or the assistant district attorney about any recent police contacts is provided. Peer-support services are available to aid the veteran in navigating these systems.

There are veteran mentors in the courtroom who support the participants in a number of ways. Sometimes the mentors meet with participants before court for coffee and conversation. The veteran mentors have driven our participants to support group meetings, doctor appointments, and the VA, and have provided transport from the jail to inpatient treatment.

2. Using a non-adversarial approach, prosecution and defense counsel promote public safety while protecting participants’ due-process rights.

In the VTC the entire team, including the prosecutor and defense attorney, share a common goal of successfully completing treatment. Each veteran has a criminal defense lawyer who advocates and defends the veteran. Defense lawyers should be trained about the VTC and ask each and every client whether she or he has served in the military.

Despite the non-adversarial nature of the VTC, the public defender’s primary concern is the participant’s constitutional rights. The lawyer advocates for the client by negotiating pleas, preparing and explaining documents, and supporting the best interests of the client. The participants continue to rely on the lawyer to protect attorney-client privilege and to help defend against allegations of noncompliance. All participants are afforded the right to a hearing if termination from the program is requested.

The prosecutor reviews all potential participants for eligibility, participates in staffing cases, and makes recommendations addressing revocations, pleas and application of sanctions and incentives. If a participant is rearrested, the prosecutor reviews the new case and assesses the appropriateness of continued participation.

3. Eligible participants are identified early and promptly placed in the VTC program.

The determination of eligibility should be made early. The Lane County Sheriff’s Department oversees the operations of the Lane County jail. Each inmate booked into the jail is asked whether she or he has served in the military. The list of incarcerated veterans is sent to the VJOP and the District Attorney’s office to begin the process of determining eligibility for the VTC. The VJOP specialist is charged with visiting the incarcerated veteran and beginning the process of connecting the person with VA services. If the veteran seems eligible for the VTC, an offer is made to the defense lawyer. Some veterans do not self-identify as veterans, and some were never in the jail; therefore, it is incumbent upon criminal defense lawyers to ask about military service.6

There are a multitude of ways that veterans qualify for the VTC: the veteran may enter via conditional discharge (ORS 475.245), probation without entering a judgment of guilt (ORS 137.533), diversion agreement involving a servicemember charged with domestic violence (ORS 135.898),7 or as a condition of probation.

Like most treatment courts, there is a two-week “opt in” period. The veteran signs a petition outlining the general rules of the program. The veteran completes the petition with the aid of defense counsel and is accepted by the judge once a determination is made of voluntariness of “trying” the program. During this time, the veteran must 1) complete an orientation with the VTC coordinator, 2) attend two VTC sessions, 3) complete an American Society of Addiction Medicine drug and alcohol evaluation, 4) sign releases with the VA to obtain relevant records and a DD-214, and 5) attend a veterans treatment group with the treatment provider.

Once the veteran is deemed appropriate and the veteran chooses to enter, she or he has a stipulated facts trial or enters as a diversion or a condition of probation. Our team then begins the task of understanding the individual barriers the veteran is facing. The VJOP will provide the team with information regarding the veteran’s service and the reason for their service connection. There are a variety of reasons: TBI, PTSD, military sexual trauma, mental illness (bipolar disorder, major depression, anxiety disorders), and physical disabilities from injuries sustained while serving. The team will also be advised regarding addiction issues either documented by the VA, the veteran or the substance abuse disorder provider.

4. VTCs provide access to a continuum of alcohol, drug, mental health and other related treatment and rehabilitation services.

In addition to the team members previously listed, LCVTC also coordinates services with the Lane County Sheriff’s Department, Sponsors Inc., The Relief Nursery, HUD-VASH,8 the Vet Center, St. Vincent de Paul, the Department of Human Services, and the local VA.

The purpose of LCVTC is to restore the veteran to who he or she was before service and to remove barriers negatively impacting the veteran’s life. Services include navigating the GI bill, managing medication, substance abuse treatment, navigating VA medical care, employment/vocational rehabilitation and stable housing. We also team with a local law firm, Access the Law, which acts as the 501(c)(3) to both fundraise and receive donations to LCVTC, and which also provides, among other services, one free legal consultation to each veteran regarding family law, social security, employment law, and housing issues.

Besides medical and mental health treatment, the veteran may be receiving—within the auspices of the VA—substance abuse treatment. Support is offered for 1) group and one-on-one counseling, 2) mental health counseling, 3) anger management, 4) drug testing, and 5) referrals for employment (either Goodwill or Sponsors, Inc.), housing, residential treatment, education, and medical care (i.e., navigating VA medical care or applying for public insurance) with the aid of a peer-support counselor.

5. Abstinence is monitored by frequent alcohol and other drug testing.

A treatment provider is contracted to provide at least two random UAs per week. These UAs test for substances both illegal and substances legal (i.e., marijuana and alcohol). All participants must call the UA hotline throughout participation in the program. Failure to provide a UA is considered “new use,” even if the participant performed a UA the same day in another venue (such as a hospital or halfway house). Providing a dilute UA, a UA that shows new drug or alcohol use, or any failure to provide a UA or “resets” or extends the time that the veteran must participate in the VTC. To graduate from the program, the veteran must have 120 of verifiable substance-abuse abstinence.

6. A coordinated strategy governs Veterans Treatment Court responses to participants’ compliance.

An outlined continuum of strategies and procedures is key to the success of the VTC. A sanction/compliance grid is utilized to encourage compliance. But flexibility and consideration of the veteran’s individual circumstances is optimal. For example, if a veteran suffering from TBI is not compliant with treatment, a response is tailored to address her or his particular barriers. The unique balance of behavioral modification while protecting the public is the goal.

The incentives include gift cards, having the veteran’s name on a “100%” whiteboard, having the veteran’s name case called early, receiving a special coin (“change attitude, change thinking, change behavior”), verbal praise, ability to travel, and an extension of time between court appearances.

The sanctions for noncompliance include verbal reprimands, writing assignments directly related to noncompliance, returning to court more often and sitting in the jury box for the morning during a court session, extra UAs, community service such as road crew, or jail.

There are times when these sanctions are insufficient to deal with noncompliance and termination is necessary. Reasons for termination include committing a serious program violation (such as delivering controlled substance to another participant, threatening participants/staff, and falsifying a drug test), engaging in serious threatening behavior, failing to engage in the program, continued use of a controlled substance, and exhaustion of all program resources.

7. Ongoing judicial interaction with each veteran is essential.

The judge acts as the commanding officer of the courtroom. Veterans flourish within the structure and formality of judicial proceedings. The recent implementation of a dress code, concurrent with a community resource who provides free clothing, gives the veterans, who are familiar with the notion of wearing a uniform, an opportunity to “dress for success.”

The interaction with the judge includes addressing the veteran’s treatment progress, educational status, family issues or housing. The judge is briefed in staffings before court by the treatment provider as well as the VJOP about progress regarding the veteran. The veteran receives a report from the treatment provider, as well as from the VA, regarding progress, accolades, upcoming appointments and missed obligations. Staffings before each court appearance provide the judge with relevant, updated information, and informs the conversation within the court proceeding.

Judicial interactions must be positive, even if progress is not. Justice-involved veterans carry a heavy dose of shame, guilt and embarrassment. The judge must strike the delicate balance of accountability, compassion, and swift response to non-compliance. VTC judges should be familiar with and have access to the latest DSM to understand and identify the disorders of the veterans.

Anecdotally, one current participant deals with bipolar disorder and hoarding. Employing strategies such as connecting the veteran with a therapist specializing in hoarding disorders, talking to the veteran regarding progress in his home, the probation officer giving progress reports after visiting the home, and requiring the veteran to take photos of progress, are all ways to provide support and ensure success.

8. Monitoring and evaluation measure the achievement of program goals and gauge effectiveness.

Before each court appearance the veteran is given two reports. The first report highlights treatment compliance (i.e., group attendance, Moral Reconation Therapy progress, UA findings). The VJOP specialist generates the second report and documents VA appointments, medication compliance, physical therapy goals, prescription changes, and diagnoses. The team supports the veteran’s goals by assisting the judge in addressing each veteran during the court session. The judge might commend the participants for completing a treatment module or attending medical appointments with the VA. Best practices dictate that the judge address the veteran for no less than three minutes per court session. The team monitors progress as the veteran progresses through the phases of the program.

Lane County’s VTC integrates court supervision and an extensive network of treatment services and community supports. The program offers two tracks—the Alpha Track and the Bravo Track. The Alpha Track is for participants who are assessed as having moderate to high risk of reoffending and need for treatment. For them, a four-level structured approach is used which first focuses on stabilization, then orientation, engagement, and lastly on the participant’s independence and community reintegration. The Bravo Track is for participants who have been evaluated per LS-CMI (Level of Service/Risk, Need, Responsivity) as having a low risk of recidivating and no/low need for treatment.

The veteran attends court hearings weekly until the first phase of the program is completed. The veteran then “graduates” to the next phase, where court appearances are less frequent. Throughout the program, UAs are done no less than two times per week, are randomized and observed. Accommodations must be made for veterans who are alter-abled due to a disability (such as collection of urine from a catheter bag).

Participant data is both analyzed and monitored throughout the program. Data is collected and stored through a statewide information system to compare and analyze performance. All VTCs are subject to periodic peer review every three years.

9. Continuing interdisciplinary education promotes effective Veterans Treatment Court planning, implementation, and operations.

All team members are trained in the most up-to-date best practices to assure success. Training resources are abundant both online and live. There are biannual trainings for VTCs, and each team member is educated in her or his role on the team. Lane County consistently trains each team member through the auspices of the National Association of Drug Court Professionals (NADCP) and Justice for Vets.

The training and education is multidisciplinary and includes best practices, substance abuse, motivational interviewing, moral recognition therapy, rewards and responses, drug testing standards and practices, ethics, and mental health treatment. These organizations also offer training through in conjunction with the National Judicial College (www.judges.org). These organizations also offer training through the National Judicial College specific to training judges. A website specific to veterans courts is available at the NACDCP here.

10. Forging partnerships among Veterans Treatment Court, veterans Administration, public agencies, and community organizations generates local support and enhances Veteran Treatment Court effectiveness.

Lane County’s VTC has an advisory board consisting of representatives from the following agencies: Lane County Circuit Court judges, an LCVTC coordinator, the district attorney’s office, the public defender’s office, law enforcement, parole and probation, the director of Emergence (the SUD provider), the VJOP, Lane County Veterans Services, Access the Law (a 501(c)(3)), the local Vet Center, a veteran mentor coordinator, the Marine Corps League, Sponsors, Inc., Relief Nursery, the Lane Council of Governments, the Eugene Mission, and child welfare.

The advisory board oversees operations of LCVTC and establishes and maintains a written plan that addresses sustainability of the court’s operations, resources, information management, and evaluation needs. The board meets quarterly to review and evaluate the program, to recommend adopting or changing policies, and to address any outstanding issues facing the program (such as budget cuts and changes in VA policy). The board also evaluates and recommends changes to the operation of LCVTC and/or changes to the policy manual.

The community-based support and partnerships have enhanced LCVTC tremendously. Sponsors, Inc., Relief Nursery, the Eugene Mission, housing (HUD-VASH), the Vet Center, the Lane County Sheriff’s Department, and the Eugene Police Department consistently and unwaveringly provide excellent resources for the participants. All VTCs must seek community support and partnership to enhance the effectiveness of their programs.

The local law firm Access the Law acts as our 501(c)(3) organization. Partnered with the LCVTC, Access the Law provides free legal consultations to veterans and represents veterans at reduced rates. Through Access the Law’s Veterans Fund, donors may contribute monetary donations and tangible goods for the benefit of the LCVTC and its participants.

The Eugene Police Department (EPD) helps veterans in a number of ways. A sergeant assigned to the LCVTC alerts the team when there is a veteran in the community who has recently been arrested. The sergeant also attends staffing and court and provides solutions to barriers (such as helps with gas money, aids in the changing of locks, assists with bicycle resources). The department also recently implemented a program requesting that veteran officers wear military pins on their uniforms signifying their service. Moreover, the EPD has also trained their officers in how to deal with veterans in the community who are in crisis and need support.

The Future: Veterans Legacy—A Therapeutic Wellness Center for Veterans

Lane County is home to more than 30,000 veterans. Veterans Legacy is a work in progress developed to heal and support veterans suffering from PTSD and other mental health issues, while also providing therapies for co-occurring disorders such as addiction issues. The community is located on the former grounds of a Lane County Forest Work Camp. The rural setting of more than 100 acres is perfect for farming, self-supported agriculture, vocational rehabilitation, and therapeutic care. Resources are being gathered to revamp the grounds and buildings to habitability. The mission statement of Veterans Legacy encapsulates the importance of this work:

Harnessing the energy, enthusiasm, and talents of the membership will allow creation of a pro-active, holistic care paradigm for the multi-faceted needs of veterans in a safe and unique environment that will fulfill the promise: “Taking Care of Our Own so That no One is Left Behind.”
