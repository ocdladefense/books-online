Chapter 13. Defending Veterans in Federal Court

By Rich Federico*

[2020 update fixes the link to Porter v. McCollum under IV. Investigating for Mitigating Evidence]

Introduction

At one point in his or her life, your client raised their right hand and took an oath that included a pledge to “support and defend the Constitution of the United States against all enemies, foreign and domestic; that I will bear true faith and allegiance to the same.”1 It is now under that same constitution that your client is being prosecuted by the sovereign he or she swore to support and defend.

Representing clients in federal criminal cases presents a challenge of navigating a complicated web of statutes, rules, guidelines, and local practice and culture. Representing a veteran in federal court includes those same challenges but also an opportunity to use the client’s military service to advocate for better outcomes for the client. This chapter is not intended to be all encompassing. It cannot, of course, cover every possible substantive rule or individual case circumstance you may face in a federal case, nor does it endeavor to do so. It will also not go into detail about other topics already covered in other chapters, such as plea negotiations, trial strategy, and use of expert witnesses. Rather, the purpose is to scratch the surface by highlighting some common parts of federal practice that are worthy of consideration as to how the client’s military service may be used to supplement the normal approach to a case.

This chapter will examine an overview of federal case process, meeting the client, getting clients released from custody, the mitigation investigation, sentencing, and post-sentencing issues.

The Federal Public Defender (FPD) in the District of Oregon also has information and resources available for clients and defense lawyers in the district. In addition to other valuable information and resources, the FPD website contains a link to veteran’s services and a switchboard for the Oregon Criminal Defense and Reentry Community.

I. Federal Cases in Oregon—An Overview

Your client has been charged with a federal crime, which means either 1) a crime that violates a law passed by Congress or 2) a crime that happened on property owned by the United States government, such as a VA hospital. In the last year reported as of the date of this writing (Fiscal Year 2015), the U.S. Attorney’s Office in the District of Oregon filed 409 criminal cases in U.S. District Court against 501 defendants.2

Generally, the federal trial process is as follows:

1) Initial appearance and arraignment 2) Detention hearing 3) Discovery and investigation 4) Motions 5) Plea negotiations and change of plea or trial 6) Sentencing 7) Violations of probation or supervised release 8) Appeals and other post-conviction relief

The initial appearance must occur without necessary delay and occurs before a magistrate judge.3 For felony cases, the judge must inform the client of the charge against them, right to counsel, right to remain silent, any right to a preliminary hearing (if charged by complaint), and circumstances in which they can seek release.4 For those clients who have been indicted by a grand jury or charged by information,5 they will also be arraigned by ensuring they have notice of the charges and are asked to enter a plea.6

Following arraignment, the magistrate judge will also set a jury trial before the district court for a date within the range of the statutory speedy trial dates.7 The judge will then order the government to produce discovery8 within 14 days, as required by the District of Oregon’s Standing Order 2015-5 (In re: Criminal Discovery). The lawyer should then expect a Trial Management Order to be filed by the district court setting out deadlines for the case in advance of the trial date.

II. Meeting Your Veteran Client

The lawyer will likely not meet the client until after he or she has been charged with a federal crime. The exception may be if the lawyer was appointed or hired once the client was informed they are a target of a grand jury investigation, usually through a target letter sent by the U.S. Attorney’s Office. Thus, it is never the best of circumstances in the client’s life when the lawyer meets them for the first time.

Prudent lawyers should always endeavor to gather as much information about the client at the beginning of the case. There is the practical benefit of setting the course for the case investigation, and it can also strengthen rapport building with the client by demonstrating to them the lawyer cares about who they are as a person. The client may not volunteer information about their military service, so they need to be asked about it. This may also be a topic the client will want to talk about, as it can be a time in their lives when they can (and should) feel proud of their service.

The lawyer should ask during the initial client meetings whether the client ever served in the armed forces or the military. That question is recommended over asking whether they are a veteran, as some persons may not consider themselves a veteran (for a variety of reasons) even if they did serve in the military. For example, some clients may not believe they qualify as a veteran if they have received a punitive discharge or were involuntarily administratively separated from the military such that they believe they are ineligible for federal Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) services and benefits. For those clients that did serve, the lawyer should gather the following information, at a minimum, as it will be helpful to guide the investigation and records requests later in the case process:

  • Branch of Service. Be specific. For example, if they say Army, be sure to clarify whether it was Active Component, Reserves, National Guard. If the Guard, ask which state.
  • Dates of Service. Many clients served in several branches and had breaks of service. Again, ask the client to be as specific as possible in terms of dates and branches of service.
  • Characterization of Discharge. There can only be six—three administrative discharges and three punitive discharges that can only be adjudged as punishment at a court-martial. The three administrative discharges are: honorable, general (under honorable conditions), other than honorable. The three punitive discharges are: dismissal (officers only), bad conduct (enlisted only), and dishonorable (enlisted only).9 A veteran’s discharge papers, his or her DD-214, will specify the characterization of discharge. Note that the client will likely have several discharges, so multiple DD-214’s, if the client ever reenlisted at the end of an enlistment period, or served in the reserves or National Guard.
  • Duty Stations/Deployments. Ask them about their history of assignments—locations, bases, posts, ships—and whether they were ever deployed. Get dates.
  • Rank/Grade at Discharge. For officers, ask whether they were prior enlisted and, if so, their highest rank.
  • Awards and Decorations. Any awards or decorations they can recall. For the current generation, also ask whether they are veterans of Iraq and Afghanistan and, if so, whether they earned a Purple Heart, combat badge or combat action ribbon.
  • VA Health. Inquire whether the client has ever been to the VA and, if so, whether they ever filed a claim to receive medical or other benefits or services. If so, ask what benefits they receive, whether they have a VA disability rating and basis for it, and whether they have ever had a claim denied.

The military and the VA leave a paper trail the defense team will need to follow by requesting records. The client may, but likely will not, have ready access to many of their own records. If the client does have copies of any of their records, find out where and a point of contact who can assist in acquiring them (if the client is in custody). Also, and this is critical, have them sign release forms10 during the initial meetings so the lawyer can request records as soon as possible.

III. Detention and Release—Getting Your Client Out of Custody

In many cases, the first substantive decision the lawyer will make is whether to seek release for the client in custody. The denial of pretrial release has a profound impact on every stage of the case, including the ultimate outcome. Incarcerated defendants are generally hampered in their ability to assist in their own defense, and are more likely to plead guilty and be sentenced to longer terms of incarceration.11

The Bail Reform Act of 198412 governs pretrial release in the federal system. It provides four options for release or detention: 1) release on personal recognizance or unsecured bond, 2) release on conditions, 3) temporary detention to permit revocation of conditional release, deportation, or exclusion, or 4) detention of the defendant. The most common scenario for release is to be released on conditions under the supervision of a U.S. Pretrial Services Officer.

The lawyer must be wary of “presumption cases” in which the charged offense creates a rebuttal presumption of detention; however, in most cases the Bail Reform Act requires the pretrial detention of a defendant only if a judicial officer determines that no conditions or combination of conditions exist which will “reasonably assure the appearance of the person” and “the safety of any other person and the community.”13 Stated another way, the government must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that your client presents a risk of non-appearance (flight risk) or by clear and convincing evidence that your client is a danger to the community (danger).14

The statute sets forth the factors15 that the judicial officer shall take into account when deciding whether to release or detain a defendant, such as: 1) the nature and circumstances of the offense charged, 2) the weight of the evidence,16 and 3) the history and characteristics of the person. It is this last category in which the lawyer can use the client’s status as a veteran to weigh in their favor towards release.

In the District of Oregon the pretrial services officer will write a report for the magistrate judge and make a recommendation about release or detention. Prior to completing the report, the pretrial officer will request to interview the client. If the client is seeking release and the lawyer and client determine an interview is in the client’s best interest to gain release, the lawyer must be with the client during the interview. The lawyer can assist the client in determining whether to answer all or just some of the questions, depending on the case. The interview will also include questions whether the client ever served in the military, so it will be in the report provided to the court.

The statute lays out the “history and characteristics of the person” factors that the court must consider regarding detention:17

  • The person’s character
  • Physical and mental condition
  • Family ties
  • Employment
  • Financial resources
  • Length of residence in the community
  • Community ties
  • Past conduct
  • History relating to drug or alcohol abuse
  • Criminal history
  • Record concerning appearance at court proceedings
  • Whether at the time of the current offense or arrest the client was on probation, parole, or other release pending trial, sentencing, appeal, or completion of a sentence
  • The nature and seriousness of the danger to any person or the community that would be posed by the defendant’s release.

The client’s military service, past or present, can be relevant to these factors. For example, the client’s character is exemplified by his or her military service. The more the lawyer knows about it, the stronger the argument about character. Additionally, the client may currently earn income from military service or through a VA benefit such as a service-connected disability or individual unemployability. Their community ties may be strengthened by reference to their belonging to a military unit or veterans’ organization. They may also have someone in their unit or from a veterans’ organization who would be willing to assume responsibility over them if they are released. In short, do not overlook their status as a veteran in your arguments for release.

Additionally, the magistrate judge will certainly inquire about the “release plan,” or where the client will live, work, who they will associate with, and whether any treatment is required. For those released on conditions, they must be released under the least restrictive condition or combination of conditions that will reasonably assure the appearance of the person as required and the safety of the community.18 A few release conditions are mandated by the statute, while several are discretionary but routinely placed on clients.19 For example, one discretionary condition commonly used is that the client must participate in a mental health evaluation and counseling at the direction of the pretrial officer. Keep in mind the client has to pay a percentage of all fees for services obtained while under pretrial supervision so don’t forget about the VA as an option. It will be cheaper for the client and has been generally accepted by pretrial services to conduct the evaluation. Your client earned VA benefits, so use them as part of the release plan.

IV. Investigating for Mitigating Evidence

The client’s military service is mitigating evidence that is relevant at sentencing.20 However, the lawyer cannot wait to the end of the case to begin the mitigation investigation. This type of mitigation evidence can also be extremely useful to present to the prosecution during plea negotiations. The defense team should begin gathering mitigation at the beginning of the case, similar to the review of the discovery and evidence regarding the charged offenses. As previously mentioned, it is highly advisable to have releases signed during the initial client meeting so the lawyer can send the requests as early as possible. The various agencies the defense will request records from (National Archives, Department of Defense, Service Branches, National Guard, VA) will take considerable time to process the request and return records.

To the extent the client is willing and able to talk about their military service, they are often the best source of information. One approach after gathering the basic information is to ask them how they felt about their service while in uniform and how they feel about it now. For those who served in combat, the lawyer should request the client write a letter that describes, in detail, one event that they recall most vividly from their time in combat. For those who may have a service-connected mental health disability or suffered trauma based upon their military experiences, it may be difficult for them to talk about their experiences. There is no substitute for consulting with a mental health expert with experience treating veterans who can guide you through this discussion (or counsel you to avoid it). For the clients who have or are currently undergoing mental health treatment, they may be able to describe their symptoms and the challenges they have to overcome. All of this information will be relevant in mitigation and it is the lawyer’s job to paint the picture of the client’s experience in the military and the scars they suffer from their service.

Additionally, gather as much media information from the client’s military service as possible: pictures in uniform, pictures from a war zone, videos of service, etc. Once information is gathered about the client’s military awards and decorations,21 attempt to recreate their “ribbon rack” in a pictorial format.22 Also attempt to gather award citations, letters of appreciation, and military certificates. The lawyer must show the court the client’s military service. Do not just tell them.

Other servicemembers can be critical defense witnesses. Locate and interview persons who served with the client. Have them describe the person they knew in uniform and determine whether they are willing to write a letter or testify on behalf of the client. It is extremely compelling in federal court to have a veteran testify on behalf of a veteran, which requires early investigation to figure out who may be available and presentable to do so.

The use of experts is described in detail in other chapters. However, it is worth noting that the Criminal Justice Act provides that the court shall authorize counsel to obtain services, including experts, deemed necessary for adequate representation by filing an ex parte application for funding.23

V. Sentencing

This chapter skipped trial and went straight to sentencing. Trials are covered in another chapter and, as a practical matter, the vast majority of federal cases result in a guilty finding.24 The reality in the federal system is that you must prepare for sentencing in anticipation of what the odds say will be the outcome.

A. Presentence Investigation Report

Once the client is found guilty (usually through a plea), the U.S. Probation Office will be ordered by the court to prepare a presentence investigation report (PSR).25 In the District of Oregon, Standing Order 2013-2 requires the probation officer to complete a draft PSR no later than 35 days prior to the sentencing date so that the defense may submit written objections and comments on the draft PSR 14 days thereafter.26 Additionally, under Local Rule 3003, dissemination of the PSR provided to the defense is limited to the client and persons working on the defense team.

The probation officer will inquire whether the client desires to be interviewed for the PSR. The interview should be focused on Part C of the PSR, which is the section that describes the history and characteristics of the client. Just like the pretrial services interview, the lawyer must be present at this interview. For cases in which the lawyer and client determine an interview is in the client’s best interests, this is the opportunity for the court to learn about the client (through the probation officer), including their military background. Nothing prevents the lawyer from providing military records to the probation officer for inclusion within the PSR, with the type and volume of documents varying by case and by probation officer.

Additionally, the probation officer will calculate the sentencing guidelines and make a sentencing recommendation to the court.27 The lawyer should not miss the opportunity to provide evidence or argument for why a downward departure or variance from the applicable guideline range may be appropriate for your client based upon their military service, as described below.

Finally, the final PSR will also follow the client after sentencing. For example, it will be used by the Bureau of Prisons (BOP) to make determinations concerning classification, designation, and program eligibility. The importance of the PSR cannot be overstated, so the lawyer should take care to ensure the relevant history and characteristics of the client, including military service, are well documented in the PSR.

B. Statutory Considerations for Sentencing

After the completion of the final PSR and the parties submit written sentencing memoranda to the court, a sentencing hearing will be held. In deciding the appropriate sentence in a federal case, the court shall impose a sentence sufficient, but not greater than necessary, after considering several factors that include: the nature and circumstances of the offenses and the history and characteristics of the defendant; the need for the sentence imposed to reflect the seriousness of the offense, to promote respect for the law, and to provide just punishment for the offense; deterrence; to protect the public; and to provide the defendant with needed education, medical care, or other correctional treatment.28 The “history and characteristics of the defendant” factor allows for admission and consideration of your client’s military service as relevant evidence in mitigation.

C. Sentencing Guidelines

The lawyer must also be intimately familiar with the federal sentencing guidelines.29 Although the guidelines issued by the U.S. Sentencing Commission are advisory only,30 they provide the framework for all federal sentencing proceedings. A district court must begin all sentencing proceedings by correctly calculating the applicable sentencing guidelines range.31 District courts impose a sentence within the guidelines (or below the guidelines based on a government motion) over 80 percent of the time.32

Chapter 5, Part H of the U.S. Sentencing Guidelines Manual addresses the relevance of “Specific Offender Characteristics.” The Sentencing Commission cautions that the most appropriate use of specific offender characteristics is to consider them not as a reason for a sentence outside the applicable guideline range but for other reasons, such as in determining the sentence within the applicable guideline range, the type of sentence, and various other aspects of an appropriate sentence. Nevertheless, the guidelines are advisory only so these characteristics should be presented and argued in a way that can achieve the best result for the veteran client.

Several “Specific Offender Characteristics” may be helpful to a veteran client. Military service may be relevant in determining whether a departure or variance is warranted, if the military service, individually or in combination with other offender characteristics, is present to an unusual degree and distinguishes the case from the typical cases covered by the guidelines.33 The use of the term departure is significant because it means that military service may be a reason for the court to depart downward from the applicable guideline range in determining a sentence. Thus, military service can be offered to argue for a lower sentence under both § 3553(a) and § 5H1.11.

The guidelines also state that mental and emotional conditions may be relevant in determining whether a departure is warranted, if such conditions, individually or in combination with other offender characteristics, are present to an unusual degree and distinguish the case from the typical cases covered by the guidelines.34 Further, a downward departure may be appropriate to accomplish a specific treatment purpose.35 This could be accomplished by intermittent confinement, community confinement, or home detention instead of imprisonment. The guidelines state that a departure should be considered only in cases where the court finds that (A) the defendant is an abuser of narcotics, other controlled substances, or alcohol, or suffers from a significant mental illness, and (B) the defendant’s criminality is related to the treatment problem to be addressed.36 One can imagine the case this guideline may be worth arguing to the court, as there have been a number of cases throughout the country in which veterans suffering from post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) committed crimes as part of their disassociation or extreme symptomology of the PTSD.37

Physical condition or appearance, including physique, may also be relevant in determining whether a departure is warranted under the guidelines.38 If the client is a wounded warrior who suffers from excessive physical health issues, it would be prudent to argue for a lesser sentence under this guideline. For cases of a seriously infirm defendant, the guidelines state that home detention may be as efficient as and less costly than imprisonment.

VI. Post-Sentence Considerations

The client’s case may technically end at the filing of the judgment, but their need for legal services will undoubtedly continue. The universe of potential legal issues the client may face is vast, but some common questions or issues are worthy of brief mention.

A. BOP Security Classification and Designation

For those clients sentenced to a term of imprisonment, many of them will inquire about where they will serve their time. The BOP has the sole responsibility in determining where a client will be designated for service of a sentence.39 However, it is common to request the judge to make a recommendation to BOP in the judgment as to the place of designation for the client to serve a term of imprisonment.
Classification levels, location designations, and sentence computation functions for the BOP are carried out at the Designation and Sentence Computation Center (DSCC), located at the Grand Prairie Office Complex in Grand Prairie, Texas. One factor for classification is “history of violence,” which involves only conduct adjudicated as a crime and a finding of guilt, so that conduct in military service should not be considered.40

B. Reentry Plan

When possible, it is best to take a holistic defense approach and engage in preventative legal practice. To the extent possible, the lawyer should consider the challenges the client may face when coming to the end of a term of imprisonment. The BOP has reentry programs, and clients who qualify will be placed at a residential reentry center near the end of their sentence prior to release. Additionally, the U.S. Department of Justice recently created a “Roadmap to Reentry” in an effort to reduce recidivism.

A constant theme to reentry success is the need for adequate resources and advanced planning. The lawyer should counsel the client to attempt to maintain or establish services and benefits with the VA while incarcerated.41 Additionally, during the reentry phase, military and veterans’ organizations may be able to assist the client with clean and sober housing, in addition to other practical needs. The client should be counseled to work with his or her probation officer, in addition to the lawyer, to find a veterans’ organization that may be the right fit for the client’s needs.

C. Supervised Release

In the vast majority of cases, a term of supervised release will be ordered as part of the sentence and judgment.42 Several criminal offenses include mandatory terms of supervised release. Clients will be assigned a probation officer who will supervise them and monitor compliance with the conditions of their supervised release. There are both mandatory and discretionary conditions of supervision.43 The goal of supervised release is to ease the client’s transition into the community after serving a long prison term for a particularly serious offense, or to provide rehabilitation to a client who has spent a fairly short period in prison for punishment or other purposes but still needs supervision and training programs after release.44

Beginning at the draft PSR, the lawyer can attempt to set the client up for success on supervision by objecting to discretionary conditions that are not reasonably related to the purposes of sentencing or supervised release.45 For those clients who are successful on supervision, a client may move the court for modification of conditions or early termination.46

Conclusion

Representing a criminal defendant in federal court is a daunting challenge. However, the law provides several instances in which your client’s military service may be used to advocate for better outcomes. Lawyers are zealous advocates for all clients, of course. However, for veteran clients, no matter the charged offense or conduct, lawyers should never lose sight of the fact that at some point in their life, the client raised their right hand and swore an oath similar to the one the lawyer swore when admitted to the bar. For the courage it took to take the oath and serve, the veteran client demands equal courage in the defense of their criminal case.
