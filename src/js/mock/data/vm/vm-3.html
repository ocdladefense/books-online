Chapter 3. Investigation and Mitigation Services

& Veterans Justice Outreach Program

By Cindy Borders

Oregon’s large population of incarcerated veterans makes crystal clear the importance of investigating the extent to which a client’s military service may factor into defense and mitigation strategies. However, such investigations can be time-consuming, tedious, and frustrating.

I. Investigation Is Critically Important

First and foremost in the course of such investigations, be honest with your client, especially if he or she is a combat veteran. They do not have time or care for people who say one thing and do another. They have to trust you or you will never be able to work with them. If you are going to visit them in jail, go visit them in jail. If you cannot keep the appointment but show up another time, explain to them why you did not make it.

Second, understand that preparing a defense and mitigation strategy is an excuse or a plea for leniency. Rather, it is solely an examination of circumstances that be considered when determining a defendant’s culpability. It may not justify or excuse an offense reduce the severity of a chargeFor example, in cases where the death penalty could be imposed, the Supreme Court has held that jurors must be instructed that they may consider mitigating circumstances such as the defendant’s youth, mental capacity, or childhood abuse, so they may reach a reasoned and moral sentencing decision.1 Hopefully your client is not facing the death penalty. But regardless of whether your client is facing capital or non-capital charges, the methodology for preparing and presenting circumstances based on military service is very similar to that which is outlined in cases such as and .

The mitigation portion of investigating a veteran case can make all the difference in the outcome of the sentence. Veterans, especially combat veterans, usually commit crimes in a very obvious way, such as shootings, high-speed chases with the police, or home invasion robberies.

II. Beginning the Investigation

The basic steps to begin an investigation for a veteran defendant case are acquire records, conduct interviews, understand the specific military terminology and build a timeline. These and other particulars are set out below.

A. Step One: Gather Records

Veterans have two sets of records which, for the most part, do not cross over.

1. Service-related records (or “active duty/reserve” records)

These can be requested by using a form called an “SF 180” (available in Chapter 4 and here). I have had the best results when requesting records by FedEx economy rate. This process can take months. In order to speed things up, contact your local member of Congress and speak with the veteran advocate.

  • Each individual post or base retains personnel files, so you may have to contact each base or post to request copies of the client’s files.
  • In-country files are next to impossible to obtain. You may do a Freedom of Information Act request (see also Chapter 4), but this can takes months or longer. Your best source for information on in-country time is through other members of their units.

2. Service-related medical records

When a service member separates from the military, they are given their complete medical file to provide to the federal Department of Veterans Affairs (VA), including the actual file folders. I have had one client who retained theirs. Additionally, many members of the military receive medical treatment for traumatic brain injury (TBI), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) including military sexual trauma (MST), and addiction at private facilities while active duty. These are easily obtained through the regular Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPPA) process.

3. VA Records

If you are fortunate enough to have a veteran who has gone only to the Portland VA Medical Center (VAMC) you have an easy assignment. Have them sign a VA Form 10-3288 and a VA Form 10-5345 (form 10-5345 is available in Chapter 4 and here), drive to the Portland VAMC, get a cup of coffee and a muffin from the coffee stand, bring something to read, go to the Release of Information office to the left and down a long hall from the main area, give them your request, sit down, read, drink your coffee, and within a fairly short period of time you will be given a disc with the records.2 But you must ask the VA for the client’s entire file, dating back to the beginning of their treatment at the VA. If you do not, the VA will send you only the records for the previous three-year period. You also need to ask them to include the client’s military medical file if the VA has it.

If you have to deal with any other veterans’ hospital, patience and persistence is all I can say. send the requests to the main Veterans Affairs offices which are located in St. Louis, Missouri. Think black hole. You have to contact the individual hospitals for their records because they are not centrally maintained. I send my requests via economy FedEx to the various facilities. The White City facility is very difficult to deal with. You can call the patient advocate at any facility and ask for their help. Also, if you are being given the run around too much, contact your Congressperson.

B. Step Two: Conduct Interviews and Service History Research, or What to do while you wait for records

1. Interviews

If your client has serious TBI issues, he or she may not recall what day of the week it is or whether they ate breakfast, but they will remember those who served with them. Social media is a treasure trove in these cases. Many military units have Facebook pages. If your client has a Facebook page, get their login and start there—look for their friends who have served and send them messages.

Practice Tip: Only do one or two a day; if you send out ten messages, you will more than likely receive 15–20 phone calls within a couple hours.

Talk to your client’s family members and friends. Most family will be fairly open about the “before” and “after.” Get photographs of the client before enlistment, after basic training, during deployment, and after deployment. Sit the photographs side by side. Learn the client’s entire social history. One issue the state hospital has had with veterans is labeling them “antisocial.” Learning about their lives pre-enlistment helps the attorney and experts combat this assumption.

2. Interview Tips

When interviewing your client, patience is key. As always, build a rapport. Do not begin your first interview talking about the client’s experiences while on deployment. Save that for a later date. When the day comes and they begin to open up to you about their experiences, put down your pen and actively listen, watch their body language, their eyes, their facial expressions. If the information is particularly traumatizing to the client and they appear very agitated at the end of the meeting, please notify the jail staff so they can be aware of any changes in behavior.

Practice Tip: I wait until the interview is over and then make my notes in my car regarding what was said and noting any change (or lack of change) in the client’s behavior. Your team psychologist will want to know about these changes. Frequently your client will “leave” the jail while telling their story—you will see this in their eyes. Be aware of this and note it.

When you begin to interview the client’s fellow servicemembers, they will be open about some things and very closed about others. Do not force them, and be patient. If your client is accused of a sex crime, getting the people who served with them to cooperate will be very difficult. If your client is accused of a homicide, they will actually be more understanding. Explain to the person you are interviewing that you are not asking for them to condone what your client did, but that you are looking into their service record as a consideration for the sentencing judge.

My best luck to connect with fellow servicemembers is by using Facebook. I send them messages with my phone number. Usually I receive three to five calls within a few minutes.

Practice Tip: If you are contacting people who are currently on active duty or are working as a contractor for the military, they may call you from Afghanistan or Iraq, so you may receive the calls at odd times. If a person calls you from an active duty base, do not discuss sensitive or overly personal matters on the phone. Instead, ask that they call you from outside the base on their personal cell phone. Assume that all calls on bases are recorded whether the witness is a civilian contractor or active duty personnel.

3. Research service history

If veteran clients were deployed, look up their unit, learn when and where they were deployed, and read up on what they encountered while deployed. This includes battles as well as exposure to toxins. Learn about significant dates in their deployment, such as the anniversary dates of when their buddies died. Learn those dates and if one occurs during the time you are working with the client, acknowledge it. Frequently clients’ crimes occur on or around these types of anniversary dates. Also be prepared for times when the client will be irritable or difficult—you will find that these times usually coincide with anniversary dates.

4. Talk to the client

Do not patronize the client with “thank you for your service” type statements. When they start talking, do not look at your watch and think, “I’ve got to go.” That extra 10 or 15 minutes may make all the difference in a case’s outcome.

C. Step Three: Study the Glossary

The military has its own language. First and foremost, if you do not know what a veteran client is talking about or saying, just ask them. Do not pretend. The following is a small glossary of terms you will frequently hear:

  • DD-214 (Certificate of Release or Discharge from Active Duty)—the form that all servicemembers receive when they are released from active duty. It shows their term of service, rank when they were discharged, discharge type, specialty (job description, in civilian parlance)3; awards/commendations, and any special notations.
  • Ma’am/Sir—self-explanatory and annoying, but get used to it. Until they are comfortable with you, you are Ma’am or Sir.
  • IED—Improvised Explosive Device, commonly called a “roadside bomb.” Depending on where they have been deployed, these take many forms.
  • COB—Central Operating Base. This is the base in-country where the unit’s administration, called its “rear detachment” (or “rear-D”), is located. It is also where the main hospital and other such facilities are located.
  • FOB—Forward Operating Base. This is an outpost apart from the COB where units are sent to engage the enemy.
  • Patrol—both a noun and a verb. A “patrol” is a group of soldiers who go outside the COB or even the FOB to engage the enemy. To “patrol” (or to “go outside the wire”) is the act of going out to engage the enemy.
  • “On your six”—refers to the positions of the hands of a clock, with six being the lowest number on the face. It literally means “I’ve got your ass covered.”
  • Hajj—anyone from an Islamic Middle Eastern country.
  • Terp—interpreter.
  • Doc—several important distinctions here. In the Army, a medic is a “doc.” In the Marine Corps, there are no medics. Instead, such personnel are drawn from the navy, and are called corpsmen. But a corpsman is also a “doc.” Docs are the first responders in battle who tend the wounded.
  • Green Zone—an area in the desert that may be an oasis. In Afghanistan this is frequently where farming occurs. In Iraq, this was an area outside of Baghdad. Frequently they are areas of hostility.
  • Combat Action Badge (CAB)—if earned, this will be reflected on the client’s DD-214. Without it, the client did not engage the enemy in active combat, they did not take or return fire. This does not mean they were not in a combat zone. It only means they did not engage in combat.
  • “Battle buddy”— the people your veteran will have served with.
  • “The Wire”—the line of demarcation between the base—and safety—and hostility. Can be a literal wire or an area.

Below is a list provided by one of my clients of terms relating to weaponry. This is very important if you want to understand the terms when talking with a combat veteran (especially if the veteran was an infantryman, a cavalry scout, or in special operations).

  • IAW—Individual Assigned Weapon. Not likely to run across this one except in records, profiles, and other documentation. Can also mean “In Accordance With,” so use common sense and apply rules of grammar.
  • “Pig”—usually the largest caliber, portable machine gun in an infantry unit. Examples are the M240B, M240G, Mk 48, and M60.
  • SAW—Squad Automatic Weapon (or M249). This is a light machine gun. But unlike a “pig,” which is served by a two-man crew, a single servicemember operates a SAW. The Mk 46, which is simply a pared-down, lighter version of the SAW, may also be referred to as such.
  • “Crew Served”—any weapon system that requires a crew of more than one servicemember to operate. Usually refers to machine guns that qualify as “pigs,” but also encompasses missile and mortar systems.
  • “60” or “The 60”—depending on context, can mean an M60 machine gun or a 60mm mortar, both of which are crew served. If someone says, “I was hanging rounds with the 60,” they mean they were dropping mortar rounds. If someone says, “I smoked the barrel going cyclic on the 60,” they mean they fired an M60 machine gun to the point of its overheating.
  • “81” or “The 81”—an 81mm mortar system.
  • “120” or “The 120”— a 120mm mortar system.
  • Cyclic Rate of Fire—the highest rate of fire that a weapon system is capable of firing. While theoretically this applies to all weapon systems, it is most applicable to machine guns; in particular, during the first 30–60 seconds of contact with an enemy element. “Going Cyclic” refers to firing as much and as fast as a gunner can in order to regain fire superiority.
  • Sustained Rate of Fire—the highest rate of fire that a weapon system is capable of sustaining without overheating. After regaining fire superiority, gunners slow down to a sustained rate of fire and choose targets more carefully.
  • Grazing Fire—a method of firing across a lateral area target, such as a woodline, clearing, hedgerow or field. Fires are dispersed horizontally across the gunner’s field of fire 18–36 inches above the ground.
  • Point Target—in essence, a man. A small target.
  • Area Target—a large target. Examples include a building, clearing, woodline or the dead space created by any obstacle on the battlefield.
  • Dead Space—any space on the three-dimensional battlefield that creates a blind spot for the maneuver unit.
  • 203—a single-shot grenade launcher, typically mounted under the barrel of a rifle. 
  • Rifle—never a gun, because only crew-served weapons are called “guns.” In present times, rifles are the M4 carbine, M16A2 and M16A4 service rifles; in older times, the M14 service rifle.
  • Gun—a crew-served weapon, such as a machine gun, mortar, or artillery piece. Context is important.4
  • Long Gun—a sniper rifle.
  • Mk 19—a fully automatic belt-fed grenade launcher. Despite what some veterans may think, ; it does not have rapid interchangeable barrels. Sometimes it is referred to as a “grenade launcher machine gun” in order to simplify its explanation.
  • Can— a suppressor (or “silencer” to those who do not understand its function).
  • ACOG or RCO—Advanced Combat Optical Gunsight or Rifle Combat Optic. The former is the name used by the manufacturer (Trijicon) and the Army, the latter is the name used by the Marine Corps.
  • 68 or CCO——Close Combat Optic. This covers a variety of models of “red dot” sights, manufactured by Aimpoint and used by the Army, generally in conjunction with a 3x magnifier. The official nomenclature is the M68 Close Combat Optic.
  • AN/PAS-13—a thermal scope with white and black hot capabilities. May be mounted on a variety of weapons. Generally referred to as PAS or PAS-13.
  • AN/PVS-14—single monocular night vision device, standard issue to line infantry troops. Generally referred to as “14’s.”
  • AN/PEQ-2, AN/PAQ-4, AN/PEQ-15—infrared (IR) laser aiming devices. Each device has its own unique capabilities, but all have the ability to shoot an infrared laser beam, visible only under IR night vision, allowing a weapon to be reliably aimed in complete darkness. The AN/PEQ-15 also has a visible laser.
  • AN/PSQ-22—the new generation night vision monocular, capable of IR night vision, orange and black hot thermal night vision, and IR/thermal overlay night vision, often referred to as predator vision.
  • ITAS—Improved Target Acquisition System. The targeting system used to aim, fire and guide TOW missiles.
  • TOW Missile—Tube-launched, Optically-guided, Wire-tracked Missile. An infantryman’s best friend.

D. Step Four: Build a Timeline

Timelines are helpful in showing a client’s life. Begin with the social portion of the timeline—birth, school, marriages and jobs, then enter items such as enlistment dates, dates of training, dates and locations of deployment, anniversary dates of events such as deaths in-country (including suicides), whether your veteran was in significant battles, and date of discharge. Then address events that occurred since they were released from the military, such as marriages, divorces, legal problems, jobs and treatment programs. Review their VA records (if any), and include dates they received treatment at the VA or private facilities.

E. Other Items for Consideration

1. Dealing with Paranoia and “Kool-Aid Drinkers”

I have found that many of the people who have served feel—frequently with good reason—that they have been deceived, which can cause their perceptions of events to become tainted with paranoia. Be careful how you address this. I have found the best way to handle it is to guide the subject at hand back to your client again.

“Kool-Aid drinkers” are true believers. If you speak with one of them, you may find that they may be ashamed of what your veteran did and see him as nothing but a “bad apple.” I suggest taking their statements, asking them questions, but always keep in mind they are very biased in their opinions.

2. Superior Officers

It would be a wonderful world if a veteran client’s superior officers stood up for them. Do not expect it. In my experience, the superiors turn tail and run as fast as they can. But if you are able to obtain a copy of commendations or other documents signed by their superior, use those.

3. Subpoenas

The VA and its employees may believe they are immune to the subpoena process. Active-duty servicemembers may believe so as well. For information about dealing with federal agencies and employees who resist subpoenas, see Chapter 4.

4. Appearance in Court

While it would be nice if servicemembers could testify in court while wearing their dress uniforms, chest ablaze with medals, whether they may do so is a matter of some complexity. For guidance, see The Hon. Daniel J. Hill, (Aug. 13, 2010).

5. Awards and Commendations

The military likes to give out awards and medals. When you look at your client’s DD-214, section 13 will list all of his or her decorations. The client should have a certificate of award as well, but they frequently get rid of them, as well as the medals. Please be sensitive to the fact that while the Purple Heart, Bronze Star, and medals for valor are revered in civilian society, most veterans see only that they were injured that day, or that their buddy was wounded or killed that day. If you are able to locate them, there should be a letter from their command explaining what led to them being awarded their particular medal.

6. Suicide

When talking with their fellow soldiers, ask about the suicides from their unit. This is very important, given the high risk of suicides in the veteran population. , Oregon Violent Death Reporting System, Oregon Health Authority, Public Health Division (May 12, 2014). The national estimate is that on average, 22 active duty and veterans commit suicide each day.

7. Medications

The average veteran suffering from PTSD (including MST) and TBI is prescribed numerous medications for their mental health, their physical pain, to stop nightmares, to dull the voices in their heads, to calm the headaches, to reduce the tinnitus. Side effects of these medications include irritable bowel syndrome and impotence, so they are prescribed additional drugs. Locating prescriptions and learning how well they were taking them is vitally important. Many veterans come to Oregon because of our lenient marijuana laws and receive most of their relief from marijuana. Discuss how you wish to disclose this with the attorney, as it may have an impact on the client’s treatment at the VA if it becomes public knowledge.

2023 Update

The following materials were prepared by Chapter 3 author Cindy Borders, describing cases where proper investigations were conducted and used by trial counsel, to achieve positive outcomes.

Sample Case Studies

1. “DB” served two combat tours in Iraq. He was charged with first-degree assault in one case and criminal mischief in another. Investigation obtained his military records. They noted that DB served two long tours in Iraq as a “sapper” (a combat engineer), and DB was exposed to blasts from 56 improvised explosive devices. VA records disclosed that owing to PSTD with dementia, DB was retired at age 35 from the U.S. Army, as a sergeant. After one and a half years of trips to the state hospital, extensive evaluations, interviews with fellow soldiers who disclosed the number of explosions was closer to 500 (including training explosions), and the victim learning of DB’s military history and injuries, the criminal mischief charge was dismissed at the victim’s request. The assault charge also was dismissed, after the court learned the same information. To date, DB has had no more interactions with the legal system.

2. “BM” served one combat tour in Iraq. He was charged with many crimes, including attempted aggravated murder of a state trooper and attempting to elude. Investigation established that on the anniversary of his first sergeant’s arrest for war crimes, BM was suffering a blackout when he committed the acts that resulted in the charges. Numerous police agencies engaged in a high-speed chase to arrest BM. Police stopped him by using “tack stripping,” shot BM in the side, and tased him while he begged police to kill him. After speaking with family members and people he had served with, and after learning of his medivac to Germany from Iraq after being found in a fetal position after a patrol, the case was successfully settled for probation with the blessing of the state trooper. BM was admitted to the Roseburg VA hospital for intensive in-patient drug and alcohol and PTSD treatment. To date, BM has had no more interactions with the legal system, has purchased a home, and is a successful entrepreneur.

3. “JD” is a veteran of the first Gulf War, as part of the initial invasion. While in Iraq he suffered extensive exposure to burn pits. Investigation showed JD had a history of alcohol abuse and erratic behavior. He was arrested at a bar for harassment and pled out at arraignment. This behavior was due to an anniversary of JD’s wife’s suicide. His behavior continued to deteriorate. Several days later he was arrested again, after viciously assaulting his neighbor. After obtaining his military records and speaking with various people, the case was pled in such a way that JD received a non–jail sentence. He then entered treatment and is doing very well.

4. “VD” served a difficult enlistment tour in the Army, although he never was deployed to a combat or hazardous-duty zone. VD was charged with various drug crimes and being the leader of a theft ring. The charges were negotiated, resulting in a sentence of probation. VD now is working full-time in construction.

Veterans Justice Outreach Program

In 2009, the federal VA started the Veterans Justice Outreach Program (VJOP). Every VA hospital has a VJOP office which can be contacted by calling the direct number.

1. The VA’s web page explains:

“The purpose of the VJOP initiate is to avoid the unnecessary criminalization of mental illness and extended incarceration among Veterans. VJO Outreach ensures that eligible justice-involved Veterans have timely access to VHA mental health and substance abuse services when clinically indicated and other VA services and benefits as appropriate. VA is requiring justice-focused activity at the medical center level. (Emphasis added.)

2. How do I work with the VJOP?

First, do not assume they know your veteran client has been arrested. Once you have interviewed your client, call the VJOP office (contact information for all VJOP offices nationwide here) and leave the client’s name, jail (if not released), and your contact information. You will need to provide the VJOP office with a VA Form 21-0845 authorizing you to speak with the VJOP.

Once you contact the VJOP—and this can take several calls and emails—program staff should visit your client in jail. They will require a copy of the police reports and any outside service providers that your client may have seen in relation to their mental health.

3. What does the VJOP do?

The VJOP can make recommendations to the court via a letter for your client to enter a treatment program. Moreover, several judges are familiar with the VJOP and its services.

VJOP staff can also assist with getting your client into an inpatient program when there appear to be waiting lists. They will also assist with transportation to and from the programs.

4. What types of cases does the VJOP handle?

The short answer is: All cases. The VA cannot deny services to a veteran because of pending criminal charges, or because they have been convicted of a crime. If it seems that a a VJOP representative is reluctant to help you because of the charges, contact the patient advocate office, the administrator of the hospital and the federal representative for your area.
