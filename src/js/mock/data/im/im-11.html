Investigators Manual

2010 edition

Interviewing Witnesses

Jerry Elshire

Introduction

Preparing for an interview requires preparing for things you know as well as things you don’t know. Investigators must recognize and deal with significant issues when they unexpectedly arise during an interview. The circumstances are analogous to a script that is not yet complete. You have a theme and know many of the characters, but the dialog is mostly unknown. Thorough preparation will usually lead to a successful ending.

I got my first interview adrenaline rush over thirty years ago. My style has thankfully improved, but the rush remains. While waiting for someone to answer my knock, I mentally twist and turn the possible reactions to my invasion. Investigators face a constant challenge to get the subject of the interview to open up.

Preparation

Review and Organize All Discovery, Letters, Requests (Including Client Statements)

One cannot say enough about reviewing police reports. They are the guts of your client’s case and an investigator must become very familiar with them. Organize the materials and prepare an outline that divides the reports into sections—date, officer, witness and summary of their statement(s). You will need to quickly find information during interviews. Some information contained in police reports will support the defense theory, some will not. Police officers seldom take their reports to the witness for review. Investigators should let the attorney decide when a witness should review the officer’s report of their statement. This can be an opportunity for the witness to deny statements and offer more complete explanations.

In addition to police reports, investigators must be aware of and access other discovery materials. Items such as 9-1-1 recordings, medical records, newspaper articles and client statements are only a few examples of information that may or may not be available.

Familiarize Yourself with the Law

There is no need to attain a lawyer’s level of knowledge in the law. Some understanding of the law, however, helps an investigator participate in development of the case theory, deal with the unexpected, as well as develop an understanding of what attorneys must deal with. Investigators are encouraged to read Oregon

Evidence, Fifth Editionby Laird Kirkpatrick (2007, LexisNexis). This book offers a helpful analysis of legal issues concerning evidence, including privileges, witnesses, opinions, expert testimony, relevance, character evidence, the hearsay rule (and its exceptions), presumptions, and the contents of recordings and photographs. OCDLA and the Oregon State Bar also have many other publications that can help the investigator understand specific areas of the law.

My familiarity with the law paid great dividends in State of Oregon v. Judah Larson,159 Or App 34 (1999). In that case the lawyer believed there was a legal issue concerning the search and seizure of marijuana plants at the client’s residence. The attorney provided me with case law that dealt with privacy protections in the area surrounding private dwellings. When I found a witness at the apartment complex who had placed a sign in the backyard that read, “We like you, but not in our backyard. PLEASE KEEP OUT!” I understood the potential legal significance of the sign. I photographed the sign and interviewed the witness based on my reading of the case law. The witness’s testimony at the suppression hearing provided the legal grounds for the court to suppress the search and seizure of the marijuana plants.

Talk to your attorney about legal restrictions on the government advising witnesses not to speak with the defense. Be prepared with a series of questions and responses for the witness who claims the police or prosecutor told them not to talk to anyone. Your notes of the witness’s responses should be detailed because the witness will likely try to change their tune at a later time. It is a fine line between “The prosecutor told me not to talk to you,” versus “The prosecutor told me I don’t have to talk to you.”

Background Information

Complete a thorough background check on all the witnesses. An outline for investigating the background of witnesses and informants is given in Appendix A. You can be sure the prosecution will conduct a background investigation of all defense witnesses.

Don’t forget to background the officers involved in the case. A sample letter of request to DPSST for training records is given in Appendix B. Check federal and state court records for the history of any civil or divorce cases against the officer. Try to obtain information about the officer’s activities leading up to the contact with the client or witness. Many agencies can provide a computer printout detailing the officer’s activities. You can also request a copy of the officer’s notebook. See Appendix C for a sample of Portland Police Bureau records requests.

Large police agencies, such as the Portland Police Bureau, often maintain their own databases for names of people, locations and vehicles involved in their criminal investigations. A sample request and the result of a name search are provided in Appendix D.

As a part of preparing for the interview, the investigator should research the types of investigative resources available to government agents. Government wiretaps are common. To obtain a wiretap, the government must allege that all avenues of investigation have failed and that only a wiretap will provide the needed information. In order to attack the wiretap, defense investigators are often asked to interview the agent who applied for it. One source of particular interest in wiretap cases is the El Paso Intelligence Center (EPIC). EPIC was established by the Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) in 1974 to gather drug-dealing intelligence from different local, state, federal and international agencies into one place. The center attempts to monitor the movement of drugs across international borders and tracks illegal aliens, fugitives, firearms, aircraft, goats, motor vehicles and currency. Agencies also provide the center with informant information about suspected drug dealers and their organizations. EPIC has a lot of information that agents should know about, but often say they don’t, before applying for a wiretap.

Review the Evidence and Go to the Scene

Attorneys can usually get permission from the prosecution for the investigator to view, copy and photograph evidence in police custody. Hopefully the attorney will accompany you as these items are very helpful in case preparation. Exercise caution, however, in discussing the case in the presence of the police personnel.

Go to the scene and take photographs, prepare drawings and take notes about landmarks. Define the scene as clearly as possible. Note the time and date of the alleged event. Seasonal changes can alter important details of the scene. (For more on photographing scenes and evidence, see Chapter 8.)

Who, What, and Where?

Before interviewing witnesses you should have a meeting with the attorney to discuss a game plan and possible case theories. When you are ready to start interviewing witnesses, ask yourself the following questions:

1.Who should I interview first and who should I interview last? Do I want to work from general to specific or vice versa?

2.Who am I about to talk to? Is the witness a professional (police, fire, etc.)? Is the witness the client? Is the witness friendly or hostile?

3.Why am I talking to the witness? Am I fact-finding? Do I expect the witness to support my position?

Am I assessing the opposition?

4.How do I want to set up the dynamics of the interview? Should I make an appointment (probably with a professional witness, client or key witness) or do I just show up (the hostile witness)? Can I do an effective interview over the phone? Should the interview take place in an office, at a home, or a neutral site? Should anyone else be present during the interview?

Conducting the Interview

Investigators depend on the willingness of the witness to talk about the case. We have no power to compel anyone to talk. The most crucial point in obtaining an interview comes immediately after the witness opens the door or answers the telephone.

Initial Contact

Advise the person who you are, why you are calling, and who you work for. In criminal cases alleged victims must also be advised that they do not have to talk to the defendant’s attorney or agents and that they may have the district attorney present during an interview (ORS 135.970(2)). Never give or leave the impression that you are an attorney. It is possible to let a witness know what your role is without answering questions about the case (“Oh, we are just gathering facts right now”). Some telephone conversations between two parties can be recorded with the consent of only one of the parties (ORS 165.540). Never record a telephone interview with a witness unless you have discussed the specific legal issues involved with the attorney. Likewise, you should seek legal advice before intercepting (recording) oral communications. It is a misdemeanor crime to intercept oral communications to which you are not a party (ORS 165.543).

Develop a Lure

Always develop an initial question that serves as a lure for the witness to bite on. With police officers, you might point out a part of their report that obviously leaves out an important fact or detail. Blanket requests for interviews seldom work with police officers.

Careful review of the discovery and case background will often help prepare a lure. I worked on a sex abuse case, for example, that involved a very convincing twelve-year-old girl. After reviewing the reports, I prepared a lure that indirectly presented a problem and asked for help in solving it. I put together the following introduction:

Hello, I’m Jerry Elshire, I work as an investigator with John Doe, Attorney. We represent Mr. X, who is accused of sexual impropriety with Jane Roe. She formerly lived next door to you. I apologize for bothering you. If you think I should go on my way, I will. If there is something that you feel you should talk to me about I would greatly appreciate it.

Several witnesses invited me into their homes and told me they expected something like this to happen. They then provided me with damaging character evidence about the alleged victim. Their testimony provided the basis for a not-guilty verdict.

Witness Reactions

Be prepared for some typical witness reactions to your request to speak with them. “I don’t think I should talk to the defense.” “How can you represent someone like that?” A good answer will not offend the witness. Maybe you can agree with the witness and tell them that you feel bad about the case also. Help people understand that sometimes you represent people who are not guilty. Let them know you are an experienced and reputable professional. Perhaps you can refer to a recent case concerning an innocent or falsely accused person in the media in order to keep the door open and start a conversation.

Outlines

An outline assures that you cover all necessary points. Incorporate what you have learned from prior interviews. Organization can help you maintain important control over the interview. Don’t be over- committed to the outline, however. Always remember to listen to the answers and follow up on opportunities as they present themselves.

Respect the Witness

Set the person at ease. Use a humane approach and show respect for the witness. Try to engage in preliminary conversation about family life or common interests. Look for photographs, trophies, cars, or anything that will encourage conversation and respect. The witness may even forget why you showed up at their door. This interaction lets the investigator see how the witness reacts to questions and clearly facilitates communication. Bringing out a tape recorder or immediately making copious notes can quickly turn off a witness.

Let the Witness Talk

Always make the witness think they know more about the case than you. Let the person talk. Let the person tell their entire story. Do not interrupt. Encourage and allow the person to explain, describe and review the situation in their own words, phrases and expressions. Using your outline, come back to issues to confirm or clarify key points. Distract and re-ask questions for consistency. (“I’m sorry, give me a second to catch up here. Okay, now what was that you said?”) For additional information about interrogation and cognitive interviewing techniques, see the Criminal Investigator’s Handbook(Matthew Bender, 2005).

Control the Pace

Control the pace of the questions. You normally want to use a steady pace in order to keep the interview focused and under your control. Master the art of the tactful or pregnant silence. This can be used when you are at the crucial point of the interview. Leave time for the witness to decompress after the interview. It may have been a stressful event and your respectful style will pay dividends.

Your Impressions

Remember to note your subjective impressions of the witness. The attorney needs to know how the witness presents. Was the witness nervous, angry, fearful, or quiet? Or, was the witness composed, cooperative, concerned, open, or spontaneous? This information needs to go in the separate and clearly marked work- product portion of your notes or reports.

Conclusion

Interviews must produce relevant evidence admissible in court. The “I sense” or “I consider” statements may not be admissible or useful in court. Clear, positive statements such as “I saw” or “I clearly heard” are much more convincing. The investigator has the responsibility to provide substantial and useful facts to an attorney by thoroughly preparing for interviews, using appropriate approaches to witnesses, and professionally controlling the interview.
