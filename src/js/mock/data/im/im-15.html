Juvenile Delinquency Cases

Miriam Widman

Juvenile Court Basics

In delinquency cases allegations are presented in the form of a petition. This is similar to an indictment or District Attorney Information in the adult system.

The number of agencies and players involved in the juvenile system is more numerous and different than in the adult arena. Juveniles are taken in to detention, not jail. Juvenile detention centers are county facilities. Juveniles can have a trial, but there is no jury.

If a youth is picked up in County X, but has allegedly committed a crime in County Y, the charge may be handled in either county, depending on the charge. For example, a youth who lives in Portland commits a crime in Lincoln City while on vacation and is arrested, released to parents and returned to Portland. Lincoln City can decide to send the police report to Portland and ask that they file the petition, or Lincoln City can file it. However, if Lincoln City officials decide to do that, they have to serve the youth and parents back in Portland. If Lincoln City decides to ask Portland to file the case, the defense could always try to get it moved back to Lincoln City, but normally the youth and parents prefer having a court closer to home.

Another key difference between the juvenile and adult systems is the emphasis on treatment or rehabilitation. Compromises and formal agreements that allow the client to avoid detention time appear to be more prevalent in the juvenile arena than elsewhere.

Key Players in Juvenile Cases

•The juvenile defendant, often accompanied by relatives.

•Parents – as parties during the dispositional (sentencing) phase of the juvenile proceeding and can be

required to do services. They may also be represented by counsel.

•The juvenile’s defense attorney or agent (legal assistant, investigator).

•The Deputy District Attorney – the person prosecuting the case.

•The juvenile court counselor or JCC. This person can play multiple and often contradictory roles as a

pretrial release officer, pretrial disposition investigator, social worker and probation officer.

•Oregon Youth Authority (OYA) – responsible for youth corrections, including supervision of youth committed to OYA on probation, juvenile corrections facilities and parole supervision.

•Department of Human Services (DHS) – roughly 80 percent of caseworkers will be from DHS, with the remainder coming from OYA. Your client may already be in the custody of DHS due to the family’s prior involvement with the system because of abuse or neglect of the youth. A youth involved in a delinquency case may also be placed in the temporary legal custody of DHS for out-of-home placement in foster care, in a group home or in residential treatment.

It is important to understand the various players’ positions in the case and to determine who supports your client and what type of information is needed to bolster your client’s case. For example, does the DHS caseworker mention your client’s positive school record? Talk to teachers and school staff to build that aspect of the case.

The Juvenile Court Process

•Detention Hearing: As in the adult arena, a judge or referee (who is not an elected judge, but can rule in juvenile cases) must find probable cause to believe a crime has been committed in order for the youth to be detained and for lawyers to argue for the youth’s pretrial release.

•Discovery: Most aspects are identical to discovery in adult cases. A key difference is if other youth are being charged and/or serving as witnesses. You cannot simply access these records, as the Oregon Judicial Information Network (OJIN) has rules that limit such access. Check first about access to juvenile records.

•Trial: Juvenile trials do not have juries. Decisions are made by judges or referees.

•Compromise Agreements: Many cases are resolved in ways that are more favorable to the client than in the adult system. Depending on the violation and your client’s prior record, sometimes the DA will agree to a Formal Accountability Agreement (FAA), which will spare your client a more onerous punishment in exchange for an agreed upon set of conditions, such as counseling and/or drug and alcohol treatment. In the case of a theft charge, this is likely to include paying back the victim the value of the stolen goods, writing a letter of apology to the victim and/or attending a class on how theft hurts the community. These FAAs may also be available for assault and other charges.

Investigating Juvenile Delinquency Cases

Investigators should ask the attorney for a written investigation request and a copy of the discovery. The investigation request should include, at the minimum, the following:

•Names of key parties in the case and notations as to who is represented.

•List of questions or topics the attorney wants you to explore.

•Deadline.

You should also meet with the attorney to discuss the theory of the case. Knowing what the attorney thinks happened and where the attorney is going with the legal proceeding shapes the investigation.

As in all investigations, investigators are agents of the attorneys for whom they are working and must not have contact with represented parties about the subject of the representation without permission from the other attorney. So, before contacting any witness or victim, the investigator must ascertain whether the victim or witness is represented by counsel.

Locating Teen Witnesses

Often, police reports will have location information for witnesses, though this is not always the case. Sometimes what they have is incorrect. In today’s Internet world, social media websites like Facebook and MySpace can be very useful. You might consider establishing work-related Facebook and MySpace accounts just for your investigation purposes.

Don’t misrepresent yourself on these sites. If you need to “friend” someone and they send you a message asking what this is all about, be upfront and honest. However, many teen sources will confirm your friend request without any follow-up questions.

Facebook and MySpace are also great for finding other witnesses. Connections are key in the teen world and someone’s friend list can become a wealth of information. Review the person’s profile and check photos – there may be some key information there.

Finally, don’t forget to check your client’s Facebook or MySpace pages. There may very well be some incriminating or unfavorable information there, including photos that would not be favorable to your client if seen by the DA. Let your attorney know the contents of the pages and, if needed, familiarize them with the sites. Not all attorneys are fluent in the language of social networking and some may be intimidated by the multitude of places people can post their personal views and pictures. After reviewing the client’s profile, the attorney may decide to tell the client to remove his or her profile from the sites.

Interviewing Juvenile Witnesses

One of the stickiest and more difficult issues in investigating juvenile delinquency cases is when you have to try to interview your client’s alleged victims or other youth who may have witnessed the event. The victim issue is addressed separately because there are specific legal requirements that must be met for such interviews.

Interviewing Witnesses—Obtain Parental Consent

Parental consent is a key factor when interviewing juvenile witnesses and friends of your client. Best practice is to ask parents for their permission first and then approach the youth. Then, of course, ask the youth if she is okay speaking with you.

In instances in which there are a large number of high school–age witnesses who may be difficult to locate, it may be impossible to ask the parents first. Often these teen witnesses – when you can locate them – are not with their parents. In such cases, ask the teen if he is willing to talk now or would prefer that the investigator contact the parent first. If the teen wants the parent’s okay first, don’t do the interview until the parent has agreed to it. An investigator talking to a known minor without parental permission risks complaints and threats of legal action.

From an investigator’s point of view it’s also good to get the witnesses’ parents on your side. They may know something their child does not, have knowledge of your client’s home background, can tell you something about your client’s parents or friends, the school environment, or other difficulties. When investigating an alleged sex offense, parents may be able to tell you that your client has always been appropriate with their younger children and there have never been any complaints.

Interviewing Victims

There are very clear rules on what you must do and what you cannot do when interviewing the victim of a crime (ORS. 135.970 (2) and (3) (see Appendix B for all statutes referenced in this chapter). Victims – and if the victim is a juvenile the youth’s legal guardian is also considered a victim (ORS 419A.004(31) – must be given specific information about victim’s rights before they can be interviewed. In contrast to the adult world where this information must be provided by an attorney, Oregon law (ORS 419C.276) allows both the attorney and an agent of the attorney to provide this information in person or in writing.

There are victims who are more than happy to speak with you. Many of these victims are more interested in rehabilitation than punishment and can provide background to a variety of social and emotional problems your client may be facing.

Best practice when approaching a victim for an interview is to provide the victim with a victim’s rights letter identifying who you are and who you represent. The letter must inform the victim that they have a right not to speak with the defense and that should they agree to speak, they have a right to have a DA present during the interview. See the Appendix for a sample letter.

In cases with longer deadlines, send this letter via regular mail. It should be addressed to both the victim and the victim’s parents. As stated above, the parents are considered victims as well. In cases with a tight deadline, call the victim’s parent, introduce yourself and read the victim’s rights letter over the phone. Ask the parent if she or he understands the information and is okay speaking to you. Then ask if you can speak to the child. Depending on the age of the child, the parent may agree. Oftentimes a parent will speak with the defense but not make the child available. Assuming you obtain an interview either by phone or at a later date, send the victim and the parent a written copy of the victim’s rights letter via the Postal Service. Always make a copy of the victim’s rights letter for the file, noting when it was sent or read over the phone.

Victims of Sex Offenses

These are some of the toughest victim interviews. Many times you will be speaking with the victim’s parent or guardian. Many parents of alleged sex abuse victims will not allow you to speak with the actual victim but they will speak with the defense. In the majority of cases the victim is either related to your client, or is a close friend. Often these parents can provide key family background about your client and the circumstances of the event.

On occasion you may run into a situation in which you make the initial contact in person with the victim and the parent. After your introduction and reading of the victim’s rights letter, it could be that the actual victim is willing to speak with you but the parent is against it. This is an unfortunate situation, but there is nothing you can do. In such cases, if the victim has an adult sibling, that person may be able to provide information. The victim’s friends may be useful too, but once again, it is best practice to get parental permission before interviewing any minors.

Tips for Speaking with Teens

As with any interview, try to make sure you are on the interviewee’s level. This is especially true when interviewing teens, who will only turn off to a know-it-all attitude. If you are a parent, let them know you have kids and that you understand it’s not easy being a teen these days. Make yourself human to the child. If you see they have a bicycle and you like to ride, tell them about that. If they have a pet and you do too, talk about that. If they play a sport or have a hobby, ask them about that. What position do you play? How often do you train?

Interviewing Prosecution Witnesses

Since these are likely to be children or teens, the same procedures should be followed as when you interview defense witnesses. Best practice is to ask parents for permission and then approach the child.

Observing the Scenes – Make Diagrams

This is no less important in juvenile cases than in adult ones, but your client’s physical limitations may be a factor for the defense. Does the DA maintain that your client stole a cell phone and wallet from the house and then jumped the fence to escape? How high is the fence? How big is your client? Does your client have any physical limitations that would prevent him or her from jumping the fence?

Gathering School Records and Exhibits

School records can be very useful in your defense. You may have a client accused of assaulting another student. Is there anything in the school record of problems between these two in the past? Does your client’s school counselor know about the tensions? Did the alleged victim ever make a racial or ethnic slur about your client?

ORS 419C.200(2) permits court-appointed attorneys to inspect and copy any records the school has of your delinquency client. The statute doesn’t address the confidentiality of other students who may be named in incident reports, however, schools will usually redact those names. Even if some student names are redacted, there is information about the school official who wrote the report. Most of the time school personnel are willing to talk and share information. School records are also useful for information about disabilities the client might have. A disability could impact a defense issue, such as the ability to aid and assist.

Occasionally when you have a client accused of shoplifting, you may be asked to get a copy of a store’s security video. It is worth making the effort, but keep in mind most retailers retain their videotapes for 30 days and then record over them. Also, many “local” stores are actually owned by nationwide chains and often have policies that prohibit store personnel from speaking with the defense.

Subpoenaing Witnesses and Keeping Track

The subpoena process is similar to the adult one (see ORS 419C.405–.408). Younger children are subpoenaed through their parents. For older children, a courtesy copy of the subpoena should be provided to the parent and the child should be personally served. When in the custody of DHS, the caseworker must be served the subpoena.

Keeping track of witnesses can be different than in the adult arena, as making sure juvenile witnesses get to court requires a bit of extra effort. Teen witnesses don’t often have a driver’s license and even if they do, it is unlikely they will have access to a vehicle. How is your teen witness coming to court? Can parents help with transportation? Does your witness have a bus pass? Do they know which bus to take? Where to transfer? Of course it is incumbent upon you to let the witness know if the trial has been set over and she needs to be there on a different date.

Conclusion

Someone who has investigated adult cases should be perfectly capable of investigating juvenile ones, if they follow the additional rules and pay attention to the specific challenges of interviewing youth. Teenagers are not mini adults, and younger children require a special understanding of their situation. Be cognizant of your approach and the language you use. Getting information out of youth can sometimes be frustrating and difficult, but juvenile delinquency investigations are some of the most rewarding in the business.

The greater emphasis on rehabilitation instead of punishment means you have the chance – through information you’ve gathered – to help the attorney reach a compromise agreement with the DA that will not only reduce the legal consequences for your client, but provide him or her with counseling and education to help turn their life around.

Good work can help save clients from consequences that range from a short stay at a juvenile detention center to a mandatory lifetime of sex offender registration. The impact of saving a youth from such registration, which makes it extremely difficult to get housing and employment, is hard to quantify. It has a lasting and positive effect – both for the client and the investigator.
