Investigators Manual

Forensic Photography

Clyde C. Gideon

2010 edition

Introduction

This chapter is intended to cover the basic concepts of forensic photography that may be required during the course of a criminal defense investigation. These concepts are designed to produce a photograph or video that a criminal defense attorney should expect to have available for pretrial preparation, any subsequent negotiation, and/or for presentation at trial.

Each heading in this chapter could easily be expanded into a chapter on its own. Some details will have to be omitted. Every effort will be made, however, to cover the situations that are usually found in a typical criminal defense investigation and a few situations that occur less frequently. There will also be care given to include “pitfalls” to watch out for and some helpful hints and tricks. Seasoned and novice investigators alike should find the information useful.

Since the “architecture” for this chapter will be very broad and all-encompassing some of the sections will seem like overkill. In the section on equipment I have included a description of the equipment that I use. Photography is a hobby of mine as well as a necessary part of my investigative work. Consequently, I have much more equipment than is necessary for basic forensic photography. The reason that I describe it all is to assist you in determining what format of equipment you want to work in. I use Polaroid (more to be said about the demise of this format later), digital “point-and-shoot,” cell phone camera, 35mm SLR, Hi-8 video, and digital video–digital still combination. Though not included in this chapter, I also use large format cameras. This multiformat equipment is not absolutely necessary, but I will try to explain some of the benefits and drawbacks of each one.

I will also explain basic concepts of photography in terms that are synonymous with “film” cameras. If you are using a digital camera, many, if not all, of these concepts may be utilized on the digital camera by referring to the camera menu or the instruction manual.

Purpose—Accuracy

Photography is multifaceted. It is often used as an art form. If you have ever read, practiced or taken classes on how to take photographs with good color, composition, creativity, balance, framing and contrast – you can forget all of those rules. In forensic photography you will break some of those rules all of the time and all of those rules some of the time.

Generally your task will be to take a photograph which represents an object or a scene precisely as it actually exists at the moment the photograph was taken. The challenge is accuracy –without embellishment. Creativity is not a virtue unless it assists in accurately depicting the scene or object. In other words, you might be creative in climbing onto the roof of a house and hanging over the edge to photograph the distance between a bush and the house. But creatively tying ropes around the bush to get in behind it and photographing the distance is not permitted.

There is a phenomenon which seems, at first, to be contradictory to accurate photographic representation –storytelling. We will be discussing the art of storytelling throughout this chapter. Indeed, an attorney may use your photographs or video to tell a story about the scene as it existed months or years before you took the pictures. There is no contradiction here. Your charge is still accuracy without embellishment. You must not use tricks or gimmicks to adulterate or alter a photograph to help the attorney tell a story. Attorneys are very good storytellers. Let them practice their craft. Storytelling for the forensic photographer is a state of mind that ensures a complete and logical series of photographs that will with little narrative, let the viewer see and understand a scene or an event as if they were there.

When To Photograph

One of the advantages of photographs and video is that they can assist in the investigation and for that they need to be available long before trial. Remember, the attorney may want to use the photographs for pretrial preparation and any subsequent negotiation.

Know the case before you go out to take photographs. Although there may be several photographic tasks throughout the case investigation, the primary tasks require specific knowledge. The most basic information will come from the initial discovery, and you need to have a full and complete understanding of what the state has done and thinks was the crime and the players within the crime. The scene where the crime allegedly took place will be identified and various witnesses or pieces of evidence will begin to tell a story. If there are obvious gaps in the discovery, such as missing witness interviews or missing evidence receipts, these must be obtained before proceeding. Also, the photos taken by the police, coroner, crime lab, etc. are considered to be part of the discovery that you have perused.

Before, during and after reading the discovery you should have several discussions with the attorney. These discussions should lead to deciding on a theory of defense and the direction your investigation will head. Your client conferences will also shed light on the path for your investigation. The knowledge obtained from the discovery and the conversations with the attorney and client will form the minimum basic information before proceeding.

Of course, guidelines need to be adjusted as necessary. If on the day you first get the case and prior to reading the discovery you learn that the crime scene is going to be demolished, run right out and photograph the scene even if you are ill-prepared and may miss something.

Equipment

Polaroid

The benefits of this camera were obvious. I used an SLR 680 SE, which allowed viewing and flash readings through the lens like a 35mm SLR. It enabled me to see precisely what the print would look like. This was a good camera for taking a quick photo that needed to instantly be in hard copy form or for backing up another camera on a critical shot.

Picture quality was poor compared to other formats, but the instant viewing of a shot in hard copy was something that only Polaroid could offer. Unfortunately, Polaroid no longer makes their product.

35mm

This has been the mainstay of forensic photography for many decades. Digital cameras are catching up, but if I could only have one camera, this is the one. The picture quality is very good, so detail and enlargements for court exhibits are no problem. I use a Minolta system, a brand choice I made over 25 years ago and have stayed with because of accessories and the interchangeability of lenses. There are times when changing the aperture, speed and lenses of the camera are critical and a 35mm SLR offers this total versatility. The film still needs to be developed and you have no proof that you got a good picture, but the camera’s ability to “bracket” shots and “paint with light” are close to guarantees of a good picture. More about these last two concepts later in this chapter.

Digital “Point-and-shoot”

The benefit of these inexpensive cameras is their ease of operation. They also have a self-timer and fit on a tripod. This can be handy if you need to take a shot that is obscured by a tree limb or if you are alone and need to hold a tape measure up to your subject that is more than a few feet away. These cameras do not have interchangeable lenses or detachable flash units, but you should always have one in your glove box that you can grab, point and shoot in a few seconds while still driving your car or if you don’t have the camera you really need at hand.

I include cell phone cameras in this category. The quality of pictures for some of these cameras is amazing. My Blackberry cell phone camera takes a picture that is superior to my 5.2-megapixel digital camera, which used to be my point-and-shoot choice. Although they are difficult to stabilize, the instant email of the photo makes these cameras the one tool that has done the most with recent technology.

Video Camera

These cameras come in digital, VHS, Hi-8 and various 8mm and 8mm cassette formats. TV news cameras are the highest quality digital video, but they use a digital format that costs around $50,000. For the rest of us there is the consumer-professional market that ranges from a few hundred dollars to $15,000.

I use a Canon L2, which is a Hi-8 camera that uses all the lenses available from the Canon Auto focus 35mm cameras. There are dozens of types of Canon AF lenses available so the versatility is unlimited. This camera is more than what is needed for forensic video. It does have several important features, however, including an S-Video terminal which allows playback or download to a VCR. This provides the least generation loss. Most video cameras download using RCA plugs—the red, yellow and white wires that hook your TV and VCR together. This allows multiple sound systems such as remote microphones and lapel mikes to be used simultaneously. It has all of the manual and automatic lens speed and aperture settings of a good 35mm system.

Digital still and digital video combination cameras are common and within a few years will rival 35mm camera systems and Hi-8 video cameras in both price and quality. The discussion on the use and attributes of digital versus other camera systems could take many pages to explain in detail, so let me shorten it all by explaining what my camera does and why I bought it.

My Canon Optura is a combination video and still camera. It will take 600 to 700 photos on one ten-dollar cassette or one hour of video or a combination of photos and videos. While taking still photos you can flip a switch and automatically you are videotaping, without changing the camera position or your own position to look for the proper setting. Each time a still photo is taken, the camera records 30 frames per second for six seconds of that one shot. During that six seconds, the camera will record the sound occurring at the scene. This enables the operator to record a number or description of the photo for later use. It also records the conversation going on around the camera, which can be very interesting, however, disable this feature in the event of consent issues.

It has a “progressive scan” which is like a motor-drive feature. It enables 30 frames per second of full-quality action to be recorded. These are not half-frame shots like a video camera; they are 30 full-quality still photos. Most fast 35mm cameras have motor drives that peak at about five to eight frames per second.

The camera downloads using RCA plugs, S-video cables or “firewire.” It downloads to TV, VCR or computer. Once in a computer the photos or videos can be emailed, put on web pages or written to a CD or DVD. I recently took over six hundred photos at an evidence view and never stopped to change film. I gave each of the other five members of the defense team a copy of the photos on CD. The cost for the CD, label, case, padded mailing envelope and postage was less than five dollars each. Considerably less than five copies of six hundred 35mm photos.

There are drawbacks to digital systems. My camera has one silicon chip in the lens. This limits quality. Some cameras have three chip systems and the quality of the video is very near that of studio television.

There are also costs associated with the computer software and other related accessories, but there is a place for digital photography in forensic investigation and it is growing fast. Some have been concerned that there would be challenges in court due to the ease with which digital photos can be enhanced and manipulated. I feel little will be made of this issue, at least as it pertains to criminal court. Most of the use of sophisticated digital photo and video systems that I have observed has been by police departments. This leaves little room for argument by the prosecution when the defense presents digital exhibits. Any major and long-lasting court battles that I foresee would likely be on the civil court side.

You should never be without a camera and therefore at least one point-and-shoot camera is mandatory. I also recommend a good-quality 35mm film camera. Auto focus is handy, but not mandatory. A variety of lenses is advisable but a high-quality 50mm lens is a good starting point. After that a 28mm wide-angle lens and a zoom to 120mm is good. For very close work I use close-up filters on a 50mm lens, but a macro lens is the common way to photograph very close detail.

After the 35mm, the field is open. If you need video, digital is great. Above all, don’t make a purchase in haste. Quality equipment costs money and quality equipment is worth buying. If waiting a few months will give you more money for a good system, then wait.

Film, Lighting and Accessories

Film is simple. Pick a major brand that is readily available and stick with it, though it should be noted that film is becoming harder to find. Pick a couple of different ASA ratings and stick with them. I would suggest 100 ASA for normal shots with good light and outdoors, and 400 ASA for low light situations. Carry a few 12-exposure rolls of each. Load your camera with 12-exposure film when you think you are taking only one or two pictures. Then take all 12 exposures on your subject. More is better and film is cheap. Carry many rolls of 36-exposure film. Load this when you are taking several photos. Shoot all of the film in the camera on your subject before you put the camera away.

Another tip: Don’t shoot pictures for two cases on one roll of film. Use all the film in your camera on the case at hand, unload the film, and mark the roll with the case name or number using a felt tip pen. The negatives have sequential numbers underneath them and you need to account for all the negatives of a roll if asked. And, if you are in this business long enough, you will be asked.

Speaking of numbers, here is another tip. Don’t permanently place any sequential number on the back of a print you give to the attorney. Use some easily removable label, even post-it notes. If the attorney should ask you on the witness stand about a photograph, opposing counsel might turn it over and notice the number “16” and then ask you about the other 35 pictures on the roll, hoping one of them would show the subject more to his liking.

Lighting is pretty straightforward. Your camera needs to be able to see with the light that you provide for any situation you are trying to photograph. The factory-recommended strobe or movie light for your camera is a minimum. A battery-powered emergency flood is a necessary backup. I also have two sets of dual 500-watt halogen work lights on telescoping stands, and three single 500-watt lights that sit on the ground. These are mandatory for arson photographs of a burned-out building. A black-charred room will eat the flash from a strobe light as if it never fired. Remember, though, most burned-out buildings have no electricity, so you need very long, heavy-duty extension cords or bring your own electrical generator. More about these problems later.

Other necessary accessories include a tripod, a cable for remote shutter firing, a cable for moving the strobe light away from the camera without detaching the synchronizing capability, batteries and any chargers necessary for the batteries.

All of the lenses of all of the cameras should have UV haze filters on them at all times to protect the end of the lens. The filter is not removed when the camera is in use. It removes ultraviolet rays from the ambient lighting and improves picture quality. A polarizing filter can be used instead of a UV filter. More about that later.

One concept that is extremely important is containment: Containing all the items necessary to take a photo or video. Each and every camera system you use should be contained in a camera bag. That bag should be complete in every detail for the operation of that camera system. This is no problem if you use only one camera system. But as soon as you expand to two or more systems, you will find that lights, film, batteries, battery chargers, remote light and shutter cords are interchangeable from one system to another. The tendency to not purchase one of these items for your new camera because you have one that will work from your older camera is a mistake if both cameras are going to be in service. Out in the field you may not be able to carry each of your cameras to the location of the shoot, or you may not have brought each camera. Film and accessories that are required will do little good if they are in a camera bag you did not bring to the scene, so stock each camera system completely.

Other Stuff

Other stuff is a category of accessories and supplies that will be necessary with any camera system and should be taken to most shooting locations. I carry measuring and marking devices in a multipocket fishing vest: a roll of string, wire, a knife, pliers, bright fluorescent yarn, a bubble level that hooks onto string and various hooks, snaps, tacks, push-pins and swivels. Also, a 12-foot measuring tape, a 50-foot measuring tape, and a very handy 6.5-foot metal tape that is one-inch wide with very large numerals that can be seen easily when photographed from a distance. This last tape can be pulled out completely from its container and stood up next to an object to illustrate size. It is formed in such a way that it will not bend over, so it does not have to be held in position. These items are useful because they compensate for the fact that you are working alone.

I also have an accessory bag that I bring primarily for video shoots. It contains heavy duty extension cords and electrical connectors, a hammer and nails, gaffer’s tape, duct tape and various lamps and lighting necessities. By the way, once you have used gaffer’s tape you will never want to be without it. It will hold a heavy object like a halogen lamp on a painted wall. But unlike duct tape, it won’t pull the paint off the wall when you remove it. It is also invaluable in taping extension cords to a floor or carpet, which is a mandatory safety procedure.

Photographing a Crime or Accident Scene

Before going out to do any photographic assignment, some thought has to be given to the purpose of the shoot. Your photographs or video, remember, are for use by an attorney in pretrial preparation. Most likely, the attorney has not yet been to the scene. The attorney may be presenting your work to a prosecutor or a jury, who may not be familiar with the city where the incident occurred. Here is where storytelling comes into play.

  • Begin with generalities and work slowly toward specifics.
  • Try to tie each picture into something from the previous shot to lead your audience along a narrative.
  • Document in notes or on tape what each photo depicts and the location of the camera.

Sequence

Your sequence of shooting—whether video or stills—must start with familiarizing your intended audience with the area. Landmarks are the best way to familiarize an audience with a location. A good way to start this process is a shot of two intersecting street signs, then a shot of the accident location with the same street sign in the picture, or the front of the house where the crime occurred with the same street sign in the picture. For more familiarity, take a shot both up and down the street (all four directions at an intersection) and one across the street from the house. Photograph all sides of the house (including the back of the house and the back yard) and the houses on either side of it. Shoot all four street intersections with the camera at the diagonal intersection.

At this stage it is very important to know the locations of every witness and potential witness. Take a photo as near as possible to their location at the time of the incident. And remember, if you subsequently do a neighborhood canvass looking for additional witnesses, have your camera along to augment their statement. Here is where the point-and-shoot camera in the glove box will really pay off.

At an accident scene, photograph specifics such as skid marks, tire marks on curbs, pot holes and grade changes. Remember, if visibility approaching an intersection is a possible factor, you must photograph the path taken by all participants. These photos should be at approximate eye level for each driver or pedestrian. Normally a camera held by a standing photographer will properly show the eye level of a standing adult or a driver in a car. But if one driver was in a large truck, you may need to rent a similar truck or mount the camera on a jib to get the elevation correct. Be sure to show the driver’s ability to see all stop or warning signs.

If a stop sign is near foliage that appears to have been trimmed back since the accident, photograph the cut foliage in detail, including close-ups of the cuts. Then take a series of shots with measuring devices in the pictures that show the diameter of the cut limbs and the distances from the foliage cuts to the near and far sides of the stop sign. An expert arborist or biologist may be brought in to court to testify as to the length of the limbs before they were trimmed. These photographs will also be a reminder to you to follow-up regarding the date of the landscaping changes.

If the crime scene is in a house and assuming you have permission to enter, photograph the front door and walk inside. Take at least one shot standing in the doorway showing the first glimpse upon entering. Turning around, take another of the view of the street from the front door. It goes without saying that if the discovery has revealed an alleged break-in, this area should be photographed in detail before entering the house. Don’t try to remember to return to an area and photograph it later if you know it is important. You will find new reasons you were not aware of from discovery to retake some shots later.

Take photographs of all the rooms of the house even if they were not the actual crime scene. This includes closets, bathrooms, hallways, the basement and attic. Show where the access is to crawl spaces under or above the living area and, if possible, gain access and take at least one photograph to show that they were examined and did not appear to show anything of evidentiary value.

Also, if your access to the scene permits, do not fail to open and photograph the inside of all cupboards and drawers. Do not forget to photograph under the kitchen and bathroom sinks. After the initial shot of these areas, remove any garbage bags or containers and take one shot of the contents as they appear undisturbed.

Finally, you can begin to photograph the detail of any blood spatter, dried blood or bullet holes. Photograph any areas where the scene was altered by crime lab personnel, such as holes left by carpet or sheet rock removal, etc. If an area seems to have been cleaned up by someone prior to the arrival of the police and therefore incident to the crime, attempt to locate any cleaning supplies and photograph them well enough for product identification. Traces of these products may appear in subsequent laboratory tests.

During this part of the procedure, you will probably be using measuring devices to show relative scale in some photographs. Perhaps you are “stringing” blood spatter to show directionality or photographing dowels in bullet holes for the same reason. A good rule to follow is don’t take any photograph that includes an object that you have added, without first taking the same shot without the additional device. This has not been a big issue in recent years, but there was a time when major courtroom arguments ensued regarding admissibility of photographs that contained objects that were foreign to the scene. Protect yourself and be able to show one of each. Prosecutors are reluctant to argue this issue since their own crime labs and pathologists’ photos have measuring devices included. Civil attorneys may still want to bring the issue up.

Another caveat: Unless you are only accompanied by associates or experts who are also couched within privilege, do not talk about what you are doing or things that you notice at the scene. An onlooker or homeowner is just as dangerous as a policeman for transmitting information to the district attorney and therefore to the jury. If there are such people present, communicate with your colleagues in writing or not at all.

Photographing Vehicles

Photographing vehicles in a civil case can be a very detailed and time consuming matter. It may involve proving liability on the design, manufacture or maintenance of the vehicle or shifting causation to another driver or an entity responsible for design and maintenance of the roadway or adjacent landscaping.

All of these issues can be present in a criminal case as well, if the driver is being accused of criminal conduct in the use of the vehicle.

First and foremost in the photography of a vehicle is the proper identification of the vehicle. If the license plates are attached and they are the ones that identify the vehicle for the purpose of the case, then the first series known as “basic eight” will suffice.

Basic eight is a series of eight photos taken of the front, rear, both sides and four corners. If the camera position is similar to the numerals on a clock face, a photo is taken at the 12, 3, 6 and 9 o’clock positions and the 1:30, 4:30, 7:30 and 10:30 positions. If the plates are missing, then the Vehicle Identification Number should be photographed. On older cars the V.I.N. number was located on the driver’s door jamb or the firewall but late model cars have the number on a strip attached at the top of the dash board where it meets the windshield in front of the steering wheel. This was designed to give a policeman an easy way to identify a vehicle even though locked and without plates, to determine if it was stolen.

Photographing anything through glass can occasionally create problems because of the glare of the sun. The quickest remedy for this is the polarizing filter which was noted earlier as an alternative filter to be used for protecting the end of a lens. A polarizing filter is a two-piece accessory that can be rotated with the attached lever to remove the effect of the sun’s glare. This trick is standard operating procedure for investigators who do surveillance and need to identify people in cars or standing at plate glass windows.

In addition to the number on the vehicle for identification, the investigator must consider the information given by witnesses for identification. Witnesses most frequently include color as a means of vehicle identification. Just taking photographs of the vehicle does not necessarily give a true representation of color. The amount and type of light that is present will determine what color a witness perceives. The same color in sunlight will appear much different under halogen light, for instance. So, your photograph of a car in the sun may not be the same perception that a witness experienced of that same car in a parking garage or under a streetlight.

To keep it simple, the way to get the most accurate film photograph of a car’s color is to shoot the car in the same light conditions as the witness saw the car, and to use film which is made for those conditions. In other words, use daylight film outdoors and indoor film under artificial lighting. A close-up picture should be taken of a “color card” next to the car door or fender to show how the car appears in relation to true colors. These cards can be purchased at a photo equipment store. The color card could then be presented in court as an exhibit to show accurate representation of color in the photograph.

Another way is to videotape the car in the same ambient light that the witness experienced after setting the video camera “white balance” to that light. Video cameras can be adjusted to accept any “temperature” of light so that two types of light will reflect the same color. If your camera has a manual white balance you turn auto white balance off, zoom in on a pure white object such as a white piece of paper and push the white balance button. Until the camera is turned off and restarted, the white balance is set for those conditions. You would need to download the video to produce a color print, but some photo shops can do that for you.

Photographing Evidence

Evidence photography appears elementary and straightforward but it is not. Too many investigators take this procedure lightly and reveal information that they should not and overlook information that was readily available and may be extremely important. Discovery probably contains photographs of evidence; you are not there to reinvent the wheel, thereby wasting your client’s or the state’s money. Your purpose is to gather information you did not previously have. Your scope of permission to be in the vicinity of the evidence is to view, inspect and photographthe evidence. The photography, though very important, is probably the least important of those three tasks. Remember, you are an investigator and photography is merely one of your tools.

The evidence will normally be made available in a police department property room. A property room technician will be present to ensure that the chain of custody is not broken and that the evidence is not tampered with or altered. Keep in mind that the property room technician works for the government, not the defense. You should assume that every word you speak will be relayed back to the police detective and/or the district attorney. Remember the following at all times:

  • Do not discuss the evidence with the technician.
  • Do not discuss anything about the case with an associate or the attorney who may have accompanied you while in the presence of the technician.
  • Do not discuss how the evidence fits into the crime scene or the case. Pass a note to an associate if you must. Better yet, just make a note about it to discuss later. Before you enter the property room, remind your associates about the limits of open conversation.

When the technician opens the bag or other container to remove the evidence item, look to see if the seal has been broken and resealed. This is an indication that someone has viewed or tested the evidence after it was originally gathered. If the seal was broken, there should be a chain of custody within or on the evidence container that lists who took it and when, what they did with it and when it was returned. Ask for a photocopy of that custody document. Watch carefully for an evidence container that has a fresh, unbroken seal but inside is another evidence container with a broken seal. Sometimes the bag that contained the evidence originally is so badly torn when opened that a new bag is used to reenter the evidence into property control.

You should take at least one photo of each piece of evidence. In that way, no single piece of evidence appears to have more significance than any other.

Make a note regarding each photograph on a piece of paper and check the item off the evidence receipt. One of your tasks is to see if all pieces of evidence that you have been told about are in property room custody. If an item is not in custody at the property room you need to track it down. Ask if it is at the crime lab. Take notes about what you are told.

Another of your tasks is to see if evidence exists that you have not been told about. If you do not have all property receipts with you and accessible, you will not realize when a “new” item is presented that had not previously been revealed.

Look at the evidence closely. An old, dirty shirt with holes in it might actually be a bloody shirt with swatches cut out by the crime lab for testing. If holes appear in a garment, ask if it has been processed by the crime lab. If you see an evidence item with circles drawn around small specks with a felt tip pen, this is an indication that the crime lab has “lifted” these specks by removing a portion of them with a moistened swab.

Some tips to assist in getting good quality out of your evidence photos:

  • Black or dark-colored items, especially fabric, will absorb light. Take more than one shot of each item and open the lens up or slow the shutter down on repeat shots.
  • Watch for glare from a tabletop or whatever surface you are using to lay the evidence on. Use a piece of butcher paper, if available, for a background.
  • Do not try to photograph evidence while it is inside a plastic evidence bag. It will usually flash-back and will diffuse detail in the photograph.

Unusual Circumstances

There are some circumstances that require creativity to photograph. I have found that the best way to handle these problems is to realize in advance that they will exist and to go out and experiment before the actual situation arises.

Outdoor Night Photographs

The best example of this is to photograph an automobile at night that is located in a remote location away from any electricity. Naturally, if you have a power generator that you can take to the scene, part of your problem is solved. Remember that you still need to practice getting the right exposure under these circumstances, because your camera is not connected to your light source.

Assume you are caught without your generator and racks of halogen lights and need to get some pictures of this car in a remote area. And you are alone. You will need a tripod to set your camera on. When you are trying to place the tripod as close to the car as possible and realize that the camera view finder is not revealing how your subject is framed, you will see the importance in practicing this exercise in advance. Take your strobe light off the camera and point it at the car while looking through the view finder. Hit the “test” button on the strobe to make it flash and for a split second it will light up the area enough to see if the car is framed properly. For this example, lets say you are shooting the side of the car.

Open the camera lens to its largest setting. Set your camera shutter to “b,” the setting that opens the shutter when the camera trigger is hit and leaves it open until the trigger is hit again. Trigger the camera so the film is being exposed and immediately take your strobe and walk toward one end of the car. Point the strobe away from the camera at one end of the car and pop the test button so the light floods that end of the side of the car. Now walk to the middle of the car at about the same distance from the car and hit the test button again. Now do the same at the other end of the car. You should have flooded light over the entire side of the car from three different angles. Return to your camera and trigger it so the lens closes. Now you need to repeat this procedure firing the strobe four, maybe five times during each photograph. I have had some instances where the best pictures came out where the strobe was fired twice at each of five locations. That is why you need to practice these situations in advance. To be sure that you get a good photo, you should make at least a dozen photos, all with different strobe positions and number of strobe firings.

Another way to handle night situations such as photographing a large building where the strobe will not send its light to the upper reaches of the structure is to “paint with light.” This is done in the same manner, only instead of a strobe being fired, a flashlight or flood light is shone back and forth over the subject as if the light were a paint brush. The variable in this technique is the number of passes back and forth over the building and the speed that the light is moved in each pass. Again practice, practice, practice and take lots of exposures, all different.

Bruising On People

“Alternative light source” is a technique used to bring out faint or deep bruising that does not appear in regular photographs. The examples that I have seen show that it is an imperfect art that in most cases does not produce better results. Ultraviolet and other light sources are used to illuminate an area of skin, sometimes at very oblique angles. I believe there may be good uses for this technique, but not on bruising, at least with any consistency. The best technique for photographing bruises I have found is regular color film. Black and white film will show bruising in good detail, but without coloration there can be no precise age determination of the injury. I have experimented with infra-red film and had some good results. My suggestion is to shoot bruising from varying angles and with subtle changes in exposure.

Black Tire Marks On Black Streets

This can present a very difficult problem, especially if the streets are wet. The best trick is to reshoot the area many times at differing angles. The light source will predominantly be natural light and since you cannot alter the source, you must alter the camera relationship to the source. This can be enhanced with flash or flood lighting as a supplement, but be aware of glare when using two or more light sources. This is another instance where infrared may produce the best results.

Speaking of infrared, you do not need infra-red light, just the film. The light is only needed for taking night photos where the light source is intended to be hidden. I have played around with infra-red film since I was a kid with my first “Brownie Box Camera.” A sunny day photograph of a park with fresh green grass will appear as if there is an inch of snow covering the ground. Infra-red film with available light is an interesting exercise that should be tested under conditions where lighting—the type of light, not the quantity—is an issue.

Conclusion

Photography is a valuable and necessary tool in investigation. Get good equipment that you are comfortable with and learn to use it well. Carry more film than you think you will need and take more rather than fewer photographs.

Remember, however, that you are an investigator using photography as just one of your tools. Although you may have been assigned to go to a certain place and take pictures, what has really happened is that you have been given an excuse to be in a place where you can further your investigation, some of it with a camera and some without. Remind yourself before you go out on any seemingly simple, routine task: my goal is to learn things about this situation that I do not know and could not find another way.
