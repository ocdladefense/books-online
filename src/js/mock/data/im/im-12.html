Investigating Informants

Don Grant

Overview

Investigating an informant can be very productive if carried out in a timely and organized way. The pressure of time will almost always be the limiting factor in how far you can go toward discrediting the informant, so it is critical to begin the background investigation of the informant as soon as you start the case. Many of the procedures I recommend will require several rounds of subpoenas duces tecum,with some delay between rounds as the records suppliers comply. Start early and keep at it, regardless of what else may be going on in the case (including negotiations).

There are several meaningful categories of informants encountered in criminal cases. One of the more common is the “CRI” or “Confidential Reliable Informant” who provides the inside information necessary to obtain a search warrant. One variant of this type of informant is referred to by the affiant in a warrant affidavit as a “CI” or “Confidential Informant,” which may indicate that the affiant could not bring herself to refer to this scum-dog as “reliable.” The CI or CRI is often on the payroll of one or more police agencies, and those agencies may not be willing to give up the true identity of a working CRI just to make your case stick. Occasionally you will encounter the “concerned citizen” who claims to be assisting the police purely as a public service, or the truly concerned family member or friend who is convinced that by giving

information to the police she is forcing the client to get the “help” he needs. While these well-motivated informants might seem to be unassailable, when their true motivation for providing information is unmasked, their credibility can still be severely undermined.

The type of informant used in your case and the stage of the proceedings where you intend to attack the informant’s credibility may determine which methods are most suitable for you. In some cases the informant is used to obtain a search warrant and then is never seen or heard from again. The focus of this investigation is to show how the affiant, who attested to the affidavit in support of the warrant, knew or should have known the information that suggests the informant was not reliable and should have disclosed that information to the magistrate. In other cases the government will have to call the informant at trial when, for example, the informant is a percipient witness to crimes in the indictment, or to rebut a claim of entrapment.

Attacking the credibility of an informant is not materially different from attacking the credibility of any witness, except that informants tend to have more “dirt” in their backgrounds. The successful attack will be one that gets as much of this dirt as possible in an admissible form and where the dirt is sufficiently cross- referenced to make each part of the impeachment support all of the other parts.

Finding the Rat

In some cases the informant’s identity is not explicitly disclosed in discovery materials and the client may not know who the informant was. Do not accept at face value the client’s assertion that he does not know who the informant is; go after the information that shouldhave warned him and ask the background questions. Some productive areas to inquire about are the following: Were any of his friends, suppliers, associates or acquaintances busted at roughly the same time he was? Were searches conducted of premises controlled by any of these people within a few weeks of client’s bust? If so, talk to the representatives of the people searched or busted. Get search warrants and affidavits from those busts; they may unwittingly reveal the identity of the rat. Were any of client’s friends, relatives, suppliers, associates or acquaintances busted shortly before client was? There could either be a common rat or an earlier “bustee” could bethe rat. As soon as any search warrant affidavits associated with the client’s bust come to light (either through discovery or through investigation — that is, going to the appropriate courthouse and copying the warrant), go over these documents with the client in detail. There is often a high level of denial at the outset of a case, when the client truly does not want to believe that “good ol’ Joe” sold him out. Even reading in the affidavit that the informant visited on a particular day and purchased a specified quantity of product from the client may not convince the client that his ex is the informant, but focusing him on those details may help break through the denial. Do not assume that the client is lying about knowing who the informant is; as an investigator your ability to sift information to find the important part is not an attribute most of your clients will share. Help them figure it out.

The Two-pronged Attack

1. Attacking the Story

The task ahead of you is not simply to show that the informant is an unreliable person, you will also have to prove he is lying about your client. To effectively discredit an informant you need to attack the “story” in addition to attacking the storyteller. A certain amount of organization and preparation is necessary to effectively attack the informant’s testimony. To begin with, you have to ascertain what that testimony will be. You can start by trying to discover as many versions of the story as possible given to cops, DAs, the grand jury or whoever else. You may find references to what the informant has said in comments made at the informant’s sentencing on recent charges, or at a resentencing where the informant is given a reduction in sentence. There may be references in institutional records to the informant having provided information against certain individuals or groups.

Once you have a sense of the informant’s story, analyze it for inconsistencies. Does the informant’s chronology make sense? I have seen informant statements where drug deals were described in which the provider gave up the dope and the buyer owed the provider for the drugs, but I have never talked to a drug dealer who did business this way. I always wonder what happened to the buy money when I hear that the dope and the money did not change hands near simultaneously. While looking for internal contradictions, also look for facts that could be objectively verified. Weather, work schedules, release date from custody, meetings with lawyers, club meetings, weddings, funerals or public events can be checked to see whether they support the informant’s account. Similarly, other witnesses, especially other government witnesses, should be questioned and their statements analyzed to see whether they support the informant’s account.

2. Attacking the Rat

To attack the rat, you need to find the cheese. What is the informant’s motivation? Let’s start with the worst case scenario: the informant is a concerned citizen who is determined to stop the dope dealing in his neighborhood and is helping the cops as a public service. If you can show that the informant had a personal motive to get the client out of the way, the “public service” rationale will be exposed as the smoke screen that it is.

What motivates someone to become an informant? The most common answers are these: money, fear of prosecution or incarceration, and personal advantage. Many informants are professional rats who make a good living by betraying the trust of their friends and associates. If this kind of informant is exposed or has his credibility severely damaged in court, he simply gets passed on by his police handlers to an area where he is not yet known. Police handling the informant gain the advantage that they can remain ignorant of misconduct by the informant in the past, or about money or drugs or property given to the informant by police in other agencies. Agents will go to some length to conceal how much their rat has been paid and the basis on which they are paid. Some informants get paid only after the case is made, so they can not be cross- examined about how much they are getting for setting this client up; the agency will argue that amounts paid for other cases are irrelevant to this one. Informants can be paid by the number of deals they set up, the quantity of drugs seized, the value of property seized for forfeiture or the length of a defendant’s sentence, or they may simply get a certain amount each month they work for that particular agency. A paid informant may receive most or all of his compensation “in kind” in free rent, furniture, food or vehicles. One informant we researched, who was also a fence, got to keep some of the property taken in the burglaries he arranged for the defendant to commit.

Consideration in a pending case or cases can be a powerful motivation to become an informant. The payoff can be as obvious as a case suddenly dismissed or as subtle as a favorable recommendation for a downward adjustment. When looking for case consideration as motivation, it is critical not to miss consideration given someone close to the informant. This caution is particularly appropriate when looking for motivation for the “concerned citizen” type of informant. That citizen will look much less credible if you can show that his activities resulted in a dismissal of his child’s case. If the informant or his family are not citizens of the U.S., immigration considerations may provide a powerful motivation.

There are many kinds of personal advantage that might motivate an informant. Revenge for past disputes is not uncommon (especially revenge for the defendant having informed against the witness or against a friend or family member). Sexual motivations are sometimes involved. Gang rivalries, even within a single gang, can provide a motive to get rid of some competition. Certain personalities simply enjoy “getting over” on other people and would set people up for fun but get special pleasure (and other advantages) from doing this for law enforcement.

Discovering (and proving) the motivation for the informant to dump on your client is an important part of discrediting that informant.

Personal History in Public Records

There may be cases in which the client can tell you where to look to get the goods on your informant, but if so they are rare indeed. It is more common that your client can tell you nothing relevant about the informant, so you have to find your way to the documents and people who can tell you. In effect you need to reconstruct the informant’s life history. By life history I mean his criminal history, medical history, employment history, marital history, and perhaps his military record, educational background and financial history. As a legal investigator you should be thoroughly familiar with preparing a complete history of an individual, but this can seem more difficult when preparing a history of a person where you have to discover all the relevant facts.

The first things to ascertain are the informant’s “identifiers,” that is full name, date of birth, and Social Security Number. (See also Chapter 10, “Locating Witnesses.”) You may be able to start on this list through the Dept. of Motor Vehicles if you have enough unique information to identify the informant conclusively. If not, you may have to start with his last known residence. He either owned it or rented it. If he is the owner, there should be documents recorded that will identify his lender or mortgage holder. The seller’s realtor may have records showing his Social Security Number, his employer and his bank. If there are photocopies of checks in the file, they may reveal a spouse’s name. If the informant was a renter, you may be able to get a look at the rental application. These applications will typically have information about prior residences, personal or financial references, employment and family. You will want a copy of this and any other application you discover the informant has filled out because such applications are a fertile source for lies he has told outside of setting your client up. You will want to compile as many lies as possible to prepare for cross-examination of the informant. So, once you have seen the rental application you will want to backtrack each assertion made on it and see whether you can verify it or not. Once you get an idea of where the informant has worked in the past you will want to start gathering employment applications also and do the same kind of back-tracking on these documents. You may find that an undocumented alien suddenly says he is a citizen, produces an SSN that does not belong to him and assures his prospective employer he has never been arrested or charged with a felony before.

As soon as you develop a date of birth, try to locate the informant’s driver’s license and the records of vehicles he owns or has owned. Motor vehicle records can help you start to piece together where the informant has lived in the past since each vehicle’s history may reveal a different set of addresses. The vehicle records will also list any lien-holders, which may lead you to credit applications where the informant found it useful to lie about his livelihood and his criminal past. His driving record will reveal where he has been stopped for moving violations and may also have some historical information about where he has lived.

As you figure out where the informant has lived over the years, check court records for those areas. Court records are one of the most fertile public records sources for developing a life history. Remember, at this point in the investigation, you are not only interested in offenses that can legally be used in court to impeach the informant, you are trying to develop more information about who he is, where he has lived and who he has associated with. Each parking ticket is a record of a place the informant once visited and a vehicle he at least once drove. A small claims case shows someone he once did business with or had a problem with. As you continue to organize and work these records you will want to get in touch with as many people who have known the informant as possible and to locate those witnesses who will candidly tell you what kind of person he was then.

Among the most significant court records are domestic relations cases. Here you will find the restraining orders detailing his misdeeds and lies, and the support enforcement proceedings that are running parallel to the large cash payments the cops are giving the informant. It is wonderful to disclose to jurors that while the informant was knocking down serious money for turning in your client, he was also ducking his obligations to pay for the support of his children. The items mentioned are the tip of the iceberg of what you may find by researching domestic relations files.

To the extent that you find court records of criminal cases, you will want to obtain the police reports describing the arrests. These reports will contain references to associates and codefendants and will certainly detail lies told to law enforcement. The court file of his criminal cases may include sentencing letters or motions for reduction of sentence, which may give you impeachment options. Pay particular attention to any sealed documents you find in these files. For obvious reasons, information submitted to a court about cooperation with authorities is often filed under seal. You may want to have a motion presented to that court to unseal these documents or to provide them for an in camerareview by the judge in your case. You will also want to get Department of Corrections records to look for pre-sentence reports, psychological evaluations, parole and probation conditions, and parole violation reports.

The first focus is usually on easily obtained public records like court and motor vehicles records because locating these will not alert the informant to your interest in him. To prevent the informant from warning people not to cooperate with you, you will want to gather as much information about the informant’s life before you start knocking on the doors that will alert him to your interest. As you move from the public records to follow up with the people referenced in them, you will want to start with those who are least likely to alert him and move inward to those who may still be in close contact.

Aside from formal public records searches, you should also make inquiries within the criminal defense bar (for example on the OCDLA or OALI listserves). Your informant may have been active in previous cases. If he was, try to find out whether he testified in any of those cases, and if he did get a transcript of that testimony. At the least such a transcript will give you an idea of how he is going to respond to questions about his motivation, method of payment, whether he pays taxes on the blood money he earns, etc. Occasionally, since you have more time to follow up on the answers he gave in the earlier proceeding than the previous trial team did, you may be able to prove he perjured himself when he claimed that he could not pay income taxes because his handlers had not fixed him up with a Social Security Number, or that he had no idea how methamphetamine is manufactured and sold. Even better you may be able to establish that his handlers (and ex hypotheothe prosecutor) knew he perjured himself. Why didn’t they mention this in the search warrant affidavit in your case?

While researching court documents, do not forget to look for records of bankruptcies typically filed and indexed separately from other cases.

If the informant has ever run a business, there may be records on him at the Department of Revenue or the Corporations Commission. These may in turn reveal who his business associates were and provide more insight into his financial history.

As you compile and organize the public record history of your informant, you will want to keep running lists of people and organizations to follow up with and also of lies you discover on the face of the documents. For each address you can associate with the informant you will want to interview neighbors; for each employer you may want to interview supervisors and co-workers, and you will certainly want to obtain copies of applications for employment.

As you develop leads to follow, keep in mind that you will want to analyze each step to decide whether you have enough information to bring your investigation of the informant into the light, then strategically decide which leads to follow in what order.

There may be cases in which you will want the informant to know he is the object of intense scrutiny. While certainly no ethical investigator would intentionally harass the subject of the investigation, in a case where you want to interview the informant and he is not coming forward, conducting interviews with his ex-wife or adult children may precipitate a call or visit from the informant. Just as certain interviews are likely to trigger a response from the informant, attempting to obtain certain kinds of documents such as mental health records or drug and alcohol treatment records or DHS files will almost certainly provoke a motion to quash. There is no reason to be afraid of such a motion, but you and the attorney will want to plan your strategy so that the motion to quash does not come up before you have obtained all the records you are likely to get without a fight. A judge may react badly to a set of subpoenas that appear to be a “fishing expedition,” while the same judge may not be at all concerned about turning over records when you clearly have a basis for believing those records contain relevant information. In other words, you want to interview the adult child the informant abused before you have to defend your subpoena for the records showing that abuse (and the lies he told denying it).

If you know where the informant is staying, you may want to consider running a “trash cover” on that residence—intercept the garbage after it is put out but before it gets picked up. This can be a risky investigation move but it could yield a huge benefit if you discover the informant’s prescription bottles for psychotropic medications or his notes from the meeting with the agents to discuss his testimony at the motion to suppress. You will almost certainly gather more information about his financial dealings and what he has been buying and where.

Subpoenas Duces Tecumand Discovery Motions

The most important point to keep in mind is that this level of research takes time to do properly. After exhausting the freely available public records of your informant, it is time to prepare and serve the first round of subpoenas for documents that are not subject to the Public Records Act or the Freedom of Information Act. The first round of subpoenas should usually be served and returned by a subpoena return date. The subpoena return date might be an arraignment or a pretrial conference hearing, or it might be a date you set up with the court specifically for the return of subpoenas.

You will not want a motion to suppress before you get the documents you want to impeach the informant in that motion. To be used effectively, any documents you receive will require review and follow-up interviews. It is best to prepare for several rounds of subpoena returns before the hearing or trial where you intend to impeach the informant. Each round gives you a separate opportunity to educate the judge about the many ways this informant has shown himself to be a liar and a cheat in the past, and perhaps to tweak the prosecutor for not digging up and disclosing the very information you are having to locate on your own.

In both state and federal criminal cases, subpoenas can be used to require a person or organization to produce documents or tangible objects. There are two distinctly different uses of such subpoenas duces tecum:to obtain the records in conjunction with testimony at an otherwise regularly scheduled hearing or trial, and to obtain documents in advance of the trial or hearing. There is a debate among lawyers about whether the rules require a specific motion to obtain the court’s permission to issue the second type of subpoena. I think of the distinction as focusing first on why the person or organization was subpoenaed for that date: If it was to obtain testimony relevant to the scheduled hearing and the documents or objects are relevant to that testimony, I would consider the duces tecumportion of the subpoena to be an adjunct to the testimony and this should not require a motion and order. If, on the other hand, the hearing date is simply a convenient time for you to receive the documents and there is no plan to introduce them that day in that proceeding, I would consider a more specific motion and order appropriate.

The governing law in this area for federal court derives from Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure (FRCrP)

17. This rule provides authority for the court, upon motion of a party, to direct that documents or other items be produced in advance of trial or hearing. The state law on criminal subpoenas is in ORS 136.567 (issuance of subpoenas), and ORS 136.580 (books, papers or documents). Note that ORS 136.580(2) requires a motion before the court can direct that such items be produced at the court prior to the time when they are to be offered, and provides that the court can order that both parties be permitted to inspect or copy such documents. I am aware of substantial debate among practicing defense attorneys about whether or when such motions can be made ex parteor under seal. Be aware that the debate exists and make sure the attorneys you are working for do the necessary research to back their position before the motions to quash are filed. (Also see Chapter 13 for more on subpoenas.)

Every document you receive should be examined carefully, both as a record of lies or contradictions and as a source for new people to interview or documents to seek. You will want to interview anyone you can find who has ever had a dispute with the informant or has been charged with responsibility for supervising him. Previous crime victims, ex-spouses, ex-lovers, and former employers can be very difficult witnesses for the prosecutor to cross. Does she want to bring out in front of the jury that while the informant’s wife and kids were on welfare in Texas he was screwing around with his ex-girlfriend here in Oregon and eventually stole her car and left with her sister? At times, the witness’s relationship with the informant may be more

significant as impeachment than the bad things they have to say about his reputation for truthfulness. Parole and probation officers can be great sources for lies the informant told in the past. How does the prosecutor explain why her witness even had a parole officer, much less lied to her repeatedly? You may be surprised to find how freely the informant’s old POs are willing to share dirt on their former supervisee—somehow they do not seem to feel that parolees are entitled to much privacy about their past indiscretions…. A true bonanza is finding the PO who was supervising the informant during the period when he was working your client. It is almost always a condition of probation or post-prison supervision that the defendant tell his PO about any contact he has with law enforcement. Do you really think the informant told his PO he was going out and buying drugs to work off his latest beef? And do you think the informant’s handlers on the task force told the PO how they were using the person the PO was supposed to be supervising? How do you suppose the PO feels when she learns that the task force agents have been treating her like a mushroom (keeping her in the dark and feeding her manure)?

Spreading the Stench Beyond the Rat

In some cases neutralizing the testimony of the informant will not be enough to win the case. If you have the right facts, you may be able to infect the handling agents with the lies and cheating of the informant. You should always get the agency’s or task force’s rules for dealing with informants. When you can show that the handler ignored some of those rules to avoid a problem with the informant, you may be able to taint the handler with the informant’s bad behavior. Among the common agency rules are that all funds or other consideration promised or given to the informant must be documented, that any supervising probation or post-prison supervision officers be kept informed of the informant’s activities, and that the informant be

told not to break any laws and to pay all applicable taxes.

Why bother looking for stuff the Rules of Evidence won’t let you use?

At times, the attorney you are working with will be less excited about a particular piece of impeachment than you are. They may react immediately that the lie you caught the witness in is collateral to his testimony, or is “only” a prior bad act and therefore not admissible directly. Sometimes this reaction comes from a less creative view of the Rules of Evidence. Information that is not directly admissible against the informant may become admissible if he denies it, or if another witness testifies to the informant’s character for truthfulness, or as an example of the reasons your witness has the opinion that the informant is not a truthful person. Occasionally you may have an extensive list of admissible lies the informant has been caught in and a few that are probably outside the Rules’ scope. If you get the informant admitting to admissible lies during cross-examination, the inadmissible ones may get past the opponent, or put him in the position where he has to sit silently while his witness admits he has perjured himself, lied to the IRS and his bank, only to rise indignantly to object when you ask about the lies the witness told his kids. This scenario is one of the many reasons why you need to gather as many lies and cheats by the informant as possible, without regard to their individual admissibility.

Not all cases end up in trial. It may be that the information you have might not be admissible in trial, but that doesn’t mean it won’t affect your opponent’s decisions about settling the case. In short, gather the dirt early and get it all. If you do not end up using some of it in court, so be it.

What kind of dirt should I look for?

The answer: “Anything you can find!” This is probably not specific enough in this context, but the fact is you should get whatever you can find that would make youless likely to believe the person; leave the legal theory to the attorney who will have to get it admitted. That said, a few ideas that have worked for others would include the following:

1.Other bad acts, Oregon Evidence Code (OEC) 404(3) and Federal Rules of Evidence (FRE)

404(b). This approach will work best if the evidence is related to one of the factors listed in the rule, such as motive, intent, plan, etc.

2.Opinion or reputation, OEC 608 and 405 and FRE 608 and 405. If the witness can be asked for the basis of their opinion, you may be able to get into specific acts. Both rules appear to limit inquiry about specific acts to cross-examination, especially for lay witnesses. Expert witnesses may be permitted to go further in testifying to the basis of their opinion. If the evidence is offered under part (b) of either Federal Rule, testimony of specific acts of bad conduct is admissible if the acts bear on the witness’ character for truthfulness. OEC 608 prohibits inquiry into specific instances of conduct for the purposes of attacking or supporting the credibility of a witness.

3.Drug use or dealing. If not admissible as a prior bad act under 608, it may be admissible to attack the witness’ competence to perceive or accurately recall what occurred.

4.Mental condition also may be admitted to attack ability to accurately perceive and recall.

5.Bias. If the evidence you have reveals a reason why the informant may be motivated to lie about your client, otherwise inadmissible evidence may be admitted for this purpose.

6.Motive. If you can show the witness will avoid prosecution, or a longer sentence, get revenge, or resolve a marital problem by having your client put away, you should be able to bring these facts out at least through cross of the witness. If the witness denies the motive, extrinsic evidence may be admitted. This may be where you can use the pending charges you turned up, or where information gleaned from prison records might prove useful.

7.Inconsistent statements. Depending on how the questions are framed, evidence that would be inadmissible under FRE 608 and OEC 613 may be admissible as a prior inconsistent statement, for example, where a witness is testifying he was motivated to be an informant because he is opposed to drug use and you have evidence he used to deal.

8.There are more, just keep digging!

Organizing the Material

How you keep your materials organized is much less important than understanding that you have to keep them organized. I will generally base my system on the plan for presenting the material.

If we are planning to use our impeachment of the informant to attack the affiant and seek a Frankshearing, I would probably keep the material about the informant in a three-ring binder with a separate section each for the lies, crimes, and misdeeds we can prove the affiant knew about. On the other hand, if we are planning an entrapment defense at trial, I might have a separate binder for each witness or document source we might want to present independently, and a separate binder for the cross of the informant. I like to use binders both because they make the documents easy to review and share within the team and because they can be effective courtroom theater. In one case involving seven informants we made sets of binders for each of the seven. The court had one, the witness was given one as he took the stand, the AUSA had one, and each juror had a copy so everyone could follow along. As our attorney started the cross, the witness would initially deny that he had a long history of lying and our lawyer would direct him to identify exhibit #1 in the binder. After two or three of these documents were discussed, when our attorney would ask if the witness had ever lied about X, the witness would try to get out of the trap by saying, “I don’t recall lying about that, but if you have it here in your book, it is probably true that I did.” Our attorney could then use the document to refresh the witness’s recollection about a lie he had apparently forgotten. By the third witness, the jurors were obviously eyeing the size of the notebook to get an idea either of how long the examination would take or how big a liar this witness was.

Cross-referencing the material is also an important part of keeping it organized. Impeachment is much more effective if the points of impeachment reinforce each other, so it is critical to corroborate the facts you are proving and to provide a narrative thread to show that making things up about other people is a tactic this person has used repeatedly and successfully. The previous “victims” of an informant’s lies may well know each other and be somewhat aware of what the informant has done to the other people in their social set.

Cross-referencing the materials also makes it easier to examine the informant thematically. You might, for example, start with his testimony in a prior case that he wouldhave paid taxes on the $150 grand he knocked down as an informant the previous year if only the agents had gotten him a Social Security Number as they promised, then move to the rental application from two years prior where he listed a Social Security Number, then to the loan application he filled out a year before that testimony where he used the same Social Security Number and listed his occupation as “security consultant” and his income for the year as sixty thousand, and then on through the other half dozen times he was happy to use that same Social Security Number in other contexts.

Another organization issue arises from the need to keep track of subpoenas, FOIA requests and Public Records

Act requests. You may be allotted a limited number of subpoenas duces tecum,as in federal court, in which case it is important to know how many you have served and how many you have left. As responses come in it is vital to know which subpoenas were simply ignored because you will want to follow up those subpoenas with a motion to compel. FOIA and the Oregon Public Records Act have clear deadlines, and once those deadlines pass without a response you will want to initiate the next escalation in each of those fights.

Conclusion

I never met or did a background check on George Washington or Abraham Lincoln, but I feel quite safe in advising you that no matter how squeaky clean the informant may appear from the description in the search warrant affidavit, if you do a thorough, concentrated, organized life history of the informant as outlined in this chapter you will find a few instances where he lied to someone about something to get some benefit or avoid some consequence. A few mythological figures aside, informants are human and if you look hard enough you should be able to prove it.

Good hunting.
