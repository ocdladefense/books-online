Investigating Dependency and Termination Cases in Juvenile Court

Barbara Builder

Juvenile court dependency cases exist when the conditions or circumstances of a child or children are such as to endanger their own welfare or the welfare of others. Children come to the attention of the Department of Human Services (DHS) when someone—either a police officer, mandatory reporter, friend, neighbor, or concerned citizen calls the child abuse hotline [numbers available on the DHS website athttp://www.oregon.gov/DHS/children/abuse/cps/cw_branches.shtml] and reports abuse or neglect. A Child Protective Services worker screens the report and determines if the allegation falls into one of four categories:

1) information only, 2) referral to other services, 3) not a situation that is abuse or neglect, and 4) possible abuse or neglect. If the caseworker determines that the child in question is in immediate danger, the report is promptly sent to the appropriate DHS office to conduct an immediate assessment of the family. Many reports received through the hotline are screened out and no case is opened. The reports that warrant a safety assessment are sent to a protective services worker who meets with the child, the family, and others who are familiar with the child.

The caseworker’s focus at this point is to determine if the child has been abused or neglected and if that child can remain safely in the home. Because DHS is child centered and family focused, the primary responsibility is to ensure the child’s safety and well-being. DHS will file a Dependency Petition in juvenile court if they believe that the family cannot ameliorate the circumstances that led to the referral without the intervention of DHS and the court. Once a petition is filed, all parties are appointed attorneys (in most counties). As reported by DHS, less than ten percent of total child abuse reports resulted in a child being removed from home and placed in relative or substitute foster care.

Petition

DHS files a petition in juvenile court based in part on police reports, witness information, medical/mental health records, and child and family interviews. At times the agency has very little detailed information to support its allegations. Close review of records and witness statements is important to ensure that your client is accurately portrayed. This is especially true if DHS filed a petition based on previous involvement with a parent. When the petition is filed, DHS must attempt to locate all legal parents. DHS must at least attempt to notify a man that he has been identified as a potential biological father and give him the opportunity to address paternity.

Jurisdiction

Unlike criminal cases, dependency cases are heard in civil court. Jurisdiction is defined as whether the court will have the power to make decisions about the family and the child’s placement. The petition filed by DHS outlines the agency’s concerns and how they adversely affect the child. The standard to find jurisdiction of the child as to one or both of the parents is a “preponderance of the evidence,” which means 51 percent or better chance that the child has been abused or neglected, or is at imminent risk of being abused or neglected If there are two parents listed on the petition, only one allegation of each parent has to be proven in order to establish jurisdiction.

Investigation is crucial to either support or refute the basis of the allegations, depending on who your client is and how they want the situation resolved. If an attorney represents a young child, that attorney will represent what is in the child’s “best interest” since the child cannot take part in the case due to their age. Attorneys who represent older children (around seven years old) will represent their client’s “expressed wishes.” The court makes findings and orders the parents to complete services based on the allegations proven to be true. DHS may not compel a parent to complete a service if the service is not related to the proven allegations on the petition.

Parties

There are many parties involved in dependency cases. The District Attorney represents the state’s interests, which at times may conflict with DHS. If DHS believes they need representation, a lawyer from the Attorney General’s office appears at hearings. The biological mother and legal father are also appointed attorneys. The legal standing of a father or fathers can be confusing and may need clarification from your attorney. The children are appointed an attorney or attorneys, depending on their ages and wishes. Often the agency will have one plan for a younger child and another plan for an older child.

Players

The players include all parties listed above as well as foster parents, teachers, medical providers, drug and alcohol providers, mental health providers, parole officers, disability workers, daycare providers, family, friends, and neighbors. DHS often requests written reports from providers involved in the case and provides them to the court. It is DHS’ responsibility to tailor and coordinate services that best meet the family’s specific needs.

Setting Up Your File

Your file should include discovery provided by DHS and the DA’s office at the initial shelter hearing. That discovery will often include the Dependency Petition, police reports, the hotline referral, and medical records of the children. If a child has suffered significant neglect or physical or sexual abuse, the child is often examined by specialized doctors. In Multnomah County the program is called CARES, and the doctors work closely with police and DHS to determine the severity of the abuse and the identity of the perpetrator. DHS should request reports from any provider involved with the family prior to the hotline referral. Due to HIPPA privacy laws, without a subpoena you will most likely only be able to gather reports on your client.

Dealing with the DHS

While many caseworkers and supervisors mean well, they are frequently overworked and under trained. There is a high turnover rate, and your client may have several caseworkers before the case is resolved. It is important to know that DHS generally takes the mother’s allegations of abuse by the father as the truth. It is vital to order all police reports to determine if the mother’s statements are accurate. It is also a good idea to talk with neighbors, family members, employers or anyone who may have intimate knowledge of the family. Cell phone records can be obtained through subpoenas, as can prison visiting records and phone calls. Never forget to review MySpace, Facebook and other online social networking sites for information about your client and/or witnesses involved in the case.

DHS frequently requests psychological evaluations of the parents to determine the best course of treatment or to determine if the treatment the parents have been involved with have been beneficial. The attorney may request that the investigator speak with people who can provide background information about the client. This can assist the attorney in preparing a letter that can be given to the expert as a way to augment DHS’ information. Your client is not always portrayed in the most positive light by DHS.

When subpoenaing an existing DHS file you will need a Qualified Protective Order (QPO) signed by a judge. Request a copy of the complete file, including all child abuse hotline referrals, visitation log notes, written case notes, and FACIS case notes (these notes are kept on the computer—all caseworkers are supposed to keep copious notes on each case in the computer; these notes are often not included as part of the DHS file). You will also need a QPO to subpoena any medical or mental health records.

Interviewing Children

Talk with your attorney about the parties in the case. If any witness is represented by counsel, you will need to obtain permission from that attorney before interviewing the witness. If your attorney does not represent the child, chances are you will not interview her. If your attorney does represent the child, spend time getting to know her before asking questions directly related to the allegations against the parents. Many of the kids in foster care want to return to their parent(s), regardless of the situation. Many of the children you will meet have been through a great deal of abuse and/or neglect—never underestimate their feelings.

A General Guide to Interviewing Children

Consider the age and gender of the witness and the nature of the information you are trying to illicit. If the witness is under 16, I always consult with the parents as a precautionary measure.

Find out as much as possible before interviewing the witness. Many children involved with DHS can present with a myriad of issues, and certain approaches are more effective than others. If the child/witness has a specific diagnosis, spend time learning about that diagnosis and how it manifests itself in children.

Schedule interviews at the witness’s convenience. Schools will not release a child to an investigator without the parents’ permission. Allow yourself plenty of time for the interview. Establishing any commonalities will always help.

Keep the questions simple and straightforward. Give a child witness the option of answering the question with, “I don’t know.” Use significant dates such as holidays or summer break to help with timeframes. Use words kids can understand when explaining your role in your client’s case. You may not get all the information you need in the first meeting.

Always review the child’s statement for accuracy before you end the interview. (Also see Chapter 15 for sections on interviewing children, teenagers, and victims.)

Life of a DHS Case

If children are removed from a parent’s custody, that parent basically has twelve months to engage in services and ameliorate the circumstances that brought the family to the attention of DHS. Parents will be referred to such services as alcohol and drug treatment, domestic violence, anger management, mental health therapy, family counseling, psychological evaluations, parenting classes, and skills training. The child may be referred for a mental health evaluation and treatment. The court or a CRB (Citizens Review Board) will review the case every six months to determine DHS’ compliance with court orders and the parents’ compliance with treatment expectations. Many of these cases come down to the mother or father’s capacity to parent. Sometimes parents resolve many of their own issues and are still unable to parent given their child’s significant mental health/behavioral issues.

If neither biological parent appears fit to resume physical custody of their child at the end of twelve months, DHS will staff the case with the District Attorney or Attorney General’s office to determine if a Termination of Parental Rights Petition should be filed.

Termination

DHS pursues termination of the parents’ rights if it does not appear that the child can return safely to the parent within a reasonable amount of time—as in the next three or four months. Another factor that must be considered in termination cases is the adoptability of the child. Older children and children with high special needs can be more difficult to place adoptively. Sometimes DHS recruits for an adoptive home before the termination of parental rights petition is filed.

After the Termination of Parental Rights Petition is filed the court schedules trial dates which are often months out. The parent can continue to engage in services and work to regain custody. An investigator should review the client’s treatment reports and speak with treatment providers in preparation for a termination trial. It will be your job to help the attorney disprove the allegations by showing that the parent has made sufficient and significant progress in treatment to safely parent the child(ren). Your job may also be to prove that DHS did not provide appropriate services to the family. Termination trials can take days or weeks and are very difficult and emotional for all parties.

Conclusion

Working in the juvenile field can be emotionally draining. You will meet many children and adults who have endured severe trauma. Many families who come to the attention of DHS are heavily involved with drugs and criminality. There can be generations of families involved with DHS. It will be important to educate yourself about topics such as alcohol and drug use, domestic violence, gangs, mental heath issues and trauma. For books that I have found helpful, please see the appendix.
