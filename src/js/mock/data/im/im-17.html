2010 edition

Infant Death Investigations

Ron Miller

An Introduction to a Difficult Case Investigation

The death of a child under any circumstances is a tragedy. When such a case occurs, our society demands a thorough and complete investigation to determine whether or not the death was avoidable or the result of criminal conduct.

Investigation of this type of case is one of the more complex and emotionally disturbing inquiries an investigator will conduct. This relatively short discussion on a fundamental approach to these cases is intended to be a helpful guide to investigators. The complexity of these cases is such that the likelihood of investigative error can result in either the guilty avoiding responsibility or innocent persons being wrongfully convicted. This discussion merely scratches the surface of the technical and meticulous nature of the case investigation, data collection and interpretation, and case presentation. This chapter primarily focuses on infants under three years old who present in the emergency room in a rapidly deteriorating physical condition resulting in unconsciousness and eventual systems failure.

The author has been involved in a number of infant death and child abuse investigations. This chapter relies heavily upon this case experience, as well as the generosity of numerous experts in the field who have shared studies and relevant information. This summary guideline is by no means a complete discussion of the topic but a “hot start” guide for the investigator. The reader will likely find some of the content and statements a bit controversial. It is meant to be so. There are extremely controversial theories in the medical field which are argued by medical personnel in these cases. Stimulating and challenge the though processes is critical in these cases.

The Reporting Process

The majority of cases involving the death of a child first comes to the attention of the authorities when either the parents or other parties bring the child to the hospital emergency room or report the child’s medical distress to emergency services. The reporting process is abbreviated in the occasional case when a child is discovered deceased at home or other location, as the child advances immediately to the custody of the medical examiner.

Regardless of how the child, living or deceased, comes to the attention of the authorities, the circumstances are highly emotional and there are many opportunities for failures in investigative logic. Physicians, by the nature of their educational instruction on diagnostics (more on this will be discussed later and in Groopman; see References), are susceptible to these errors in thinking. They are trained as clinicians, not as criminal investigators. Remember that medical examiners are also physicians and subject to the same foibles as the emergency room doctor. All subsequent investigators can then easily be caught up in the cumulative errors of analysis and hypothesis. Much will be said about this later in the chapter.

Children brought to the emergency room are usually in an extremely critical medical condition and or may have already died. From the time of earliest contact by EMS personnel to arrival at the ER, it is not uncommon for the child’s condition to deteriorate rapidly, even terminally. These children may only survive for a very brief period of time to a few days. In the course of the first hour of medical treatment, many factors are at play which have a significant impact on how this child’s medical condition will be attributed to causal factors. This is a critical time for accurate observation and analysis.

Today’s emergency rooms are generally extremely busy, with inadequate staffing to handle high volumes of seriously ill patients. In the last decade more emergency rooms have become primary care facilities for the uninsured and indigent. This has stressed the ability of emergency room staff to appropriately balance the delivery of critical and noncritical care.

Working in the emergency department environment has been challenging for years for physicians to improve and maintain high-quality decision-making in their diagnostic and treatment responsibilities. Contrary to popular belief, the decision-making process in the emergency room is based on medical diagnostic practices built on statistical probabilities and ruling out the worst-case scenarios. In an environment where time is often limited and many demands are being made on the treating physician, the methodologies used for diagnosis and treatment are based on experience and assessing the most likely scenario. Groopman and Croskerry, respected physicians in the United States and Canada, address the process of “how doctors think” (see References).

This is an important process for the investigator to understand. Most of us go through life believing that our physicians are scientists and they make their decisions based upon clear and concise evidence. The reality is that physicians make literally hundreds of decisions regarding patient care in a given work shift. From interpreting laboratory values and reconciling those against patient complaints, the physician is taught early on to develop shortcuts also known as heuristics. These cognitive shortcutting strategies are proven to be effective in delivering care in an environment in which there are inadequate resources and not enough time (Croskerry, Groopman). When they don’t work the physician can readjust his diagnosis and treatment plan to meet the needs of the patient. The risk, of course, is that the more serious the condition, the more dangerous the sequelae of an incorrect diagnosis and treatment. To reduce the chances of such an error, physicians are trained to initially rule out the worst-case medical scenarios. Once that has occurred, they then move on to looking for the most likely diagnoses.

The reader is likely wondering why so much time is being spent on this topic. As it will be increasingly clear, the initial medical evaluations and opinions of treating staff have great influence on the directions and focus of subsequent criminal investigations. Physicians are often the first persons to formally identify a child as being the victim of abuse. Their authority as physicians has great power and influence on attending staff and law enforcement. The initial diagnosis is critical.

A diagnosis is the identifying medical “condition” resulting in the symptoms or injuries displayed. A diagnosis is medical categorization, not a legal conclusion.The medical condition will be treated aggressively and as best as possible. The “treatment” of parents or caregivers, in the form of identifying third-party responsibility for the medical condition, exceeds the responsibility of the treating physicians. Healthcare providers have a legal and moral responsibility to report suspicion of abuse; however there is a fine line between suspicion and accusation. Frequently in the highly emotional state of these cases this boundary is forgotten. These two functions—treatment and investigation—are role-dependent processes. Doctors should not reach investigative conclusions and investigators should not make medical diagnoses or translate a medical condition to such third party behavior and intent without independent corroborative evidence.

Beware of these frequent role and boundary failures in these cases. To act prematurely in assigning blame encourages and nurtures the potential for bias.

Early Opportunities for Bias

When a child is brought to the emergency room in a critical state the tension and stress placed upon healthcare providers is increased geometrically. It is human nature to treat an infant in distress with much more concern and emotional energy than older persons. In the case of a child who presents with traumatic injuries such as a subdural hematoma, hemorrhaging or broken bones, the worst-case scenarios include intentional abuse. If the traumatic injury is such that abuse cannot be ruled out, or if the last known caretaker cannot provide an “adequate explanation” or does not “show appropriate emotional response,” the applied heuristic (shortcut) frequently becomes “intentional abuse.” It is possible for a child to incur injuries under circumstances that the parent might not have any knowledge as to what happened. To begin the blame game this early in the case is putting the cart before the horse.

This failure in “reacting appropriately to the situation.” This is a classic example of “confirmation bias” and “diagnostic creep,” discussed below.

“Child abuse” in it’s purest legal sense, is a behavioral and sociological phenomena. It is not a medical condition although it is commonly seen as such. It can be argued that it is inappropriate for any healthcare provider to diagnose injuries as being the result of child abuse without independent witness or testimonial evidence that a specific intentional assault or gross negligence occurred. Suspicions are appropriate and need to be acted upon. Conclusions on the other hand are usually premature in the early stages of these cases.

Errors in Investigative Logic

The reader may be asking why all of the attention is being given to the dynamics and methods of clinical decision-making and diagnosis. If responsibility for the child’s injuries are inappropriately implied, fundamental errors of attribution begin to occur early in the case. The author has worked in emergency rooms and has observed this process occur with predictable regularity in cases of child abuse, domestic violence, sexual assault and physical assault. Blame is often times assigned to a “suspect” without all or any of the facts being known. This is human nature but it can have devastating results in the integrity of the investigative process.

Croskerry describes a number of errors of logic in failed heuristics and biases, and several of the more predominant ones will be discussed briefly. Although these are described in the context of medical diagnostics, the same process occurs with investigators.

Anchoring

This is the tendency to narrow one’s focus on specific issues with a child presenting a number of features. This occurs too early in the diagnostic process where there is inadequate data to accurately predict the likelihood of a particular event, i.e. intentional abuse.

Ascertainment Bias

This occurs when the examining medical practitioner or investigator is thinking in a manner pre-shaped by what they hope to find. This leads to “pseudo-information collection,” which may later prove to be misleading. This may result in oversimplification of the causative factors of the child’s condition.

Confirmation Bias

This is a powerful bias which may seriously confound the problem-solving, clinical and investigative

decision-making process. When a hypothesis is developed on relatively weak or ambiguous data, it may later interfere with superior and more plentiful data. This leads to the preservation of the original hypothesis and diagnoses that were weak in the first place. New data or information may not be given the weight that is

due. In other words, data which supports the original hypothesis is elevated above that which does not. This is a common bias that physicians and investigators (both for the state and the defense) can be particularly subject to if they do not continually challenge their existing hypotheses.

Observer Bias

Turvey, in his text, Crime Reconstruction,has a thorough discussion of observer affects and examiner bias. Early in the investigation, there is a high risk that medical and law enforcement professionals succumb to the common foible of tunnel vision. “Observer effects[bias] are present when the results of a forensic examination are distorted by the context and mental state of the forensic examiner to include the examiner subconscious expectations and desires.” This may not be an intentional process but one of human nature.

Diagnostic Momentum

This is the tendency for a diagnosis to become established early in the examination or investigation. It carries with it a “momentum” that makes it difficult to incorporate new information or to slow down and verify assumptions that have been made. This bias may delay or preclude other potential diagnostic explanations of the injuries. This writer has investigated a case in which laboratory data was ignored and overlooked until several days after the defendant was arrested and charged. To his credit, the physician reviewed his report several days later and amended his diagnostic impressions to include a more accurate description of the likely clinical processes involved. This modified report was not included in the original discovery to the prosecuting attorney or to the hospital to which the child had been transferred. By that time in the investigation, the defendant’s fate was sealed and he was charged with aggravated murder. The critical data in this case was available at the time of the initial diagnostic exam and early interviews by law enforcement. This information was not known by the prosecuting attorney’s office until it was provided by the defense.

Fundamental Attributional Error

In cases of suspicions of child abuse, there is all too frequently a stereotyping of the persons thought to be responsible for the circumstances surrounding the child’s injuries. The tendency of a fundamental attribution error is to blame a person rather than a medical condition or circumstances. This bias can open the doors to additional observer bias and confirmation bias. If there is any diagnostic momentum at play, this becomes even more powerful.

Hindsight Bias

When the outcome is known it can have a profound effect and influence on how we view past events. When events are observed in hindsight, there can be a tendency to attach a concrete cause and effect relationship and deterministic logic to such a degree that no other outcome or causal factors could have occurred. When there is a failure to attempt to disprove the hypothesis, hindsight bias usually wins. If there is one factor in the case that disproves the hypothesis, then the hypothesis cannot be true.

Order Effects

critical aspects of the child’s condition are communicated early in the case. In the course of this information transfer, selective pieces of information tend to be retained more readily than others. This can result in a polarized view of the patient and the circumstances surrounding their admission, and the influence of

subsequent decision-making and information transfer. In the case of a seriously injured and deteriorating infant, it is not uncommon for notations to be made on the emergency room chart such as “NAT,” an abbreviation for “non-accidental trauma.” Of course, this is a deduction made regarding non-medical evidence. Unless there is a confession or witness statements made available to the emergency room staff at the time of evaluation, this is an inappropriate “diagnosis.” This designation, however, sets the stage for fundamental attributional errors and other biases with future treatment staff. It is not unusual to see the progression of notations in a medical chart assigning cause and blame without any additional information regarding how the child sustained the injuries. Even in cases where the injuries are so prevalent that no other explanation is apparent, these opinions mustbe kept as suspicions until the investigation is complete.

Such notations and comments have tremendous impact upon subsequent investigating officers. There is a tendency to assume that the physicians are “experts” in child abuse. Ironically, if the evaluating physician is aware that law enforcement is involved, that can reinforce attributional errors.

Overconfidence Bias

Overconfidence can be the result of a physician’s or investigator’s desire to maintain a positive self-image, the inability to admit that perhaps they made an error or misjudgment in the course of their evaluation or investigation.

Premature Closure

Premature closure is the phenomenon in which a diagnosis has been decided upon early in the encounter, may have been accepted prior to the incorporation of other data, and it may not have been fully verified. In infant death cases initially investigated by law enforcement, such premature conclusions have a huge impact on the direction and momentum of the ensuing investigation. Police officers assume that when a physician makes a diagnosis the diagnosis is accurate and based upon sound clinical data. Most physicians do not realize that a medical diagnosis and the legal violation of a statute are two entirely different processes. An incomplete diagnosis has huge implications for a person suspected of a crime. Unfortunately, the medical diagnosis of “child abuse” has been used in the past as the stand-alone component in probable cause for arrest

Search Satisfaction

Search satisfaction occurs when there is a decision to call off the search for additional diagnoses or data once the evaluator determines that something has been found. In other words, if the child’s injuries are initially thought to be the result of abuse, no further explanation for the injuries will be sought. The problem with this bias of course is that all further explanations are suspended and that the initial hypothesis was never tested. If the child dies, the medical evidence may very well go with the body and not be available for future examination.

Sutton’s Slip

Famed bank robber Willie Sutton, when asked why he robbed banks, replied, “That’s where they keep the money.” To relate thisto our scenario, this kind of simplistic approach can lead to misdiagnoses where there is a failure to modify or make new diagnoses. Later in the investigation this can result in a failure to look at other explanations or theories of how the injuries were obtained. In an infant death case it might go something like, “Well, the baby was being cared for by the boyfriendand has a head injury the mother knows nothing about so he must have done it.” There are numerous faulty assumptions here. The first is that “boyfriends” tend to injure their girlfriends’ children. The second is that the injury must have occurred in his care early on; it is very questionable as to how accurate injury aging can be. The third is that the mother didn’t injure the child, and the fourth is that the injury was not accidental, etc. Each one of these points must be disproven as causative.

Triage Cueing

Croskerry indicates that this is an emergency room phenomena, however, it could easily also occur at a 9-1-1 call center. This results from an abbreviated assessment that is known to be imperfect. It may miss or under assess or over assess the accuracy of the information relative to the child’s injuries. It may be presented as an assumption which is never tested. Get all 9-1-1 call and radio tapes. Get all triage nurse notes and logs. It sets the stage for observer and confirmation bias.

Visceral Bias

In ideal circumstances, all human emotion should be eliminated from decision-making processes. Likewise, our prejudices for or against individuals should be eliminated when making an objective evaluation. Unfortunately it is human nature to develop both positive and negative feelings towards people. In the emergency room with an emotionally charged case, it is easy for medical staff and law enforcement to project either positive or negative feelings towards individuals related to the case. This may result in reduced quality of clinical decisions and evaluation. Dress and appearance have an impact. A caregiver who is dirty and unkempt is going to be assessed differently than a caregiver in nice clothes with good hygiene. The classic example in child abuse cases is when one parent reportedly “does not display appropriate affect given the circumstances.”

When There Is No Emergency Room

If the child is determined to be deceased prior to the arrival of emergency medical services, the emergency room portion of the investigation will be bypassed and the child’s remains will be transported directly to the medical examiner’s office. In such a case, only one physician and one exam will determine the nature and cause of death. It is absolutely imperative that the defense team immediately acquire a preservation order to allow the opportunity for another exam by a defense forensic pathologist. It is important to remember that pathologists are also physicians and were trained in many of the same ways as emergency room physicians, and the possibility of bias and error exists in their profession as well. Professional medical examiners expect to have their work reviewed and examined by others. Failure to do so could result in the defense attorney later being found as ineffective in his counsel and representation of the defendant. Unfortunately, the reality is that someone may be charged with homicide but may not be provided with legal counsel until long after the body has been released to a mortuary for burial or cremation. Therefore, it is imperative that the state’s medical examiner be extremely thorough and complete in his exam and retain sufficient samples of tissues, body fluids, organs, etc. Unless this is done, it will be impossible to enable a thorough pathological examination by the defense. All infant death autopsies should follow an established and approved protocol which can be replicated. (An example of such a protocol from the State of Minnesota is given in Appendix C.)

The defense investigation typically begins days or weeks after an arrest or indictment is handed down. Although counsel may be appointed or hired early on, it is often weeks before the discovery starts to trickle in. Consequently, it is rare that the defense gets an “early start” on the case. The police are usually pretty much done with their investigation by the time discovery shows up. However, it is a mistake for defense counsel to wait for discovery before actively investigating the case. Critical opportunities may be lost if investigation does not begin immediately.

Emotional Pitfalls

Despite beginning the defense investigation at a time which may be quite temporally removed from the death of a child, the emotional impact to all involved remains significant and a major task to overcome. Many defense investigators find these cases difficult to work because of the emotional demands. Law enforcement personnel have similar visceral responses any time a child dies.

The defense investigator is now tasked with a complete review of the reports by law enforcement, the immense collection of medical records, graphic autopsy photos and interacting with traumatized family members of the deceased.

The Accusation Against the Accused

As in all homicide cases, the state must prove that the death was the result of a negligent or intentional assault of some sort on the child. They must also prove that the defendant was the person perpetrating the assault or neglect.

The defense completely understand and challenge the theories purported by the state for each of the following:

•Motive for the death.

•Mechanism – medical cause – of death.

•Manner of death – how did it happen?

Motive

The intentional killing of a child is so abhorrent to most people that establishing a motive, if any, is a high priority by the state. The usual motives for homicide are generally not applicable to these cases (money, sex, drugs, love). Secondary gain is rare but does occur. In this scenario, the child is an obstacle to something the defendant wants—independence, another relationship, or concealment of the birth. Similarly, mental illness—wherein a caretaker is acting upon a psychotic delusion that they take a child’s life.

The most frequent motive offered for infant death cases is that of “frustration.” The accused is described as being frustrated over the child’s inability to stop crying, over unemployment or some other stressor. This is usually the first motive presented by law enforcement in their interview of a cooperative defendant; oftentimes, the defendant will agree after much discussion and review of their life circumstances that perhaps they were “frustrated.” The challenge for the defense investigator is to determine whether or not the “frustration” is an actual factor or whether or not it was implanted in the interview process and agreed to by a compliant suspect. Frustration in other life venues does not necessarily translate to acting out against a child. This must be proven. Therefore, an in-depth psychosocial background on the accused is necessary to determine whether or not this motive is consistent with the defendant.

In many of these cases, the identified perpetrator is young, immature, uneducated and of low socioeconomic status. They are not sophisticated in their thinking and are probably struggling to manage life. As a result, they frequently do not invoke their Mirandarights. Dealing with client statements is a huge challenge for the defense investigator. An appropriate, legally obtained, nonmanipulative and well-documented interview (audio or video recorded) by law enforcement can be the state’s or the defense’s strongest evidence.

Mechanism of Death

The mechanism of death is the medical “diagnosis” or condition that resulted in the child’s death. For the defense investigation team, the mechanism of death represents a major component of the investigation. In the course of this inquiry, it is necessary to reinvestigate the medical conditions leading to the child’s death alleged by the state. It is likely that several medical experts will be consulted. A complete review of the child’s medical history and prenatal history and a complete review of the incident injuries or conditions is necessary to determine the accuracy of the state’s allegations. This will include experts in histology, radiology, endocrinology, blood chemistry, neurology, orthopedics, and histopathology (microscopic and cellular pathology).

Manner of Death

The manner of death is established by the medical examiner or coroner. It is the legal definition of how the child died. There are generally six categories:

•At the hands of another

•Accidental

•Natural (disease or illness)

•Suicide

•Unknown

•Pending

Investigative Priorities

After a complete review of the state’s discovery materials, investigative priorities must be established. Due to the emotional nature of the case, it is likely that the defense will encounter resistance and obstruction by relatives of the deceased, care providers, or other witnesses and information sources. It is likely that you will need a number of experts for various aspects of the case. The defense team is then tasked with building a team including experts and key witnesses to assist in developing a defense theory and examination of the state’s case. This process is usually shared by counsel and the investigator. And, of course, this will be very expensive. In Oregon, the majority of infant death defense cases are funded by the Office of Public Defense Services. Establishing a rapport with OPDS early on is important because these are among the most expensive cases to defend.

Timeline

Developing a timeline of data is critical in infant death cases. The exact time of injury is frequently based upon medical opinion and as such is subject to question. The time period in question should initially be set at two weeks but may be expanded if necessary. Prenatal and medical history should go back as far as there is information. A timeline should be established with information related to the following:

1) Child’s prenatal and pos- birth medical history with documentation

2) Establish what persons were with the child at all hours of every day for two weeks prior to the incident.

3) Child’s behavior and attitude towards:

•Sleeping

•Eating

•Fussiness

•Coordination

•Interaction with others

•Any other changes in daily routine or behavior

•Toilet training

4) Child’s medical history for every hour of the previous two weeks:

•Fevers

•Nausea

•Diarrhea

•Headache

•Difficulty in use of extremities

•Balance

•Attentiveness

•Alertness

•Crying

These may be done on a single timeline so that interrelationships are apparent between the child’s pre-sentation of symptoms and potential witnesses. This timeline will be the most complete description of events and their sequelae and the basis for hypotheses and their challenges. A sample is included at Appendix B.

The Infant’s Remains

If the child’s body is still available, the defense attorney should immediately get an order for preservation. The defense will usually want a second autopsy performed by a forensic pathologist selected by the defense. If the defense team does not already have a relationship with a qualified forensic pathologist contact other investigators or attorneys who have handled similar cases and begin developing a list of experts. More will be said later about the autopsy.

Medical Records

First, have a qualified medical professional such as a certified legal nurse consultant review the medical records, in addition to other medical experts. These clinicians are less expensive and usually more accessible. This person can help the defense team understand some of the more routine questions as well as the complexities of the medical treatment, and establish further investigative priorities. Assistance in formulating investigative questions for the medical experts can be crucial. Medical records provided in discovery are frequently incomplete; your consultant can assist you to identify what records may be missing.

These records may have been incomplete when initially given to the prosecutor by the treating facilities. The larger the health care institution where the child was treated, the greater the likelihood that medical records are kept at several locations within that institution. For example, some radiology records may be kept in the radiology department, laboratory records may be retained in the lab department along with histology and other disciplines. The legal nurse consultant should be able to recognize subtle indications in the medical records referring to other documents or where a document would be expected. An example would be a reference to a laboratory value in the chart. For each laboratory value shown in the chart there should be a corresponding order by the physician requesting the tests. That order may have other notations explaining other aspects of the case. Similarly, one may find an order for a test and yet not find any test results. This writer has had occasion to make multiple requests for the same medical records on a case from a large medical institution and on all three requests obtained different records that had never been obtained before.

On one occasion there was a notation of a specific laboratory analysis request. A record of the results of that test could not be located in the medical record. That test could have supported one of the defense theories in the case and could not be replicated with a postmortem blood sample. Upon further inquiry and after much insistence, the ordering physician admitted that he “canceled the order because we knew the cause of death was child abuse.” This failure in investigative logic is a classic example of how medical professionals and legal investigators think differently. Premature closure may have denied the defendant important evidence. Therefore, be sure to hire a legal nurse consultant and have that person examine medical records thoroughly.

Another early task is to obtain releases signed by the non-charged parent to obtain medical records of the deceased child from the primary care physician and all subsequent medical providers, as well as birth records and prenatal records. Obtaining the “run reports” from the ambulance and fire department personnel are extremely valuable in understanding the child’s progressive deterioration. The importance of these early observations cannot be overstated.

It is also necessary to get records specifically for the child’s mother. For particularly young children, many disorders have their genesis during pregnancy or may be related to pre-existing medical or genetic conditions the mother may have. It is important to obtain these records as soon as possible.

A generic HIPAA compliant release should be obtained from the non-charged parent. Several signed copies with the addressee left blank are necessary because it is likely there will be new providers discovered whose names will not be known at the time of signing the release. For facilities you know you will be requesting records from, call their medical records department and request they fax you a copy of their records release form. Although your generic form may be legally adequate, many institutions insist on using their own form. In child abuse cases, there is a high possibility that such requests by the defense will be stonewalled. Using an institution’s preferred form will save you time and frustration. Obtain the appropriate signatures on these forms. “Expiration dates” should be extended out as far as possible, as in 18 months you may need to make another request. Should the non-charged parent decline to provide a signed release, a subpoena duces tecum should be obtained as soon as possible.

The medical records received in discovery should be re-requested by the defense six or eight weeks after the defendant has been charged. The reason for this is twofold:

1) The medical charts obtained by the police or prosecuting attorney’s office at the time of charging or indictment may not be complete. It is not unusual for there to be a delay of several days or weeks before all reports are entered into the chart.

2) You need to be absolutely certain that you have allrecords. The prosecuting attorney’s copy may be incomplete.

It is unlikely that the discovery will include any pre-existing health care records for the child from the primary care physician or other treating facilities. Hospitals generally send summary treatment and discharge reports to the primary care physician of record following a hospitalization or emergency room visit. These may post-date the records in discovery. Even though the child is deceased and no longer a patient of that physician, that clinical record will likely be maintained for some time and include those records sent by the treating facilities.

Caveat: Obtaining medical records can be frustrating. Defense teams are frequently met with hostility when such requests for records or interviews are made; the slightest irregularity in format or release can result in denial or delay. Hospitals are increasingly wary of civil litigation in these matters. Expect roadblocks and be prepared to deal with them in a professional manner.

Interviewing Surviving Family and Witnesses

It is extremely important to establish a relationship with the non-charged family members as soon as possible. This should be done within 72 hours of death if possible—but rarely accomplished. In many cases the non-charged parent or relatives do not believe that the defendant is guilty of the alleged crime. In time this gradually changes, and they may become increasingly less cooperative with the defense. It is therefore absolutely necessary to gain some initial trust and rapport for future contact. Although their opinions may continue to change, they will be more likely to cooperate in the investigation if the defense team is known and not seen as “the enemy.”

All family members of the deceased child should be approached in a sensitive, respectful and sympathetic manner. The genogram can be of assistance in identifying potential witnesses. Most law enforcement interviews of surviving family members and witnesses have probably been conducted by this time. However, those interviews should be reviewed carefully and validated. Those interviews should be examined to determine whether they were appropriately audio and/or video recorded. Determine if Mirandawarnings and custodial status were described in a clear manner and recorded. Are they supported by any written documents or transcripts?

The defense investigator must cast a wide net to locate additional witnesses that can describe aspects of the child’s life or the accused that might lead to additional evidence challenging the state’s allegations. The state’s investigators rarely go beyond those witnesses that can contribute to the probable cause for the charging instrument or otherwise “make the case.” Record all critical interviews for later use.

Medical and Family Histories

One of the most important tasks to undertake is a complete medical history of everyone in the home. This includes medications, clinic and hospital records, birth records (to include any complications or assisted deliveries, growth and developmental analysis of each and every child), growth charts, well-child and urgent care in emergency room encounters. Oftentimes, insurance records and department of health and human services records are helpful in describing some of these histories. All of these should be appropriately photocopied or preserved. Entries of all doctor visits and injuries should be entered into the timeline. Relevant medical records previously obtained should be included in the timeline.

A complete family history should be obtained to include family size, structure and race, and a genogram is frequently beneficial. Any family members or other children in this genogram should be identified that have any unusual illnesses, disorders or premature death. Some genetic disorders and conditions are more prevalent in some populations than others. In addition, gather information related to socioeconomic conditions, recent changes or stressors in the family structure, the medical background of the parents, use of welfare, child protective services, and 9-1-1. When available, gather daycare reports, school reports for siblings and landlord records. Do not equate the brevity of this paragraph with the size of the task of gaining a full and complete history.

The Crime Scene

Autopsy: The “Internal” Crime Scene

In conducting an investigation of an infant’s death, the most significant and valuable “death scene” or “crime scene” is the child’s body. The alleged abuse most often occurs within the child’s residence or other location that they frequently abide. Due to their small size and inability to resist, the usual crime scene evidence that is sought in other crimes of violence is generally absent. Most infant homicides result from assault injuries. They rarely die by firearm or bladed object violence.

The most valuable evidence relative to the child’s medical cause of death will be determined from the autopsy and medical records. Of particular importance are the various radiology studies that might exist—X- rays, MRI and CT scans are extremely valuable. They will depict critical clinical issues at various times in the course of treatment until death. However, remember that the interpretation of those studies by radiologists is subject to the same errors in logic discussed previously. Radiologists have stated that it is impossible to mechanically describe how a specific fracture occurred in a specific incident—that only generalities can be made. This is an area of diagnostics with much controversy.

Complete photographic studies of the child’s body before, during and after autopsy are important for future pathology studies. Multiple photographs from a variety of ranges and exposures should be taken of all significant injuries noted in the autopsy. With the advent of digital photography it is far more preferable to have too many photographs rather than not enough. Using high-resolution cameras is encouraged so as to facilitate close examination and “blow up” of images.

The vast majority of evidence will come from the autopsy and samples of tissue and organs. Expert examination can determine how long the child had been injured, the presence of recent and older injuries, and coexisting or dominant medical disorders which may have contributed to the death.

In the course of reviewing and understanding the reports and examinations relative to the child’s body systems it is imperative that the investigator have the appropriate reference materials available. A good medical dictionary, anatomical atlas, and manual of laboratory tests and interpretation are indispensable (see References.)

There are literally hundreds of medical journal articles relating to various aspects of child abuse and the investigation of a child’s death. Some of the topics related to infant death are controversial in the medical and scientific communities. Be sure to ask the experts for copies of all journal articles they feel are pertinent. If there is controversy regarding an aspect of your case, your experts should be well aware of the research.

The “External” Crime Scene

The location where the child was residing at the time the medical crisis occurred or where the child was discovered is the primary geographic location of interest. There may be multiple locations to examine such as grandparents’ houses and daycare facilities. Upon first exam there may be little evidence of a crime. Accurate recording of the scene is crucial and should be done as quickly as possible. Extensive photography of the contents of all cupboards, medicine cabinets, utility closets, refrigerators, freezers, toy boxes and play equipment as well as multiple views of each room of the residence or location should be taken. Photographs in these cases are extremely helpful in retrospective review of clinical symptoms that may develop later on. An example would be a child with a high level of acetaminophen in their bloodstream. The toxicology results may be delayed by several weeks after the event. It is important to be able to corroborate this substance with the presence of acetaminophen within the medicine cabinet or cupboards of the location.

Likewise, measurements should be taken of the height of all furniture from the floor, the height of all play equipment from the ground and any other objects from which a child might fall. The surface of all floors should be well photographed and in the case of carpeting samples of the carpet and the underlying pad should be obtained. An examination of the nature of the subfloor should be conducted. It is important to know whether it is wood or concrete. Similarly, play areas outdoors should be photographed.

Any evidence of stains, blood or other body fluids, medications, hazardous objects, recent medical documentation, photographs and sketches by other children or other family members should be recorded or sampled.

Other relevant observations of the home or locales frequented by the child include the general cleanliness, presence of toxic substances, odors and other conditions that could possibly pose a hazard to a child.

Motor vehicles in which the child has recently been a passenger should also be examined to include child restraint seats in general condition of the vehicles. If there is evidence of damage to the vehicle from an accident, all records should be obtained or incidents documented.

Conclusion

These cases are complex and demanding. Research and much reading are a huge component of these cases. Get organized. Become systematic in information collection. Rule out all possible explanations for the injuries and/death and the case will come together.

Included at Appendix A is a “Hot Start” list which is by no means exhaustive but a reminder of critical tasks. Several useful references are included to assist in understanding the complex terminology used in these cases. Law enforcement agencies generally have their own protocols which very from agency to agency.

Appendix B is a sample medical timeline and Appendix C is the “Minnesota Infant Death Investigation

Guidelines” published by the State of Minnesota. It is one of the better and most complete to date.
