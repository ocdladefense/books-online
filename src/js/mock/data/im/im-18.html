Introduction to Mitigation

“Mitigation” literally means “to make less severe or painful.” In the world of criminal defense, mitigation is a term used to describe either 1) immediate circumstances to the commission of a crime, or 2) life circumstances of one’s client that contributed to the commission of a crime. Mitigation may be a single fact or an entire body of information used to explain why a criminal act occurred or to put the criminal act into the context of your client’s life circumstances or mental health. “Mitigation” does not mean “excuse.” Prosecutors tend to equate mitigation evidence with an attempt by the defense to make excuses for a criminal act. There is a profound difference between an explanation for an act and an excuse. In the case of a criminal act, an explanation does not take away or eliminate guilt; it puts the criminal act into perspective, and thus may potentially diminish guilt.

Refer to ORS 137.080–137.100 to become acquainted with aggravating and mitigating circumstances relevant to sentencing. Prior to Ballot Measure 11, the language in these statutes was relevant to criminal cases in general. Now, for all practical purposes, these statutes are relevant only for misdemeanor crimes and non-Measure 11 felonies.

Considering the legal decisions that will be made, it is critical to gather complete information regarding your client’s behavior, strengths, needs and life history. This effort will be based on the principle that in order to fully understand a client, it is necessary to examine both the individual and the environmental factors that have affected him or her in life and development.

From the standpoint of an individual, it is vital to comprehend the client’s strengths, thought processes, moral position and identity, as well as any exposure to trauma. From the environmental standpoint it is vital to examine the contexts in which a client lives and has lived (family, friends, education, employment, criminal history, etc.). After gathering all of the information it is possible to gather on a particular client, one must synthesize this information into a format that is conducive to a criminal defense case. This is all in addition to the case fact investigation.

The American Bar Association’s Supplemental Guidelines for Mitigation in Capital Case Defense defines mitigation evidence as follows:

Mitigation evidence includes, but is not limited to, compassionate factors stemming from the diverse frailties of humankind, the ability to make a positive adjustment to incarceration, the realities of incarceration and the actual meaning of a life sentence, capacity for redemption, remorse, execution impact, vulnerabilities related to mental health, explanations of patterns of behavior, negation of aggravating evidence regardless of its designation as an aggravating factor, positive acts or qualities, responsible conduct in other areas of life (e.g. employment, education, military service, as a family member), any evidence bearing on the degree of moral culpability, and any other reason for a sentence less than death.

Although you are not conducting a mitigation investigation with the purpose of saving your client from a possible sentence of death, you are using mitigation evidence in an attempt to improve the outcome of your client’s case.

While the passing of Measure 11 removed the ability of defendants to present mitigation evidence to a judge in the hope of receiving a discretionary sentence, presenting mitigation evidence to a prosecutor became the alternative to the sentencing forum. The intended goal is to alter the final charging instrument.

Mitigation Investigation

As a facts investigator conducting a mitigation investigation on a non-capital case you must review all of the police reports, lab reports, photographs, interview recordings, the charging instrument and any and all other evidence the state provides or is known to the defense team. This evidence will provide your beginning point and may also contain mitigation evidence as well. It is essential to understand your client’s entire life story in order to understand what led to their current criminal charge(s).

Identify Mitigation Issues and Gather Records

Multiple in-depth interviews with your client are essential to establish a detailed social history that includes family and educational histories; medical, substance and mental health histories; military and occupational histories; birth event, religion and criminal histories, as well as all other factors specific to your client. The client-reported history needs to be verified by in-depth interviews with family, friends, significant others, teachers, neighbors, professionals, treatment providers and anyone else who has had an impact in any way upon your client. You will need a HIPPA release for information form and a general release for information form both signed by your client. You will also need every member of the client’s family that you will be obtaining records on to sign one as well. It may be helpful to have them sign several of each type as not all agencies will accept a photocopy of a signed release. See Chapter 5, Public Records/FOIA, regarding the specific suggestions for obtaining records.

Obtaining a complete social history on your client is extremely time-consuming. When possible you should have the copies certified, as this will make them more readily admissible in court. Each record can potentially lead to additional records you will need to obtain. The following is merely a sample list of the kinds of records you will need to obtain.

•Birth certificates (client/family).

•Birth records (including mother’s prenatal care records).

•Medical records, including early childhood for all family members.

•Parents’ marriage/divorce/death certificates.

•Client’s marriage/divorce records.

•All police reports, response calls and incident reports involving or mentioning your client.

•Social service, child welfare, and public assistance records.

•Juvenile justice records.

•Military records, including parents’ records.

•All prior Department of Corrections records.

•Current jail records on a monthly basis while your client is in custody.

•Mental health records for client and family.

•Probation and post-prison supervision records.

•All media – news articles and television news clips, including any comments individuals may have provided as an editorial comment on a media web site.

If possible, obtain family generational information two or three generations back. For juvenile Measure 11 clients, complete the Developmental Assessment Format with your client, included as Appendix E. Depending on the information your records search turns up, you may need to enlist the assistance of an education consultant to interpret the language contained in reports such as IEP’s (Individualized Education Plan).

It is imperative before you embark on a mitigation investigation to discuss in detail with the attorney exactly what you need to do in order to develop the information you will be seeking. Many jurisdictions aren’t set up to allow the time needed for you to conduct a thorough mitigation investigation. Your attorney will need to be prepared to request the additional time from the court to assure that the investigation not only occurs, but that it is complete. If your client was appointed an attorney by the court, the Office of Public Defense Services is paying for the legal representation, the facts investigation, and any experts and mental health professionals for the client. Regardless of your particular payment arrangement, a mitigation investigation is extremely time-consuming and expensive. Most places charge fees for copies of records and generally inform you before they make the copies what the fees will be. These records also take a great deal of time to obtain, especially military records, so plan accordingly.

You will need to develop a much deeper, trusting relationship with your client in order to obtain the social history that will point you toward all of the resources you will need. As your relationship develops you will be able to get more detailed and intimate information about your client’s childhood and home life. Regardless of the trust built and the time spent with your client, you may experience reluctance and resistance to your questions. Your client and his or her family may not understand why you want to know such personal, and most likely embarrassing, humiliating, and painful information. Your client and the family may be protective of their closely held secrets. You must be supportive, reassuring and nonjudgmental, while expressing how necessary the information is in seeking an acquittal or at least a lesser sentence.

Check with your attorney before asking your client to write anything down, but it is generally helpful for both the client and your investigation if they provide a list of the schools they’d attended, towns lived in, jobs held, etc. Being able to participate as an active member of his own defense team often helps a client feel that they have some say in how the investigation is proceeding. You must adapt to each particular client and mold yourself around his or her needs. Inform the attorney about your methods and techniques for gathering information— the attorney is the primary source of direction and the person who will utilize the information you receive. The attorney will also let you know exactly how they want to receive the information, whether in detailed written reports, verbal reports, or simply just the original records you obtain.

Many aspects of the assessment tool in Appendix E can be modified for gathering information from your adult clients as well.

Effective Ways to Present Mitigation Evidence

Discuss with the case attorney how he or she wants you to present the information you obtain. Records obtained from schools, hospitals, law enforcement agencies and the like may be placed categorically into binders with a table of contents. Include an index in the records section, as well as in the section containing your interview reports.

A Life Story is your client’s social history written as the “story” of your client’s life. Included in the Life Story is a complete chronology of every significant event in your client’s life and should include a genealogy (a family tree showing graphically any relatives that suffered from alcohol and drug addictions, mental illnesses or criminality, and death. Violent deaths should be graphically set apart as well). As you create your chronology you will discern patterns and recurring issues in your client’s life. Patterns of any sort will be useful in determining potential case themes, as well as in helping to identify any mental health issues that

may not have been addressed prior to your investigation. Childhood photographs, school pictures, sports pictures, report cards and family photos can say much more than some Life Stories can. The family information you don’tfind can sometimes tell you more about how your client grew up than volumes of records can.

If you have a client that has been convicted of a crime and will be having a separate sentencing hearing, your client’s attorney can present a “defendant’s Pre-sentence Report” for the court’s consideration. The more organized the material, the better it will be received.

Conclusion

Mitigation can make a significant difference in the outcome of your client’s case. As the mitigation investigator, it’s up to you to identify and obtain everything available that you can use to tell the story your client isn’t able to tell without you.

It would be beneficial for you to attend at least one seminar a year on mitigation investigation. “What” happened in your client’s criminal case begs the questions of “why” and “how,” and mitigation is how you answer those questions. Here are a few organizations that offer mitigation seminars:
