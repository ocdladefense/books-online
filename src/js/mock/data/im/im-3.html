Investigators Manual

So you want to Be in Business!

Rhonda Coats

2010 edition

This chapter will examine running a small business from an investigator’s point of view. I have been a full- time, self-employed investigator since 1988. I hope my experience can save you some time, money and mistakes. The failure rate of small businesses in Oregon is high. Therefore, there are many serious considerations you must address before you hang out the shingle.

Are you ready?

Do you have the necessary experience to be an investigator full-time, in private practice? Do you have six months of easily available savings (money not tied up in CDs, money market funds or life insurance) that you can use to pay your rent or mortgage and feed your kids? In addition, you need cash to run your business during the same six-month period. Do you know how much money it takes on a monthly basis to run an investigation business? You will also have up-front costs. Know your cash-flow needs. Do not consider starting your business without this six-month fallback.

Where will you find clients and how will you keep them? If you are depending on advertising in the yellow pages for the majority of your business—forget it. You will go under. If you are going to depend on a website for the majority of your business—forget that. These are expensive tools and mayenhance your business, but you cannot depend solely on them. If you work for only one or two attorneys, keep your day job. If you are working for only one insurance company, make sure you have a contract to do all of their investigation and quickly go after more clients.

The point I am trying to make here is this: if you do not have a strong client base, finding work as a beginner is going to be nearly impossible. Consider working part-time and building a client base before you start a full-time business.

You may be a great investigator working for a public or private agency, have the client base and look forward to giving notice. Before you do, think about running a small business. If you have never had any experience starting, managing or running a business, think twice. There is a lot more to having a successful

investigation business than being a great investigator. I have known many good investigators who have left

their jobs, only to have to ask for them back. They had to return not because they were not capable and experienced investigators, but because they did not know how to run a business.

Plan, plan, and plan some more. Here is just a small sampling of the kinds of questions you need to be

asking yourself: What kind of computer do I need? Software? Camera? Tape recorder? Do I need a secretary? Do I have the up-front money to get several thousand pages of discovery copied, hire a translator or fly to southern California to conduct interviews? Do I really have enough business insurance? What about health insurance for my family and me? Could a client sue me and take my house? Do I need to be licensed in Washington? Do I have my DPSST license?

Considerations in Running a Successful Business

Below is a general checklist of things you must consider and research before you take that big step.

√Have a business plan! I cannot stress this enough. Just reviewing a business plan template will get you thinking in the right direction. There are a number of state and federal programs available (listed below) that can assist you. (A quick overview of a business plan is included in the appendix.)

√ Consult a CPA, certified bookkeeper or a business lawyer to select your business structure. If you are running your business as a sole proprietorship or with another person or with several others, you must weigh the pros and cons of the legal structure of your business. Do you know the difference

between a limited liability company (LLC), a partnership or a limited liability partnership (LLP)? Do not take the advice of anyone but an expert. The tax laws are too complicated and your neighbor or another investigator is not taking the risk—you are! Talk to a tax consultant or attorney and get advice on how to structure your business and legally maintain your business.

√ You, your CPA or lawyer must file your business name and legal structure with the state of Oregon.

You can get the forms from Corporation Division, Business Registry Section, Public Service Building, Suite 151, 255 Capitol Street NE, Salem, OR 97310-1327, (503) 986-2200. You can also register your business name and legal structure through the website at http://filinginoregon.com.

√ It is fun to name your business. But investigator licensing rules require you to register an assumed business name with the State of Oregon Business Registry. Make sure no one else is using the same name. A phone call to the Corporation Division will tell you—(503) 986-2200, choose option 5.

√ Be sure to check with the state of Oregon Corporation Division regarding the current laws on obtaining a business license. See Laws and Rules on their website.

√ Also check with local city and county offices to make sure the location of your business is properly zoned. If you are going to operate your business from home there may be city, county and homeowner association restrictions. I have always maintained a home office and it works for me. I do not have clients visit my office. The one time I had an outside office it was not efficient to run in terms of time or money. Keep in mind that the fewer expenses, the greater your chances of financial success.

√ Know something about copyright law. Written materials, computer programs, works of art, business names and logos, as well as many other intellectual creations come under the copyright laws. So, you might spend some money getting your logo on your business card only to have it pulled because someone else owns it. Contact the Register of Copyrights at the Library of Congress in Washington, D.C, www.copyright.org.

√If you are considering using a trademark or service mark, you must register with the State of Oregon.

Forms for filing an “Application for New Trade and Service Mark Registration” can be obtained from the Corporation Division of the State of Oregon.

√ How about equipment? I am going to assume you know your way around a computer. You cannot operate a successful business without strong computer skills. Do you have the most up-to-date equipment? You have to stay competitive in private business and that means having the latest software, a good digital camera, as well as a regular camera, a cell phone and pager. You have to be available to new clients, as well as old. Are you familiar with public information sources online? Do you know how to find Macon, Georgia, or conduct criminal background checks online? Believe me, an attorney is not going to pay you to drive to the courthouse to do a criminal background check.

√ Do you know accounting software? Many CPAs and accountants use QuickBooks and that is what I would recommend. If you do not know QuickBooks or QuickBooks Pro buy it, learn it or take a class on how to use it—before you give notice. Allocate a specific time each day to do your business housekeeping. That means entering your billable activities into QuickBooks, TimeSlips or another program. Do it faithfully. You will not remember what you did last Monday on the Smith case. You may log the interview in your case notes, but what about that cell phone call to Mr. Smith on the way to the Jones case team meeting?

√ This brings me to tax information. Make sure your accountant has fully explained to you what is deductible, how to document expenses, keep records and for how long, and to make proper filings. As your business grows you may add employees or subcontract work to others. Educate yourself on the difference between a subcontractor and an employee. Talk to your accountant beforeyou hire either to determine your obligations. Make certain you understand Oregon Unemployment Insurance Tax, Workers’ Compensation Insurance and the vast array of employment responsibilities. Talk to your accountant on filing personal property reports. Examples of taxable personal property include equipments, tools of the trade, leased equipment and libraries (such as sample books and law books). You must report all property you own. There are also federal taxes that will apply to your business. I cannot urge you enough to contact and maintain a solid relationship with your accountant. Small, home-based businesses are red flags to the IRS. Make sure you stay on top of all of your taxes—federal, state, county and city (hotel-motel, Tri-Met, and Lane Transit District to name just a few). I recommend setting aside a portion of your fee when it is received in order to pay state and federal quarterly taxes.

√ Talk to your accountant or business lawyer about the state’s occupational safety and health standards, set and enforced by OR-OSHA, and other employment obligations. It is a lot more complicated than just hiring some help.

√ With the exception of certain sole proprietors, all businesses are required to obtain a federal tax identification number. To obtain a federal tax I.D. number, complete an SS-4 Form, available at your local IRS office and most banks. You can also call the IRS at 1-800-829-1040 or apply at the IRS website at http://www.irs.gov/businessesand click on Small Businesses and Self-Employed Tax Center.

√ How do I get clients? This is a commonly asked question. The best business is based on referrals.

Network—join professional associations such as the Oregon Association of Licensed Investigators, the Oregon Criminal Defense Lawyers Association as well as national associations. I would encourage you to volunteer, get involved and join committees. If you enjoy writing, volunteer to write articles for association newsletters, assist at seminars or conferences. Get to know people in your business. Let them know your specialty. Market yourself! Never walk out the door without your business cards.

√ The best marketing tool you have is the quality of your work and your reputation. Do a good job! If you are just beginning your business, work twice as hard as you think you should. Be prepared to work 12 to 14 hours a day. This is not an exaggeration. Your work will speak volumes both negatively and positively.

√ Find experienced and successful investigators you admire. Ask them to lunch, discuss your goals and get their advice. I have never met a successful investigator who would turn you down. Ask them to what they attribute their success. Ask them what they might have done differently. If appropriate, let them know that you are hard working and available.

√ This leads me to working with other investigators. There is more than enough work for good investigators. When asked to assist as a subcontractor by another investigator, nevertalk to the client or attorney without permission of the lead investigator or the investigator who hired you. When working on a team, do your job! Do not tell another investigator how to do theirs. Suggest ideas, share your experiences, but assume your colleagues are professionals and treat them as such. Keep everyone on the team up-to-date with your findings.

√ The most successful businesses have the best customer service. The investigation business is no different. Practice good customer service. Don’t assume you know your clients’ needs. Every attorney I work for is different. Meet the attorney’s or client’s specific needs. Is it a fast and speedy investigation report or a verbal report first? Be very clear about what the attorney expects from you and respond accordingly.

√ When billing, do not nickel-and-dime your clients. There is a cost to doing business. Do not bill for a stamp or fifty cents in parking. Bill promptly. Do not let hours accumulate. Report regularly. Good accounting software will do this for you.

√ To keep and maintain your clients, do not ever pretend you know what you are doing when you do not. In 24 years as an investigator I have never had a lawyer get upset with me for asking questions. It was my investigative skills he needed, not my knowledge of the law. By asking, it helped me understand the elements of the law and I got a better interview. But if you cannot get in the door, you are useless. Just be sure you know exactly what you are doing and why. The better you understand a case, the defense and what the attorney expects, the better job you will perform for your client. Know your limits. Most lawyers enjoy educating. Take it upon yourself to do research and acquire knowledge.

√ Learn how to manage your time. As you start your business, you will take every case that comes through the door and, at some point, like all of us, you will spread yourself too thin. As your business grows, managing your time will become more of an issue. Starting and updating your calendar on a daily basis will be essential, whether your calendar is on your computer, cell phone, Palm, or even a hand-written scheduling product such as Franklin Planner.

√ Study, study, and study some more. Attend seminars, classes and conferences. Consult other investigators. For every suggestion I have made here, there is a book, article or class available to you that can expand your knowledge. Do not just attend “investigator”-related classes. Take business classes as well. There are many small business assistance programs available. Take advantage of them! I have. Here are just a few:

•U.S. Small Business Administration—www.sba.gov.

•SCORE (Service Corps of Retired Executives/Counselors to America’s Small Business)—

www.score.org.

•The Oregon Small Business Development Center Network. There is an SBDC at every community college in Oregon.

•Oregon Economic Development Department, 1-866-467-3466—www.oregon4biz.com.

•Oregon Business Network—www.oregonbusinessnetwork.com.

•Oregon Association of Minority Entrepreneurs (OAME)—www.oame.org.

•Office of Minority, Women & Emerging Small Business (OMWESB), also located at—

oregon4biz.com.

•Native American Business Network (ONABEN)—www.onaben.org.

•Oregon Innovation Center—www.innovationcenter.org.

•Small Business Health Insurance Program—www.sbhi.net.

Conclusion

In closing, the most important piece of advice I can give you is to conduct your investigations with complete integrity. If you also maintain sound business principles and practices, you will be successful!

Appendix

Business Plan Overview and Sales & Marketing for the Investigator

By Gary Rice, Vancouver, WA

A business plan is useful in a start-up as well as expanding a current business. A business plan should contain the following elements:

•Title Page

•Table of Contents

•Executive Summary

•A Vision or Mission Statement

•Company Overview

•Product Strategy

•Market Analysis

•Marketing Plan

•Financial Plan

Each of the above categories contain subtopics which are very important to the success of your business plan.

Elements of Your Business Plan

Title Page

The title page of your business plan provides the name, address, and telephone number of the company and lists the name of the CEO.

Table of Contents

The Table of Contents includes a sequential listing of the sections of your business plan.

Executive Summary

This section is the most important portion of your business plan. The Executive Summary should briefly discuss and provide a “Readers Digest” version of each of the topics listed in your completed plan. Because of this particular element’s importance it should be the last document written for your business plan. More often than not this is the only portion of the plan that gets read by potential investors or lending institutions.

Vision / Mission Statement

This category is a snapshot of the current stage or success of your business. It should also present to the reader a picture of where your business is going and what it will look like when it arrives. Your goals and objectives should be described at great length.

Company Overview

This section provides the basic information regarding your company. It will present and explain in detail your company structure, management, staffing and any alliances that you may have.

Product / Service Strategy

This section reviews and highlights your current products or services and what makes them unique and competitive. This section should also contain any research and development in fields or products that your company has targeted for growth and how these products or services will be produced and delivered to your clients.

Market Analysis

This section defines your current market, your target customers, your competitors’ products or services and any risks your company may face.

Marketing Plan

This section contains your sales strategy, advertising, product or service distribution, promotions and any public relations details.

Financial Plan

This section reflects and demonstrates your company’s capital requirements and your current and forecasted profits.

Sales & Marketing

All too often when an individual or a group begins a new business, they usually try to provide too many services. My suggestion is to have one field that you feel very comfortable in as it pertains to your knowledge and aptitude. Have a secondary or even a third field in which you could market, sell and complete the assignments given to you.
