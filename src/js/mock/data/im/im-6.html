Investigators Manual

Computers and Computer Evidence

Jim Strupp

2018 edition

Introduction

Not too long ago, an investigator’s interaction with a computer used to be to write reports and check email. Computers have now permeated every aspect of our professional lives, and the investigator’s is no different. We know how to use them, and depend on them perhaps more than any other device we use to investigate law cases. Although robust Internet access has been available for many years, recently it seems like a new age. Data is everywhere. It is ridiculously public at times, and often frustratingly private and opaque. Formats change with the color of the leaves. Practices which worked two years ago no longer do, but practices developed ten years ago still stand the test of time. The Internet has been transformed from a collection of static web pages to a world of dynamic, highly individualized and fluid areas of opinion, raw data, arcane information and, of course, entertainment.

Using computers to assist in investigation, as well as conducting investigations involving computers, means grappling with the unbelievable proliferation of data, devices, and methods for managing both. The enhancement—some would say intrusion—of computers on the investigator’s practice touches on so many areas as to make the topic “computers” less than useful. Computers and digital information affect the crimes of which our clients are accused, how we find witnesses, how our witnesses find us, what sorts of evidence we examine, how we present that evidence in court, how we communicate with the attorneys we work with, the potential perils of representation, our ethics, and the list goes on.

This chapter is intended to give the legal investigator an introduction to some of these areas. It does not cover all computer-related topics pertinent to the legal investigator. Nor does it cover any of them deeply. It is hoped that the reader will at least form a conceptual framework, which may develop into certain investigative practices and techniques.

I have tried to strike a balance, and offer the investigator a window on some topics of computers and investigations I see as unavoidable. I offer advice where I have a settled view. Experienced readers, please forgive the basic tone. Novices, please pardon me when I slip into jargon.

This chapter offers thoughts first about the investigator’s computer setup, both for hardware and software. Internet based, or “cloud” services are also considered. The acquisition of electronic evidence is drawn out, with some usage scenarios offered to model common issues. Conducting Internet searches in investigation is addressed, followed by some thoughts about digital resources for courtroom presentation. And concluding this chapter are some thoughts about computer forensics and mounting challenges to state’s evidence using an expert. Several appendixes appear at the end to support four of these topics.

What is left out, sadly, is a serious discussion about electronic discovery, electronic documents, and databases. Mainly, electronic discovery and document databasing have really become an industry unto itself, drawing primarily from the world of civil litigation. Many legal investigators will not be called upon to manage large electronic discovery sets. When they are, the endeavor becomes more project management than traditional legal investigation, and qualified paid professionals are there to assist. Were I to set forth a detailed discussion about electronic discovery and documents, this chapter would be twice as long as it is.

Some of the tools and issues in this chapter bear on ideas and evidence encountered in defending computer crimes. (Editors’ Note: Chapter 7, “Computer Forensics,” discusses the ins and outs of investigating crimes involving computers, with an emphasis on child pornography.)

It is hoped that the topics below will appeal to broad range of investigators, with a broad range of backgrounds. Of course, I am happy to be contacted about what I might know something about, and answer any additional questions I can.

I. The Investigator’s Setup: Hardware, Software and Services

An investigator’s main tools used to be a notepad, a car, a telephone, and a camera. Now, critically, the personal computer with Internet service is added. Much less needs to be said now about the strength of the computer you choose to buy; almost any standard desktop computer or midrange laptop will have sufficient processing power and memory, as well as hard drive space, to accomplish the basic tasks of drafting reports, reviewing scanned documents, researching the Internet and communicating by email.

I recommend that you consider your computing setup as a critical tool in your practice, which will affect not only the outcome of your work but the quality of your performance. Accordingly, you should invest in the best components possible and the right ones to get the job done, with affordability as your secondary consideration.

Hardware and Operating Systems

A rough distinction can be made between a regular user and the user who may be “application heavy.” If your primary work involves a word processor, a browser and email, even the most affordable machines will have more computing power than you need. If you anticipate using a browser, a word processor, media viewing software, dictation software, desktop search software and a database all at the same time, you will need a setup that has ample memory (4 GB of RAM) and an enhanced processor. Investments in these upgrades will make your operating system more stable and save you hours of frustration. Hard drive storage space is now relatively cheap, and even low and mid-level machines have more storage space than regular users or most heavy users need.

Far more important than strength, however, are the options presented to the solo practitioner in overall setup, including type of machine, type of Internet server, peripherals and security.

I presume most users use Windows and I generally recommend Windows—if only to accommodate the wide number of applications built for it, which you may encounter from time to time for specialized tasks. Macintosh users can always use VM Ware fusion or other dual-boot technologies to run Windows inside a Mac environment very successfully (see: www.vmware.com/products/fusion). There are ever-growing varieties of Linux-based operating systems, however, unless you are interested in managing your operating system, I would stay with Windows. As of this writing, most Windows users are XP, but your ability to choose flavors has been eliminated, with the release of Windows 7. Although I’ve not used it, very experienced technical associates have given very positive reviews to Windows 7.

Notebook versus Desktop

Whether you choose to work primarily on a desktop machine or a notebook is a personal choice. Of course many practitioners use both. Notebooks obviously offer the great convenience of portability and ever-growing Internet access in remote locations. Achievements in processing power and battery life also make many notebooks attractive. However, notebooks can be easily stolen or left behind. Be assured that if you do primary computing on a notebook and travel often for your investigation, you will have a heart-stopping scenario involving at least temporary loss of your machine. You need to know, at a minimum, how to secure the data through password protection, and possibly local encryption. Given that a notebook is not as robust as the typical desktop and endures much more strain, the chance of hard drive failure is greater. Explore back-up options for your files. Backup can be copied to an external hard drive or another machine or server. The clearest impediment to successful back up is laziness.

Preventable data failures result from failure to set up or routinely employ a back-up procedure. Do not be one of the sorry many. Your client’s interests and your practice are simply too valuable. The simplest backup is to export your important work files to an external hard drive, which itself is then secured in your office. Automated back-up tools such as Sync Toy or Karen’s Replicator, make this an easy low-cost solution. More sophisticated home or business servers can create image-based backup of the entire machine on a regularly scheduled basis, ensuring restorable files. Windows’ home servers are relatively inexpensive and have this functionality built in with plenty of storage space.

Finally, web-based back-up sites such as Mozy, Carbonite, and Jungledisk all back up your information to third-party servers, accessible from almost anywhere. Some back up in real time. They all work differently, and have different security measures. If you choose to back up in this manner, first investigate exactly how they work and what their security provisions are. (See “Cloud Computing” section below.)

If you choose a notebook as your primary machine, be sure it will have enough power to do the jobs you routinely cover. At a minimum, a notebook should have sufficient assets in power and memory, as many USB ports as you can find, and a robust network card. It should also have a DVD/CD-ROM drive with a burner. Notebooks are often segregated into personal versus business-based machines. The business machines tend to have some very useful features, such as physical security devices, multiple batteries and longer battery life, with various business software installed.

If your practice involves acquiring and reviewing lots of data such as scanned documents, spreadsheets, databases and collections of files recovered from other computers, you will be well served considering a desktop as your primary machine, perhaps with a laptop as a portable machine. Data heavy files and applications not only require robust memory and processing power, they are often optimally used with dual screens, allowing you to review documents and perform data entry or reporting on the second screen.

Computer Peripherals

Peripheral devices to be plugged into your machine include microphones, video cameras, media players, printers, scanners, and Wi-Fi receivers. Which peripherals will be most useful to you is a judgment you have to make. However, a few quick recommendations:

1. USB Ports. Buy a machine that has as many high-speed USB ports as possible. The more you have available, the less you will have to plug and unplug devices as you go. New machines should have the most recent speed and bandwidth specifications available.

2. Printers. While computing has become relatively cheap, printing for some reason has not. It is now possible to send jobs to FedEx Office remotely, and pick up high-quality print jobs there. For many reasons, confidentiality among them, this may not always be a good option. Buy a laser printer. They are more expensive up front than a dot matrix, but over the life of them, your printing costs will be lower and your work product will always have a professional look that the attorneys you work for will admire and appreciate.

3. Scanners. Scanners, like printers, are not always cheap. You will often see combination machines that will include a printer, copier, scanner and fax. My experience has been to avoid them or seek the combination that is the most well designed and makes the most sense for you. I consider a scanner a necessity these days, given the expectation and need to transmit, view and store documents electronically. Scanners range from the incredibly simple to the highly technical and professional. If your practice deals with a few hundred pages at a time, there are several scanners which do a nice job converting paper to PDF format in one step (e.g., the Fujitsu Scan Snap). Research scanning options and buy one. You will be repaid in convenience and efficiency many times over.

4. Removable Media. This includes forms of static computer memory such as flash drives, SD cards, and external hard drives. Computer storage is now so cheap that it is quite possible, if not commonplace, to be able to store an entire case, including one hundred thousand pages of documents, all legal pleadings, all investigation reports and memoranda, as well as a database to manage them on a piece of removable media no larger than a postage stamp. This is not an exaggeration. While portable storage, and even portable applications, represent a great advance in convenience and efficiency, they also present a very real security hazard. The primary hazard is loss of data, as flash drives and SD cards are particularly susceptible to loss. They can fit in a pocket as easily as they can fall out of one. Because they are not used as often as computers, it is easy to lose track of them. Further, it is all too easy to multiply copies of case-related files across multiple pieces of media.

Here are two simple recommendations: First, password protect and encrypt the contents of your hard drive. It is not hard to do. Several flash drive password protection programs are available for cheap or free (e.g., USB Safeguard). Two free programs—TrueCrypt and Cryptainer LE—can create a fixed-size “container” on a flash drive, which then needs to be decrypted and its letter drive specified for each use. Both are free, easy to use, completely portable, and hackable only through extraordinary means. True Crypt can be found at http://www.truecrypt.org. Cryptainer is at http://www.cypherix.com/cryptainerle/).

Second, when using removable media for transport only, delete the files after they have been removed to the separate system. Try to keep the number of flash drives to a minimum, so as not to lose track. Try to keep the contents of flash drives segregated between personal files and work files on separate media.

Failing to protect removable media is asking for one of those data loss nightmares everyone has a story about, and which can dramatically alter the course of a particular case and your professional reputation in a moment.

5. Smart Phones. While, strictly speaking, your cell phone is not a computer peripheral (it is a computer in its own right), you have a decision now whether to do any of your computing on your phone. Smart phones such as the iPhone, Windows Mobile or Android all allow us to compute in ways and places we never thought possible before. The options for computing by your smart phone are too numerous to mention, and change at an incredible pace. However, I cannot impress strongly enough that to the extent you are doing investigative work through your phone, you need to protect it. Phones can be password protected, as well as lost forever. Data on phones can be recovered by other users, either through direct access or through viruses. Many people now review their email, both work and personal, on their phones. Depending on the email system used from the phone, confidential client data could be sitting in your pocket at all times. You must be aware of this, and take professional measures to protect data on your phone, and avoid breaching client confidentiality or loss.

Netbooks

These machines offer a lot to the portable user, particularly because they are lightweight and not very expensive. Think carefully before employing one as your primary machine. Netbooks do not have CD/DVD drives, and often have greatly reduced processing power and memory. Their smaller keyboards make it difficult to type (think lengthy reports or memoranda), and the screen size is difficult to use for even standard websites in a browser.

Additionally, most media (audio/video) files encounter difficulty because of the reduced power. You may be very well served using a netbook as an adjunct machine that you take with you to review and record limited data, to review documents or data with clients or witnesses on a limited basis. However, my assessment is that a netbook would be most useful as a secondary machine, with a desktop or even robust notebook as your primary setup.

Software

Perhaps far more important, and potentially more costly, than your hardware will be your software. The last decade has seen a revolution in the production of less expensive software for more precise tasks, as well as the refinement of commercial software. You need to be familiar with both to choose your software wisely but because of this revolution I must caution about installing too much software. It is easy to locate and test free software quite rapidly. It is also easy to find specialized tools to address a particular need in one case. Manage the number of applications on your machine and reduce them to the essential few to prevent headaches down the road. The Windows operating system is particularly susceptible to bloat with the more applications that are fully installed, many of which have services running in the background. Further, if your hard drive is approaching full, you court disaster due to fragmentation and use of temporary disk space, which most applications in Windows require. If you install it, ask in a month whether you still need it. Again, considering that the computer is one of your primary investigative tools, your work and your professional practice will be enhanced by investing the appropriate amount of money in software that ;you use on a regular basis.

1. Office Software. Home and business computers used to routinely come shipped with Microsoft Office, which includes Microsoft Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and others. For better or worse, it remains the standard office software for most users. If you do not have it, you should consider purchasing it, if only because many of the standard files you’ll get from other people are created using Office applications. The good news is that these are solid programs, which are feature-rich and able to handle almost any form of communicable data.

If you cannot afford the Microsoft license, Sun Microsystems publishes a free open-office software suite which attempts to mimic all the features of the Microsoft products. While there are some features to quibble with, and some annoying incompatibilities with other programs, generally does a very good job at the most basic office functions. See www.openoffice.org.

Simple Officelike applications are also available now online, without any installation necessary. An Internet connection is usually needed to work with these applications, although some off-line capabilities are available. Google Docs and spreadsheets are the most common. These applications contain built-in collaborative functions that may be very useful to the investigator, but they are typically stripped-down versions of their more robust brethren. Further, residing on someone else’s server poses security and ethical considerations that you must weigh (see the “Cloud Computing” section below).

2. PDF Software. Portable document format (PDF) files are ubiquitous. There are many free and low-cost programs which create and read PDF files, for example, CutePDF, Foxit Reader and Nuance PDF Converter. Some of these work very well and are easy on the budget. However, the PDF format was created by Adobe Systems, which most people are familiar with through Adobe Reader. I recommend purchasing the paid version of Adobe Acrobat, either standard or professional version. Managing a moderate number of PDF documents, as well as merging, bookmarking, annotating, extracting needed pages and assembling documents for delivery to an attorney or to court is made much easier through the fullly functioning PDF software. Given the central nature of documents to most investigations, I personally do not recommend cutting costs or corners here.

3. Browsers. The heart of searching and researching on the Internet is the browser, the software which allows you to access webpages. Internet Explorer used to be the default option, the one available to most people. In its current version, it is a good browser. However, Firefox, Opera, and Google’s Chrome (not to mention Macintosh’s Safari) are all available now. Most browsers now have the ability to install add-ons, which help with individual browsing tasks, such as saving web addresses or managing the clipboard. Which browser you use is less important than knowing how it operates and a little bit about what is under the hood. For example, knowing how to save the contents of one’s research session, to return to it at a later time is well worth knowing. (Firefox’s Session Manager add-on is useful here). Note: The reader is advised to review the subject of web browser history and caches in Chapter 7.

4. Dictation Software. Off-the-shelf dictation software was introduced several years ago, with a great promise: to eliminate the need for lengthy and difficult typing by speaking into a microphone to your machine. Writing seems to be about half of what investigators do. Dragon’s NaturallySpeaking, though not cheap, is very effective. It comes in a standard and professional edition, costing several hundred dollars for a license. Like any other software, it requires learning and practice to become effective. Should you opt for dictation software, you will need a machine that can handle it, preferably one with as much RAM and processing speed as you can afford. It is not worth installing dictation software on a machine with insufficient system requirements, and you should expect to install it on a machine that exceeds the minimum requirements specified by the software. Once working, it can save you a lot of time.

In addition to having the basic software necessary to perform and report your investigation, equally important is the ability to find the software you need when you need it. There are dozens of situations in which a particular piece of software may be required to accomplish the task at hand. Usually, there are free versions available for these utilities and devices. For example, Windows XP does not let you print the contents of a directory file list very easily. There are free programs that do so quite nicely. It is a skill in and of itself to find narrowly tailored software for limited purposes. Usually searching one of the main search engines will quickly present options, and further clues as to how to proceed.

5. Data Security. All users need antivirus and firewall software without exception. The rampant proliferation of viruses and intrusions make it a certainty that your computer’s door will be knocked upon, so to speak. McAfee, Symantec and Norton all publish antivirus software for a price. Microsoft Security Suite is free and has the advantage of rapid updates to threats to Windows systems. It generally gets good reviews. Windows XP, of course, now has a firewall built in, which is also free. ZoneAlarm Basic has long been a useful firewall utility.

If you get an infection or a piece of persistent malware or ad ware, use CCleaner or Malwarebytes. SpyBot Search and Destroy is also a long-standing reputable free program to deal with the effects of ad aware and unaware.

No system is completely secure. This is a fact, particularly in the network age; any system is vulnerable to intrusion if enough resources are thrown at it. However, there are a number of things that you can do to minimize data insecurity:

  • Follow the flash drive advice above.
  • Keep your passwords safe and keep them complex.
  • Install antivirus and firewall software.
  • Do not let other users on the computer you use for investigation.
  • Follow such good practices as keeping a spam filter, never clicking on banner ads, and avoiding suspicious websites. It is amazing how few people actually follow such common-sense practice.
  • Secure wireless networks with the highest level of security you can. Low-level encryption can be broken by a 12-year-old. Higher levels are available without too much fuss. Many people recommend limiting the use of wireless networks at all when involving confidential legal files. Avoid using open wireless networks, such as airports or free Wi-Fi spots as much as possible, particularly when doing investigative work. At large, free Wi-Fi areas such as airports, it is commonplace for hackers to spend hours targeting users of the network.
  • Be conscious of your emailing habits. Remember, whatever you email can be forwarded anywhere by the recipient and will reside on their system. That means that the security of your email is only as strong as the recipient’s system, which you have no way of knowing. When you are transmitting sensitive files for work containing confidential client data, recognize that email presents vulnerabilities. Often, professionals working with legal professionals have more secure means of transmitting files such as secure file transfer protocol sites for file upload, which can be deleted after use. Of course, transmitting files in person is much more secure. Keep separate email accounts, and if possible separate machines, for work and personal email.
  • Choose complicated passwords and do not replicate them across all your services. Poor password choice is one of the most widespread security weaknesses. Use numbers, special characters, and upper/lower case combinations of more than six characters long. According to a recent study, the most common password is “123456.” The second is “12345,” the third is “123456789,” and the fourth is “password.” Don’t do this.

Cloud Computing and Third-Party Services

Perhaps the greatest change in the last ten years for networked computing has been the development of online applications and resources which both operate and store data on the server end of the system. This is commonly referred to as “cloud” computing, referring to the Internet as a whole as the cloud. The services—personal- and business-level applications such as word processing, spreadsheet creation, collaboration and data networking achieved through applications—do not reside on the user’s computer. Rather, they are served via centralized or distributed systems by a third-party provider, who also stores and secures the data contained on them. Web-based email was perhaps the very first generation of cloud computing.

While the term Web 2.0 has been around for several years and refers to a multitude of platform-shifting developments on the Internet, it certainly refers to the development of online applications run on a provider’s server through the use of high-speed Internet connections.

To name just a few, some of the more visible applications in this class of services are the following:

  • Email: Google, Yahoo, MSN Hotmail.
  • Wiki’s: Socialtext, CC Text, PBwiki, Atlassian Confluence.
  • Collaboration and Project Management: Basecamp, Jive Software, MindTouch, Central Desktop, PBworks.
  • Office Applications: Zoho Suite, Google Docs, WebOffice.
  • File Syncing and Transfer: Dropbox, Drop.Io, SugarSync, Live Mesh.
  • File Access, Backup and Storage: Mozy, IDrive, Carbonite, Jungle Disk.
  • Calendaring: 30 Boxes, Google Calendar.

These services provide numerous benefits and advantages, especially to a small business person or solo private investigator. First, the cost of these services, compared with license fees for desktop applications, is relatively minimal and often free. Second, there is very little overhead expertise needed to install and maintain these applications beyond basic computer and Internet connection. Third, online applications are more easily able to roll out changes and updates to their service, because it does not require releasing new software in the traditional way. Only changes on the back-end server need to be made. Fourth, in many cases the server performance and security exceeds that provided by the typical small business or solo practitioner. But for the occasional untimely downtime, server performance rates are exceptionally high.

For the legal investigator, using these services can offer immediate benefits. Often, criminal defense teams are loosely assembled ad hoc teams of independent lawyers, investigators and paralegals. Many of these services excel at just this kind of collaboration. Documents can be shared, updated and stored in a manner much more conducive to collaboration and simply emailing. Media files, scanned images, scheduling, work product collaboration and so forth can all take place online in a shared space, with fine-grained permissions. Often, these documents and collaboration materials are available anywhere there is an Internet connection. Finally, these applications tend to be relatively easy to use and “click centered,” allowing for a shallow learning curve for the least experienced users.

One drawback of these services is that the applications are not as robust and feature-rich as desktop or local network services. However, the cautionary note here is one of privacy and security. The confidences of your client and the attorney-client privilege are of the highest duties to the legal investigator. Use of online services necessarily allows your data to be shared with at least the host of the services, if only for technical reasons. Further, maintaining these materials online means that their physical security is out of your control. Finally, the service provider does not share the same duty that you do to your client, nor do they serve a duty beyond a commercial transaction to you or the attorney working with you.

It is an individual judgment on the part of the investigator whether to utilize third-party cloud-hosted services. It is certainly incumbent upon the investigator using computer resources to look into the particulars of these services to see if they could be useful. Further, if one is to make an informed decision, at a minimum the following should take place:

1. Read the privacy policy and legal policy of the online service. If it is not posted online, request it directly before subscribing. Specifically, understand the policy for responding to subpoenas, search warrants, and other legal processes. Often, privacy policies are not written with considerable detail, or what they leave out is significant. Almost all reserve the right to inspect your data for “network security or abuse.” Understand how they define those terms. Further, developing case law about online data storage is just that, and often provides less than clear guidance about what the obligations of third-party storage services are.

2. Understand how the data is stored and how to secure it. Again, the worthy cloud computing providers will provide some description of their storage security. Ones worth giving money to will provide it upon request. For example, many providers back up their own data to yet another service in a remote location. Is this overseas? Is it in turn secured? Still other services can provide encryption “on the back end” so that only a restricted number of employees have access to encrypted data, and only under special circumstances.

3. Your attorney and client should understand that these services are being used and what the potential security risks, if any, are concerning the client’s legal data.

4. What happens when services are discontinued or data is deleted? Are versions of data continually stored on the machine despite its removal? Very importantly, if you would like to remove all your data and retain it in a form that can be viewed off-line, does the service provide for that? Reputable cloud services providers will tell you, often in the FAQs section of their website.

5. Test and demo the service before you sign up. Almost all cloud services provide a demo or a reduced-functionality free service. Do not just sign up for an online service or collaborative platform without testing it first, knowing how it functions, and understanding what it is designed to do and whether that fits your needs.

I have known highly respected death penalty practitioners who have managed their trial cases online through a collaborative platform. I’ve also known well-respected attorneys who shy away from third-party online services, only to violate every reasonable local security precaution when it comes to storing and transmitting their own case-related data. As important as the quality of the service is the quality of your practice in using it. I believe it is an individual judgment for each legal investigator whether to use such services, but do so only in conjunction with the attorney and with all the necessary information to make a sound decision.

II. Internet Searching

Ten years ago, the search capabilities of the Internet and the World Wide Web were considered relatively novel, a little thrilling, and incredibly useful. They are in many ways much more useful now, though hardly novel. Every investigator now incorporates some form of Internet-based searching into his or her practice. It cannot be the aim of this section to explain or replace all of the methods that investigators have come to rely on to conduct Internet searches for case-related information, however, some areas are explained. In addition, an Appendix A and B to this chapter lists some useful sites to the investigator, as I have come to know them.

The near ubiquity and ease of Internet search has brought both a blessing and a curse to the practice, probably more blessing than curse. The increase in the sheer amounts of available data makes it more likely that some information about the person or entity you are investigating can be found through Internet means. The drawback is that in many ways this information is harder to find, as search hits multiply and large areas of the publicly available Internet go unindexed by the major search engines. Further, Internet search has added to—as much as it has replaced—the steps needed to perform most defense investigation. It is almost unreliable to perform Internet searches regarding witnesses and entities before conducting traditional field-level investigation. While successful Internet searches have eliminated many of the time-consuming (not to say brain numbing) hours spent making phone calls and waiting on phone trees, it is also easy to get “lost” in Internet search without some focused strategies.

Here, a brief word of advice: while Internet search has brought us to corners of information we sometimes did not know existed, relying solely on Internet search can never be an effective replacement for traditional investigation. As a colleague once said about OJIN, “It is no substitute for pulling the file.” The truism behind this is that often the most important case information resides in the handwritten notes on the margins of pages or the direct recollection of a witness rather than in a digital record. However, Internet search cannot only help us locate those witnesses, but might make us sharper about which pieces of paper exist for us to look at the margins. Again, data alone is never evidence. Witnesses are needed to tell the story behind evidence and attest to its veracity.

Search Strategy

Each investigator has their own style of conducting Internet searches. Generally, the most effective strategy follows a “feedback” model, wherein the results of one search such as a name or address are then fed back into the search engine to generate additional information. That information might then be fed back again to develop more leads.

Often the information you are looking for is not available online. Very useful information about how to find that evidence is available online, however, whether in the form of a records custodian, a contact and phone number, a business address, an ownership record, etc.

Generally, Internet searches are either for locating people, looking for information about people, or researching subjects of interest or institutions. Further, Internet searches can roughly be divided into paid searches and free searches. What follows below are some search tools I find useful when beginning an investigation using the Internet.

Paid Searches

Public records resellers and data aggregators sell subscription-based or individual record access online. Public access resellers compile vast databases of information collected from public and semipublic sources and provide search tools based on identifiers such as name, address, Social Security Number or zip code. They then aggregate the results and provide hopefully up-to-date search returns. Several headline-making episodes of stolen or lost data from the public records resellers caused them to increase their security and re-examine instruction lists to make sure that their users were properly authorized. The last decade has seen an industry consolidation and restructuring for the most well-regarded paid subscription services. Others remain relatively untouched. The net result has been generally less access for the private legal investigator, although quality paid subscription services still exist.

ChoicePoint and AutoTrackXP have been eliminated, as the Choicepoint company was initially bought by LexisNexis. As they had also purchased the Accurint public records service, they divested the service to the West publishing group, which modified and eliminated. The AutoTrackXP product was folded into a product called Clear, which is available only for government and law enforcement users.

Accurint remains available, but is more restricted to government and other authorized users. Whether they will give a subscription to a licensed private investigator in Oregon is an open question. They can be contacted at www.Accurint.com. Accurint has long maintained a companion service called IRBsearch.com, which offers a similar subscription-based search with relatively modest fees that is specifically designed for private investigators.

OPENonline (www.openonline.com) is a long-established, Oregon-based public records reseller. They will maintain an account for you for $25 a month. In some instances they will waive the fee permanently. They have excellent Oregon-based search tools, but their searches are not cheap. Much of what they used to offer has been replaced by free open records searches by State of Oregon public agencies. However, you may find it useful to keep an account for special needs searches. They remain the best collection of Oregon-based public records online, for a fee. For example, one can search OJIN records by address in OPENonline.

A second tier of paid search exists nationally under several different resellers such as Intelius, SearchAmerica, Melissa Data, DOBsearch.com, and LocatePLUS. I cannot vouch for the quality of the searches as I have not personally tried them. Several have been around for many years. Their price structure seems prohibitive to the professional who does a lot of paid searching. Often, these companies are catering to the non-professional who is interested in searching for lost family members, former classmates, and debtors. It may be worth your time to know their capabilities for special needs searches.

A third tier of paid search exists and needs to be strictly avoided. For example, search “cell phone records” in Google and you will find companies that purport to legally provide you with records such as bank records, credit histories, and cell phone records for a fee. Several noteworthy cases have occurred prosecuting these companies, as often the records are acquired by deception or unscrupulous employees of the data providers. All three categories of the records listed above are protected by law, and no public record reseller or data aggregator should have access to them. Your access should be nothing less than lawful.

Court Records

Online court records are an area that has seen a revolution in the last ten years. Availability and pricing varies widely from state to state and by judicial district. Some states and counties offer images of court record filings online for a fee. Others offer lookups for free, and still others require a subscription to do record index searches. Outside of Oregon, locating online court records is relatively easy through a simple search engine search.

Within Oregon, two main court records searches are available, for state and federal courts:

1. The Oregon Judicial Information Network (OJIN) provides county-by-county and statewide court record index searches on a subscription basis. In addition to criminal cases, civil case records such as divorces, foreclosures and evictions are all referenced within OJIN. Social Networking Sites

Perhaps the most explosive area of Internet communication currently are the social networking websites. These sites emphasize collaboration, sharing, and the creation of individualized webpages or collections of web posts, which can be viewed publicly, semi-publicly, or privately. MySpace, Facebook, Twitter, Linked-In, and Flickr are among the most popular. There are many, many more. Currently, Facebook is the fastest growing website in the world, with an announced user base of 400 million and growing. That is almost one-half billion users.

Unlike email, which is almost completely walled off, social networking sites have presented the investigator with the tantalizing opportunity to find out information about third parties and witnesses remotely, through a web interface. Like any other page, once displayed, these sites can be captured and information preserved as evidence, if relevant. (See acquiring web-based evidence below.) However, often one is not able to view the full contact details and content on a social networking site belonging to a person of interest unless one logs into the service oneself, then sends a request to the page’s owner.

It has been tempting to some to create dummy logins or credentials with fake information in order to gain access to a subject of interest’s webpage. The ethical question has asserted itself whether this is permissible for a legal investigator. (For more on this topic, see Chapter 2 regarding ethics, and Chapter 15, on the section on interviewing teens.) Without exploring the many arguments in this debate, it is recommended that one enter or capture a social networking site only when properly identified through one’s login. Generally, it is never permissible for the legal investigator to fail to identify themselves or misidentify themselves in the course of their duties. Online should be no different. The negative outcomes of doing this are all too clear: when the witness who owns the account testifies to a contact or inquiry made by an investigator using a fake name, the investigator is then exposed on the stand about the use of mis-representation. Without question, this harms the interests of both your client and the attorney who has hired you.

That said, when encountering a social networking site for information about a witness, explore all publicly available avenues to information without logging in, for often they are many. On occasion, social networking sites have been subpoenaed for copies of web-based information. One may assume that the owner of the account would be contacted upon receipt of a subpoena unless the site owner is not legally obligated to keep the subpoena secret.

Whether logged in or not, often the information from a social networking site can be leveraged or compared with other information from different avenues of investigation to guide your search and your questions to a witness.

Conversely, if you use a social networking site for personal use, it should go without saying that no case-related information should be posted there. As with anything you post on the Internet, whether semi-private or not, personal or not, you should do so with the idea that you might be asked about it on the stand at some point. This is especially true for personal blogging, as blogs tend to be completely public. The rule of thumb is once it is posted on the Internet, it is there forever. If something is posted by you, whether personal or not, it becomes part of your professional reputation.

III. Computer Forensics

The field of computer forensic investigation is no longer a nascent area. Further, a greater variety of cases now involve original computer evidence, and this will continue to grow. Recovering, analyzing, and understanding computer-based evidence in a manner sound for court presentation requires that you hire an expert. When computer forensics was relatively new to law enforcement, many computer examiners in police agencies were susceptible to an attack on their qualifications, having completed several specialized courses lasting only a few hours about how to recover particular kinds of data. Back then, these officers were often vulnerable to challenges in their qualifications. With the passage of time, many of these same officers have gained the requisite expertise needed to testify competently in this area, and are less susceptible to challenges to their expertise.

A computer forensics expert is trained, preferably certified, in using computer forensic software, techniques of recovery, technical information about operating systems and how they manage their data. A forensic expert worth hiring is also sensitive to the types of questions and issues that defense teams have.

Most importantly, a computer examiner can access computer data in a way that does not alter the data or the operating system in which it is stored, can search for and recover data from hidden or inaccessible parts of the hard drive, and can competently testify to the methods generally accepted to do these things.

I will not treat computer forensics in great detail here. Indeed, the field of computer forensics is highly detailed and ever changing. I refer you to the next chapter in this manual concerning computer forensics.

The Role of the Investigator

Lack of formal expertise notwithstanding, as an investigator you play a vital role in the recovery of evidence from digital sources. There is a difference between data and evidence. Until data is recovered and analyzed from a computer system with a particular set of issues and questions surrounding a legal theory, it remains data. It becomes evidence through the proper communication of those questions to the forensic examiner, the sound recovery and analysis of the data, and the defensible introduction of the data into evidence in court. Chiefly, the investigator can do the following crucial tasks:

1. Hire the computer expert and interface with them. As with any expert, you will want to interview a potential expert witness for their background and qualifications. Obtain a curriculum vitae. You’ll be looking for someone with sufficient experience in disputed legal matters, preferably with criminal case background. You want to know what computer forensics software packages they are qualified to use, and what certifications they may or may not have. Many computer forensics specialists come to the tree through the civil world of “data recovery,” which places an emphasis on several discovery production, rather than the standard of proof in a criminal case. Other specialists are former law enforcement and may lack particular sensitivity to issues raised by the defense team. Further, if your case has particular kinds of evidence or issues, such as computer networking, you’ll want to find an expert with particular knowledge in that area.

You will be responsible for acquiring and delivering the computer evidence to them, along with a detailed explanation of the evidence you’re trying to uncover, and questions your investigation is presenting.

2. Be a translator. As you develop the questions for the computer expert and communicate with him about his results, you will need to learn and comprehend the specialized terminology the expert deals with. In essence, you need to familiarize yourself with the concepts surrounding the examination and a particular language commonly used to express those concepts, if only to ensure that the attorney understands what the results are. Often, a computer forensics examiner will rely on the investigator to review preliminary output data for possible results and refinement. You will need to be able to understand his results, and if you do not, interview the expert so that the layperson can comprehend them. Further, you’ll need to refine the examination according to your understanding of the case and the attorney’s needs. This process of translation between expert and attorney begins before the actual engagement of the expert and continues all the way through trial preparation and the conclusion. It is an essential defense function and one best handled by a traditional defense investigator, whose nonexpertise is actually an asset to the defense team in this area.

3. Gather preliminary and supporting evidence. Computer evidence rarely stands on its own. Indeed, computer evidence is often used to prove the state of mind or behavior of individual actors. Those people, whether your client or other witnesses, often need to be interviewed either prior to the examination or after the results of some computer questions have been made known. Interviewing your client about his or her computer environment and behavior is particularly important, often to decide whether a computer examination would be helpful and to support the attorney in understanding what the examiner’s results might signify for the defense theory. Appended to this chapter (as Appendix C) is a brief, suggested questionnaire to use when interviewing a client about their computer and usage.

4. Evidence presentation. In addition to guiding the computer examination and interfacing with the expert, the investigator also has a role in how the output is presented in court. The individual computer evidence, its significance to the case, its complexity and volume, as well as its technical nature are all highly variable from case to case. What does not vary is that it must be understood by a judge or jury who are not computer experts. The traditional investigator can prepare courtroom trial exhibits, whether they be graphics, spreadsheets, imaged documents, or recovered media files. Depending on the type of computer evidence recovered, there are myriad ways to convert file formats and present evidence electronically in court, which the investigator will need to grapple with.

Scenario One

Your client is charged with possessing child pornography. The state has made available several thousand images of contraband and has threatened to charge the client with distribution if he does not accept the current plea offer. Your attorney wants you to hire a computer forensics expert and find out “how the files got there” and whether there is any evidence of distribution of contraband. You select and hire a computer expert, and explain that you would like to know where in the directory structure the files were recovered, whether there are other similar files in other parts of the operating system, and what sorts of software are installed on the machine which might explain their appearance. The expert explains to you how Internet search history can be recovered, the probable contents of the recycle bin for file deletions, and what settings in the peer-to-peer software might be examined for evidence of distribution. You suggest the expert examine fragments of web-based email for possible distribution of evidence, as well as the last access date of the contraband files compared to their creation dates. Together, you and the expert isolate relevant date ranges which can be examined for other user activity, and agree on several other steps to isolate data which might prove or disprove distribution. After two or three rounds of examination, you summarize the expert’s reported findings in a memorandum to the attorney. (See Chapter 7 for more on this type of investigation.)

Scenario Two

Your client has been charged with drug distribution. He has told the attorney that his communications with others in the drug trade are in his email on a laptop computer at his girlfriend’s house, which the police have not searched. The attorney wants you to get the laptop, boot it up and print out all the emails with the co-conspirators. You suggest to the attorney that a computer forensics expert be hired to secure the laptop and recover the emails. You retrieve the laptop, deliver it to the expert, along with the information you have from the client about the type of email he was using and what you are seeking to recover. The expert images the hard drive, isolates an archived Microsoft Outlook PST file, converts the contents to individual PDF files and makes your copy of the original PST file in its native format. You deliver the attorney forensically defensible emails in a format he can read. If needed, you and the expert can testify to the validity of the email and how it was recovered.

Challenging the State’s Computer Evidence

Perhaps the primary use of the computer expert is to challenge the admissibility or interpretation of the state’s computer evidence in their case-in-chief. Such challenges may take the form of suppressing computer evidence in a motion. Whether to suppress or cast doubt, challenging the government’s expert requires hiring your own expert—and is a multilayered process.

The investigator’s role is to assist the attorney in coordinating the challenge and developing the evidence alongside the computer expert. An expert can assist the investigator in identifying those types of materials that the state’s computer examiner may have used during their examination. Often, police forensic examiners write fairly brief reports about what they did. An expert can assist you in preparing questions about what actually may have taken place during the examination that are not contained in the report. Additionally, police computer examiners will gather and store data during the course of their examination, which is not routinely turned over to the defense. An expert can help identify the types of data which the attorney should ask for in discovery. The expert can also help explain how the police examiner used the forensic software to recover the data being offered by the state. Often, police examiners use shortcuts to obtain certain types of information, which leave out collateral information that may set the evidence and context or explain it in a different way.

The point is, in preparing for a challenge the investigator needs to assist the attorney in identifying and obtaining all the computer evidence seized, examined, generated and reported on in a case before the areas of challenge can be identified.

Once the attorney has developed the legal theories, the investigator needs to explain and translate the legal theories to the computer examiner to elicit possible modes of examination to further test the theory. Often a computer examiner is in a position to suggest data to recover which may or may not bear on the issue.

Additionally, a fruitful area of questioning with your expert is what computer investigation the state might have undertaken to more fully explore the evidentiary issue in a less biased fashion. For example, the state may be relying on a document recovered from a computer without having examined the same hard drive for other versions of the document in different states of development. Some computer files contain metadata—data within the file which conveys certain properties about the file itself—which could show meaningful information about the development of the state’s document. If the police examiner never looked for this evidence, he can be questioned about why not. If your examiner did recover the evidence and it affects the trial issues, then you have advanced the defense theory.

In almost all cases, challenging the state’s computer evidence requires other traditional investigation, through witnesses and traditional documents and objects. Who else uses the computer? They need to be interviewed. Where was the computer located at the time the search was executed? Others at the scene can tell you. Was a computer networked and if so how? The computer systems administrator at the client’s place employment may need interviewing. The universal fact about computer-based evidence is that it never stands alone. Computers do not tell their stories by themselves, and witnesses to their admissibility and the meaning of the data are always needed. Equally universal is that at the “end” of every computer examination, the attorney will have numerous questions which only the investigator can answer through traditional evidence gathering and interviews.

Appended to this chapter is also a brief outline (Appendix D) of principles and ideas to be considered when challenging the states computer evidence and expertise.

Scenario Three

Your client is charged with stealing business secrets from his previous employer. The state executed a search warrant at his home and discovered numerous computers in various states of operation in various places in his residence. All were unplugged and seized. The client has said that his employment contract allowed him to access his employer’s computers remotely and required routine and permissible storage of employer-owned data on his own system. That system was networked in a particular way.

You are seeking to challenge the government’s introduction of employer-owned data on the client’s laptop, allegedly without the permission of the employer. Your computer expert and client both inform you of ways in which the computers in the home were networked. You request reports from the search that the state failed to provide which show that law enforcement did nothing during their seizure of the computers to diagram or otherwise document the state of the network when it was taken down. Further, your client supplies you with password information and details about the remote access software that he used to access the employer’s computers legitimately. Your expert takes this information and examines the software to confirm the client’s story and recover other files that are legitimately possessed by the client.

Your expert assists you in preparing questions for the computer seizure team about their failure to properly document the computing environment in the home. He also helps prepare questions for you to ask other employees who work remotely with the same employer. Finally, the attorney and the computer expert confer about computer examination techniques and terminology that the attorney can use in cross-examining the state’s computer examiner. The computer forensic investigation and the traditional investigation are combined to prepare a challenge to the admissibility of all the computer evidence, based on the failure of the state to follow generally accepted digital evidence collection practices. Failing that, you are prepared to question the examiner at trial about the meaning of the recovered evidence, given the other investigation they failed to perform.

Cell Phone Forensics

As mentioned above, cell phones are computers. Even the simplest cell phones with basic dialing, address book, and text messaging function with the use of an operating system, processor, data chip, and storage card. As such, cell phones and the data contained on them should be treated forensically. Unlike the plain vanilla examination of a standalone hard drive, the field of cell phone forensics is relatively new and evolving. A computer forensics expert will need to be hired to properly receive, process and extract information from a cell phone in a manner that is presentable for court.

Cell phone forensics, while simpler in some ways, are far more variable and complex in so far as operating systems are particular to particular phones. Symbian, iPhone, Windows Mobile, Android and Blackberry are all examples of cell phone operating systems. They vary not only by type of phone but also make and model. Cell phone operating systems are highly customizable, and communities of users exist around each to “hack” the system to customize the phone. Most cell phone systems are closed, as manufacturers have an interest in controlling how the systems work on their phones. The relatively new Android system is intentionally open source, hoping to spur community development of the system.

In any event, the variability in hardware and software for cell phones, makes cell phone forensics less standardized, and always in a continual state of development. For the investigator this means primarily two things. First, when collecting evidence for cell phones, it is best to power off the phone unit without touching any keys, and transmit it to a forensics examiner. This is not always possible. The user may offer to give you the storage card from the phone, or offer to forward information, or save information from the phone to external media. To properly examine the phone as a whole, it is necessary to possess the entire unit, at least temporarily, in order to create data images from it.

Second, many forensics examiners are offering cell phone examination services, but not all are equal. In hiring an expert, have them explain to you the hardware they use to gather data from the phone, the examination software they use to look at that data, and most importantly what experience they have in doing cell phone forensics. The types of data available on a cell phone and the methods for getting it are variable and evolving, so look for an expert with as much experience as you can find, who has the ability to adapt their methods to your particular evidence. Email Recovery and Review

Although recovering email files, and to some extent reviewing them, uses similar techniques as other file formats, recovering email should be considered a special endeavor. Email remains our favorite application by far and is a rich repository of evidence in certain cases. Almost 50 billion non-spam emails are transmitted every day worldwide (Radicati Group, September, 2009). Although in many cases computer evidence is not focused on email, when it is relevant it is often very relevant. Email is content rich. It is user specific. Very importantly, it is time-based and automatically time-stamped. Finally, email often contains file attachments which are the most contemporaneous record of certain documents, as they may have evolved over time.

Email files come in different formats depending on the system used by the email server and the users connected to it. Distinct email formats include .pst, .nsf, .msg, .dbx, .eml, .mbx and .mbox and to some extent can be converted from one to another after recovery. Email recovery review can be a relatively simple endeavor, if the amount of email is limited, identified, and structured. It may be as simple as copying a single file from a computer and reloading it in off-the-shelf software to see what it contains. More often, email recovery review is more complex and often very voluminous. This requires the services of a computer forensic examiner and some basic understanding about how stored email is recovered and reviewed.

Generally, email recovery and review can be thought of as having four steps: 1) recovery, 2) de- duplication, 3) standardization, and 4) review. Various recovery processes are employed depending on the type of email sought, often due to cancer found in several places on a system or across multiple machines. To reduce volume, de-duplication strategies have to be employed. If email is sought and discovered in multiple formats, these will be converted to a single format so that they can be uploaded to a review platform. Finally, several common review platforms exist to systematically examine email and preserve it for use as evidence.

Often, email comes to the defense investigator in the form of discovery. Generally, email can only be obtained from an Internet service provider through a search warrant. When providing emails under a search warrant, email providers often convert their own native files to a common email format that can be read by native software or standard email review platforms. For example, Yahoo! may return searched email as an .mbox file. This is not the format in which the email resides on its servers, but can easily be loaded into an email client such as Thunderbird.

Other times, email is recovered from an enterprise or business email server. In that event, it is typically structured in an organized way and has been produced as the result of a specified set of filter and query parameters, such as all email from date one to date two of a named user. In this instance, the recovery of the email is less difficult and is typically provided in a review ready format.

Recovery of email from an individual user’s machine is often a more complex endeavor and can become very complex indeed if multiple hard drives are involved. Recovery of email from a hard drive involves the forensic examiner looking for known types of email files, visible (allocated) hard drive space, as well as recovering files and folders that might contain email from deleted or unallocated space. Typically a four-part approach is used:

1. From the visible file structure, a search is performed for all known email file types such as .pst, .msg, .eml, .dbx, etc. Typically, with default programs these files usually reside in default parts of the directory.

2. A “folder restore” process is running as the hard drive, which attempts to recreate entire folders that have been deleted or otherwise removed from the directory structure. Often these folders can be identified along with part of their original directory structure. The examiner looks for known folders which typically contain email files.

3. An “undelete files” process is run, scours the hard drive for all files that have been previously marked as deleted files, and attempts to restore them to the extent possible. These are then examined to see which are email types.

4. A “file carving” process is employed, which seeks to identify the beginning and ending of certain types of files from unallocated space. Computer files often have a signature “header” telling the system that the following strings are a certain type of file. Sometimes they have a footer as well, signaling the end of the file. However, some email types, notably .pst files, have a header but no footer. In that event, the examiner locates the beginning of the file and tells the recovery software to “carve” a certain amount of data following that beginning and call it a complete file. A series of validation steps are run on the recovered file to attempt to restore it to its original working order. File carving is partly a manual process and is very time-consuming.

It should be noted that web-based mail can also be recovered using forensic processes, but is almost never complete and usually very fragmentary. It is not uncommon for a user to have both locally based email and web-based email in some combination.

Recovered email may have little or no content. Often it is highly duplicative and fragmentary. Once the uniform collection of email has been established, it is often converted to a standard format, one being the Microsoft Outlook .pst format.

So, after all this email has been recovered, how can you look at it? The answer depends on what has been recovered and in what format, as well as what the purpose of the review is. As noted, a collection of .eml files provided on a CD from the state can simply be backed up and loaded into Microsoft Outlook Express. By contrast, multiple mailboxes residing on multiple machines in different formats may very well create a huge volume of email to review.

I know of four basic tools that can serve as review platforms for recovered email. The first and easiest is the native application for that type of email, such as Outlook or Lotus Notes. The application is pointed to the collection of email files and read as if you are the original user. Emails of interest are marked or exported for print.

Certain file type viewers such as Quick View Plus can handle a limited number of email file types, and allow viewing of mailboxes, as well as searching and exporting for print. These allow review of the underlying file without changing it. Paraben Forensics has created a program called Email Examiner, which both converts and loads email files onto an investigative review platform and can handle multiple mailboxes and individual mail files at one time. It has robust search and bookmarking capabilities for noting emails of interest. Again, this review platform leaves the original file untouched. At $200 it is relatively inexpensive, and is designed with the investigator in mind.

CaseMap legal review software, for an additional price, has an email converting and review plug-in which works with the paid version of Adobe Acrobat. Essentially it cans a PST file, unpacks the messages and converts them to PDF files with metadata, populates the fields of a spreadsheet with the email contents (to, from, date, subject, contents, attachments), and allows for sorting and filtering according to any of the contents of those fields. This is a hybrid solution invented by the makers of CaseMap to handle a moderate amount of recovered email in a fairly standard way. It is neither a native review application nor a fully capable document platform. Still, it is a fairly user-friendly way to process emails, especially if you are already a user of CaseMap.

Finally, and most soundly, electronic discovery review platforms such as Concordance and Summation are designed to be able to load certain types of email files, all of their metadata and relevant fields and their attachments and incorporate them into a larger review database that can be accessed by multiple users. This access is typically at a local area network. These programs are complicated to use and have expensive licensing costs. Most civil litigation teams use these platforms to review huge amounts of email involved in electronic discovery.

Similar platforms are now available from a legal services vendor through a web interface. The vendor loads email files onto their servers and the user logs in and reviews the email collection in a secure environment online. One standard system is called iCONECT. A multitude of other web-based solutions have proliferated as well (e.g., CaseVault, Relativity, Catalyst, Case Central, and many others). These tools are designed for litigation review teams, and can also be quite expensive.

Whatever your particular case, and the type and amount of email, it is incumbent on the investigator to know through witnesses and experts what relevance the email might have. It is recommended that once the broad outlines of the email universe are established and a judgment by the attorney is made as to the email’s importance, a review plan be drawn up. You will find relevant emails, and conduct further investigation from them, much faster and more efficiently if some foresight and planning is employed.

IV. Acquiring Electronic Evidence

Undoubtedly there will be many occasions when you will have to acquire and preserve electronic evidence from an original source computer. The sheer variety of types of electronic evidence means that no one uniform method can be prescribed to guide the investigator in collecting this type of data. Electronic evidence can be as large as an entire network or a server, or as small as an individual text message from a cell phone. Some principles and methods can guide you along the way.

Before discussing the capture of electronic evidence further, a few terms are helpful:

Native files: Native files are files in their original format, as they are used by the operating system and program which created them. For example a .doc file is the format commonly used by Microsoft Word. An .msg file is a native file that is a product of Outlook email. These examples and more are file types which can be converted to another file format, which makes transmission or presentation easier. We speak of “native files” when referring to the original file formats.

Hash value: A hash value is simply a unique numerical value produced by an algorithm which is processed against the digital values making up an original file (the 1s and 0s). Hash algorithms are standardized, although there are more than one kind. The result of processing a file or collection of files using a hash algorithm is a unique value. Hash values are used to uniquely identify electronic evidence, when being compared with a file that is purported to be the same.

Metadata: Literally, data about data. Metadata are values and information about particular files, either from the operating system in which they reside or within the file itself. Metadata provides the examiner with information concerning the file itself. Date created, last accessed, and date modified are all commonly understood Windows metadata values for files contained in that system. Image files often have metadata embedded within them, capturing not only the date of creation, but the type of equipment and settings of the camera which created the image. Metadata can be relatively simple, such as the last date printed found within a Microsoft Word document, or tremendously complex, such as metadata values within databasing programs. Native files are often of value because metadata has been preserved along with the content.

Ideally, all electronic evidence would be preserved by making a mirror image of the electronic media using forensic software. Any data evidentiary value would then be extracted from the forensic image and preserved to an additional media for analysis and presentation. However, ideal situations rarely present themselves. Often the original medium cannot lawfully be seized or possessed by the investigator permanently. In other instances, the electronic data is “second generation,” provided in a format different from its original. Often, electronic evidence is available to the investigator in an unplanned context, necessitating some method of capture that is far from ideal.

In these cases, the investigator should employ every means to document what has been received, how it was acquired and what manner in which it was stored, in what manner it was reviewed, and what the ultimate disposition of it was. When collecting electronic data, bear in mind several ideal principles. Your preferred method of acquiring electronic evidence:

  • should not disturb the original evidence.
  • should reproduce the original evidence faithfully in as many aspects as possible.
  • should be reproduced by generally accepted methods that can be explained.
  • should be directly sourceable to the original material through a method of acquisition and chain of custody, preferably with the original in your possession.
  • should be preserved in a manner that is presentable in court. Any conversion methods for presentation should also be understandable and explained.

Choose methods that most closely hue to the principles above when gathering data.

A few examples may help you:

1. Your client ran a web server from home and posted pages to his Internet site which the government will seek to introduce against him. With his permission, you go to his home, pull the plug on the server and transport the entire box undisturbed to your computer forensics examiner, who images the hard drive on the server and preserves the networking equipment for further examination. Here, very little if anything has been lost in acquisition.

2. Your client is alleged to have committed a crime at work. You subpoena the client’s email, both incoming and outgoing, from the employer. They return a CD-ROM to you containing several thousand individual PDF files, representing email. You demand a CD-ROM containing the original file format and all the original email. You make a working copy of the CD of native files and keep the original untouched. From the working copy, you load the native files into the original email application and review them. Here, you demanded that the employer provide you native files, which included all the email metadata, so that you could review it. What they provided you was saved in the original for later use as evidence, without being disturbed.

3. You are interviewing a witness in the field, and she claims to have received recent text messages which might benefit your client. She offers to show them to you on her phone. She, of course, will not release her phone to you as evidence or allow you to copy it. You ask her to show you the first text message. As she does, you photograph the phone’s screen, and note the date and time. You then ask her to forward that and all subsequent text messages to your office email. Once received, you copy the original emails to separate media and review them from the backup. You save the original photograph, and append it to an investigation report, explaining the details of how the text messages were originally encountered and provided to you.

4. Your client’s mother has been saving his computer data since his arrest, thinking that someone might be interested in it. Now she wants to sell it to help pay for his mounting commissary bills. It is, in fact, her computer and she does not want to give it to you. You get her to agree to loan you the hard drive, which you deliver to your forensics examiner. He “clones” the hard drive to a new hard drive, which he then installs in the operating system of her machine, and keeps the original. From the forensic image, he recovers whatever files are of interest to you. You have preserved the original evidence, and provided her with a new hard drive with the identical contents, which will work in the original machine.

As the examples above demonstrate, the acquisition of electronic evidence and the means necessary to acquire and preserve it are complex and highly variable. In some cases, the sheer amount of electronic evidence and the need to review it is staggering, and will require careful planning and specialized review methods. What is most important to you as an investigator is to seek and keep the original electronic evidence whenever possible, and acquire copies or next generation versions of electronic evidence when the original is not available to you, in a manner that is defensible in court.

Subpoenaing Data

One special case of electronic evidence acquisition that should be mentioned is getting it through a subpoena. For many years the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure have recognized the need for the defense to subpoena data. State court subpoenas are recognized to be available for the same purpose.

When issuing a subpoena for data, it is critical to bear in mind:

1. You need to understand as much as you can about the original format of the data you are subpoenaing, and craft your subpoena to describe that format in detail (you are seeking the original or a faithful copy).

2. You need to specify in your subpoena in as much detail as possible the format in which the data will be returned to you.

3. When necessary, the party should also provide a document describing how the data was originally kept in their system of records, what was done to locate and isolate it, and what if anything was done to format it for delivery. The document should also attest to its accuracy.

For example, if you served a subpoena to a hospital for radiological images for a particular patient, and the medical records department sent high-resolution color printouts of all the radiological images, this would be an inadequate response to the subpoena. If you fail to specify the original electronic format, common now to all medical imaging, the expert you hire to review the images would be missing much. Medical images are now commonly made and stored in DICOM format, which not only has the pixels containing the image, but also reports numerous pieces of data about the patient and the medical exam itself. They can also be commonly reviewed and adjusted for review (zoom, contrast, etc.) by a medical expert with standard imaging software.

If you subpoenaed data from a database and it was returned to you in an Excel table, this may very well capture the data in the database but not fully explain what it is for. You would also need to specify in your subpoena what each column or field in the table represents, and how these particular values were isolated from the larger collection of data. Otherwise, an expert reviewing the data may be missing something critical.

Both attorneys and investigators, as well as responding parties, are still grappling with how to specify evidence and to fill the duty to provide it in the electronic realm. The more you can educate yourself about the evidence you’re seeking to subpoena, the better.

Acquiring Web-based Evidence

With the proliferation of information on the web, the investigator needs to preserve what has been found online in some format for review by the attorney in court. This could range from the webpage of a witness to an article, or part of a company’s website, or simply a portion of an individual webpage. Depending on what is needed, several methods exist, varying in complexity or relative ease:

1. The simplest is to simply print out a webpage to your printer and put it in your file. Sometimes, this is the quickest and still very effective way to capture what you viewed on the web, often because it contains a date stamped letter.

2. Another simple way is a screenshot, which can be pasted anywhere. Far more useful are screen capture tools such as FastStone Capture, or Snagit. These small applications have useful features such as annotation, capturing more than what is visible in the window, or outputting the contents of the capture to different formats other than graphic.

3. The webpage could be printed to a PDF file, in the same manner as being sent to a printer. The paid versions of Acrobat all install a printer driver that converts any print job to a PDF automatically. This can be very useful, again because it preserves the timestamp but also allows you to use the PDS to be emailed, shown in court, etc. Cute PDF Writer is a free tool that simply has the printer driver, and works just fine for this purpose.

4. Adobe’s Acrobat Professional has a built-in module which allows the user to capture several interconnected webpages, such as a small website, to a single PDF, containing bookmarks for each page interlinked. In essence, it follows the links on the webpage you specify, digs at the contents of those links, and prints pages to PDF. The result is a PDF representation of several levels of the website. Although this is a useful capture tool, and good for smaller amounts of data, it can become a little tricky viewing the resulting PDF anywhere other than on screen.

5. Finally, “off-line browsers” or website capture tools exist. One good free tool that accomplishes this is HTTrack. (http://www.httrack.com/). This tool recreates the website’s pages on your local hard drive and preserves the internal links between pages. It can confine its capture to just one Internet domain, or follow links out as many levels as you choose.

It should be noted that the now very popular media format of flash .flv video (YouTube, etc.) is not commonly saved through these methods. There are multiple flash video “rippers” and saving tools which you would need to employ separately to preserve the video media found on the site.

Capturing information from a webpage is often a simple and inexpensive things to do. In many legal cases it becomes the “picture that is worth thousand words.”

V. Digital Courtroom Presentation

In some ways, but perhaps not in the most basic ways, computers have transformed presentation in the courtroom. Foam core is almost a thing of the past, but not quite. Most modern courtrooms these days are equipped with some in-house system that allows the attorneys to present information through the use of a computer, either that of the legal teams, or a preconfigured computer provided by the court.

Digital presentation of evidence has obvious advantages:

  • It can be made large enough for the jury and witness to see at the same time.
  • It can handle multiple media types in addition to documents.
  • It works out much of the logistics of shuffling paper in advance, so that courtroom presentations are more efficient.
  • It provides for relatively low-cost tools that allow advocates to emphasize the evidence through compelling visual means.
  • It provides a visual way to capture the judge’s and jury’s attention in a way that traditional document-based question and answer somehow fails to do.
  • Digital presentations can be structured and restructured relatively quickly, and are easily reproducible for multiple parties.

Of course, all of these advantages come at a high price—namely, the price of preparation. I will not treat the topic of digital presentation with any rigor here, as it is a field unto itself. Moreover, often the investigator is not called upon to assist the attorney with in-court presentation. However, to the extent that an investigator’s own testimony may be the subject of a digital courtroom presentation, a few things are worth noting.

Several tools, from the cheapest to the more expensive, are available for courtroom presentation. The standard software programs are TrialDirector and Sanction. They are essentially souped-up versions of PowerPoint, which are made for legal professionals. They provide for robust presentation tools, such as transcript callouts, audio and transcript matching, on-the-fly exhibit creation, real-time highlighting by witnesses and attorneys, and so on. The license fees are relatively high, though not ridiculous for well-developed software. An investment in these tools should only be made by an investigator or paralegal who is committed to learning how to use them effectively.

Much more common is the ever present Microsoft PowerPoint. Despite recent lapses in favor from many regarding PowerPoint, it is still a very useful tool for law presentations. A collection of slides based on document images can be put together relatively quickly. Accompanying text is added in the same manner as familiar word processing tools. Formatting can be kept relatively simple, or made more sophisticated. Audio and video can be added in. Finally, it is a tool that any owner of Microsoft Office already has, and is probably the most teachable to an attorney. With enough attention and learning, PowerPoint can be an incredibly effective tool in the courtroom.

If organized in correct sequence with bookmarks, Acrobat can be a very easy-to-use presentation tool. Any PDF file can be presented in full screen mode. I have seen defense teams manage all their exhibits in court solely with Acrobat. However, it lacks some of the more useful presentation features of presentation software. Still, the paid version of Acrobat has a presentation format built-in, and it appears to be part of the software that Adobe is paying more attention to. Enlarging parts of image files, inserting highlights, annotations, screen splits, etc. are all built-in presentation functions within Adobe Acrobat.

Finally, and not to be overlooked—showing a piece of evidence in its native application (or simply preserving it as an image to PDF) can be a very useful way to show evidence. No special software is needed, and relatively little planning is needed for a small collection of exhibits. If your case or examination boils down to a few pages and your theory is less is more, a lot can be done with the simplest of tools.

Perhaps the most important aspect of courtroom presentation is this: It does not work without serious preparation. One should never attempt any level of electronic presentation in the courtroom without knowing exactly how the courtroom software and system works. Schedule time in advance to review it with the courtroom personnel. Test your system on it first. Then, be there early. Also, have some back-up capability should the system fail. It is an axiom of courtroom presentation that some technology will be buggy just when you need it. Know this in advance.

As important as knowing your equipment is having your presentation completely prepared ahead of time, rehearsed and reviewed multiple times, and having the attorney commit it to memory. I have seen both successful and unsuccessful courtroom presentations, and by far the biggest difference is the level of preparedness of the lawyer and the defense team. Even the most compelling or interesting evidence will fall flat if sufficient preparation has not been done to work out the kinks. Jurors will pay attention more to the mistakes and the glitches rather than the evidence itself, and you will be fighting an uphill battle in the best of cases.

Conclusion

If I am lucky, I have given the reader a flavor of some of the issues surrounding the use of computers and computer evidence in criminal defense investigation. I hope to have left you with the impression that this is a highly fluid and ever-changing field, but one anchored by a few core principles that should help guide the way regardless of changes in technology.

One of the greatest challenges facing the defense investigator is not only the technical operation of computers and computer-based equipment, but the unyielding proliferation of data on the Internet and in our cases.

The legal investigator’s role with computer evidence ranges from generating leads about witnesses to formulating strategy about challenging the state’s computer evidence. Additionally, you should be prepared to testify in a responsible manner about computer-based evidence. Ideally, this chapter has reassured you that when your expertise is limited, resources are available with the answers you seek.

Finally, recognize that there are no “computer cases” and no “computer investigations.” There are criminal defense cases with computer evidence, and there are witnesses who need to testify about it.

Understanding where computer evidence and computer investigative techniques fit in will allow you to leverage the unique character of computer and digitally based information, and understand its relation to your client’s case as substantive evidence – either of guilt or of reasonable doubt.

Appendix C

Interviewing the Client About Computers

One goal of interviewing your client is to assess his or her relative sophistication as a computer user. What follows is an outline of some areas to ask about. It does not represent all possible computer scenarios or areas of inquiry. Rather, it is to be thought of as a starting point for an in-depth discussion with your client about the possible computer evidence in your case.

If possible, have your expert with you and your attorney in the client conference about the computer.

1) How was computer seized? Police have warrant? Consent to search computer? Restrictions on the scope of consent?

2) Where was the computer? Home, work, educational setting? Who owns the computer? Length of ownership. Manner of purchase.

3) What is the computer setup?

A) Brand of computer, type (laptop, desktop). Size of hard drive. Multiple drives? Operating system & version. Modifications to system: Additional drives, additional memory, partitioning drives, wiping or reformatting drives, installing additional operating system, any customization.

B) Is computer “stand alone” or networked? If networked, what network system software, what network configuration? What system networked to? Wireless networking in the home? Manner of access to network. Who has network access rights, and administrator rights?

C) Peripherals: types, brands and models of hardware associated with the machine: External hard drives, modems, printers, wireless routers, CD/DVD burners, cameras, phones, PDA’s, monitors, stereos, or televisions. Almost all require drivers.

D) Internet Service Provider (ISP), type of Internet service (Dial-up, Cable, or DSL)? How long service established? Prior ISP’s? Name of the person who has the Internet account?

E) Applications Installed:

With what applications did the computer come installed? What software did the client or others install? How was it obtained? Freeware, shareware, or licensed? Word processors, browsers: Internet Explorer, Firefox, Safari, Opera, Netscape, MSN, AOL Browser and software? Email clients. Instant messaging / chat software (ICQ, mIRC, AIM, Yahoo IM, Trillium). Newsgroup viewing / management software. Webpage builders, Webpage rippers. Smartphone (iPhone, Windows Mobile, Android?) PDA software (Palm, HP). Address book, calendaring, scheduling. Security / firewall software, antivirus, disk utilities. Password insertion software. Disk cleaners / “wipers”. Scripting software: PERL, SQL, UNIX. Spreadsheet, database software. Specialized software: taxes, compression/zip utilities, pop-up blockers / adware, anti-spam, filtering, banking. Media software: CD/DVD burners, MP3 / music management, peer-to-peer (P2P) clients (Kazzaa, Morpheus, or Blubster). Media Players: RealPlayer, Windows Media Player, Flash. Photo printing software, graphics, Photoshop. Games.

4) Access and Accounts:

A) All email addresses associated to client. All email addresses associated with any user of the seized computer.

B) All Internet accounts, past and present.

C) All logins to machine, network logins.

D) All passwords.

E) Screen names/monikers, chat identifiers.

F) Membership to groups, bulletin boards, message services, regular postings on sites.

5) Typical Uses / Patterns of Use:

A) Geographic location of computer. Always in the same place? Computer always on?

B) What is the computer used for? How often? Kinds of activities? Recreation only? Any employment use?

C) Any experiences being hacked, receiving viruses, Trojan horses, strange replies to emails client never sent, or unwanted software installations?

D) Names, email addresses, monikers, information of people client communicates with by computer.

E) Dial-in or remote access services? Other servers: Monetary transactions, online banking, bill pay, employment computers, server based mail/photos, e.g., Hotmail.

F) Free emails, chats, photo, newsgroups, downloads. File sharing – music, video?

G) Favorite web sites – Bookmark or Favorites lists?

H) Web searches, commonly used web pages and services.

I) Client or any user of machine rent server space, or have a domain or web page hosted by an ISP? A blog, or online social networking page?

J) People finding or address look-ups associated with charges?

K) Any computer activity automated? (Email check, newsgroup downloads, system updates.)

L) Any remote access pathways to client’s machine? Any remote pathways to other computers?

M) History of computer use - how started? Who taught? How sophisticated does the client see him or herself as a computer user? Any formal computer training? Work related computer training or use?

N) Who are the other users on the network and how do they access it? If so, how? Were separate logins required?

6) Recall of specific events underlying charge. Client’s explanation of events or of computer evidence. Does client claim unknowing possession of computer files, or automated processes governing computer? If so, when did client notice the activity and how? Are there others associated with the client’s computer use, and do they have details to contact them, such as email addresses or online screen names?

7) Recall of search: What, when, and where of computer when the police first entered. What did they see happen? How did law enforcement handle the machine? What did they ask client about computer contents and computer search? What did they inform client they were looking for? Did they inform the client what they would do with seized computer? Any assistance by client to law enforcement in searching for files? Did they grant rights to law enforcement for access on-line accounts?

Appendix D

Investigating the Computer Investigation

Below are some general areas of inquiry when trying to uncover reasonable doubt and error in law enforcement’s computer investigation. The questions are meant to suggest thought and examination about particular areas. The list is, of course, not exhaustive.

A. THE SEARCH WARRANT AFFIDAVIT

1) Read it closely. Read it twice.

2) Highlight any computer term you do not understand. Highlight any term you think you understand but may only have a cursory knowledge of. Research the terms and the underlying process they describe. Does the affidavit explain the process accurately, and completely?

3) Examine all of the “boilerplate” language. Often contains a computer “definitions” section. Are the definitions correct? Are they outdated? If asked, could the affiant explain what they mean? Are they characterizing the computer process correctly to the court, are they oversimplifying it? The boilerplate language could be wrong, or wildly inapplicable if the agent has not reviewed it in a while.

4) Examine the explanation of what will be done with the computer once seized. Research whether these are reasonable. Was it necessary in this case to seize the entire machine? Was it necessary to seize more than one machine?

5) Often contains assumptions about computer behavior which could be subject to challenge: e.g. Your client is a pedophile, and pedophiles never delete pornography from computers. What is the empirical basis for such assertions?

6) Establish the timeline. Are there reasonable explanations for lapses in time between investigation and applications for the warrant? A computer’s makeup changes every day. Are there aspects of the timing of events which do not make sense, or reveal something the agent is leaving out of the affidavit?

7) Pay particular attention to reliance on computer investigation and expertise of others. This may give you a map to additional discovery the government won’t produce initially.

8) What investigation did the affiant not do, particularly in the area of corroboration. Were more traditional forms of investigation employed, other than verifying names and addresses? Was any investigation done on the source of the initial information? Was the initial information wrongly presumed to be reliable?

B. TRAINING

Law enforcement computer examiners are cops first and computer professionals second. They are not usually true experts, although they may have been qualified as such in previous cases. The manner and level of training varies widely among members of state and federal law enforcement.

1) How many “computer investigations” have they participated in? Even a hundred computer investigations may have amounted to routine deployment of Encase, without much in-depth technical examination. The number one observation from Encase Customer Support is that the majority of the questions sent to them are by officers who have had no formal training in the use of the software.

2) How long has the affiant been doing computer related investigations? When, in relation to your case, did they receive computer forensic training?

3) What is their formal training? It is typically listed in the affidavit. The agent or officer may be very forthcoming about it when questioned by your investigator.

4) Sources of Training Information:

Request training history with Oregon Department of Public Safety Standards & Training. (DPSST). It is a public record. Request a curriculum vitae, either in discovery, or informally. There is cross-training and cross-staffing between members of the corporate computer security community and law enforcement. Informal seminars / workshops. e,g., Computer Related Investigations

Management & Education (C.R.I.M.E.); or Computer Technology Investigators

Northwest (CTIN). Interagency training. Former Hillsboro Police Department High Tech Team. Encase or SafeBack in-house training programs: one or two day seminars.

Forensic software certification? Typically signals more comprehensive training.

IACIS: International Association of Computer Investigative Specialists. Open to law enforcement only. Certified Electronic Evidence Collection Specialist & Certified Forensic Computer Examiner.

Federal Agency training: Federal Law Enforcement Training Center (FLETC) in Glynco, Georgia. Offers several training courses to state and federal law enforcement. The FBI trains their own agents. They have also developed their own forensic software.

New Portland FBI Regional Computer Forensics Laboratory (RCFCL) and training center. One of 10 in the nation.

All of the training sources above can be subject to public records requests or subpoena for course materials and individual officer’s test performance. Seek out other cases the officer or agent may have investigated or testified in. The community of forensics examiners in Portland is small. Take advantage of email lists, public defender experience, and word-of-mouth. Your hired computer forensics examiner likely knows about the officer or agent. If an agent has extensive training, your point is stronger when they fail to do certain things. When they have less training, they can more easily be depicted as novices.

C. SEIZURE AND POST-SEIZURE INVESTIGATION

1) Did they process the evidence correctly? Did they boot the computer? This is a beginner’s failure. Booting the computer alters the hard drive. If the computer was on, how did they shut it off?

Did they do any on-site viewing of the computer? If so, how? Did they ask the suspect to assist in “looking around” the computer? Procedures exist to preview a computer on site safely.

2) Did they seize everything they need? Sometimes peripherals, external drives, contain relevant evidence. Particularly in a network setting, did they seize the networking equipment? Did they diagram any of the network connections? They did inquire of the suspects what the network configurations were, or how the network was set up? What was left behind?

3) Did they properly document the conditions under which the hard drive was found, and removed? The make, model, serial number, and operating system of the machine from which the drive was removed? Location of the computer in the building, and the condition of the machine? If more than one hard drive seized, did they adequately document the identity of the drive copies correlating to the originals? Is the chain of evidence clean and preserved? Did they document the seizure with photographs?

4) Did they enquire of anyone at the place of search about computer usage by the suspect, or of any other people who had access to the machine?

5) Did they seize original computer material, and return any of it back to the suspect, or to other people, after having mirrored the hard drives?

6) Did they use clean media to store the copy of the drive, or did they recycle disks from an old case?

7) What software did they use to examine the drive? Have they been formally trained in its operation?

8) Were their any technical glitches or mistakes in acquiring the drive copy that necessitated them doing it again? If so, is the error documented?

9) Did they use more than one type of software to obtain the imaged copy of the drive: cross-validation. If not, can they articulate the process by which error free acquisition was verified?

10) In what form are the results of examination reported: The original Encase reports? Summaries of found files? Printouts of file lists? Printouts of images? Screen captures? CD-Rom? Narrative police reporting? Copy and pasting “search strings” into a present browser, or visiting current versions of websites identified on the computer. In most cases, only the partial products of the examination are provided to the defense, and in many instances, the prosecutor.

11) Hard drives are large. In almost every case a complete computer examination is not performed or documented, or “extraneous” findings are not reported. In many cases, the examination was done only until sufficient proof of the offense was believed to have be found. Additional searching by your forensics examiner could unearth mitigating or inculpatory evidence.

12) Have your computer forensics examiner review the search warrant affidavit, search warrant execution reports, and any forensic reports or other evidence regarding the computer.

Your best questions will come from an intelligent dialogue between the two of you, matching legal issues with forensic concerns.
