Investigators Manual

Public Records and the Freedom of Information Act

Rhonda Coats and Lynette Lazenby

2010 edition

Public Records

Public records can be evidence or doorways to finding other information. They are used extensively to locate people and to track a person’s assets and activities through contact with public agencies. Brainstorming ways that people interact with government in order to pursue their paper trails is a constant process for investigators. Public records ordinarily do not require a subpoena.

Definition of a Public Record

Records can take many forms. The definition depends on each state’s law. Some states are slow to include electronic versions and specify just older forms, while others acknowledge electronic only if it’s also “written.” Oregon takes an inclusive view concerning any information created or used by a public body, except by the state legislature, staff and committees. They have their own disclosure rules. The term “writing” includes, but is not limited to, maps, faxes, electronic recordings, computer tape, microfiche, photographs, tape, films, video tape, disks and email.

Databases

Records are massaged, sold or relayed into remotely removed versions. Nuggets can be mined by marketers and information brokers who resell them again to businesses, attorneys, investigators and law enforcement agencies. Public and commercial databases are heavily used as platforms to launch further action. The more removed the database is from the source, the more cautions apply. Most databases seldom offer complete or up-to-the-minute information, with the exception of some judicial networks or other government services that are now indexed on the World Wide Web.

When accessing commercial or government databases, it’s important to know how current the files are and how far back they date, as well as the supplier. The dates can differ per record and per agency. Good providers answer these questions or offer codes or bulletins, though commercial providers may bury those “minor details” deep inside their system. Public agencies are usually more upfront in cautioning the limitations. This chapter is concerned more with accessing original individual records, their copies, certified copies, and compilations.

Originals, Copies and True Copies

Original records, not the databases, are more often the smoking guns. But few can be released by law. Copies are more likely to be actual evidence. They provide more content or clues than you will usually find on databases. They can have full text with notations, date-received stamps, cross-indexing, forgotten sticky notes and other fascinating additions that enhance the paper trail. They are carefully archived. They are protected by laws from being altered or destroyed for certain periods of time. They are often microfilmed, photographed or electronically stored to where your “original” may be only a copy of a photograph but that is often considered close enough. Actual originals often cannot be removed without a court order. The public is allowed to view originals, with some exceptions, and can request only copies. Remember, records are not just documents. They may be original tape or video recordings or other types of records.

Certified Copies

Courts accept certified copies for most purposes. Not all records must be certified. It is usually up to the requester. Attorneys often like to be asked if they want that extra step taken before they are charged for the cost and time. If you ask for a public record, you are usually asked if you want it certified. If you are not asked, and forget, you have to go back and ask all over again. Certification requirements are in the state statutes. Fees are set by law, rule, ordinance, policy or practice. The Attorney General’s manual has a model certification format with room for a seal.

Each State Has Its Own Brand of Sunshine

The definitions and steps to request public records from all public entities, except federal agencies, differ in each state. Each state has its own version of a public records law, sometimes called a sunshine law. Some states incorporate the federal Freedom of Information Act in the name of their records law, which adds to the confusion between state laws and the nationwide FOIA. Attempts to suggest uniform public records laws for all states face uphill battles from special interest groups and freedom advocates who say the variety encourages new approaches and easier amending. Some states have established separate agencies to deal with public records issues. Others send petitions to district attorneys, the Attorney General, the secretary of state, the Governor’s office or other agencies where potential conflicts of interest are apparent.

You do not use the FOIA in Oregon unless you are requesting a record from a federal agency. Though it is slang to “Foy-yah” a request from any government, using that term can lead to misunderstandings and wasted time. A memory trigger is to think of the “F” for federal. The central premise that public records are “for you” to see or copy is the same in most states, but the federal act does not apply to local, city, county or state records unless copies are in a federal source. The 10-day clock, for instance, does not apply in Oregon for non-federal records. More on the FOIA at the end of this chapter.

Exemptions to the State Public Records Law

“Every person has a right to inspect any public record of a public body in this state, except as otherwise expressly provided by ORS 192.501 to 192.505.”

Oregon’s sunshine law started out with this statement in 1973 and it still does. The devils are in the details. It is the same with other states. The loopholes keep expanding as more exceptions and categories of exclusions are added. Agencies, industries and other special interest groups convince lawmakers to close more records from the public every two years. More than 300 types of “closed doors” now exist. Investigators outside law enforcement or government agencies continue to be cut off from public records access when new laws are passed to protect personal privacy. They are seldom named in closure exceptions. This is usually because they are not watching or they have not generated a lobby to explain or demand their need to access records outside of discovery and subpoena tools.

Efforts to include Oregon licensed investigators’ access to DMV and other vital records succeeded in the late ’90s. Members in a statewide association of legal investigators successfully lobbied to have the words “licensed investigator” plugged into the list of the those privileged to access motor vehicle records as allowed under the federal act that went into effect in 1997. The group concurrently lobbied to enact statewide licensing that same year and changed their nonprofit’s name to the Oregon Association of Licensed Investigators (OALI).

Driver and Motor Vehicle Records

Motor vehicle records get high-volume use, scrutiny and blame for abuse. Many states are contracting to sell their records in bulk to commercial database providers and no one else. Some states, such as Pennsylvania, shut off this supply after one illegal use was tracked back to a provider’s lack of tight controls on their clients. Even if bulk sales narrow or stop it is usually still possible to get your own account or find a local retriever.

Oregon DMV account holders can choose to call a real person during office hours to answer requests over the phone. Information from the website states the following:

IVR/DAVE is an automated record inquiry system available only to established Record Inquiry Account Holders.  If approved for an account, you are pre-qualified to receive record information. Account holders have access to DMV's Interactive Voice Response System (IVR/DAVE). For more information refer to the IVR/DAVE User Guide.
 
Advantages for IVR/DAVE users include 24-hour access to certain driver and vehicle record information, and overnight turnaround time for prints ordered through the system.  Through IVR/DAVE, you have the following six menu choices:

  5. Order a three-year employment driving record.
  6. Order a vehicle record print.

Application information is on the Department of Transportation DMV web site.

Oregon’s version of the national Drivers Privacy and Protection Act (DPPA), like others, requires all account holders and those who buy from resellers to log each record request by case reference number, client or other tracking clue. This is supposed to make it easier to double-back should information be inappropriately released. If a question comes up, investigators who do not keep a DMV-dedicated log might have to search all their case files, those of their operatives and anyone else who has called on their business account. Keeping a separate log also serves as billing information and prevents “sticker shock” when the monthly invoices arrive. It’s easy to forget to log from the field, but account privileges can be pulled if a failure to comply is determined.

The DPPA has also made it more difficult to obtain personal information, such as home addresses, from police and accident reports. As the clamor for more privacy increases, it is battled only by volunteer efforts and investigators with scant funding locally and nationwide.

The loudest advocates for open records continue to be journalists. They provide some of the best resources on how to dig. Press organizations spearhead access efforts in Oregon and lead charges across the nation, often in conjunction with the state bar organizations or other professional groups, but sometimes against them. The press increasingly takes the position that media should not be given special access to what is not open to the public. So journalists, like others, can be shut out of unconditional closures that prohibit any release of a record without individual consent, such as a home address, student records, or juvenile records.

Most exemptions to the Oregon records law are categorized as conditional where agencies have some discretion to release information. The custodian is required to evaluate whether the public interest is better served by disclosure than not. This can hinge on the identity of the requester, although giving the identity is not required under the law. The public interest flag is most effectively waved by the press, police and other government agencies. It can be hoisted by private parties but attempts are rarely successful.

In addition, someone who is party to litigation or who has filed a tort claim with a state agency is required to notify the Attorney General when they make a records request of that agency through the Public Records Law instead of discovery tools. (The person’s attorney may request records from that agency without getting the consent of the agency’s legal counsel, but the attorney could violate the ethical code by asking questions about what the records mean, according to a 1996 opinion of the Oregon State Bar.)

The custodian is allowed to deny records to anyone “reasonably likely” to become party to expected litigation if the custodian shows it is likely to occur, except for those requests for administrative hearings. Once the litigation is concluded, the records are supposedly available.

Investigation Records

Criminal Investigations

Police and other government agencies generally can withhold records relating to a criminal investigation as long as it is “ongoing.” Officer’s logs, incident reports and arrest information are public record, but police still have discretion to decide a “clear need” to withhold or redact. Arrest information usually released to the public via the media includes the suspect’s name, age, home, job and marital status; mugshot, charges, terms for release, name and other information of victim or reporting party; place, time and circumstances of arrest; arresting agency/officer/aiding agencies; the investigating agency/officer, and anything that might help catch others involved or those who got away. Each agency interprets “clear need” to suit their own purposes and personnel because state law is not more specific.

On the Job

Civil rights investigations of discrimination in the workplace are closed except for the complaint itself. They are available at the Bureau of Labor and Industries. Unfair labor practice investigatory records can be withheld except for the complaints at both the Employment Relations Board and the Employees Benefit Board. Workplace safety investigation records are closed except for the original complaint filing and status. Professional and trade licensing and regulatory boards vary according to which parts of complaints, investigatory and other records can be released. Application records are usually protected while continuing education logs are often open. Other types of investigations are handled by a variety of government agencies with their own statutes and administrative rules.

Records Retention

Records in state, city, county and other political subdivisions must follow retention procedures and minimum time limits set by the state archivist. An investigator can find these rules useful as a prompt for brainstorming the specific or range of records that may show someone’s dealings with various agencies. They outline common titles of records and reports routinely kept by similar entities. They show the range of divisions with an agency and the variety of public bodies covered by the record retention schedules. State, county and municipal schedules are listed separately and further defined by department such as finance or public works. If you’ve hit a dead end, a review of these rules can bring new ideas. They are accessed in the Oregon Administrative Rules for the Secretary of State’s Office, Archives Division. See resource list at the end of the chapter.

Altering a Record Is a Crime

It is a crime to knowingly destroy, conceal, remove or falsely alter a public record.

How to Request a Public Record

• Know where to ask. Determine which agency is the originator or custodian. While most public bodies will allow inspection and copying of records as allowed under law, they can refer you to the originator of the record if it is another public agency. Knowing the jurisdiction or boundaries of the agency is useful. If you want an airport’s records, you might need to know if it is a private, municipal, county or consortium airport. Finding out how it is managed may be helpful later to interpret the significance of certain actions or lack of them.

• Get the right “addy.” Obtain the correct name, address, phone, fax, email and website address of the agency thought to keep the record.

• Get the name of the records unit or gatekeeper. If it’s not on the website, call and ask. The keeper may be an executive director, manager, a division head or clerk. You may not need a person’s name, but it can help later.

• Check the agency’s website for instructions. Many public bodies post general questions and answers for the public on the web.

• Call first and ask for the record. Depending on the delicacy of your need for information and other factors, this may or may not be a step you want to take first. Sometimes an informal request tips off the keeper to destroy or alter the records, which is illegal but many famous cases have shown it occurs.

• Ask what format the original is in and how it is released. Is it on paper, tape, disk or other format? You could be told the type of data you seek “does not exist” because it would have to be compiled. This may require a minor instruction to a computer so always ask what it would take to compile it. If you are told the agency does not have the record, ask who does. Ask for a guess. It might be a more informed guess than yours. Things change. Do not assume it does not exist because they do not have it. If it has been “sent to archives,” ask if that is an in-house archives or the county or state archives. Ask where else the record could be. Someone may be able to give useful information over the phone or they may tell you to put your request in writing. Most public bodies accept an informal letter or email as a written request, but you may not need to go to the effort of writing a formal request if an agency volunteers to send the information or has already published it elsewhere. Many files are on the web. The state library in Salem as well as the state archives and some university libraries are designated repositories for government records. The University of Oregon in Eugene has an Oregon Collection, for instance, that can often save a trip farther north.

• Ask about payment. Some agencies require payment before the search is undertaken. Others let you pay when it is concluded. Some do not accept anything but exact change at the counter as they are not large enough to keep enough cash on hand for a variety of currencies. Showing up unprepared could delay your request another day, and could cause you to repull a file and remark all the pages you want to copy. Most places let you bring your own copier, but you can still get charged for staff time to find the records and prepare them for inspection.

• Ask to see the record first. This is a step that many people forget in this age of faxed requests and express mail. It can actually save time and reduce the copies needed when it is possible to visit the agency in person. Oregon grants the right to see the original records in most cases; then you can decide which ones to copy. You may only need to take notes on an entire file and copy just the pertinent pages. This is particularly handy when there are long waits to get copies. The agency may ask that you make an appointment to inspect records, and staff may stall to a “reasonable” time which could be weeks if they are understaffed. Some offices have tables or reader stations where you can peruse in view of someone who is working, if not watching you personally. Others may feel it is necessary to dedicate an employee to make sure you don’t take or alter the records. Some might charge you for this time. Don’t take it as a personal affront to your integrity or reputation, or attribute it to their fears you’ll find something.

• Be aware of watchful eyes. Observers often report back to officials or parties what pages were copied, notes taken or how much time was spent. Overt or covert cameras are not unlikely in some places. Many news reporters have found angry messages when they returned to their office, wanting to know why the agency or individual was being scrutinized or when the story will run. Others are often curious to know what you are looking at and could look over your shoulder or at the scrap-paper notes you tossed in the trash. Some investigators get tailed by anyone who suspects what they are working on would interest a friend or party. While you have the right to inspect records on site you have to wait for them to be made available. You usually cannot rummage through file cabinets or other storage areas—though this is sometimes allowed, with obvious problems for everyone. Sometimes you are told to leave the file on a counter when you are done. You can usually ask to hand it directly to someone so that it is not left sitting out. They might remember you left it behind if it comes up missing later.

• Make a note of pages you wish to copy and check your copies. It’s common to just attach paperclips to pages you want copied and to hand over the file for that purpose. However, when you ask for copies the original records are usually not returned for your further perusal. If you have not made a note of the pages by number, or how many per section or topic, you may not be able to make sure they are all there. Even if the quantity is the same you may not realize until later whether harried clerks copied the correct pages or both sides correctly. If you have to leave the marked file in a batch where it sits around or gets moved a lot, the clips could fall off. If your copies are not what you wanted, you might have to submit another formal request on the spot, when you get back to the office or when they arrive by mail. Making notes can be time consuming, even for quick job. Having them, however, can assist the clerk to do it by mail and prevent your having to travel back to another city. Copying entire files is a good idea if they are small and the cost is not a problem. Court record retrievers go after specific documents in case files that can be relatively standard for most requests or specific to each need. An investigator asking to “check” a case file, pull a file, or copy a file should know for what purpose and what they are looking for, and make sure they get all volumes for a big or protracted case. Clerks often pull one at a time and wait for you to ask for subsequent volumes.

• Redacting may be required by law on copies of records before they can be viewed or copied further. You may be asked to pay for the hourly staff time to do this, or for the agency’s attorney to advise what needs to be redacted. Oregon does not allow the agency to charge for an agency attorney’s time to do the actual redacting, only to review the request and pertinent laws and give advice. Attorney and staff redacting costs are often the biggest hurdle for the public to obtain public records, after access issues and before copy fees.

• If you are told it is not a public record, ask if that is their official response. They may refer you to the statute, which can be helpful, or ask you to put your request in writing, or get someone more knowledgeable who may know differently. Get the name and job title of who denied access and what was said.

• Read “The Manual.” Look at the exemptions from disclosure in the Public Records Law to compare with what you were told. You can get more information on these in the Attorney General’s Public Records and Meetings Manual. This is the bible to have on hand. The manual includes a wealth of interpretive text, letters of advice, opinions, orders, tables and indices that flesh out the statutes. Older orders might be superceded by newer amendments or could still apply. The manual is updated every two years, coming out six months after each legislative session ends. New laws can take effect immediately and are most often flagged in the introduction. One appendix table lists agencies or people covered by statutes, the material affected and where those statutes may be found that are outside the main public records law. These are generally found in the laws affecting the agency’s operation. Checking the manual for your type of request might take anywhere from 5 to 20 minutes or longer. It can go quicker than it sounds, and becomes quicker each time you use the manual. New amendments, portions of the manual and responses to petitions of denials (orders) are also indexed on the Attorney General’s website. See Resource List.

• If it is clear to you that the custodian was right in denying your request, you could stop there and look for another avenue. Or you could skip the legal research entirely, write directly to the agency and wait for their official denial or turnaround. But if you sit and expect the record to arrive when it clearly may not you could be wasting days or weeks instead of looking elsewhere. The Attorney General’s Public Records and Meetings Manual may provide an explanation of your situation and options you could pass on to others.

• Use a template to write a records request letter. Oregon disclosure law does not require a letter but some agencies do, which can be a good idea. It establishes an easier record of the request, the timeline and scope, and the identity and address of the requester. It can be useful later to petition a denial or go to court. Make it easy on yourself and use one of the templates available to fill in the blanks for the name of the record keeper, agency name, address and date. Be as specific as you can regarding the records you seek. The rest is done for you and all you do is print it out, or you can send it electronically without even printing it. An original signature is seldom needed. An electronic signature may be kicked back if the agency is not yet set up to receive the secure ones. The law and templates can be found linked from Open Oregon, a website of the Freedom of Information Coalition at www.open-oregon.com.

• Be specific. Narrow the time period, the names of people and the type of information. Gatekeepers are able to deny or produce less if the wording can be interpreted in other ways. They can cause repeated requests, more charges or produce more volumes than you intended. If you ask for all invoices to Vendor John Jones, they may exclude all for Vendor J. Jones or Vendor 3507, for instance, if they are having a bad hair day or that’s their general attitude or policy on how to respond. Useful phrases can be “those invoices referring to Vendor John Jones by full name, partial name, or ID number or code.” Remember that if you ask too narrowly for “invoices,” you might miss donation receipts, for instance. You might also indicate a cost ceiling of what you are willing to pay. Some people even send blank checks but this is not recommended or desirable.

• Identifying yourself and your purpose. This is tricky for several reasons. Normally it should not be necessary to identify yourself or your purpose in order to inspect or copy records at most public bodies. But the law also closes some records to all but certain categories of requesters. This is the only time an agency really can require your name or purpose, but some do so by practice. If you write a letter the same should apply, but it works better to include your name and address. It is important to realize that every request for a public record becomes one. If you put your home address on a letter it can be disclosed by that agency, even if it is confidential at the state licensing board or elsewhere. Many law enforcement agencies want to know your name and purpose in order to provide the records. The purpose usually can be one general word such as “investigation” or “defense.” If you are unsure ask your attorneys what they prefer. The record clerks receiving your request may be willing to suggest the minimum needed, but may say more is needed than is really necessary. The purpose is not required for most requests.

• Sign, copy and mail your written records request. Enclose a check if appropriate. Some agencies do not want your check enclosed at this stage. Asking for automatic acknowledgement of receipt from the mailing service, whether through electronic or other carriers, can let them know you are serious. Asking the keeper to acknowledge response is generally not appreciated. They are required to respond eventually. It’s better to seek other ways to confirm delivery.

• A cost estimate of the staff time and copy fees may come back instead of the records themselves. You may have to start your “reasonable” clock again with a subsequent request and the money draft. You can narrow your request and specify it not go over a certain dollar figure.

• If your written request is denied, go back and look at the laws that apply. Compare what you research to what the denial letter says. If you are adamant you have a right to see or copy the records, you can informally ask either the district attorney or the Attorney General’s office for their informal opinion over the phone. This may be all it takes to relay back to the gatekeeper. A separate arm of the AG considers these requests, as opposed to the assistant who serves as counsel to the state agencies that might be denying release. They have to pay for legal advice from the AG. You don’t.

• Petitioning for order to release. Requirements for petitioning are outlined in the public records law and in the manual. A model petition letter is available in the manual and on the websites. Petitions for state agency release go to the Attorney General at the Justice Building in Salem. Those for city or county agencies go to the district attorney in the local county. Either must decide within seven working days or it’s an automatic denial of your petition and request. If the record is ordered to be released, the agency has seven working days to comply or take it to court. If your petition is denied, you can also seek judicial review. Either side of state records disputes must file in the Marion County District Court in Salem instead of the county where the custodian or record is located. Local challenges can be filed in the local county court.

The Freedom of Information Act

The Freedom of Information Act of 1974 does not define the term “agency record” but the courts say it includes photographs, computer tapes or databases if they already existed at the time of the request. An agency has “control” over a record if it has the power to dispose of it. An agency does not have to compile or create a record or database under the FOIA. You have the right to obtain this information from these agencies even if they can be collected from another source.

The FOIA does not apply to the federal courts, Congress or the White House staff. It has nine major exemptions to allowing access to records from other federal agencies. These range from national security to oil wells. They include aspects of law enforcement and “private matters” deemed an invasion of personal privacy such as medical records.
