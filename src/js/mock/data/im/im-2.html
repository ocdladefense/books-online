Investigators Manual

Ethics for Criminal Defense Investigators

Wendy Kunkel

2010 edition

Introduction

The interlocking nature of investigator and attorney ethics is especially apparent in the criminal defense team. Both lawyers and investigators are obligated to zealously represent their clients within the bounds of the law. Because a criminal defense investigator, through principles of agency, is considered to be within the attorney-client privilege, an investigator needs to be aware of both investigator and attorney ethical rules. The Code of Investigator Ethics is found in the Oregon Administrative Rules 259-061-0190. This rule is attached as Appendix A. The code for Attorney Ethics is found in the Oregon Rules of Professional Conduct, Rules 1.0 through 8.6. Some of these rules are referred to in this chapter and are attached as Appendix B. Although the code of ethics for investigators may be patterned after the ORPC, that does not give the investigator a license to practice law. Therefore, an investigator should avoid the temptation to provide legal advice to witnesses or anyone else the investigator may have professional cause to contact. The investigator’s role is to provide support, and obtain evidence and information that will be used in the decision-making process for the end result of the case.

Personal and Professional Ethics

Who you are as a person and how you conduct business go hand in hand with the question of ethics in criminal defense. As an investigator, one is entrusted with the job of finding truth in statements, conduct and location of evidence. The statements, and evidence production, need to be obtained in a lawful and honest manner. An investigator should conduct business with diligence and thoroughness, yet should act in a way that is above reproach and within the bounds of the law.

A case that guides the conduct of legal investigators is Gatti,330 Or 517 (2000). In Gatti, the Oregon Supreme Court upheld the Oregon State Bar’s view that a private attorney had acted unethically by intentionally misrepresenting his identity to the employees of a medical records review company. The lawyer had misrepresented himself in order to obtain information from the company. In so doing, the court

rejected the attorney’s defense that his misrepresentations were justifiable because he was engaged in an investigation to seek evidence of fraud and other wrongful conduct. The court expressly ruled that there was no “prosecutorial exception” to either the state bar disciplinary rules or the Oregon statute.

As an agent of the lawyer, an investigator’s conduct needs to reflect the same duty that a lawyer is held to. Misrepresentation to obtain information is a violation of the ethical code. (See ORPC 4.1.) The lawyer an investigator works with can be held accountable by the Oregon State Bar for an investigator’s actions.

Since the emergence of social networking sites, investigators have struggled with the question of using inaccurate information to gain access to MySpace or Facebook accounts. Gattimakes it very clear that misrepresentation is a violation of the bar ethics code.

Under ORPC 4.3, an attorney has a duty to make reasonable efforts to correct any misunderstanding on behalf of a witness. When is an investigator obligated to clear up any misunderstanding of one’s own role in a case? As the agent of the lawyer, an investigator should be truthful and should accurately reflect his or her identity, and, in the opinion of this author, clear up any misunderstanding the witness may have regarding the identity or role in the case.

Clients and Confidentiality of Information

Lawyers are bound by ORPC 1.6 – Confidentiality of Information. As investigators working on a defense team, one is held to the same standard. An investigator often finds his- or herself dealing with clients on a one-on-one basis. After gathering pertinent information about the case, the investigator may be interviewing witnesses in the field. Anything the client has shared previously is protected under the attorney-client privilege and cannot be disclosed to witnesses without the consent of the client. Discretion and confidentiality are expected of an investigator. Information obtained or learned during an investigation should be kept as part of the legal file.

Confidentiality extends to the investigator’s case file. An investigator’s case file should be maintained in a way that promotes security and privacy. Any written report of a witness or client’s statement should not be released without permission from the lawyer representing one’s client. Investigators should also be aware of any restrictions in place when releasing privileged or protected information such as medical records, Department of Motor Vehicle records, People Finder results or discovery protected by court order. Access to many of these programs requires investigators to comply with specific privacy standards. (Editors’ Note: See Jim Strupp’s section on Internet Searching in Chapter 6.)

When working on codefendant cases with other investigators, one can be tempted to “shop talk” about the case. Without a joint defense agreement signed, one needs to avoid the temptation, unless the lawyer involved has agreed to share material.

Witnesses

The role of the defense team in locating and interviewing witnesses and victims most often falls to the investigator. While interviewing victims and witnesses, a criminal defense investigator has certain obligations under the law. When interviewing a victim in an Oregon case, ORS 135.970 (2) states clearly that one needs to identify oneself and give the victim their Victims Rights statement. (See Appendix C.) ORS 135.970 (1) makes it illegal in some cases to provide the criminal defendant the address or telephone number of a victim. In some counties and districts, prosecutors place restrictions on the defense team about sharing discovery with clients. Investigators should be clear with the lawyer they are working with how much of the discovery can be shared.

While interviewing a witness, investigators need to be careful not to advise them that they should decline talking with the prosecutor or to talk only in the presence of defense counsel. That could be construed as a nonlawyer giving legal advice and possibly obstruction of justice. (See ORPC 5.3 and 5.5.) It is not necessary, however, to caution a witness concerning possible self-incrimination or the need for counsel. A

criminal defense investigator may be asked some pretty direct questions by witnesses, clients or victims. As the agent for the lawyer, one should refer all legal questions to the lawyer on the case.

An investigator should not place any witness interviewed in fear of their life or safety. Every precaution should be taken not to intimidate or threaten a witness. Payment or promises should not be provided to a witness for a statement.

Investigators, while interviewing potential witnesses should be aware of the nuances of ORPC 4.2 – Communication with Person Represented By Counsel (see Appendix B). If a witness has a lawyer on a directly related matter or subject, the investigator needs to obtain permission from that witness’s attorney prior to taking their statement. During pre-indictment investigation, an investigator may attempt to interview a codefendant or witness that has not yet obtained counsel. Prior to contacting that person, however, an attempt to assess their representation status should be made by accessing OJIN, Pacer, or jail records. Verification of their lack of representation should be clarified by that witness or defendant. As long as the person does not have a lawyer, an interview can proceed. (Editor’s Note: See Chapter 11, “Interviewing Witnesses,” for more information. For specific information on interviewing teens, see Chapter 15, “Juvenile Delinquency.”)

Conflicts

Both the attorney and investigator ethic rules regulate personal and professional conflicts of interest.See ORPC 1.7 and 1.8 (in Appendix B) and OAR 259-061 (3) (in Appendix A). An investigator should not work for one client when it would jeopardize another client’s case. Loyalty to a client supercedes the acceptance of other work that would adversely affect the original client. One cannot use privileged information gained during the course of a previous client’s investigation to further another client’s case unless a signed waiver is in place.

An investigator needs to be aware of personal feelings, prejudices, political beliefs, animosities, relationships, aspirations and friendships interfering in the pursuit of the investigation. Investigations should be conducted without bias. The investigator’s role is the ultimate search for the truth. Extreme dislike or personal feelings should not interfere with an open-minded evaluation of the case. Both lawyers and investigators need a system in place that keeps track of current and former clients and should be aware of any potential conflicts.

Gathering Evidence

In gathering statements, facts and evidence, an investigator needs to be very aware of the Oregon Criminal Code and a copy should be considered resource material for an investigator. The ethics rule contained in OAR 259-061-0190 (3) states clearly that investigators need to obey all laws. One cannot misrepresent oneself in order to obtain documents. (See Gatti,330 Or 517 (2000).) Because of the nature of the investigation business, inadvertent exposure to criminal laws can be expected. In order to protect oneself, it is important to understand the law. Some of the laws that investigators may inadvertently be exposed to are:

Criminal Trespass, Menacing, Harassment, Telephone Harassment, Intimidation, Fraud, Bribing a Witness,

Hindering Prosecution, Tampering with a Witness, Tampering with Physical Evidence and Obstruction of

Justice.

Taking custody of evidence can be a thorny situation for any investigator. If an ongoing police investigation involving the same subject is being conducted simultaneously, the investigator has a duty not to interfere with law enforcement. Evidence can be gathered to conduct forensic testing. Proper documentation of the evidence collected should be precise. The lawyer on the case should be an active part of the collection decision.

While gathering any evidence on a client’s behalf, an investigator should have a system in place that logs the time in and out for the evidence gathered, a safe place to store the evidence and accurate documentation while gathering the evidence itself.

Conclusion

Criminal defense investigation is a rewarding and challenging career. One of the most important tools an investigator has is their reputation. Criminal defense is unique in that the investigator works hand in hand with the lawyer for a common goal. Communication between the two on an ongoing basis will often eliminate many of the ethical pitfalls one faces in the ongoing battle to eradicate injustice in the world of criminal defense.

Oregon Administrative Rules

DIVISION 61

CODE OF PROFESSIONAL ETHICS – INVESTIGATORS

259-061-0190

Code of Ethical Conduct

All licensed investigators must, at all times, observe the rules and requirements of conduct as follows: (1) Obey all laws in the pursuit of their investigations;

(2) Abide by all provisions of ORS chapter 703 and OAR chapter 259 as they relate to licensed investigators; (3) Never act officiously or permit personal feelings, prejudices, political beliefs, aspirations, animosities or friendships to influence their professional decisions;

(4) Never compromise and shall relentlessly perform their duties in accordance with the law, courteously and appropriately, without fear or favor, malice or ill-will;

(5) Never employ unnecessary or unlawful force or violence;

(6) Maintain each client's confidentiality within the limits of the law; (7) Be accountable and responsible for their actions;

(8) Accept sole responsibility for their individual standard of professional performance and take every reasonable opportunity to enhance and improve their level of knowledge, competence, and professional integrity;

(9) Actively seek and report the truth in the performance of their professional duties; (10) Be above reproach in the financial aspects of their relationships with clients;

(11) Keep promises, fulfill commitments and abide by the spirit of agreements made with their clients as well as the letter of agreements with their clients;

(12) Recognize that the credential of a licensed investigator is a symbol of public faith and will accept it as a public trust, to be held only so long as they are true to the ethics of the investigative profession.

RULE 1.7 CONFLICT OF INTEREST: CURRENT CLIENTS

(a) Except as provided in paragraph (b), a lawyer shall not represent a client if the representation involves a current conflict of interest. A current conflict of interest exists if:

(I) the representation of one client will be directly adverse to another client;

(2) there is a significant risk that the representation of one or more clients will be materially limited by the lawyer's responsibilities to another client, a former client or a third person or by a personal interest of the lawyer; or

(3) the lawyer is related to another lawyer, as parent, child, sibling, spouse or domestic partner, in a matter adverse to a person whom the lawyer knows is represented by the other lawyer in the same matter.

(3) the representation does not obligate the lawyer to contend for something on behalf of one client thatthe lawyer has a duty to oppose on behalf of another client; and

(4) each affected client gives informed consent, confirmed in writing.

TRANSACTIONS WITH PERSONS OTHER THAN CLIENTS

RULE 4.1 TRUTHFULNESS IN STATEMENTS TO OTHERS

In the course of representing a client a lawyer shall not knowingly:

(a) make a false statement of material fact or law to a third person; or

(b) fail to disclose a material fact when disclosure is necessary to avoid assisting an illegal or fraudulent act by a client, unless disclosure is prohibited by Rule 1.6.

Adopted 01/01/05

RULE 4.2 COMMUNICATION WITH PERSON REPRESENTED BY COUNSEL

In representing a client or the lawyer's own interests, a lawyer shall not communicate or cause another to communicate on the subject of the representation with a person the lawyer knows to be represented by a lawyer on that subject unless:

(a) the lawyer has the prior consent of a lawyer representing such other person;

(b) the lawyer is authorized by law or by court order to do so; or

Oregon Rules of Professional Conduct (12/11/09)

(c) a written agreement requires a written notice or demand to be sent to such other person, in which case a copy of such notice or demand shall also be sent to such other person's lawyer.

RULE 4.3 DEALING WITH UNREPRESENTED PERSONS

In dealing on behalf of a client or the lawyer's own interests with a person who is not represented by counsel, a lawyer shall not state or imply that the lawyer is disinterested. When the lawyer knows or reasonably should know that the unrepresented person misunderstands the lawyer's role in the matter, the lawyer shall make reasonable efforts to correct the misunderstanding. The lawyer shall not give legal advice to an unrepresented person, other than the advice to

secure counsel, if the lawyer knows or reasonably should know that the interests of such a person are or have a reasonable possibility of being in conflict with the interests of the client or the lawyer's own interests.

LAW FIRMS AND ASSOCIATIONS

RULE 5.1 RESPONSIBILITIES OF PARTNERS, MANAGERS, AND SUPERVISORY LAWYERS

A lawyer shall be responsible for another lawyer's violation of these Rules of Professional Conduct if:

(a) the lawyer orders or, with knowledge of the specific conduct, ratifies the conduct involved; or( b) the lawyer is a partner or has comparable managerial authority in the law firm in which the other lawyer practices, or has direct supervisory authority over the other lawyer, and knows of the conduct at a time when its consequences can be avoided or mitigated but fails to take reasonable remedial action.

Adopted 01/01/05

RULE 5.2 RESPONSIBILITIES OF A SUBORDINATE LAWYER

(a) A lawyer is bound by the Rules of Professional Conduct notwithstanding that the lawyer acted at the direction of another person.

(b) A subordinate lawyer does not violate the Rules of Professional Conduct if that lawyer acts in accordance with a supervisory lawyer's reasonable resolution of an arguable question of professional duty.

RULE 5.5 UNAUTHORIZED PRACTICE OF LAW; MULTIJURISDICTIONAL PRACTICE

(a) A lawyer shall not practice law in a jurisdiction in violation of the regulation of the legal profession in that jurisdiction, or assist another in doing so.

(b) A lawyer who is not admitted to practice in this jurisdiction shall not:(1) except as authorized by these Rules or other law, establish an office or other systematic and continuous presence in this jurisdiction for the practice oflaw; or

(2) hold out to the public or otherwise represent that the lawyer is admitted to practice law in this jurisdiction.

(c) A lawyer admitted in another United States jurisdiction, and not disbarred or suspended from practice in any jurisdiction, may provide legal services on a temporary basis in this jurisdiction that:

(1) are undertaken in association with a lawyer who is admitted to practice in this jurisdiction and who actively participates in the matter;

(2) are in or reasonably related to a pending or potential proceeding before a tribunal in this or another jurisdiction, if the lawyer, or a person the lawyer is assisting, is authorized by law or order to appear in such proceeding or reasonably expects to be so authorized;

(3) are in or reasonably related to a pending or potential arbitration, mediation, or other alternate dispute resolution proceeding in this or another jurisdiction, if the services arise out of or are reasonably related to the lawyer's practice in a jurisdiction in which the lawyer is admitted to practice and are not services for which the forum requires pro hac vice admission;

(4) are not within paragraphs (c)(2) or (c)(3) and arise out of or are reasonably related to the lawyer's practice in a jurisdiction in which the lawyer is admitted to practice; or

(5) are provided to the lawyer's employer or its organizational affiliates and are not services for which the forum requires pro hac vice admission.

(d) A lawyer admitted in another United States jurisdiction, and not disbarred or suspended from practice in any jurisdiction, may provide legal services in this jurisdiction that are services that the lawyer is authorized to provide by federal law or other law of this jurisdiction.

RULE 6.3 MEMBERSHIP IN LEGAL SERVICES ORGANIZATION

A lawyer may serve as a director, officer or member

of a legal services organization, apart from the law firm in which the lawyer practices, notwithstanding that the organization serves persons having interests adverse to a client of the lawyer. The lawyer shall not knowingly

participate in a decision or action of the organization:

(a) if participating in the decision or action would be incompatible with the lawyer's obligations to a client under Rule 1.7; or

(b) where the decision or action could have a material adverse effect on the representation of a client of the organization whose interests are adverse to a client of the lawyer's.

Adopted 01/01/05

RULE 6.4 LAW REFORM ACTIVITIES AFFECTING CLIENT INTERESTS

A lawyer may serve as a director, officer or member of an organization involved in reform of the law or its administration, notwithstanding that the reform may affect the interest of a client of the lawyer. When the lawyer knows that the interest of a client may be materially benefited by a decision in which the lawyer participates, the lawyer shall disclose that fact but need not identify the client.
