Investigators Manual

The Criminal Defense Attorney–Investigative Team

Tactics for Success

Robert Harris

2010 edition

Introduction

Successful and effective Attorney-Investigator teams share common characteristics.

  • Prompt mutual communication of all facts or actions that effect the case.
  • An understanding of the responsibilities of each person.
  • The ability to openly and honestly communicate and share ideas and theories about the case, the witnesses and the evidence.

Different types of cases call for different levels of interaction between parties and different depths of investigation. And, different attorneys will have preferred methods of communication and their own ideas about things like written reports.

In a murder case, the relationship between parties, the expectations and the depth of the investigation will be vastly different than in a misdemeanor case. Because of these variances, the relationship between attorney and investigator isn’t a static one. It can be as unique as the facts of each case and the personalities involved. You must remain flexible and openly communicate so expectations are met and the client is provided quality representation.

Initiating the Attorney-Investigator Relationship

Once an attorney has retained an investigator, the first thing the investigator should expect is to set up a face-to-face meeting with the attorney. In the best case, the attorney will have provided the investigator with all available police reports, or other relevant evidence, prior to the meeting. The attorney may also have provided a case summary as well as the “theory of the case.” That information may have been provided in the phone call or email that the attorney used in initiating the investigation work. If the attorney didn’t provide the investigator with information prior to the first meeting, the investigator should request it.

Prior to the meeting, the investigator should be familiar with the police reports, have a list of any questions he or she may have, and be prepared to have a substantive conversation with the lawyer about the case and possible avenues for investigation. In addition, the investigator can perform a conflict check at this time to determine if representation of the defendant would create any conflict of interest.

The investigator should have a good sense of their workload before accepting a case. It is important to consider such things as the custody status of the accused or time constraints that may be imposed by the attorney or a court. Where a defendant is in custody, the timelines will be shorter and scheduling will not be as flexible.

If the case is court-appointed by either federal or state court, the attorney should provide an authorization from Public Defense Services Commission to the investigator prior to any investigative work being performed. Beginning work on a case without authorization from PDSC may result in non-payment for that work.

First Meeting of Attorney and Investigator

Depending on the type of case, the first meeting can be as brief as 15 minutes or as long as several hours. At this meeting it’s important for the attorney and investigator to understand and agree on the role the investigator will take, what the attorney will provide, and the respective responsibilities of each.

Police reports should be reviewed and discussed together by the team. Here are some of the issues that could be addressed at this first meeting:

  • Witness location and interviews
  • Site investigation
  • Photos and videos
  • Preparing written reports
  • Preparation of exhibits for presentation to court
  • Assistance with trial and witness preparation (check availabilities)
  • Trial dates, hearing dates, availability for testimony (f these dates are available at this meeting, the investigator should ask the attorney if they need to be reserved)

If you haven’t performed a conflict check you should do so at the first meeting. You need to carefully review all reports, list the possible witnesses, discuss possible witnesses that aren’t listed in the report, and make sure that your representation doesn’t cause a professional conflict of interest.

The attorney should also be prepared to provide copies of releases of information (signed by the client) to the investigator.

At times an attorney may also prepare a written investigation request for the investigator. This is a document the investigator and attorney agree on which sets forth, in memorandum form, what the attorney is requesting the investigator do on a case. The content of this written request is best left to the styles and discretion of the attorney and investigator. Disputes over pretrial discovery sometimes arise, and written communications between the attorney and investigator could be subject to inquiry by the prosecutor and judge. In practice, written requests should be fairly general. “Sensitive matters” should be communicated verbally or in a way that is clearly protected by the attorney-client privilege or as work product. (See the Appendix for a sample letter requesting investigative services.)

Understanding the Role of the Attorney

While the attorney and investigator need to act as a team, ultimately it’s the attorney that is responsible for effectively and zealously representing the client and for the quality of the representation. Attorneys take this very personally, but good attorneys will encourage the investigator to offer opinions on issues such as the quality of a witness or whether a particular theory is reasonable. At the attorney-investigator meetings, it is perfectly appropriate and helpful for the investigator to offer opinions on these issues. But be aware that each attorney may have a different conception of how you and he or she should work together. Don’t take for granted that each attorney will want the investigator to undertake the same tasks and responsibilities.

You will also work with attorneys of different experience and ability. This will affect your role and your responsibilities. With a younger, less experienced attorney, it is perfectly proper to offer suggestions that you may not offer to a more seasoned trial attorney. In fact, younger attorneys should welcome any insights you can provide.

Foundation of a Good Relationship

Trust and confidence are the keystones to an effective attorney-investigator team. Some key characteristics of an effective team are the following:

  • Respect for each other’s role in the case, regardless of experience.
  • Willingness to offer and respect constructive ideas on gathering evidence or new theories of the case.
  • Ability to get hold of each other on short notice.
  • Taking accurate direction and following through on that direction.
  • Reasonable and understandable billing procedures.
  • Willingness to point out possible errors or “dead ends” in a constructive and positive manner.

The following are some of the issues that arise that will harm trust and confidence:

  • Failure to respond to the attorney’s requests in a timely manner.
  • Failure to provide reports and other investigatory materials (photos, videos) on a timely basis, or failing to provide them in a usable format.
  • Inability of the attorney to contact the investigator when necessary.
  • Unwillingness to take direction, or an “attitude” that is condescending or dismissive of the attorney’s role.
  • Any surprises in billing.

Meeting with the attorney is a priority, and is one of the most productive things a team can do. Plus, brainstorming is fun. Attorneys and investigators bring different mindsets to the case. Teamwork frequently results in creative solutions.

Who Is the Client?

In a criminal defense case the accused has historically been referred to as “the client.” Legislation requiring the licensing of investigators, however, has created new and unresolved questions about who, legally, is the client. ORS 703.401(2) seems to say that the attorney who hires the investigator is the client. The Department of Public Safety Standards and Training, however, may decide at some future time that the attorney’s client is also the investigator’s client.

The client question could become important because of the statutory requirement that the investigator or operative “shall inform each client that the client has a right to receive a written contract. The contract shall clearly state the task to be performed and the rate of payment.” ORS 703.455(1). Related statutes also address the client’s right to receive written or oral reports. The report issue is dealt with later in this chapter. These provisions probably have little effect on an investigator and attorney who have a good working relationship. When working with a new or unfamiliar attorney, it is recommended that the investigator deliver a simple letter of understanding to the attorney. It is highly recommended that when hired by someone other than an attorney, an investigator should provide a contract for the client and investigator both to sign. The contract should outline specifically what is expected of the investigator, the amount the investigator charges per hour, plus what the investigator may charge as additional expenses, such as mileage, meals, etc.

While acting for the attorney on the client’s behalf, the attorney-client privilege that protects communications between the attorney and the client will likewise protect communications between the client and the investigator, as long as the same precautions are taken by the investigator to maintain that confidential relationship, meaning that any communication between the investigator and the criminal defendant must be intended as confidential and no one else, other than the attorney, can have access to the communications. You should be careful to coordinate with the attorney prior to any communication with the client to ensure the privilege is preserved.

Theories of Prosecution and Defense

Lawyers often talk about their “theory of the case,” meaning how the evidence can be explained in such a way that doesn’t incriminate the defendant. For instance, if stolen property was found in the defendant’s car, did anyone else have access to the car?

Discuss with the attorney the possible legal theories of the case. What is the prosecutor’s theory as to how the evidence is explained, and what is the defense theory?

The investigator should be aware of both theories. During an investigation, you will discover new facts. And if you are aware of both the defense and prosecution theories, what may seem like an immaterial fact at first may tend to prove, or disprove, a particular theory.

A well-prepared defense team understands the state’s theory of the case and may come to know the government’s case, and its weaknesses, better than the prosecutor.

The original defense theory may become unworkable because of defense investigation. The theory may also shift or completely change based on new information provided by the government. Don’t worry, theories and investigation often become fluid before “gelling” again. Good communication is vital and the investigator should be part of any ongoing discussion.

Understand the Law Behind the Charges

The investigator must also be aware of and understand the law behind the charges. Is the defendant charged with criminal recklessness as opposed to intentional or knowing conduct? Has the defendant been charged with an attempted crime as opposed to a completed crime? Or, is the defense theory of the case that it was merely an attempted crime ? (which would carry less severe consequences than a conviction for the completed crime). Understanding these issues may effect how the investigator views and gathers evidence and statements.

Mitigation

Sometimes there may not be a theory of defense, or, as stated above, the theory may become untenable. If it appears that the defendant will likely be found guilty of some or all of the charges, then sentencing mitigation is called for. Mitigation requires the investigator to gather information regarding circumstances that puts the defendant in the best possible light, while acknowledging his or her guilt. The investigator should be prepared and able to recognize helpful statements by witnesses during the first interview. Even if a witness gives evidence that incriminates the client, there may be information that could assist the client at the time of sentencing, for instance, was the defendant contrite or attempted to avoid unnecessary harm or injury.

Be prepared, at all times to get mitigating information. You may not get a second chance to interview these witnesses. (For more on mitigation, see Chapter 18.)

Litigation Teams

Ideally, a litigation team should be comprised of attorneys, paralegals, investigators and legal secretaries. In reality, the team is usually smaller, depending on the case and jurisdiction. Don’t be afraid to recommend additional team members if you see the need for their expertise. The most successful litigation teams are characterized by high levels of communication and flexibility among members. Regularly scheduled meetings are important where all can discuss the status of tasks and brainstorm new ideas and avenues of defense.

Becoming part of a litigation team is a big commitment. Carefully consider and discuss the situation with other potential members before joining.

Defendant Interaction

The defendant will have specific facts about witnesses or a victim that may be beneficial in the investigation. If possible, avoid relying only on the attorney’s version of the defendant’s statement. Attorney and investigators have different skills. Investigators are skilled interrogators and listeners outside the courtroom and use different filters than attorneys. If the defendant’s version of events differs from the attorney, make sure you inform the attorney immediately of that divergence and discuss the reasons for that difference.

The investigator and attorney may each have a different rapport with the defendant. At some point this rapport may assist the defendant in making important decisions.

Returning to the subject of attorney-client privilege, investigators are legally considered representatives of the attorney and are, therefore, bound by that privilege. Oregon Evidence Code (OEC) 503(2) sets out the attorney-client privilege. It provides, in part:

  1. A client has a privilege to refuse to disclose and to prevent any other person from disclosing confidential communications made for the purpose of facilitating the rendition of professional legal services to the client:
  1. Between the client or the client’s representative and the client’s lawyer or a representative of the lawyer;
  2. Between the client’s lawyer and the lawyer’s representative[.]

OEC 503(1)(b) defines “confidential communication” for purposes of subsection (2) as “a communication not intended to be disclosed to third persons other than those to whom disclosure is in furtherance of the rendition of professional legal services to the client or those reasonably necessary for the transmission of the communication.”

OEC 503(1)(e) defines “representative of the lawyer” as “one employed to assist the lawyer in the rendition of professional legal services.”

Investigators must be constantly vigilant in not disclosing confidential communications.

Organize and Prioritize

Major Tasks

As early as possible identify large, time-consuming tasks such as record retrieval and multiple witness interviews. Begin these tasks as early as you’ve identified them.

Gathering Documents

Make sure you have signed releases from the client or the attorney, and clarify with the attorney to determine if you need documents to be certified copies. When large numbers of documents are involved, agree with the attorney on a consistent system of organization. A Bates stamp provides a simple and inexpensive method of numbering documents.

Witnesses

When attempting to locate witnesses or doing background investigation, be sure the attorney has provided you with as much information as possible about the witness, including their contact information, their relevance to the case, or whether they are a defense or state’s witness. Have the attorney provide you with a subpoena before you interview a witness who might be difficult to locate again. The attorney can have several subpoenas prepared for you so that you can obtain one on short notice. (For more on witnesses, see Chapter 10, “Locating Witnesses,” and Chapter 11, “Interviewing Witnesses.”)

Deadlines

Many courts require attorneys and their clients to accept or reject plea bargains well in advance of a trial date. So it can be important to have mitigating evidence prepared prior to plea negotiations between the defense attorney and the state. Motions (to suppress evidence, to limit introduction of evidence by the other side, to compel discovery) may also be scheduled in advance of trial and require specific investigator tasks.

Communicate Effectively

Keep the attorney advised of any investigator vacations or scheduling problems. Attorneys should likewise advise investigators when a hearing or trial is postponed and consult with them about future scheduling.

Investigation Results to the Attorney

Once the investigator has located and interviewed someone, or done other investigatory tasks, the attorney and investigator must decide what to do with that information.

Should a report be generated?

Whether or not to write a report, and what to put in the report, are probably the most discussed issues between attorneys and investigators. These decisions are best handled on a case-by-case basis. There are, however, some general rules to be aware of.

Statutory Requirement. ORS 703.455 requires all investigators to inform a client that the client is entitled to receive either an oral or a written report. A written report must be furnished within seven days after the investigator has received a written request from the client. Investigators are also required to maintain separate and distinct case files for each client with, among other things, copies of all correspondence and written reports.

Attorneys are responsible for complying with laws concerning pretrial discovery. ORS 135.835 et seq. Oregon law requires the defense attorney to disclose to the prosecutor before trial:

[t]he names and addresses of persons, including the defendant, whom the defense intends to call as witnesses at the trial, together with relevant written or recorded statements or memoranda of any oral statements of such persons other than the defendant.

In theory, pretrial discovery laws exist to prevent one party from being surprised or ambushed at trial. In reality, aggressive advocates often push the rules to or beyond the limits. Investigators must rely upon the attorney for the many legal interpretations of discovery law. There are a few things that investigators and operatives should know that could prevent problems for themselves and their clients.

Written witness statements. A written document reporting the statement of someone a party intends to call as a witness represents the classic example of a document that must be disclosed. An attorney, however, may ask the investigator not to prepare a formal report, though notes may be discoverable even if they never leave the investigator’s file. This depends on the nature of the notes and whether the attorney knows of their existence. Notes intended as or amounting to a full report are probably discoverable. Generally, an investigator’s “fragmentary notes” (notes not intended to be a full account of the event reported) are probably not discoverable. Notes that are merely a step in the preparation of a final report are probably not discoverable, assuming the report was disclosed. The law in this area is very dependent on the particular facts of the case and judicial interpretation. It is best, therefore, to initially assume that investigator notes are discoverable. In any event, you should always consult with the attorney employing you in order to have a clear and consistent policy regarding investigation reports and notes.

Work Product. This is a very important concept. Work product should be communicated to the attorney as a clearly labeled separate document. Pretrial discovery statutes do protect some information from disclosure. “Work product” generally refers to reports or memoranda that contain opinions, theories or conclusions of the attorney or their agent. ORS 135.855(1). A report concerning an investigator’s interview of a witness is not work product just because it contains something an attorney wishes to hide from the other side. Portions of a report that contain the investigator’s opinion or belief of what kind of witness a person would make should be set out in a separate report, or at the very least a clearly labeled separate section of your report. That way the attorney can redact the work product if the report needed to be provided to opposing counsel.

[Editors’ Note: It is important to recognize that notes and information labeled “work product” loses confidential status as the case moves from the initial defense stage to an appeal and to PCR and habeas. By the time a case hits PCR and habeas stages, work initially identified as confidential work product may not be held as such by subsequent defense teams.]

Testimony

Being called as a witness can be an unnerving experience. The effective investigator needs plenty of notice so he or she can review their work and consult with the trial attorney. Every attorney has their own style of preparing and presenting witnesses. Testimony is rarely scripted, which is why it is important to meet with the attorney and understand the purpose of your testimony.

Be prepared to explain your job and your qualifications. It is important to appear professional and unbiased. Most jurors are interested in what you do.

Typical examples of defense investigator testimony concern impeachment of witnesses for prior inconsistent statements and laying the foundation for physical exhibits.

Don’t be surprised or offended when the prosecutor decides to cross-examine you. Likewise, don’t take the attack or questions personally. Listen and understand the question, and if you don’t, ask that the question be repeated. Once you understand the question your answer should be consistent, even repetitive, with your testimony on direct. The answer should be as short and concise as “Yes,” “No,” and “I don’t know.” Never guess or use words such as “maybe” or “probably.” Volunteering information will lead to problems.

When you do testify, take the question from the attorney, but answer it to the jury. They’re the ones you’re talking to.

Should the investigator take their report or notes to court when they are called to testify? Before doing so, the investigator must confirm with the attorney that the report has been disclosed during the pretrial discovery process. If no report has been written and the investigator shows up with notes, it is likely that a judge will order the investigator to disclose those notes to the other side. Similarly, Oregon Rules of Evidence 612 provide that if a witness (such as an investigator) uses a writing (like notes) to refresh memory for the purpose of testifying, then an adverse party is entitled to inspect that writing.

Conclusion

The most consistent theme in the effective attorney-investigator relationship is communication. From accepting employment to brainstorming, locating witnesses, preparing reports and testifying, the well-informed team that has consistently communicated the relevant investigatory documents, discussed the theory of the case, accepted and carried out the responsibilities of the respective parties, will succeed.
