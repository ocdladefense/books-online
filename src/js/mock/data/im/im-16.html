Investigators Manual

2010 edition

Measure 11 and Major Felonies

Michael Zerwas

Introduction

“The case, like so much in the world of espionage, was a haze of ambiguity, in which everything from intelligence data to Dr. Lee’s activities was subject to interpretation. Often what mattered was who the interpreters were, and what perspective they brought.”

—The New York Times on the Wen Ho Lee case.

In 1994, the voters approved a mandatory minimum sentencing law known as Measure 11. Measure 11 has changed the balance of power in the justice system. So too has it changed many aspects of the criminal defense practice. Clients now face the prospect of spending a significant portion of their lives in the care of the Department of Corrections, whether or not they have ever committed a crime before. Attorneys have the increased onus of advising clients who, under the old guidelines, may have had a significant opportunity to win at trial. If they lost, they would likely have had a sentencing judge willing and able to assess the individual and the sentence thoughtfully and fairly. Like the death penalty, Measure 11 has raised the stakes of punishment to a degree that the approach to defending Measure 11 clients has become more often a task of negotiation than of litigation. (For the list of Measure 11 crimes and sentences, refer to Appendix A.)

So why would this affect the investigator? The short answer is that it shouldn’t. Each client, each case, each injustice deserves the best our profession has to offer. The reality is that Measure 11 has impacted both the intensity and the manner in which we work.

This chapter is intended to serve as a general guide to thinking about the investigation of any case, with an emphasis on the major case and with references to what can be done specifically in Measure 11 cases. The characteristics that distinguish the Measure 11 case from other cases provide an outline for the investigation and perhaps guide us into a philosophical approach, much like the death penalty provokes its own special philosophical and practical approaches. This is not a how-to manual. I assume a good basic knowledge of the art of investigation and, since many aspects of practice differ from county to county, there will be little reference to specific agency resources.

Bad Guys and Badder Guys

What separates the major crime from the minor crime? The distinction is mostly arbitrary. A single heroin dealer can cause more deaths and social woe than a serial killer, and a thief who takes an heirloom worth virtually nothing on the market can cause more suffering than if he had beaten his victim senseless. Our

depersonalized “nation of laws” has taken on the task of defining, redefining and over-defining crime seriousness and, so no one feels cheated when one person pays less for an action than another, has imposed such draconian measures as mandatory minimums. In order to ensure that no crime victim feels slighted in the eye-for-an-eye scheme of things, the judiciary has defined every complainant as a victim and the level of victimization that person is entitled to. And, just to make certain that all misery is created equal and anyone is entitled to be a victim, society has gone to extraordinary lengths to protect its complaining witnesses from scrutiny.

This is not to understate the impact that the guilty have on their victims but to decry the systematization, the imposition by the state of one punishment fitting every similar criminal act, without regard to real impact and without regard to the best resolution for everyone involved. Judicial power has shifted to an office intensely more rooted in politics and less likely to focus on the individual, whether that individual be the accused or the accuser, the harmer or the harmed. Indeed, there have been numerous notable cases nationwide of prosecutors fervently denying in the face of overwhelming evidence that a convicted person might possibly be innocent.

So, in determining a major crime from a minor crime we all pull out our handy sentencing guideline charts, factor in prosecutorial zeal, add points for press coverage, assess the effect of social prejudice, attempt to factor out our own prejudices, and produce our score for crime seriousness.

That said, there are a number of factors in the serious crime category that help define the process of investigation. First, the majority of crimes in this category have a named victim with a defined harm. Second, the mental state of the accused plays an important part in both the charging of the crime and understanding the incident. Third, the crimes are likely going to be investigated by more experienced officers, prosecuted by more experienced prosecutors and likely held to stricter judicial standards.

Information

Information being our business, this is the opportunity to shine. All three of the above factors lend themselves to scrutiny and assessment. It is important to think through every aspect of the incident and its participants, and to make certain that all relevant information is obtained, including as much useful peripheral information as possible.

Analyze the pretrial discovery. Do you have everything? Likely not. Remember that the state only has the obligation to turn over what it has, and even then we often have to go to some extremes to get good quality reproductions or access to original evidence. How many times have you found ten pages of worthless black and white copies of photographs in the discovery packet or a similarly useless photocopy of a knife? Or page after page of transcripts rife with the word “inaudible”? How often have you seen a full crime lab analysis? (What I usually see is a one-paragraph report that says something like “tested positive for the presence of human blood.”) Do you have the 9-1-1 tapes? If so, do you have anything more than the originating call? If you have medical records, do you have follow-up reports? Do you have the ambulance or EMS reports? Do you have the videotape of the CARES evaluation? Do you have autopsy photographs? Are there likely to be police bureau internal documents generated? Do you have them? Should you have them?

In Camera Process

Possibly the most important aspect of discovery is the in cameramotion. Many of the Measure 11 cases are sex cases and the relevant information is difficult to obtain. Through the in cameraprocess we get the opportunity to access normally confidential records on complainants. The most common are child abuse

cases where school records, prior medical and psychological records and juvenile justice records may indicate patterns contrary to some widely held theories of child behavior in situations of child abuse: a pattern of lying or accusatory behavior, medical referrals, counseling or psychiatric referrals, etc. The absolute importance of doing the in camerahearing early is that the records may bring to light areas of investigation previously unknown and may point to other records that may require yet another in camerainspection. I can’t emphasize this strongly enough—if nothing else, get the standard litany of subpoenas served quickly so when the motions are ready the bulk of the information is available. If other leads develop along the way or if additional leads are found in the first batch of records, you are not faced with a time crunch near trial. I have even seen cases where defense issues arise after the initial review of material and a second in camerais held to assess the material in light of the new issues.

Police Reports

Read the police reports carefully and pay attention to the summary reports that may refer to what other officers, fire and medical personnel do at the scene. Did other people write reports, take notes, or participate in a manner that might merit an interview or a subpoena? If you are having a bail hearing and the state’s burden is to show why your client should be held in custody, can you use that venue to discover what has

not been written in a report? Always try to read between the lines. What isn’t being said and why? Is a rookie officer trying to play detective and interpreting something in an inappropriate way? Is this masking the truth in some way? Pay attention to the words! Officers often give away their biases. Police try to protect complainants because, rightly or wrongly, they see that as their job. But there appears to be an innate human desire to be truthful which can lead to verbal cues. For example, if you have a report in which the officer consistently writes that the complainant said this or stated that and then throws in that the complainant claimedthat something happened, this could be a subtle signal that the officer had some doubt. Keep that potential in mind when doing your interviews or forensic examinations.

Occasionally, internal documents are generated by the police. These are never intended for discovery but may include useful or even exculpatory information. I had a case a number of years back that involved an extended low-speed chase through the streets of Portland. The client was charged with, among other things, recklessly endangering two unnamed civilian pedestrians who, I suppose, could have been injured if they had walked in front of the vehicle. It was almost an accident that we discovered an “After Action Report” generated by the precinct commander which was intended to justify the way the chase was conducted. In that report the commander, who had debriefed the officers involved, concluded that the officers were justified in not forcing a stop earlier in the pursuit because “no civilians were in danger.” Needless to say, that commander was not testifying for the state, but we got him under subpoena as soon as we found this document. (As an aside, the lieutenant who wrote the report developed a serious case of amnesia when I attempted to talk to him.) Internal reports such as this are likely to be done in cases where there is potential civil action against the police or media scrutiny. 9-1-1 tapes can be useful in discerning whether a supervisor has been called to respond after an incident.

Databases

While on the subject of police reports, when I have a case involving a large number of witnesses or a lot of evidence I like to use a computer database to keep control of the paper. There is nothing more frustrating than trying to find all references in a thousand pages of discovery to one obscure witness or piece of evidence prior to an interview. I use Microsoft Access, but there are other interrelational databases that will work just as well, and there are databases designed specifically for the investigator. My only issue with the commercial legal databases is that they try to be all things to all people and can become too cumbersome. My databases are constantly evolving and are often designed to fit the needs of a specific case. In its simplest form, the database involves Bates-stamping the documents and assigning each document a specific code. I use the document code as the primary key in the table, with the first page number of that document as a cross reference so each member of the defense team can quickly locate that document. It is important for there to be one set of discovery kept in pristine condition in page order.

The other database fields can include type of document, a brief summary of the document, a list of names and objects mentioned in that document, and the author of the document. Beyond this basic information the database can get as involved as you wish, with linked tables for additional information. Most sophisticated programs will even allow you to insert your own documents, such as investigation reports. Another approach is to build your table around the individual witnesses. The most important detail in using databases is to be consistent with the data. For instance, if you are dealing with multiple weapons make certain you always refer to each weapon by a distinct identifier. The “black gun” must always be the “black gun” and not sometimes “Bruce’s gun.” Or, assign a unique code to each piece of data. This way your queries and searches will be complete and consistent. I have found that the primary mistake I make in designing databases is to get too complex with too much information. As a result I get bogged down in data entry. Learning to design your own database is a good investment of time, even if you are using commercial case management software.

An example of a simple and useful database project occurs when we do 9-1-1 research in Multnomah County. We get a computer printout of all the police radio activity assigned to an incident. (While listening to the tapes I often discover radio activity and even 9-1-1 calls that do not get assigned to an incident.) It lists the time of the activity, the unit generating the activity, the officer(s) in that unit, a status code for the activity, and often a summary of the activity in something resembling English. The document is chronological. By reproducing this document in a database one time, you can then isolate and organize the information in many ways. When you have ten units involved in a search for a suspect, with very little effort you can produce a report that isolates each unit’s broadcasts (an extremely useful format for cross- examination). Or you can produce a report that only displays when each unit arrives, helpful in determining if there are any holes in a perimeter. No matter how you want to organize the information or isolate bits of the information you only need enter the data once.

Though often a luxury we don’t have, pre-indictment investigation can occasionally yield amazing results. I have had luck in cases where our client has a reasonable explanation for what happened and there are witnesses the police haven’t located or are completely unaware of. There is sometimes a risk in investigating before you have much information to work with. I like to begin an interview with as much information at hand so I know what to ask. Sometimes you only get one shot at a witness and you want that interview to be as thorough and as meaningful as possible. Still, it is great to get a case out of Measure 11 territory before it becomes a prosecutor’s negotiation tool. Do everything you can to get that jump-start on a case, and be certain to advise any witnesses that you will likely be back with more questions when you have had an opportunity to see all the facts.

After digesting the discovery, prioritize the case and organize responsibilities. The most effective defenses are put together by defense teams that work well together, communicate what they are working on, and share responsibility.

Motivating Factors

Complainants

A number of factors drive Measure 11 and other major cases. One factor is the increasing awareness and definition of “victim.” (One of my favorite attorneys long ago told me that he advocates referring to “victims” as complainants. This has been a great mental tool for me over the years and allows me to keep a perspective that is more amenable to unbiased investigation. I will use the term “complainant” here.) Additionally, prosecutors seem to be much more sensitive to the wishes of complainants. When possible, developing a rapport with the complainant can be effective in resolving a case. Whether the client is bang-on guilty and almost certain of losing in trial, or not as guilty as the indictment reads, or not guilty (or even innocent), the complainant is likely to have a singular view of what happened. Often part of that view is that we are part and parcel with the defendant and therefore complicitous in the crime. In these situations, much can be accomplished by at least offering the complainant an explanation of the defense role in their case, a humanized view of the client, an explanation of what the client may be facing as punishment if convicted, and sometimes (if you detect flexibility in the complainant’s attitude) some possible resolutions. As we all know, talking to complainants involves a certain amount of dexterity and caution. They often have been advised, subtly, that speaking with the defense is not to their benefit. They often have strong predisposed notions toward the defendant, the case, and the defense. And, of course, they must be notified of their rights per ORS 135.970.

In those instances where the complainant is reluctant to speak with the defense, determine whether they would like to talk outside the scope of the incident. In many cases, they will be willing to do that and are interested in our perspective of the justice process as it relates to their case. A large portion of the public perceives guilt and innocence in very concrete terms. An introduction to the nuances of intent, degree of injury, and fair punishment may be advantageous. Since many of our cases are aimed at negotiation, having a complainant who is more likely to be a tad more sympathetic is an ultimate advantage to the client. These conversations are often a dance, since we have to be cautious about what we can and should reveal. It is highly recommended that before discussing your client’s “issues” in the sense of drug addiction, past treatment, current treatment, psychological issues or prior convictions, you get both a clear understanding from the attorney as to what can be discussed and an understanding from the client that you are going to be discussing these aspects of his or her life. Just as we consider ourselves experts at ferreting out lies and obfuscation, so do many of the people we contact. Most approach us from a position of distrust to begin with, and we can do considerable harm in not being honest. I would much rather state that I am not allowed to discuss something, or preferably that I don’t know for certain, than to try to worm around an issue.

Of course, there are those cases where the complainant will be highly sympathetic, if not to the client then to the degree of punishment the state wishes to extract. This happens most often in domestic violence cases where the Assault IV turns into a Measure 11 case with the tacked-on kidnap charge. The domestic violence case often churns up the legal maneuvering to a high-pitched frenzy. I have seen complainants go to extravagant lengths to protect the clients in these cases. Prosecutors can be in a tough situation when a witness or complainant changes her story and decides living out of state is preferable to the defendant spending 70 or more months in prison.

Because of this, hearsay exceptions often play a huge role in these cases. I highly recommend becoming familiar with these exceptions so you can recognize what may exclude (or include) a statement under them.

•OEC 803(2), Excited Utterance.

•OEC 803(3), State of Mind.

•OEC 803(4), Medical Diagnosis or Treatment.

•OEC 803(18a)(b), Acts of Abuse.

•OEC 803(24), Acts of Abuse.

•OEC 803(26), Domestic Violence.

These are areas in which our jobs as fact finders often get into gray areas. A complainant intent on protecting the guilty by concocting an improbable story in total contrast to the available evidence and prior statements can be more harmful to the client than one presenting a solid account of the truth. A witness making himself unavailable can hurt more than help the case if that allows the state to present statements made in the heat of anger rather than more reasoned accounting of fact. If there is any reason to suspect that witnesses you have interviewed may avoid appearing at trial and they have given you useful information, remember that it may fall on you to show the witness is unavailable. While I may feel comfortable challenging the recanting complainant with evidence, I am never comfortable suggesting the legal arguments of a case. Often, this is a good opportunity to have the attorney involved in the interview.

Remember that much can be learned even in general discussion with a complainant. A single face-to-face rejection can reveal bias or sympathy in the manner it is delivered. It can reveal whether there is an understanding of the process. It can reveal how articulate the witness will be. I recall one attempted interview where, with just the introduction of myself I could see the complainant’s very soul drain from her body. That one physical reaction said to me that she could make a dramatic witness and might be amenable to something that would save her from the ordeal of going on the witness stand to relive the incident. Of course, there is always a temptation to read too much into these contacts, but each piece of information is part of the puzzle; if other information confirms your suspicions, you are better off than if you didn’t try. If you accomplish nothing substantive in the course of complainant contact, you should at least be comfortable with having acted professionally, and if you manage to leave without having closed the door to future contact your time has been well spent.

The State

Another driving factor in the major case is the state’s investment in the case. As I mentioned earlier, these cases carry high stakes for the state as well as for the defense. The state is politically driven, both inside and outside the district attorney’s office. It is important to keep an eye on what is available to move the state to negotiate. The most obvious fear the state has is the fear of defeat, which can result in public spectacle at some level. (One of my favorite Oregonianheadlines from some years back was, “Jury Fails to Convict.” Fortunately for the prosecutor on that case the obvious media bias landed on the jury and not the DDA.) One way to keep the fear of losing alive is to investigate the case. Even when everything about a case suggests negotiation and settlement, maintaining a trial posture with investigation can be important and, I believe, can win months of freedom for the client in the negotiation process.

While investigating the facts is the sexier way to win a case, the facts are often your worst enemy. Sometimes the facts just get worse the more you investigate. Few things are more disheartening than coming back from a day in the field with a list of additional crimes your client may have committed, not totally uncommon when investigating sex abuse cases.

Always remember that facts sometimes do lie, complainants can lie, cops can and do lie, and we do not lie. I often think what a wonderful advantage we have in our truth. If our truth lets the most horrendous criminal walk back into society, someone else has failed, and the someone who failed likely did so out of laziness or ineptness or lack of resources. If our truth devastates the defense case, so be it. Our truth is largely protected. (One major proviso here is the mandatory reporting law. It is possible that if we report child abuse by our client, using reliable information which is not part of the case we are working on, the attorney maybe required to report it to the authorities. Investigators should discuss mandatory reporting with the attorneys they work with.)

With major cases my experience is that judicial rulings are often much more favorable to the state. A search and seizure issue that is routinely won by the defense in a possession of controlled substances case may be a dead loser in a rape case. Thorough investigation can bolster pretrial motions. Are there potential neighborhood witnesses to a questionable stop or illegal search? Have you looked at the evidence, checked out sight lines and lighting? Have you been to the scene, obtained the 9-1-1 tapes? Obtained press coverage? In their hunger to feed the public appetite for excitement, television reporters may have done some legwork for you in finding peripheral witnesses or have interviewed relatives.

“I want tell you about the town of Stockbridge, Massachusetts, where this happened. Here, they got three stop signs, two police officers, and one police car, but when we got to the Scene of the Crime there was five police officers and three police cars.”

“And they was using up all kinds of cop equipment that they had hanging around the police officer’s station. They was taking plaster tire tracks, footprints, dogsmelling prints, and they took 27 eight-by-ten

color glossy photographs with circles and arrows and a paragraph on the back of each one explaining what each one was to be used as evidence against us.”(Arlo Guthrie, “Alice’s Restaurant”)

And then there are the police. They are becoming more specialized, with specially trained teams to handle different crimes. This also leads to a need to justify the specialized training, the special teams, and the extra manpower. This can lead to bias and, indeed, to illegal and unethical practices by the police. While not likely the norm, look for signs of overzealous and misleading activities by the police when these expensive operations are involved.

On a slow night there may be a lot of bored cops happy to assign themselves to a hot incident, increasing the opportunity to trample on evidence or create evidence out of nothing. The more they do, the more there is to analyze.

Intent

“Prosecutors had no hard evidence that he planned to give away the secrets, but they reasoned that the simple absence of an innocent explanation showed his criminal intent.”

—The New York Times on the Wen Ho Lee espionage case.

Intent is another major player in the major case. It is an extremely difficult area to attack, given how furtive intent can be to meet the legal standard. Effective cooperation between experts and investigators can play a significant role here. While diminished capacity is not an extremely popular defense, the distinction between the clearly intentional crime and the clearly accidental incident can be very narrow. Here there is a special need to pay attention to preludes to an incident. Why did the client carry a weapon? What emotional factors led up to the incident? Did intoxicants play a role? What is the normal level of susceptibility for the client to being led? In co-defendant cases, what actual role did the client play and what knowledge did the client have of the co-defendant’s intent? What is the historical relationship between the client and the complainant? Is there a basis to argue battered spouse syndrome? Regardless of the actual outcome of a confrontation, what words did any witnesses hear? Can forensics tell you something helpful, such as the pattern of shots fired, whether they were random or whether there were warning shots? Is it possible or even likely that the offending bullet ricocheted or tumbled?

Investigation works in both directions with psychological evaluations. Pre-evaluation investigation can help the psychologist conduct a more thorough evaluation, especially if there are prior diagnoses. Post-evaluation investigation can bolster the results of the evaluation. Especially in guilty except for insanity arguments, the events in a client’s life leading up to an incident can go a long way to explain the incident in the context of mental disease or defect. There are times when you may want your client evaluated quickly, and there have been rare times when I included the expert in some of my interviews. A recent example was a case of a father stabbing his adult son where the son was most aware of the father’s behavior leading up to the incident and we were aware of prior diagnoses. Be aware that there are risks involved in this since the expert may have to disclose all information relied upon to come to a diagnosis.

Mitigation

Mitigation does play a role in Measure 11 investigations. Prosecution response to mitigation evidence can vary widely, from the prosecutor who is very sensitive to mitigation to the prosecutor who feels that it has no place in the process. Still, it just can’t hurt to do it. One way I have seen defense attorneys utilize mitigation

is to find some, any, factual basis to put a sympathetic witness on the stand in trial if only to give the jury the opportunity to see something positive about the client’s life. Mitigation is also a good way to keep the client constructively involved in the case. Encourage the client to develop a list of positive references and to solicit letters of support. Look for aspects of the client's life that may show how the client may benefit from boot camp if eligible. (Mitigation is discussed in Chapter 18.)

The Client

There is, of course, the issue of the client. Just as there is no typical case, there is no typical client. The person who has never been in trouble before can be charged with a serious crime as easily as the most hardened criminal. The “nicest” client can be as blind to the issues of the case as the most recalcitrant client. Some factors are somewhat consistent. The serious case generally takes longer to resolve. The client is more likely to spend that time in custody. During this time, it is easy for the client to feel as though the case is being ignored. Often, the client will come under the influence of a “jailhouse lawyer.” On more than one occasion I have been surprised when a client has come up with either a factual argument or even a legal argument that had been overlooked by the professionals. Still, client control can become a serious problem. You may have 30 cases while the client has only one. You may have nothing but bad news for the client.

And often, because of the penalties involved, the client is frightened and backed into a corner. Just as we often have to project ourselves into a complainant’s psyche, we also need to do the same for our clients.

Perhaps the best we can do is to keep the client apprised of and involved in his or her own case. An explanation about why it takes time to prepare the case can keep the relationship running much more smoothly. Effectively working with clients often requires a combination of personal and professional interactions that can be both exhausting and exhilarating. In the end, what matters is that a relationship of trust is developed and sustained.

Elements of the Crime and the Art of Interviewing

It is important to be familiar with the statutory elements of a crime. I carry a copy of the ORS with me and do a quick read of the relevant statute, as well as the lesser included crimes, immediately before an interview. The other imperative in working a Measure 11 case is to be familiar with the 1997 legislation known as SB 1049.

In a few cases this may be your client’s safety net. Probably the trickiest element of 1049 is the Robbery II element “that, if the defendant represented by words or conduct that the defendant was armed with a dangerous weapon, the representation did not reasonably put the victim in fear of imminent physical injury.” Occasionally you will find the complainant who just was not afraid and told the defendant to take a hike.

This is where the art of interviewing comes into play. The logical question may be, “Were you reasonably in fear of imminent physical injury?” The good investigator will ask something along the lines of, “What were you thinking when that happened?” Think about the different responses you might get to those two questions. Question one gets a yes or no response with no real insight into the incident and the critical information needed. Question two gets the equivocal response that can lead to other questions. “Did you really think he had a gun in his pocket?” “Did he look more scared than you?” “Did he take his hand out of his pocket?” “Why did you give him the money?” Or, “Why didn’t you give him the money?” This, of course, applies to nearly any interview. Once you have extracted the relevant information to put the vital question in perspective and the witness has had the opportunity to reassess the incident you can be in a position to ask, “So is it fair to say that you were not in fear of being shot?” (Appendix B summarizes SB 1049.)

The more you know about the crime and the more you know about what it takes to be guilty of a crime in the technical sense the better investigator you will be.

Conclusion: Investigation as a Production

For years I have had the opportunity through volunteer work with the Portland Opera to observe truly professional theatrical productions come to fruition. My involvement begins about three weeks before production when the chorus, supers, stage managers, conductor and director come together in a warehouse to begin rehearsing the staging. In the course of that month the principal singers come to town, the orchestra rehearses with the conductor, the props are gathered and the set is installed in the theater. The principals are added to the staging rehearsals, the staging is adjusted and readjusted, the lighting designer and the costume shop and the stage hands all work on their contributions and a week before production we move into the actual theater with the actual set and fine-tune the performance. The final component, the audience, is added. I wish I could say that every performance and every production were winners.

Criminal investigation can be like this. Each element of the case needs to be explored and revised. New characters and new information are added as trial preparation continues. The script is analyzed for meaning as each new element is added. The investigation often determines the staging of this legal production and, indeed, which audience it is played to.

Most importantly, in the major-crime case production the investigator must be prepared to retrieve the information that is available. During each element of the investigation, the investigator must be aware of the structure under which she or he is operating. The incident itself may well lose importance as other aspects of the case come to life, but it is the incident on which most of our material is based. Witnesses are approached about the incident but end up providing information about suppression issues or mitigation or bias. It is this holistic, organic vision that I advocate. The more integrated you are with the grand scheme, the more likely you are to support the defense function—guilt or innocence aside.

Senate Bill 1049—

Measure 11 Changes, Misdemeanor/Violation Election

SB 1049 allows persons charged with Robbery II, Assault II, and Kidnapping II to be sentenced to a non- Measure 11 sentence under certain circumstances. Permits persons whose crimes were committed before the effective date of the act, including persons who have already been convicted and sentenced, to be sentenced or resentenced under the provisions of the act under certain circumstances. Adds Measure 11–level sentences for three additional offenses.

Robbery II, Assault II, Kidnapping II

If it finds substantial and compelling reasons, the court may impose a guideline, rather than a M11 sentence, for:

(A) Assault II, but only if:

(1) the victim was not physically injured by a deadly weapon, and

(2) the victim did not suffer a “significant physical injury” (as defined in the bill), and

(3) the defendant does not have one of the disqualifying prior convictions within the requisite time period.

(B) Robbery II, but only if:

(1) the victim did not suffer a significant physical injury, and

(2) if the defendant represented by words or conduct that the defendant was armed with a dangerous weapon, the representation did not reasonably put the victim in fear of imminent significant physical injury and

(3) if the defendant represented by words or conduct that the defendant was armed with a deadly weapon,

the representation did not reasonably put the victim in fear of imminent physical injury, and

(4) the defendant does not have one of the disqualifying prior convictions within the requisite time period.

(C) Kidnapping II , but only if:

(1) the victim was a t least 12 years of age, and

(2) the defendant does not have one of the disqualifying prior convictions within the requisite time period.

The court may make a dispositionaldeparture to probation if it finds that there is an additional substantial and compelling reason not relied on to justify the initial departure and if it also finds that a sentence of probation will be more effective than a prison term in reducing the risk of recidivism and that a sentence of probation will better serve to protect society.

The foregoing provisions apply to persons whose crimes are committed on or after October 4, 1997 (the effective date of SB 1049).
