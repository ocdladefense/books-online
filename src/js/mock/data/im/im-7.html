A Digital Age with Digital Crimes

We clearly live in a digital age. From awakening to our digitally programmed alarm clocks, driving our computer controlled and GPS equipped car, being recorded at the Mini Mart while buying coffee, typing on a laptop, talking and texting through our cell phones to watching cable television, we are constantly surrounded and using digital technology. It should come as no surprise then that in the practice of criminal law, digital evidence is relied on more and more.

It is a common perception that digital evidence—and, in particular, computer forensics—is associated only with crimes related to child pornography or the solicitation of minors through the Internet à la such shows as MSNBC’s “To Catch a Predator.” While the majority of cases involving computer evidence deal with one or both of these issues (and will be the subject of the majority of this article), the fact that we interact with digital technology in almost every aspect of daily life tells us that the criminal offenses people become involved with will too.

Consider a couple of scenarios. The first involves a client charged with homicide when his wife is found strangled in the passenger seat of their car at a neighborhood park. The police feel that the husband does not “act appropriately” when informed of his wife’s death, and a witness at the park identifies a man seen from a distance that matches the husband’s description, including the wearing of a “dark coat with white stripes on the sleeves.” A coat of that description is found in the man’s closet, and the witness believes the husband “looks like” the man they saw leaving the park on foot. It is also subsequently learned that a hefty life insurance policy has been taken out on the victim and coworkers of the deceased woman tell police she had reported she was “unhappy at home.” The husband’s fingerprints are in and on the car (as he drives it too), and the driver’s seat is pushed back as if accommodating a larger (masculine) driver. The husband has no defensive wounds, but the coroner believes the perpetrator was wearing gloves. Gloves belonging to the husband are found in the closet with the coat, and a hair, ultimately determined to belong to the victim, is attached to one of the gloves. The evidence isn’t overwhelming, but the district attorney feels it is sufficient to go forward.

In the second scenario, your client has been charged with a convenience store armed robbery. Although the robber had worn a coat with a hood, his face had been visible and the victim, the attendant on duty at the time, identifies your client as the perpetrator, saying he knows him as an occasional customer from the neighborhood. Unfortunately, when the store manager attempts to collect the digitally recorded security camera footage, it is corrupted and unreadable. The attendant is adamant that your client was the robber and even picks him out of a line-up. The prosecutor decides to go forward.

We will revisit these incidents later after learning some of the in and outs of the field.

What Is “Computer Forensics”?

The first misnomer with this often-asked question is the word “computer.” The word brings up images of desktops and laptops but, as we have just discussed, digital evidence can be found in many places outside a computer. Yes, most of the time the computer hard drives are indeed the source of the information and ultimately the answers in a given case, but cell phones, Blackberry’s, iPhones, GPS devices, thumb drives, cameras and security systems can contain evidence that might be important in a given case. Thus, when we talk about “computer forensics” what we really mean is any device that has the ability to record information in an electronic (digital) format.

When done correctly, computer (or rather “digital”) forensics is more appropriately described as a computer investigation. When a given piece of media is determined to possibly be relevant in a case, it needs to be investigated and analyzed in the context of the litigation, and the attorney needs to be informed of everything potentially relevant about it. Individual pieces of data are usually meaningless unless the context of the data is also analyzed and explained.

The “forensics” side of computer forensics, then, is the investigative analysis of the data that has been recovered from the piece of media in question. As we will see later in the chapter, there are very sophisticated tools for the recovery of data from devices, and mastering the knowledge of how to use those tools takes many, many hours of experience and training. If computer forensics has a golden rule, it is that once something is written to a digital device, it will remain there indefinitely until overwritten by new data. Finding that data among trillions of pieces (bits) of other data can be a challenge but, nevertheless, until it is overwritten, that data will remain and is almost always recoverable.

In essence, computer forensics is the investigative and intuitive use of sophisticated forensic software tools to recover all relevant data (active or deleted) and present it in the proper context so that the attorney can make informed decisions about what did or didn’t happen in a case.

Can I Do It Myself?

“My client is very computer savvy and wants to go through everything himself.” If the data in question is not child pornography contraband, the defendant can indeed at times offer insight into what he or others were doing on the machine at a given time; however, what more often happens is an attempt to look at the data from a skewed perspective and an attempt to create red herrings that will not hold up as valid defense strategies. With the examiner’s time almost always limited by budget, the chasing down and disproving of possible defense scenarios created by the defendant means that short of unusual circumstances, this practice is not encouraged. Defense strategies in computer cases truly need to be based on the reality of what is or isn’t on the media.

In addition, the primary reasons a review of digital evidence must involve a forensic examiner using approved forensic software is 1) forensic software is required to recover and view deleted data—there is no other way to do so, and 2) the review of any active (nondeleted) files without forensic software changes the date attributes of the file forever. Even booting a computer changes hundreds of file dates and times.

Methods and Tools of an Examination

The forensics examiner should be more than a data recovery technician and should use an investigative approach to find and interpret all the relevant evidence in your case. That does not mean, however, that the examiner does not need to be thoroughly trained and a master of the many forensic software tools at his or her disposal. EnCase sold by Guidance Software and FTK (Forensic Tool Kit) by Access Data are as close to an industry “standard” that is available and are widely used by law enforcement—however, no tool does “everything” and, especially, no tool does everything well. A competent forensic examiner will have a library of various software tools at his or her disposal. Certain steps to be followed in the investigation of computer media are described below.

File Signature Analysis

The two forensic softwares mentioned above verify file extensions with the data the file actually contains and will alert the examiner to files with extensions that do not match content. If, for instance, a letter that would have the file extension “.doc” has been saved by the user as “letter.jpg,” it might appear at first glance to be a picture. The signature analysis tool will alert the examiner that the extension “.jpg” did not match the actual data content of the file, a written document.

Keyword Searching

The forensic software also allows keyword searching through both allocated (live) files and unallocated (deleted) file space and will reveal every instance where the keyword is found. For instance, if a nickname such as those used for chatting and email are known or uncovered during the exam, that nickname can be run as a keyword search revealing previously deleted emails and chats. Likewise, certain keyword combinations related to the use of web search engines such as Google will find previously deleted searches of that engine offering clues as to what the user searched for and potentially the results of that search.

Keyword searching is generally the most efficient method of combing through vast amounts of textual data on a given hard drive. With average-sized laptop hard drives now reaching over one hundred gigabytes, it is important that the examination process be both efficient and thorough; the strategic choice of keywords enables this.

Hash Sets

When a forensic copy is made of a piece of media, like a hard drive, it is “hashed” at the time of acquisition, creating a digital “fingerprint” specific to that drive. This hash can be compared with all subsequent copies made, assuring that each copy is an exact duplicate of the original. Similarly, individual files can also be hashed, creating unique fingerprints for them. Once a hash value for a given file is known, that hash value can be searched for throughout other computers and pieces of media identifying the duplicate file. This can be useful in determining whether personal files have been taken from one user’s machine and placed onto another user’s machine without their knowledge.

Interestingly, the ability to fingerprint files with hash sets is the major reason the defense bar has seen an influx of child pornography cases. Confirmed images of child pornography from various government databases are scanned and a hash value obtained. An officer working on a computer and utilizing a file sharing application such as Limewire searches across the network through the “shared” folders of other users for these hash sets. When a hash value is noted in a user’s shared folder, it can be downloaded to the law enforcement computer, verified, and set out as an investigative lead. What the defense bar should remember in these cases is that file names often do not match file content; mass downloaders of pornography can easily and accidentally download a contraband image which, by default, will remain in the “shared folder” until discovered and deleted. Key to these cases are the keywords used as search terms as well as things like link files indicating a picture has been opened or a movie viewed—things which only a forensic examination will reveal.

Gallery Viewing

Typical Windows-based computers will have tens of thousands of images of all types on them. Avid pornography collectors will often have totals exceeding one hundred thousand images; atypically but still seen on occasion are cases with over one million images. Reviewing images on a machine would be a logistical nightmare except for the ability of the forensic software to sort every image, both allocated (live) and unallocated (deleted) and place them in a thumbnail gallery view. This gallery can then be sorted by file creation date, creating a timeline of images that can be scrolled relatively rapidly.

Deleted Data and Unallocated File Space

When data is “deleted” it is not actually erased. What happens is that pointers that allow the operating system to recognize and retrieve it are changed, but the data remains in the same location indefinitely or until overwritten by new files. The forensic software is used to view and recover these files, even if they have been partially overwritten. This area of the media that is available for the saving of new files (and yet retaining previously deleted files) is referred to as unallocated file space. Because the operating system keeps track of metadata associated with files no longer “sees” the file, data retrieved from unallocated file space will not have corresponding metadata regarding creation or last accessed dates. All that is available is the raw data itself. Many times, data that is most relevant to a case will have been previously deleted and found in unallocated file space. The important thing to remember about data recovered from unallocated file space is that it will no longer have time/date stamps (metadata) associated with it revealing date of creation, last access and the like. Depending on the data recovered, however, on occasion a date can be attributed to a particular item. A recovered letter, for instance, may have a date with the signature line. A deleted web page may have an embedded date that was part of the page itself when it was viewed by the user. Many commercial pornography portals offer gateways to various sexual paraphilias on the Internet by offering visitors links organized by sexual subject matter. These portal sites make money by funneling web users to pornography sites and compete with each other by attempting to offer the most current and up-to-date links to web surfers who use them. Many of these portal sites will have an embedded date and time reflecting when the portal was last updated. Even if that cached web page is subsequently deleted, with other browser cache, it may be recovered with forensic software revealing the embedded date and time that the user was there.

Items recoverable from unallocated file space can include virtually anything ever written to the drive that has not been overwritten by new data. Deleted Internet histories, letters, emails, chats, financial records and pictures are all recoverable from unallocated file space.

Browser History

Internet web browsers can keep files that index the user’s browser session in detail. The pages of web sites searched and visited as well as images and videos from them are captured and stored in cache files. In Windows these cache files are called Temporary Internet Files (TIFs). Windows also keeps a running textual log of browser use that does not include content called Index.dat files. Browser history can be sorted by time and date with forensic software creating a moment by moment timeline of activity. These timelines can be very effective when attempting to place a specific user at the keyboard at a certain time.

Child Pornography Allegations

Few issues are likely to stir as much controversy or are as difficult to defend as allegations of child pornography possession and/or distribution. Mere allegations alone can be enough to cause tremendous damage to professional and personal reputations and cause irrevocable damage to family relationships. At the same time, the Internet has made the viewing of pornography of virtually every conceivable paraphilia, including child pornography, a mouse click away.

When allegations of this nature are made, they obviously need to be treated extremely seriously. The state of Oregon as well as the federal government provide severe criminal penalties for possession of child pornography and it is a crime that is rigorously investigated and enforced. (See 18 USC 2252 et al in the Appendix.)

Any member of a defense team that has handled one of these cases knows that one of the biggest issues faced is getting access to the media. Prior to the passage of the Adam Walsh Act (see below), media in child pornography cases was routinely granted to defense computer forensics experts via court order. Such is not the case anymore.

The Adam Walsh Child Protection Act (18 USC 3509) signed into law by President George W. Bush in July 2006 made vast and sweeping changes to federal child protection laws. Section (m) of that act states:

(1) In any criminal proceeding, any property or material that constitutes child pornography shall remain in the care, custody, and control of either the Government or the court.

(2) (A) Notwithstanding Rule 16 of the Federal Rules of Criminal Procedure, a court shall deny, in any criminal proceeding, any request by the defendant to copy, photograph, duplicate, or otherwise reproduce any property or material that constitutes child pornography, so long as the Government makes the property reasonably available to the defendant.

(B) For purposes of subparagraph (A), property or material shall be deemed to be reasonably available to the defendant if the Government provides ample opportunity for inspection, viewing, and examination at a Government facility.

Many (but not all) state prosecutors are following this rule and refusing to allow forensics experts to have custody of media that is alleged to contain child pornography. (As an aside, it should be noted that it is the mere allegation that an image is child pornography which triggers the Act, an issue that is often the very point of the case when decisions about whether an item is child pornography is based on law enforcement opinion.) California, Washington, Idaho, Colorado and several others are notable exceptions and courts in these states will direct copies to be made and turned over to experts for analysis in criminal cases, with judges generally finding that the Adam Walsh rules apply to federal cases, not state.

The current status in Oregon (in the experience of the author) is that with surrounding states choosing not to follow Adam Walsh, arguing for court-ordered release of media would likely be successful. The experience of the author has been that media in most state and FBI cases brought within Oregon can be examined at the Regional Computer Forensics Laboratory in Portland with comparatively minor issues. However, problems definitely arise no matter how accommodating the law enforcement entity, with trial preparation. It is simply not possible to adequately prepare in the final days leading up to trial without having ongoing, as needed access to the media—access which law enforcement entities and prosecutors do have, and obviously use to their advantage.

What Exactly Is Child Pornography?

For an image or movie to be legally classifiable and prosecuted as child pornography, it needs two major qualifications:

• An image of a child under 18 years of age

• The image needs to be pornographic.

If either of these two fundamental elements is missing, the image is not legally child pornography.

“Child”

For the purposes of child pornography laws, a “child” is defined as anyone under the age of 18. This is not typically in question if the people featured in the images are prepubescent but is very much in question if the people in the images are alleged to be teens. There are literally thousands of “teen” pornography web sites on the Internet, many with extremely young-looking models that are in fact 18 years of age and in many cases much older than that. United States law requires that all producers of child pornography keep records of identification on file as to the names and ages of actors and models portrayed (18 USC 2257) and all United States–based (and many foreign-based) pornographic web pages have this disclaimer. Indeed, true child pornography web sites tend to take the opposite approach and make sure the visitor to the page fully knows that they have found real child pornography material by clearly announcing so and certainly no 2257 disclaimer will be found.

This is an important consideration because many people who have not been exposed to Internet pornography, and certainly the vast majority of people who have not been exposed to child pornography, will make the assumption that an image of a “young-looking” model or actor seen on a computer is child pornography when it is not. A forensic analysis that includes a review of web site activity may indeed reveal whether images in question came from a site with an 18 USC 2257 disclaimer, thus giving rise to a presumption the images are legal. Add to this that, as a pornography genre, web pages offering images of extremely young looking yet legal age models are a very large Internet market, and it is obvious why an investigation should occur as to the origination point of pubescent images.

“Pornographic”

Child pornography laws also require the image to be “pornographic” in nature. Pornographic, as applied to actual sexual interaction, is not hard to discern; however, the question becomes murky when no obvious sexual acts are portrayed. In these circumstances child pornography laws require there to be a “lascivious exhibition” of the genitals. The federal courts have tended to rely on the case of United States v. Dost, 636 F Supp 828, 833 (S.D. Cal. 1986), and the following six factors in determining if an image is a lascivious exhibition:

  1. whether the focal point of the visual depiction is on the child’s genitalia or pubic area
  2. whether the setting of the visual depiction is sexually suggestive, i.e., in a place or pose generally associated with sexual activity
  3. whether the child is depicted in an unnatural pose, or in inappropriate attire, considering the age of the child
  4. whether the child is fully or partially clothed or nude
  5. whether the visual depiction suggests sexual coyness or a willingness to engage in sexual activity
  6. whether the visual depiction is intended or designed to elicit a sexual response in the viewer.

While the Dost court and decisions like it have attempted to objectify the process of “lasciviousness” with standards, the reality is still that one person’s view of a lascivious picture can be interpreted by someone else as not. This issue can be exceedingly difficult with sentencing guidelines which increase with the volume of material possessed. While there may indeed be images of child pornography in the defendant’s possession, if an increase in the number will enhance sentencing, the government may include more “borderline” images, in either age or lasciviousness. Defending against this strategy by attempting an educated, objective discussion of each image can be extremely difficult for obvious reasons.

The National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC) is the keeper of a database of “known” images of child pornography. The NCMEC database consists of images that have been previously identified by law enforcement as known victims. Typically, images in child pornography cases are forwarded to the NCMEC to see if they match others in the database. When they do, NCMEC sends back a report of verification but verifies merely that the image matches one in the database. NCMEC will supply with that verification a law enforcement contact for the image, and it is actually this contact who would testify as to the authenticity of the image. Generally speaking, images in the NCMEC database are what they purport to be, however, one caveat: there may be images verified in the database as known victims that are not pornographic. In other words, images that were part of a known series but that particular image is not lascivious, such as the child was clothed for instance.

One final point as it relates to the definition of child pornography: Experience has shown that on occasion defendants themselves have believed they held and confessed to items as being “child pornography” where a subsequent investigation revealed they may not have been. In other words, not every confession to “looking at child pornography” means per se that the defendant did so. An examination and investigation into what was viewed is necessary to confirm if the images were truly of 1) a person under 18 years of age, and 2) also pornographic.

We Have Child Pornography—What’s Next?

If there is reason to believe that contraband exists on the media based on an evaluation of the two considerations discussed above, the next step is to determine how the material came to be on the media and who is responsible for its presence. The determination of how the images came to be on the media (i.e. file sharing, web surfing, transferred from other media, etc.) will greatly help in determining who was at the keyboard and their intent while there.

In a web surfing case, for instance (as anyone who uses the Internet with a web browser and search engine knows), the Internet is full of pop-ups and re-directions, particularly in the realm of pornography web pages. Every image on every page that opens is cached to the computer’s cache folders and, although they may indeed be present on the computer, they may or may not be there as a result of a purposeful intent to look for them. The key is typically what was searched for and what was done as a result of those searches.

Child pornography statutes require a “possession” or some sort of dominion and control of the image on the part of the defendant. When images are cached to the computer, they are “saved” because of the actions of the operating system, not necessarily the defendant. In order to show a purposeful (knowledgeable) “possession” of an image in the computer’s cache folders, typically the government will attempt to show an image was sought out by the use of specific search terms and then dominion and control were exercised by the defendant when clicking on a thumbnail to create an enlargement, for instance. Such cases require a careful moment-by-moment analysis of the browser’s actions on the part of the defense examiner.

File-sharing cases—such as those seen with Limewire applications—are common in many defense offices. At first blush a defendant with several images of child pornography in a Limewire “shared” folder indicates not only possession but potential distribution as well. With law enforcement hash set searching through file-sharing networks as discussed above, these cases appear with regularity. When the client informs the defense attorney he was unaware he possessed the images, is it possible to determine if that was the case? The answer can sometimes be found in the terms searched through the network and the pattern of downloads. Frequent users of these networks quickly learn that files available for download may or may not match their file titles. A movie file with a title of “Batman Returns” may in fact be anything—from what the title purports to a Hustler magazine XXX latest release. Prolific pornography collectors know this and the highlighting and mass downloading of pornographic files regardless of title is a common occurrence. The user may or may not review these files for some time, thereby not knowing a contraband file is on their computer.

Potential Defense Strategies

Obviously, the question of defense strategies should always begin with ascertaining whether the material in question is 1) pornographic and 2) depicts a minor as discussed above. Assuming these elements are satisfied, the investigation moves to how the images came to be on the media and, if possible, who put them there.

Great care needs to be taken in a decision to follow a particular defense strategy and it is vital that it be supported by the data. The random blaming of “viruses” or “Trojans” that turn a user’s computer into an Internet zombie receptor of child pornography will probably not be successful unless the computer is found to be (or have been) infected with such issues. This is not to say such programs don’t exist, just that a defense based on this theory will need to be supported by objective evidence from the computer.

Likewise, more than one vindictive spouse has attempted to win a child custody battle or move on to a new relationship with the assets of the previous one by arranging for child pornography to be on the opposing spouse’s machine. There have been instances where this has been done by the spouse alone as well as cases where confederates were recruited to help. A careful and thorough investigation by the forensic examiner will likely uncover evidence to support such a claim if it is true. The decision to affect such a defense strategy will need to be supported by data or likely will not be successful.

Assembly and Presentation of Findings

Completing the investigative analysis, the examiner needs to be able to pull the investigation together and present his or her findings in an articulate, understandable way. This is typically done with a narrative report. In addition to a conveyance of findings, the report should also explain in laymen’s terms where the data of interest was found and, in a simplified manner, any thing technically relevant about the data. For instance, if web browsing is of importance to the case and the examiner found deleted web pages that are relevant, the report should identify those pages, the information on them, as well as a brief explanation of how web pages in general are cached by the operating system and recovered by the forensic software. These explanations need to be focused on relevant findings only and not a diatribe on the expert’s computer knowledge. In other words, the report’s technical writings should be limited only to explain where the data was found, how it was found and why it appears to be relevant.

The report should also include textual screen captures taken during the forensic process. Screen capture software allows the examiner to take a “snapshot” of what is being observed on the forensic machine’s computer screen and creates a visual demonstrative aid to the report. These screen captures can also be used in the courtroom, allowing a visual aid that the examiner can use in describing how and where data was found. As the narrative of the report flows, reference should be made to individual screen captures to assist the reader’s understanding.

The Examiner

Considering the myriad issues in these types of cases, choosing the forensic examiner is obviously quite important. The expert should have considerable experience in the investigation of child pornography, can make valid calls on the presence of true child pornography, and can determine how the material came to be on the machine in question.

Experts who are willing to put forth defense theories based on technical “what if” speculation are rarely successful and do a disservice to the field. The technical aspects and jargon associated with computer technology in general leaves abundant room for an unscrupulous expert to confuse a jury and proffer opinions not based in a realistic evaluation of the data. Such experts rarely last long as they are quickly impeached by trained, experienced government experts and savvy prosecutors. Experience in the field itself with multiple positive references should be the guide in choosing the expert. While EnCase and FTK software both offer certifications, these are for demonstrated, competent use of the software, not experience in how to effectively and competently investigate a case.

Questions to ask a potential forensic expert include:

• evidence handling and storage procedures

• how they document their evidence handling

• acquisition procedures and documentation

• forensic software used

• previous experience with the fact scenario in question

• previous court experience

• verifiable references that have used the expert with previous positive results.

As with any expert hired by the defense, the computer forensics expert needs a polished and professional demeanor in court for the presentation of findings. It is exceedingly important that he be able to explain the complexities of the forensics process in a simple manner without “computerese.” Judges and jurors may have no concept of the interworking of computers and how data is stored and retrieved and few will have any knowledge at all about how forensic software recovers deleted data. The expert needs to be able to explain the process as well as investigative findings in a manner that requires no technical expertise on the part of the listener. If you are not able to follow the expert’s explanation of the process, neither will the jury.

Another critical component of the expert’s role is assistance in cross-examination of opposing experts. In cases where the examiner uncovers deleterious information and it would be unwise to put him or her on the witness stand, they should still be present for all direct testimony discussing any aspect of the digital evidence in the case (whether by layperson or expert) and, from that, deliver well-thought, relevant questions for cross-examination. The presence of a qualified expert in the courtroom often dramatically helps in keeping the opposition’s expert “honest.” Your expert should be watching and help you with cross-examination questions “on the fly” that keep testimony in check.

Returning to Our Scenarios

Scenario One

Despite convincing circumstantial evidence to the contrary, our suspect husband is actually innocent. Although the family’s home desktop computer is not seized or examined by the police, the defense attorney has the computer examined for anything that might bear on the case. Among other searches, the examiner uses a forensic software to parse and decode chat logs created by the use of Yahoo Instant Messenger. In decoding the recovered chats, she encounters a user name and associated chats that appear to have been between the deceased wife and a heretofore unknown male individual of suspicious nature. The decoded chat logs clearly show a torrid affair had been going on between the two for several months. As the examiner reads through the chats she learns that in recent weeks the deceased had attempted to end the affair, to the dismay of the lover. The lover’s chats reveal veiled threats of blackmail and/or violence if the affair is ended. The examiner further sees that the last chat with the lover is just one week before her murder. The examiner wonders, did the husband uncover the affair and kill his wife in a fit of jealousy, or did the lover finally make good on his veiled threats?

She continues her examination and does a timeline analysis of the computer the day the murder took place. She sees activity during the afternoon she associates with the wife including the purchase of a pair of women’s shoes online. There is little activity on the computer in the evening hours of the night of the murder, however, she does note that there is a web site visit to an online stock trading company at 7:40 pm, close to the time the police believe the murder occurred. The husband is questioned by the attorney if he has an online trading account; he reveals he does and that he often places orders for stock trades through it and believes he may even have done so the night of the murder but does not recall exactly when. The attorney prepares a subpoena for records from the stock trading company requesting server logs for the husband’s account. The information returned shows indeed that the account was accessed at 7:40 pm on the evening of the murder and an order for the purchase of ten shares of stock was placed.

The husband’s cell phone tower records had already been retrieved by the police and they showed that his cell phone had not been used the evening in question and ping logs as to its location were not kept by the cell phone provider. The wife’s cell phone had been found powered off with no calls having been made or received the night in question either. The attorney takes the timeline and stock information to the district attorney, who agrees to have the investigators subpoena Yahoo for the IP information associated with the recovered chats. That IP information is provided, leading the police to the lover’s residence. He is questioned by police and ultimately confesses to the murder. In his statement he tells police that he had provided the wife a second cell phone which her husband was unaware of and it was on that phone that calls and text messages regarding clandestine meetings were planned and executed. On the evening of the murder he had taken this phone with him when he left.

Scenario Two

In our second scenario, it is the client’s good fortune that he is an online pornography surfer. The examiner first attempts to recover the corrupted security camera footage from the mini mart robbery but is unsuccessful. The files are simply unreadable and have been recording unreadable footage for some time, unbeknownst to the store manager. Next, the defendant’s computer is examined. It is immediately apparent that the computer has been online and someone has been at the keyboard surfing (adult) pornography web sites. Because the defendant has roommates, the question then becomes Who was at the keyboard? The examiner creates a timeline of events by first verifying the internal clock settings on the computer are correct. He then examines the Windows event logs for abnormalities that would indicate if the clock had been manipulated. He finds none. He next begins an entry-by-entry timeline examination of the web traffic. He notes the visit to a commercial pornography “portal” site that has a server-based embedded date and time. This embedded date and time from the cached web page verifies that the computer clock is correct. The examiner continues the timeline and notes a visit to an adult “matching” site and a log-in to a user account. He sees scrolling through and access of various online profiles, something that can only be done with a paid user account. Finally, he notes that intermittently throughout the evening of pornography surfing there is access to the Internet-based email program Hotmail. Although the examiner is unable to recover any cached web pages showing the content of these emails, he is able to recover several that show the user name of the Hotmail account holder. The client indicates to the attorney that this is indeed his Hotmail account and embarrassingly admits to the adult matchmaking service membership. The examiner locates the Custodian of Records information for the attorney and subpoenas are served. The results of the subpoenas verify the client’s creation (and repeated access) of the accounts as well as an IP number accessing these services on the night in question that resolves back to the client’s residence. A timeline of use is created that shows the client at the keyboard up to, during and after the robbery.

Conclusion

While child pornography crimes continue to account for the majority of digitally based investigations for the criminal defense investigator, as we noted at the beginning of the article, digital evidence is all around us and interwoven in our daily lives. From a homicide case with cell phone triangulation tracking, to child pornography, to a vehicular homicide case with evidence from an onboard GPS, where we go, who we talk to and, at times, even our motivations for doing so are being recorded.

The field of computer forensics is really a field of investigation limited only by the imagination of the investigator. Computer forensics is not just child pornography. For the criminal defense investigator, it is quite possibly going to touch one or more aspects of their case, perhaps in ways not initially considered. It is important for the investigator to think about this new and expanding field outside the box and consider innovative ways to arrive at the truth.
