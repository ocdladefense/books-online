Still at War: A Guide for Defenders, Prosecutors & Judges Dealing with Oregon’s Veteran Defendant Crisis, 2017 edition 

Chapter 14. Prosecutors’ Considerations

By Michael Swanson*

The foundation principles of criminal law found in Article 1, section 15, of the Oregon Constitution state: “protection of society, personal responsibility, accountability for one’s actions and reformation.” As prosecutors, these four goals guide us as we review reports, prepare charging instruments, make offers, and try cases. Although we must ensure that we treat similarly situated people in a similar way, we cannot lose sight of the fact that each defendant is an individual. Therefore, we must be able to fine-tune each resolution for each defendant.

Klamath County has a rich history of treatment courts. Our drug court was the first such court established in the county. After more than 20 years, it is still going strong and serving the needs of defendants. Many of the players (judges, prosecutors, defense attorneys, and treatment providers) have changed during that time, but the court’s core mission remains the same—guiding those who struggle with drug addiction to find the help they need to lead productive lives. Drug court serves as a model for all of the other treatment courts we have, including family court, veterans court and, most recently, mental health court.

The Klamath County District Attorney’s office, along with our community partners, established the Klamath County Veterans Court in November 2009.1 Starting in the early 2000’s, we saw a disturbing rise in crimes involving veterans. The crimes ranged from DUII and PCS to violent assault. One thing that many of these cases had in common was a recently returned veteran from a war zone with a substance abuse issue. We quickly learned that most of the substance abuse was tied to self-medication for mental or emotional issues arising from their time in the armed services. As a result, the district attorney at the time, Edwin Caleb, reached out to the courts and the local defense attorneys with the hope of addressing these issues. That led to a team going to Buffalo, New York, to learn about the mechanics of developing a specialized court to address and help these individuals.

Over the years, the team has modified the entry considerations. At first, crimes of violence or involving weapons were barred. But owing to the target population and the special challenges they faced, we changed that policy very quickly. Currently, if a defendant is a veteran and has exhibited substance abuse issues, or is experiencing a service-related issue (such as post-traumatic stress disorder), we start by considering the specifics of the veteran’s case. Sex crimes and veterans who have a sexual offense in their criminal history are ineligible, but most other crimes are eligible, including some crimes covered by Ballot Measure 11 (1994)2 if the veterans qualify under ORS 137.712’s exception to Measure 11’s mandatory minimum sentence system.

We consider the criminal history of the defendant as well, although for the most part, veterans’ criminal histories are small or nonexistent. The exception to this are veterans who served prior to the nation’s current war effort. But even then, those veterans’ histories typically consist of low-level crimes involving alcohol or minor acts of violence.

Equally important is victim input. We meet with the victim prior to allowing a veteran into the court. We explain what the court does and why the defendant qualifies. Usually, once the victim hears about the opportunity provided by the court and the responsibilities it places upon the participant, they are supportive of the resolution.

Once our office determines that a veteran qualifies to be a participant in the court, then other evaluations by service providers are performed. These ensure that the veteran will in fact benefit from entering the court.

After this step, the prosecutor and the defense attorney together fashion a resolution to the case. Unlike drug court which will result in a dismissal of the charge upon the successful completion of the obligations, in veterans court we can modify the resolution in a way that is proper for the circumstances. Often the charge is dismissed upon successful completion of the court. At other times only a reduction of the charge from a felony to a misdemeanor is allowed. This flexibility is a key factor because we can be more creative and allow more veterans to join the program.

Some have questioned if having a veterans court equates to treating service members as a special class. I would direct those people to ORS 137.090(2), which states: “[I]n determining mitigation, the court may consider evidence regarding the defendant’s status as a servicemember as defined in ORS 135.881.”3 Moreover, in returning to the foundation principles found in Article 1, section 15, of the Oregon Constitution, veterans court is based upon the principle that it will only be successful if the veteran steps up to take responsibility for his or her actions. From that first step, all the other principles are upheld—society is protected because the participant is held accountable by the providers and peers within the court as each veteran learns how to reform his or her reactions to the challenges faced in everyday life.

I have been the prosecutor assigned to our veterans court for the last several years. During that time some of the transformations which defendants have undergone is nothing short of miraculous. The one which was most striking was a young man in his mid to late 20s. He had developed a serious substance addiction because he was self-medicating to address a service-connected emotional issue. He was not a fit for any of the other treatment courts, and he was being arrested weekly for crimes ranging from disorderly conduct to unlawful possession of methamphetamine.

The team was very concerned, but as a group we all agreed that this young person needed help or he would end up dead. The first few months were a struggle owing to his emotional turmoil and distrust of “the system.” But once that wall was broken down, he was a different person. He became cheerful and outgoing. He got a job and worked his way up from being a bag boy at a grocery store to being a manager. Due to his hard work in treatment and his dedication to overcoming his challenges, he graduated from the court early and in the several years since we have not seen his name on the custody roster.

That is just one person’s transformation. We have helped several more and have not seen them again. This court is a leading means of protecting our community, having people take personal responsibility for their actions, and giving them the treatment and skills to reform their ways and once again become a productive member of society.
