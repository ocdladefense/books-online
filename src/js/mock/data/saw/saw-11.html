Chapter 11. The Effects of Arrest, Conviction & Incarceration on Federal VA Benefits

(Pre-Entry Considerations)

By Katrina Eagle*

The phrase “words have meaning” is especially true when a veteran faces incarceration. An arrest does not affect the monthly compensation or pension payments a veteran receives in from the federal Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). In fact, even standing trial and being found guilty does not automatically affect a veteran’s monthly VA payments adversely. Only a conviction that includes incarceration in excess of 60 days will impact the amount paid to the veteran on a monthly basis. Even then, veterans who are incarcerated are not restricted from applying for service-connected disability compensation, pension, survivors’ dependency and indemnity compensation (DIC), or education assistance.

I. Four Criteria Required Before the VA May Lawfully Restrict VA Benefit Payments

The VA uses the following step-by-step analysis to determine whether the beneficiary (i.e., a veteran or a family member) of VA benefits has been incarcerated such that their monthly VA compensation may be divested:

  1. The beneficiary must be incarcerated.

  2. The incarceration must be in a federal, state, or local penal institution1 or a penal institution run by a private entity under contract with the federal, state, or local government2.

  3. The incarceration must be in excess of 60 days.

  4. The incarceration must be as a result of a felony conviction.

A felony is defined as “any offense punishable by death or imprisonment for a term exceeding one year, unless specifically categorized as a misdemeanor under the law of a prosecuting jurisdiction.”3 The sixty-first day is defined as sixty-one days after guilt is pronounced by a judge or jury and the beneficiary is incarcerated in a penal institution because of the determination of guilt.4 Once a veteran is convicted, the VA may apply the statute immediately. This means the VA is not required to wait until all appeals and collateral remedies have been exhausted and the veteran has received a “final” conviction.

II. Statute Limitations and Limitations on Disability Compensation and Dependency and Indemnity Compensation (DIC) Payments During Incarceration

The statute, 38 USCS § 5313, which authorizes the VA to limit the amount of monthly compensation benefit payments has its own limits; that is, it does not lower the veteran’s service-connected disability rating level. In addition, payments are not reduced for recipients participating in work release programs, residing in halfway houses (also known as “residential re-entry centers”), or under community control. Succinctly stated, the only aspect that changes when a veteran is incarcerated for 61 days or more is the amount actually paid to him or her.

The significance of this is twofold. First, upon the veteran’s release from incarceration, the amount paid returns to the level of the actual disability rating.5 Thus it is important for service-connected disabled veterans to challenge any adverse rating actions made during their incarceration. Similarly, initial applications for benefits and claims for increased ratings may be filed and pursued during incarceration, even though any favorable action by the VA in this regard will not benefit the veteran until after she or he is released from prison.6 Second, although the incarcerated beneficiary of the VA compensation benefits may receive only a limited portion of the full amount, the balance of the benefits may be “apportioned” to an eligible family member (see Section IV, below).

The question arises as to how much monthly compensation an incarcerated veteran may receive. The actual amount of disability compensation paid varies according to the veteran’s disability rating. If the disability is (or combined disabilities are) rated as 20 percent disabling or more, the veteran will receive only the amount payable at the 10 percent level.7 If the veteran is rated as 10 percent disabled, he or she will receive half of the 10 percent rate.8 Incarcerated beneficiaries of DIC are also restricted to half the amount paid to a veteran receiving compensation payments at the 10 percent disability level.9

With respect to education benefits, once a beneficiary is incarcerated in excess of 60 days, he or she may be paid only the costs of tuition, fees, and necessary books, equipment, and supplies. Moreover, the VA will not make payments for tuition, fees, books, equipment, or supplies if another federal, state, or local program pays these costs in full.

III. Limitations on Pension Payments During Incarceration

Unlike disability compensation or DIC benefits, the VA completely stops pension payments to incarcerated beneficiaries.10 Moreover, VA pension benefits are stopped for any type of conviction that results in incarceration, whether the offense is a misdemeanor or a felony.11 Likewise, cessation of pension payments begins on the sixty-first day of incarceration. That said, pension benefits otherwise payable to the incarcerated beneficiary may be apportioned to family members, as with disability compensation and DIC benefits. The effective dates of termination, and the resumption of pension payments, are the same as those for compensation. Thus, for the reasons discussed in the next section, it is important for pension beneficiaries to notify the VA promptly of their incarceration, release, and the desire for any apportionment of the benefits.

IV. Veterans’ Options Regarding Disability Compensation and DIC Payments During Incarceration and After Release

First and foremost, it is important the VA be notified promptly of the commencement of incarceration. While it is not a codified regulation yet, in January 2011 the VA proposed adding an explicit requirement that beneficiaries inform the VA if they are incarcerated.12 The penalty of not notifying the VA is that any benefits received after sixty days of incarceration will be considered an overpayment that the beneficiary will have to repay.

It is also important that the beneficiary notify the VA promptly when the period of incarceration has ended. The VA regulations provide that, if the beneficiary notifies the VA of his or her release within one year after release, regular benefits payments will be resumed at the full rate to which the beneficiary is entitled, retroactive to the date of release; otherwise, they will be resumed at the full rate as of the date of receipt of the notice of release.13

During the period of incarceration, the balance of the veteran’s benefits may be “apportioned” to his or her family.14 Thus, though an incarcerated beneficiary of service-connected disability compensation evaluated 80 percent disabled may receive only the amount he or she would receive if evaluated 10 percent disabled, the beneficiary’s family may be apportioned the balance of the compensation benefit. DIC benefits may also be apportioned to the family of an incarcerated beneficiary.15 The same is true for pension benefits, except that the incarcerated beneficiary may not receive pension benefits while incarcerated; instead, those benefits may be apportioned to the veteran’s family members.16

An apportionment of an incarcerated veteran’s VA benefits is not granted automatically. Rather, the incarcerated beneficiary or his or her family members must apply by using VA Form 21-0788. Although arising from a veteran’s benefits claim, an apportionment is an entitlement legally separate from those benefits and may be paid to the veteran’s spouse, child or children, and dependent parents, on the basis of individual need. Once an application for apportionment has been filed, the VA will request and consider the applicant’s needs based on factors such as the claimant’s income, living expenses, the amount of compensation available to be apportioned, and the needs and living expenses of other claimants. Finally, when the beneficiary’s incarceration ends and benefits are restored, apportionment also ends.

However, if the conviction that served as the basis for incarceration is overturned on appeal, the beneficiary is entitled to retroactive payment of all compensation or DIC benefits withheld during the period of incarceration.17 For this reason, it is important to notify the VA of any successful appeal. Nevertheless, this restoration does not include benefits apportioned to the beneficiary’s family during the beneficiary’s incarceration. Also importantly, this restitution does not extend to or include pension benefits which are not service-connected.18

V. How “Fugitive Felon” Determination Affects One’s VA Benefits

The VA defines a fugitive felon in the following two ways:

  1. A person who is fleeing to avoid prosecution, custody or confinement after conviction, for an offense, or an attempt to commit an offense, which is a felony under the laws of the place from which the person flees.

2) A person who has violated the terms of his or her probation or parole imposed for commission of a felony under Federal or State law.19

Related thereto, a person stops being a fugitive felon when the warrant for his or her arrest is cleared by arrest, surrender to the issuing authority, dismissal, or court documents (dated after the warrant for the arrest of the felon) showing that the individual is no longer a fugitive.20

Restrictions on the payment of monetary benefits to beneficiaries who become fugitive felons are even more severe. Payment of benefits is completely prohibited either to a veteran or survivor of a veteran while he or she is a fugitive felon, or to a dependent while either the veteran or the dependent is a fugitive felon.21 The prohibition applies to the following VA benefits: compensation, DIC, pension, medical care, life insurance, vocational rehabilitation, and education benefits.22 Furthermore, no apportionment of these VA benefits is permitted, even if the fugitive felon veteran and his or her spouse are estranged.23

VI. Conclusion

To summarize, there are several critical restrictions placed on beneficiaries of VA benefits and compensation who have been incarcerated for more than sixty days. Most significantly, the amount of their monthly VA compensation will be reduced depending upon the disability rating assigned at the outset of the incarceration. But if approved by the VA, the balance of the beneficiary’s monthly compensation may be apportioned and paid to a qualifying family members. However, even during incarceration, veterans still maintain the right to apply for VA benefits such as service-connected compensation, increased disability ratings, survivors’ DIC, or education assistance.
