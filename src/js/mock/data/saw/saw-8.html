Chapter 8. The Relevance & Influence of Moral Injury

By Jaimie Lusk, PsyD*

Contrary to current military doctrine, empathy and aggression do not go hand in hand. The more extreme one’s environment, the more obvious this becomes.… It is not possible to genuinely strive to help a people, to reach out to them, while simultaneously preparing to kill them. One cannot achieve excellence in both war and humanity at the same time.

—Tyler E. Boudreau (2009)

I. Defining Moral Injury

A. Origin

Jonathan Shay, MD, PhD, a psychiatrist who worked for two decades at the VA National Center for PTSD (Boston), first coined the term “moral injury” in his groundbreaking book, Achilles in Vietnam (1994), in describing events involving a moral wrong committed by an authority in a high-stakes situation. The concept of moral injury is as old as war, and was recorded as early as Homer’s Illiad.

B. Clinical Definition

While clinical research on moral injury is exploding, it is still in its nascent stages. Moral injury was first given this working definition for mental health research purposes in a 2009 comprehensive review:

Perpetrating, failing to prevent, bearing witness to, or learning about acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs and expectations (Litz et al, 2009). Moral injury may include events of perpetration, betrayal or loss (Litz, Lebowitz, & Gray, 2015).

C. Moral Injury Is Not Just an Event that Goes Against Morals

Moral injury is not just an event that goes against morals, but the resultant loss of trust in self and others, and diminished capacity for effective living. In their research, Drescher and Foy (2012) emphasize the implications of moral injury on an individual’s perceived ability to behave ethically, defining moral injury as:

disruption in an individual’s confidence and expectations about their own or others’ motivation or capacity to behave in a just and ethical manner brought about by bearing witness to perceived immoral acts, failure to stop such actions, or perpetration of immoral acts, in particular actions that are inhumane, cruel, depraved, or violent, bringing about pain and suffering of others or their death.

D. Legal Definition

Judge Advocate Major Erik Masick (2016) synthesized several working definitions of moral injury into a legal definition with three components:

1. An act of transgression, either commission or omission, by a servicemember or unit. This can either be witnessed or heard about, and can be experienced in real time or upon reflection.

2. This act transgressed moral identities and beliefs. Moral injury may result from transgression of individual or collective, civilian or military moral codes.

3. The transgression caused spiritual, social, psychological, emotional, and moral harm.

II. Moral Injury Is Related To—But Unique From—PTSD

A. PTSD

Diagnostically, post-traumatic stress disorder involves experiencing or witnessing a traumatic event. Events resulting in moral injury may fit this criteria, but more specifically involve acts of perpetration or betrayal.

B. Moral Injury Is Not Just a Subtype of PTSD

Before the DSM-5, the PTSD diagnosis did not include guilt and shame. With the inclusion of these emotions in the DSM-5, the overlap between PTSD and moral injury is greater. Some clinicians and researchers argue that moral injury is a particular type of PTSD. But in a recent factor analysis of symptoms of 930 National Guard veterans, symptoms of flashbacks, memory loss, nightmares, insomnia, and startle reflex were correlated (corresponding with PTSD), while symptoms of low enjoyment, low cohesion, anger, shame, guilt, hostility inward were correlated (corresponding with moral injury); both sets of symptoms were associated with depression (Bryan, et al., 2017). This study indicates that moral injury is related to—but distinct from—PTSD.

C. Moral Injury Is Not a Psychological Diagnosis

Moral injury is not a diagnosis and has no diagnostic threshold. Moral injuries encompass more than one individual’s psychology; they involve collective morals and an individual’s perceived place in society. As Brown, Stanulis and McElroy stated, “Moral Injury can be a consequence of the collection of both individual and social/institutional contradictions that affect the service member’s re-acculturation process back into the civilian culture” (Brown, et al., 2016). Given this, moral injury cannot be used to plea insanity. However, Brown and Stanulis have shown through their expert witness testimony that moral injury can be used very successfully in mitigation to explain misconduct.

III. Traumatic Events Leading to Moral Injury

A. Events Commonly Leading to Moral Injury

Traumatic-event exposure leading to moral injury may involve infliction of or failure to prevent trauma, especially the following:

1. Incidents involving civilians (such as the death of a noncombatant, killing women or children, or destruction of property).

2. Acts of disproportionate violence (such as exacting revenge or mistreating enemy combatants).

3. Leadership failures and perceived betrayals (for example, carrying out orders that violate personal values, or witnessing and failing to prevent immoral acts of peers, such as military sexual assault or friendly fire).

B. Particular Vulnerabilities Related to Current Conflicts

Modern warfare has made it particularly difficult to determine “right actions.” Beginning with Vietnam and continuing to the present, U.S. conflicts have been characterized by “ambiguous, inconsistent, or unacceptable rules of engagement (ROE), lack of clarity about the goals of the mission itself, a civilian population of combatants, and inherently contradictory experiences of the mission as both humanitarian and dangerous” (Wood, 2016).

C. Military Training Contributes to Moral Injury

While military personnel are taught to “kill people and break things” in a disciplined and proficient way, the military does not teach servicemembers to integrate their experiences and heal moral wounds. Military training is intended to habituate military members to fear. Servicemembers expect to feel fear, are taught how to cope with fear, and have had practice acting in contexts of fear. The military does not prepare members to cope with sadness, guilt, shame, or experiences of betrayal (Litz, Lebowitz, & Gray, 2015).

D. Killing May Have Particular Implications for Moral Injury

While most individuals are taught by their family and culture “Thou shalt not kill,” the military conditions servicemembers to kill (Grossman, 1995). Killing the “wrong person” may leave individuals even more vulnerable to moral injury.

1. Killing in war is associated with more frequent and severe PTSD symptoms, with those who endorsed killing in the context of anger/revenge or killing a noncombatant endorsing the most severe PTSD symptoms (Maguen, et al., 2013).

2. Veterans’ experiences of killing others and failing to prevent death weakened their religious faith, both directly and as mediated by feelings of guilt (Fontana & Rosenheck, 2004).

E. Military Ethos Contributes to Moral Injury

Cultural norms and values emphasize the necessity for peak performance and moral excellence in war. Perfection is demanded, and mistakes are unacceptable. Servicemembers are taught they are part of an ethical organization and need to do the right thing at all times. Military members are told repeatedly, “If you make a mistake, people die,” and “Complacency kills.” These expectations are easily violated in the fog and friction of war (see Chapter 5).

F. Veterans Can Experience Moral Injury Even If They Do Their Job Perfectly

Even if servicemembers make no mistakes in the line of duty, the act of carrying out their job (for instance, killing the enemy) may be perceived as a mistake later, upon re-acculturation into civilian society. The military “demands moral excellence of [servicemembers] in order to maintain discipline, but at the same time requires that combatants be prepared to perform the morally injurious act of killing human beings” (Wood, 2016, p 59).

G. Veterans Who Become Convinced the Mission Itself Is Unethical May Experience Moral Injury No Matter What They Do

Brown, Stanulis and McElroy (2016) emphasize resultant moral injury when an individual at war comes to realize that “the expectations of an infantryman are in complete contradiction to their core values” (p. 15). This individual who no longer morally supports their mission may be left with the options of carrying out morally injurious orders, offering themselves up to the enemy, or deserting their unit. All of these decisions may result in moral injury. The service member is therefore in a lose-lose situation.

H. Veterans Are Vulnerable to Moral Injury As They Transition to Civilian Life

Moral injuries may result as a veteran attempts to re-acculturate from military to civilian life. The same actions a servicemember may feel proud of in a military context may be a source of shame in a civilian context. For example, in the military a servicemember may be praised for killing, carrying out vigilante justice, “acquiring” (stealing) resources, and using aggression as a problem-solving strategy. As Brown, Stanulis and McElroy (2016) note, when a “soldier takes part in killing in a combat zone, a setting where killing is both acceptable and permissible, he must then live with the ghosts (killing other human beings) of his past behavior in a completely different setting—the civilian culture—which entertains a completely different set of values and morals” (p. 15).

IV. Risk Factors for Moral Injury

War violates many individuals’ personal code—why does this violation affect some so much more than others?

  • Personality-driven factors like being prone to shame.
  • The inability to forgive, the presence or absence of compassionate community. For example, after controlling for demographic and combat factors, one study revealed that difficulties forgiving oneself and others, as well as negative religious coping, were related to depression, anxiety, and PTSD symptom severity (Witvliet et al., 2004).
  • Choices a veteran makes (withdrawing, engaging in self-harm behavior) may exacerbate the pain of moral injury.
  • Military culture, with strict systems of belief and practice, provides individuals with an unambiguous sense of right and wrong, clear rules for living, closeness with like-minded individuals, and a distinctive identity—all are strong advantages at war but contribute to difficulties recovering from moral injury (Litz, Lebowitz, & Gray, 2015).

V. Consequences of Moral Injury

A. Moral Injury Contributes to Problematic Symptoms and Behaviors

Drescher, et al. (2012) found that moral injury uniquely contributes to symptoms such as negative changes in ethics, changes in spirituality, guilt, shame, alienation, forgiveness problems, anhedonia, trust problems (in others and in social contract), aggressive behaviors, poor self-care, and self-harm.

Maguen and Litz (2014) summarize research on moral injury, identifying symptoms such as shame, guilt, anxiety about possible consequences, anger about betrayal, anomie, suicidality, withdrawal, self-condemnation, self-harming, and self-sabotage (including substance use and undermining relationships and employment).

B. Criminality

Researchers have not yet explored a correlation between moral injury and criminality. However, top moral injury researchers hypothesize this link:

1. Diminished Sense of Right and Wrong

Dr. Litz, a leading moral injury researcher, was quoted, “All potentially morally injurious experiences create risk for demoralization and alienation, as well as altered moral expectations (informally termed a ‘broken moral compass’)” (Masick, 2016, p. 247).

2. Adaptive Wartime Behavior May Be Criminal in a Civilian Setting

“[D]iminished capacity or willingness to adhere to laws or values…‘can result in behavior that is simultaneously symptomatic and criminal.’ This [is the]most extreme negative outcome, the connection between combat-trauma and criminal behavior” (Masick, 2016, pp 248-49). This criminal behavior is often behavior that is adaptive and not illegal in the context of war. Destruction of the capacity to trust self and others may be the “single most important criminogenic feature of moral injury” (p. 251).

3. Moral Injury Is Hypothesized to Underlie Criminal Behavior

William Nash, M.D., a leading researcher, educator, and clinical consultant for the Department of Defense and federal Department of Veterans Affairs (VA), reported, “[I]f research were available, it would reveal that moral injury underlies veteran suicide, homelessness, and criminal behavior” (Masick, 2016, p. 246).

4. Shame Is Correlated With Criminality

Moral injury is correlated with shame, and shame is correlated with aggression. For instance, in a sample of 103 men with a history of interpersonal trauma, the trait shame (but not guilt) accounted for the association between PTSD and both physically and psychologically aggressive behavior, as well as the frequency of physical aggression (Schoenleber, et al., 2015). In turn, these acts of aggression may lead to more shame, moral injury, and more acts of aggression.

C. Moral Injury Contributes to Suicidality

Clinical research has associated moral injury with suicide attempts; transgressions committed by self are associated with significantly more suicidal ideation (Bryan et al, 2014).

In the study surveying 930 National Guard members, PTSD severity was associated with increased risk of suicide attempt only among participants with high moral injury. Additionally, moral injury symptoms were associated with increased suicidal ideation, regardless of the level of PTSD symptoms (Bryan, et al., 2017).

VI. Treatment for Moral Injury

If a veteran has PTSD and moral injury, it is ethically important to start with the most validated, evidence-based, trauma-focused PTSD treatments within the veteran population, those being Prolonged Exposure (PE) and Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT). These therapies address many aspects of moral injury through attempts to broaden a veteran’s perspective and to contextualize acts within the military culture, in the midst of wartime stressors. In these treatments, guilt and shame related to hindsight bias, outcome-based reasoning, and role confusion, foreseeability, responsibility, and intent are explored, along with the context in which these actions took place.

Moral injury is not just psychological, and may involve healing in a broader sense. The moral injury–specific treatments below are in preliminary stages of validation; they attempt to address “moral repair” through acceptance, making amends, forgiveness, self-compassion, and reparative behaviors.

1. Adaptive Disclosure

(Litz, Lebowitz, and Gray, 2015 offered at the Portland VA Medical Center)

A six-to-eight session intervention that focuses on moral injuries involving loss, betrayal, and perpetration in the context of military operations. The treatment employs balanced strategies of exposure (“hot cognitive processing”) and reparation, reengagement, reconnection, consolidation of positive meanings, and improved self-care. Veterans ideally learn that they are not judged or rejected when they talk about their moral injury; experiential exercises may modify and broaden perspective.

2. Impact of Killing in War

(Maguen, et al.)

A four-session group intervention currently being pilot-tested. Preliminary data suggest significant improvements in anxiety and depression, greater community involvement, and increased connection with others. Veterans first explore the context of war and biopsychosocial aspects of killing in war that might contribute to moral injury. They then identify personal meaning-making and beliefs related to killing in war. Finally, veterans practice self-forgiveness and compassion, and make amends/take valued actions.

3. Trauma-Informed Guilt Reduction

(Norman, Allard, and Wilkins, (2016, offered at the Portland VA Medical Center)

A four-session intervention including psychoeducation about PTSD, exploration of shame and guilt, guilt/shame appraisal, values development, and making amends. Guilt and shame are influenced by culture. Guilt can be helpful, because it urges us to evaluate the situation and learn from it to increase our chances of survival the next time something similar happens, and it can signal us when we act against our values; and can act as a deterrent. Guilt can become problematic when it is interpreted as a global flaw, turns into shame, or is not based on an accurate interpretation of events. In this treatment, guilt and shame are broken down into smaller parts and addressed separately.

4. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) for Moral Injury

(Morris, Lusk, 2016, offered at the Portland VA Medical Center)

ACT is a scientifically-based psychotherapy that recognizes pain is normal and important, and it can point to what deeply matters to us. Additionally, this therapy recognizes that while we cannot just rid ourselves of psychological and spiritual pain, we can stop adding to our suffering by giving up the struggle to rid ourselves of pain. Paradoxically, accepting and experiencing our pain can
release it.

Portland’s “ACT for Moral Injury” psychospiritual group was created from a partnership of mental health and chaplain services. The group is designed to address moral injury through discussion, exercises, ceremony, and ritual. Veterans learn to notice and step back from thoughts and stories about who they are, what the world is like, and what they deserve based on their moral injury. They then work on acceptance of the actions that a younger version of them perpetrated, witnessed, or experienced. They are asked to revisit aspects of their moral injury, and connect with self-compassion/forgiveness through experientials. Finally, veterans identify ways the pain of moral injury can be transformed into a gift for others through committed actions in service to values. At the end of this group, veterans can hopefully reclaim what matters and have it play a more prominent part in their lives.

A “mock trial” is a creative and potentially cathartic intervention where an individual can go “on trial” for their moral injury, and hear a “sentence” from their peers. Consult editor Jesse Wm. Barton for more
about this.

Ultimately, moral injury may best be healed through effective re-acculturation in the civilian world. Veterans likely will need to find belonging and acceptance in a tribe of humans. This tribe ideally can hear the veteran’s moral injury story, show compassion for them, and restore them to full membership.

VII. Why Attorneys Should Pay Attention to the Influence of Moral Injury

Moral Injury is a newly recognized concept in mental health research. As such, correlations between moral injury and involvement in the criminal justice system have not been established. However, related research establishes the correlation between moral injury and symptoms of shame, loss of ethical compass, substance use, self-sabotage and self-harm. These symptoms have in turn been empirically associated with criminality.

William Brown, PhD, a sociologist who has worked on numerous veteran-defendant cases as an expert witness, typically as part of a multicultural team that includes psychologist Robert Stanulis, has successfully used moral injury in mitigation for dozens of cases nationwide. Through the testimonies of Stanulis and Brown, veterans have had felony charges reduced to misdemeanors, have avoided death sentences, and have had an opportunity to enter mental health treatment in lieu of incarceration.

When defending a veteran client, paint a picture for the judge or jury of the moral dilemmas of modern warfare. Consider an 18-year-old who feared for his life and was asked to make immediate decisions about threat—without hesitation—and flawlessly executed rules of engagement in an environment where pregnant women, children, and developmentally disabled civilians acted as suicide bombers. Each servicemember in a convoy knows that improvised explosive devices are often invisible and are detonated by young children or placed in the carcasses of dead dogs and babies. The individual servicemember faces the impossible task of protecting all innocent civilians, while identifying insurgents who look like civilians. Servicemembers are ordered to bag up charred and dismembered body parts. Many servicemembers have responded to genocides or torture. Servicemembers know that they will be charged and face steep punishments if they do not follow an order, even if that order involves such actions as taking their squad through a known minefield, watching their buddies’ legs blown off in front of them. Servicemembers must entrust each other with their lives, even one who might have violated that trust by perpetrating misconduct against another servicemember. Servicemembers carrying out counterinsurgency operations—including hearts and minds campaigns—have experienced betrayal from the people they are trying to help. Moral injuries can result from a servicemember’s reactionary acts of revenge or “vigilante justice” as they avenge deaths stemming from such betrayals. If a servicemember makes a mistake and a fellow servicemember dies, he may be shamed by others. If a servicemember does her job and the enemy dies, she may then be shamed by civilians who do not understand. Attorneys must strive to help the judge or the jury understand the impossibility of a right action—and resulting moral injury—that many servicemembers face.

The evidence for the existence of moral injury is overwhelming. Moral injury causes mental torture to the very troops whose care is entrusted to American leaders. It leads soldiers to try to drown their sorrows in alcohol or the euphoria of drugs, to be involuntarily separated from the service due to disciplinary action, or to voluntarily leave the service—or the world, by killing themselves—because they feel they cannot cope anymore. It greatly burdens the U.S. military and civilian healthcare systems. It hurts the ability of veterans to positively contribute to society. It distresses and sometimes leads to the physical harm of those who interact with afflicted soldiers. Of all these adverse effects of moral injury, it is the role that moral injury may play in the U.S. military’s high suicide rate that has attracted the most attention.

—Judge Advocate Major Erik Masik, United States Army, presently assigned as Chief, Military Justice, United States Army Cyber Center of Excellence, Fort Gordon, Georgia.
