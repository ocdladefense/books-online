OCDLA Felony Sentencing Manual

Felony Sentencing in Oregon: Guidelines, Statutes, Cases, 2019 edition 

Includes January 2024 Update by Jennelle Meeks Barton

As explained in Chapters 2 and 3, a sentencing court’s first step in imposing a guidelines sentence is to identify the defendant’s criminal history score and the seriousness ranking of his felony conviction. Having done that, the court then locates the defendant’s grid block, i.e., the box on the sentencing guidelines grid where the defendant’s crime seriousness ranking and criminal history score intersect. State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 487, 847 P2d 834 (1993).

The guidelines grid has a total of 99 grid blocks. “Each grid block contains what is called a ‘presumptive sentence,’” 315 Or at 487, i.e., “the sentence provided in a grid block for an offender classified in that grid block by the combined effect of the crime seriousness ranking of the current crime of conviction and the offender’s criminal history or a sentence designated as a presumptive sentence by statute.” OAR 213-003-0001(16). A presumptive guidelines sentence is either a term of imprisonment, a term of probation, or a “straight” jail term. Presumptive probation and presumptive straight jail sentences share the same grid blocks.

A solid black line on the sentencing grid—the “dispositional line,” OAR 213-003-0001(7)—separates the imprisonment grid blocks from the probationary/straight jail grid blocks. Forty-six of the 99 grid blocks specify presumptive probation/straight jail sentences. Davis, 315 Or at 487 n 8.

In certain circumstances, three of the 53 presumptive imprisonment grid blocks allow sentencing courts to impose what are called optional probationary sentences. This chapter first discusses optional probation. It then discusses presumptive probation and straight jail sentences. After that it discusses dispositional-departure probationary sentences—i.e., sentences that may be imposed, for “substantial and compelling reasons,” when the defendant’s presumptive sentence is imprisonment. The chapter then discusses probationary sentences imposed in conditional discharge situations.

The guidelines thus authorize four types of probationary sentences: optional, presumptive, departure, and conditional discharge. After discussing the four types of probationary sentences, this chapter discusses the conditions of probation a court may impose (including jail-term conditions of probation). It concludes by discussing sanctions, including revocation sanctions, a court may impose when a defendant violates probation.

Regardless of the circumstances in which a court imposes probation, it is important to recognize that unlike the pre-guidelines era, when probation was a disposition imposed in lieu of a sentence, so by definition was not a sentence, State v. Gaither, 97 Or App 576, 776 P2d 595 (1989), under the guidelines, probation is a sentence. Holcomb v. Sunderland, 321 Or 99, 104, 894 P2d 457 (1995). Consequently, the court does not, as it did during the pre-guidelines era, suspend imposition of sentence, or impose sentence and then suspend execution, prior to imposing a probationary term. Instead, in the guidelines era the court imposes and executes a sentence of probation. State v. Tallman, 190 Or App 245, 251, 78 P3d 141 (2003); State v. Ferguson, 175 Or App 278, 279, 27 P3d 176 (2001).

§ 5-1. OAR 213-005-0006 OPTIONAL PROBATIONARY SENTENCES.

OAR 213-005-0006 sets out the guidelines’ optional probation provisions:

(1) If an offense is classified in grid blocks 8-G, 8-H or 8-I, the sentencing judge may impose an optional probationary sentence upon making the specific findings on the record:

(a) An appropriate treatment program is likely to be more effective than the presumptive prison term in reducing the risk of offender recidivism;

(b) The recommended treatment program is available and the offender can be admitted to it within a reasonable period of time; and

(c) The probationary sentence will serve community interests by promoting offender reformation.

(2) The sentencing judge shall not impose an optional probationary sentence if:

(a) A firearm was used in the commission of the offense; or

(b) At the time of the offense, the offender was under correctional supervision status for a felony conviction or a juvenile adjudication as defined in OAR 213-003-0001(11); or

(c) The offender’s conviction is for Manufacture of a Controlled Substance involving substantial quantities of methamphetamine, its salts, isomers or salts of its isomers, as defined at ORS 475.996 (1)(a).

(3) A probationary sentence imposed for an offense classified in grid blocks 8-G, 8-H and 8-I when not authorized by this rule is a departure.

§ 5-1.1. Application of Provision.

Grid blocks 8-G, 8-H, and 8-I carry presumptive terms of imprisonment. For certain defendants in these grid blocks, the court may impose an optional probationary sentence. The court may do so if under OAR 213-005-0006(1), it finds that (i) the probationary sentence would promote the defendant’s reformation, (ii) the defendant may be admitted within a reasonable amount of time into an “appropriate treatment program,” and (iii) probation is more likely than a prison term to reduce the defendant’s rate of recidivism and to promote public safety; and if none of the ineligibility provisions of OAR 213-005-0006(2) apply. Those ineligibility provisions apply to a defendant who (i) used a firearm in committing his crime, or (ii) was on “correctional supervision” for a felony when he committed his crime, or (iii) committed the crime of substantial-quantity manufacture of methamphetamine (hereafter meth). Id.

Although the guidelines do not define the phrase “appropriate treatment program,” the Court of Appeals has said it does not mean something “superficial” such as the defendant’s “brain and society at large[.]” State v. Schuh, 112 Or App 362, 366, 829 P2d 1040, rev den, 314 Or 176 (1992). Moreover, Mitigating Factor I, which is used to impose departure probation in cases where OAR 213-005-0006(2) renders otherwise eligible defendants legally ineligible for optional probation, also carries an “appropriate treatment requirement.” See Chapter 7. In a case involving Mitigating Factor I, the Court of Appeals held that “‘an appropriate treatment program * * * to which the offender can be admitted within a reasonable period of time’ * * * carries a connotation of possibility, or at the most, probability; it does not carry a connotation of certainty.” State v. Waage, 160 Or App 156, 165, 981 P2d 333 (1999). This holding should be used in construing the meaning of the “appropriate treatment requirement” in optional-probation situations.

OAR 213-005-0011 and OAR 213-005-0013 authorize sanction units and jail terms for optional probationers. Those provisions are discussed later in this chapter.

§ 5-1.2. Appellate Review of Optional Probation Sentences.

A sentencing court’s decision to impose or not to impose optional probation is not reviewable on appeal. But see Schuh, 112 Or App at 366 (appellate court may review trial court’s incorrect application of optional-probation rule). The ban on appellate review of optional probationary is further discussed in Chapter 9.

§ 5-2. OAR 213-005-0007 PRESUMPTIVE PROBATION AND STRAIGHT JAIL SENTENCES.

OAR 213-005-0007 sets out the guidelines’ presumptive probation and straight jail provisions:

(1) Except as provided by OAR 213-009-0001, or otherwise established by ORS 137.717 or 137.719, if the offense is classified in a grid block below the dispositional line, the presumptive sentence shall be:

(a) A term of probation which may include custody and conditions of supervision or

(b) Straight jail subject to the limits in OAR 213-005-0013.

(2) Each grid block below the dispositional line of the grid includes two components of a presumptive probationary sentence. The top number in each grid block is the number of sanction units that may be imposed as part of a presumptive probationary sentence. The bottom number in each grid block is the maximum number of sanction units that may be imposed as a jail term.

§ 5-2.1. Intent of Provision.

Defendants whose grid blocks fall below the dispositional line have presumptive sentences in one of two forms: probation or straight jail. OAR 213-005-0007(1)(a) and (b). This means the grid blocks below the dispositional line are “presumptive-probation/straight jail term” grid blocks.

The 1993 Legislature approved amendments to former ORS 253-05-007(1) (1989), which now is codified as OAR 213-005-0007(1). The rule authorizes imposing straight jail as presumptive sentences. By definition, a straight jail term is not a prison sentence. See, e.g., ORS 137.124 (distinguishing between sentences served in state Department of Corrections (DOC) prisons, and county jails). That fact, coupled with the rule’s use of the term “straight” jail, indicates that when a court imposes a presumptive sentence of straight jail, it does not also impose a post-prison supervision (PPS) term. See Chap. 4 (discussing PPS terms).

To confirm that straight jail sentences do not include PPS terms, the Criminal Justice Commission proposed an amendment to the guidelines definitional rule, OAR 213-003-0001. The 2009 Legislature approved the amendment, Or Laws 2009, ch 774, § 1(2), and it took effect on January 1, 2010. ORS 171.022. As amended, the rule states: “‘Straight jail’ means a sentence of jail imposed instead of a presumptive probationary sentence that is not followed by a term of post-prison supervision defined in OAR 213-005-0002.” OAR 213-003-0001(20) (emphasis added). The rule thus clarifies that when a court imposes a presumptive sentence of “straight jail,” it does not also impose a term of PPS.

§ 5-2.2. Presumptive Probation and Straight Jail Terms.

In cases where courts impose presumptive sentences of probation, they may impose jail terms as conditions of probation. OAR 213-005-0007(1)(a). OAR 213-005-0013, discussed in Section 5-8, sets the limitations on the durations of jail term conditions of probation.

OAR 213-005-0007(1)(b) explains that OAR 213-005-0013 also sets the limitations on the durations of presumptive straight jail sentences. Those limitations are specifically found in OAR 213-005-0013(1), which, depending on the defendant’s grid-block, sets presumptive straight jail limitations at 30, 60, or 90 days. But under the departure sentencing provisions discussed in Chapter 7, a court could impose straight jail sentences of (i) less than 30, 60, or 90 days; or (ii) of up to double the 30-, 60-, or 90-day limitations, so up to 60, 120, or 180 days.

§ 5-2.3. Sanction Units.

Under OAR 213-005-0007(2), each probation/straight jail grid block authorizes a presumptive maximum number of “sanction” units, which are used when the court chose the presumptive probation option. Sanction units are discussed later in this chapter.

§ 5-3. DURATION OF PROBATION.

OAR 213-005-0008 specifies the duration of probationary sentences:

(1) The presumptive duration of probation shall be determined by the crime seriousness category of the most serious current crime of conviction:

(a) Eighteen months for Crime Categories 1-2;

(b) Two years for Crime Categories 3-5;

(c) Three years for Crime Categories 6-8; and

(d) Five years for Crime Categories 9-11.

(2) (a) Subject to OAR 213-003-0001(8) and paragraph (e) of this section, the sentencing judge may without departure impose a duration of bench probation other than the presumptive durations in section (1) of this rule when necessary to ensure the conditions and purposes of probation are met, or extend the length of probation subject to OAR 213-005-0008(2)(e) upon finding a violation or violations of the conditions of probation or when necessary to ensure that the conditions of probation are completely satisfied;

(b) Subject to subsection (e) of this section, the sentencing judge may by departure impose a greater term of supervised probation when necessary to ensure that the conditions and purposes of probation are met;

(c) When an offender is convicted of violating or attempting to violate a crime of a sexual nature, as described in ORS 163.365, 163.375, 163.395, 163.405, 136.408, 163.411, 163.425 or 163.427 and the court suspends imposition or execution of the sentence or imposes a probation sentence, the court shall sentence the offender to probation for at least five years and no more than the maximum statutory indeterminate sentence for that offense.

(d) When an offender not subject to subsection (c) is convicted of a crime of a sexual nature (including but not limited to ORS 163.305 - 163.465, 163.525, 167.670, 163.673, 163.677, 163.680), or the sentencing judge finds a sex offender treatment program is appropriate, the judge may without departure impose a sentence of probation up to 5 years;

(e) A probationary term shall not exceed five years.

(3) The time during which the offender has absconded from supervision and a bench warrant has been issued for the offender‘s arrest shall not be counted in determining the time served on a sentence of probation.

(4) Nothing in this rule shall preclude the sentencing judge from imposing a period of bench probation as the probationary sentence required or permitted by the sentencing guidelines.

§ 5-3.1. Standard Probationary Terms.

OAR 213-005-0008(1) explains that crime seriousness levels set the duration of a defendant’s standard term of probation. The terms are:

• (a) 18 months for crimes in seriousness levels 1 and 2.

• (b) Two years for crimes in levels 3 to 5.

• (c) Three years for crimes in levels 6 to 8

• (d) Five years for crimes in levels 9 to 11.

The above terms apply to optional, presumptive, departure, and (presumably) conditional discharge probationary terms.

§ 5-3.2. Extended Probationary Terms.

OAR 213-005-0008(2)(a)-(d) authorize sentencing courts to impose extended probationary terms. OAR 213-005-0008(2)(a) authorizes extended terms in three different situations. First, the court may impose an extended term at original sentencing if it imposes it as bench probation, and if doing so is “necessary to ensure that the conditions of probation are completely satisfied.” Id. Second and third, OAR 213-005-0008(2)(a) authorizes the court, after original sentencing, to extend the term of probation upon finding that the defendant is in violation of his probation, or when necessary to ensure that the defendant satisfies the conditions of probation (e.g., the payment of restitution). But before extending a probationary term, the court must hold a hearing on the matter. It may not lengthen a probationary term ex parte. State v. Warner, 118 Or App 726, 727, 848 P2d 146 (1993).

See also State v. Lucas, 317 Or App 319, 504 P3d 706 (2022) (because the trial court failed to provide defendant an opportunity for a contested case hearing, the court erred in extending defendant’s probationary term from 24 to 48 months).

In State v. Westom, 320 Or App 250, 512 P3d 850 (2022), the defendant was convicted under separate judgments for criminally negligent homicide and third-degree assault. On the greater crime, the court delayed sentencing. But on the lesser crime, it imposed a three-year sentence of probation, which included a condition barring the defendant from having contact from the victim’s mother. Later, the court held that the defendant violated the condition, by knowingly remaining in the mother’s “immediate presence.” 320 Or App at 254. The court then imposed a three-year probationary term on the greater crime, and extended the probationary term on the smaller crime. On appeal the defendant claimed that being in the mother’s “immediate presence” was an insufficient contact. The Court of Appeals held that the trial court did not err. Although the term “contact” is “undefined,” “[k]nowledge, proximity, and the duration of the contact can all bear on the issue. * * * The trial court reasonably determined that defendant’s knowing decision to remain in the victim’s mother’s immediate presence * * * fell within the center of the prohibition.” Id. at 259.

OAR 213-005-0008(2)(b) authorizes extended probationary terms by departure. See also State v. Maki, 115 Or App 367, 369-70, 838 P2d 636 (1992) (upholding sentencing court’s imposition of exceptionally long probationary term); State v. Dotter, 114 Or App 1, 4, 833 P2d 1369 (1992).

The aggravating factors used to impose a durational departure term of probation are subject to Sixth Amendment constitutional requirements of Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 US 466, 120 S Ct 2348, 147 L Ed 2d 435 (2000), which are discussed in Chapter 1. See State v. Howe, 198 Or App 568, 109 P3d 391 (2005) (court erred in basing 36-month durational departure term of probation on its non-juried aggravating factor).

For convictions for completed and attempted first- and second-degree rape, sodomy, sexual penetration, and sexual abuse, a sentencing court must impose a probationary term of up to five years, OAR 213-005-0008(2)(c), and, without declaring a departure, may impose a probationary sentence of as long as the crime’s ORS 161.605 statutory maximum. ORS 137.012. Finally, OAR 213-005-0008(2)(d) authorizes a sentencing court, without a departure, to impose a probationary term of up to five years if the defendant stands convicted “a crime of a sexual nature” that is not covered by OAR 213-005-0008(2)(c), or if the court finds that “a sex offender treatment program is appropriate[.]” OAR 213-005-0008(2)(d).

§ 5-3.2.1. Maximum Length of Extended Terms.

OAR 213-005-0008(2)(e) says that under no circumstances may a court extend probation beyond a term of five years. As previously discussed, the presumptive probationary terms for crimes ranked at seriousness levels 9 to 11 is five years. OAR 213-005-0008(1)(d). Thus, the extension provisions of OAR 213-005-0008(2)(a)-(d) have no application to crimes ranked at levels 9 to 11. Furthermore, the extension provisions do not apply to sex offenders initially given probationary terms of five years or longer under ORS 137.012.

The extension provision also addresses situations where, to try to meet the purposes of probation, the court wishes to extend the term. The court may “extend the length of probation subject to OAR 213-005-0008(2)(e) upon finding a violation or violations of the conditions of probation[.]” OAR 213-005-0008(2)(a). Under OAR 213-005-0008(2)(e), the court may not extend the term of probation to longer than five years. This five-year limit irreconcilably conflicts with the six-year limit set by ORS 137.010(4), which states:

“[U]pon a later finding that a defendant sentenced to probation for a felony has violated a condition of the probation and in lieu of revocation, the court may order the period of both the suspended sentence and the sentence of probation extended until a date not more than six years from the date of original imposition of sentence.”

For two reasons, this six-year limit has no application in guidelines.

First, the legislature created the six-year limit in 1981. Or Laws 1981, ch 181, § 1(3). It created the five-year limit in 1989, when it approved the sentencing guidelines. Or Laws 1989, ch 790, § 87. OAR 213-005-0008(2)(e)’s five-year limit thus is the later in time of the two provisions, and it irreconcilably conflicts with the six-year term. Under the “implied amendment” doctrine, the five-year provision controls. E.g., State v. Vedder, 206 Or App 424, 430-31, 136 P3d 1128 (2006), rev den, 342 Or 417 (2007) (where two provisions irreconcilably conflict, provision created later in time controls).

Further, the six-year statute applies when the court “suspended sentence.” This is consistent with the statute’s opening clause, which states: “ If the court suspends the imposition or execution of a part of a sentence for an offense other than a felony committed on or after November 1, 1989[.]” ORS 137.010(4) (emphasis added). As previously explained, the concept of suspending sentences does not exist in guidelines sentencing. The is why the statute’s opening clause references “an offense other than a felony committed on or after November 1, 1989,” with that date being the guidelines effective date. Thus, the text makes clear that the six-year statute applies only in sentencing crimes committed before the guidelines effective date, rendering the statute inapplicable in guidelines cases.

In State v. Miller, 224 Or App 642, 199 P3d 329 (2008), the court examined the scope of OAR 213-005-0008(3)—in particular, by reconciling it with ORS 137.545(1), which provides in pertinent part:

“Subject to the limitations in ORS 137.010 and to rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission for felonies committed on or after November 1, 1989:

“(a) The period of probation shall be as the court determines and may, in the discretion of the court, be continued or extended.”

In Miller, the defendant had absconded from probation. As a consequence, the trial court extended his probationary term by one year. On the expiration date of his extended term, the state brought a revocation action. A week later the court issued a bench warrant.

The Court of Appeals observed, “If a circuit court commences a revocation proceeding during a defendant’s period of probation, through a show cause order or a bench warrant, the court retains jurisdiction over the defendant even after probation ends.” Miller, 224 Or App at 645 (emphasis added). In Miller, the defendant argued that because the trial court did not issue the warrant until one week after the extended probationary term previously had expired, the court lacked jurisdiction to revoke his probation. The state countered, and the trial court agreed, that OAR 213-005-0008(3) operated to automatically extend the defendant’s probationary term for an amount of time equal to the time that he was on abscond, which, the state claimed, was long enough for the trial court to retain jurisdiction to revoke probation. Miller, 224 Or App at 645.

The Court of Appeals reversed. The court explained that ORS 137.545(1)(a) “suggests that a judicial order is necessary to set or extend a term of probation. The period of probation is ‘as the court determines.’ A period of probation may be continued or extended only ‘in the discretion of the court,’ i.e., by a judicial order.” Miller, 224 Or App at 646. Although the court later noted that “we do not decide if OAR 213-005-0008(3) operates to extend a period of probation as a matter of law before a court rules,” Miller, 224 Or App at 648 n 3, the court’s analysis of ORS 137.545(1)(a) casts doubt on the state’s construction of OAR 213-005-0008(3). The court concluded:

“We do not need to resolve whether OAR 213-005-0008(3) operates independently of a judicial order on a term of probation. Any independent effect would be superseded by a judicial order reestablishing the term of probation. At best, the rule could mean that the time of absconding must be added to a period of probation until a court has issued an appropriate sanction on the probation violation. But once a court has acted on that violation, and adjudicated any change to the probation term, the ‘period of probation shall be as the court determines’ under ORS 137.545(1)(a) and not otherwise. Any different effect of the rule—that it works to reach behind a judicial sanction for a probation violation and to modify that sanction by increasing the term of probation—makes the rule inconsistent with the text of ORS 137.545(1)(a). The rule should not be interpreted to create that disharmony. Limiting the effect of the rule once a court sanctions a defendant avoids any contention of double jeopardy that might arise when a single probation violation of absconding from supervision is sanctioned twice—once by the sanction imposed in the probation revocation proceeding and later when the probationary period is recalculated and extended.”

Miller, 224 Or App at 647 (court’s emphasis).

Moreover, the text of OAR 213-005-0008(3) casts further doubt on the construction of the rule that the state advanced in Miller. The rule says that a defendant’s term of probation is tolled during the time he is on abscond status “and a bench warrant has been issued for [his] arrest[.]” OAR 213-005-0008(3) (emphasis added). Thus, there is no tolling until such time that the court issues a bench warrant.

In State v. LaCoe, 323 Or App 74, 81, 522 P3d 18 (2022), the trial court revoked the defendant’s probation based on a supplemental allegation that was filed four months after conclusion of the probation period. The four-month delay would have supported the revocation, but only if the defendant had absconded from probation. OAR 213-005-0008(3) (“[t]he time during which the offender has absconded from supervision and a bench warrant has been issued for the offender‘s arrest shall not be counted in determining the time served on a sentence of probation”). But “the state did not allege that defendant had absconded, but rather alleged that defendant had failed to ‘[r]eport as directed and abide by the direction of the supervision officer’ when he missed a single meeting with a probation officer.” LaCoe, 323 Or App at 81. The court explained:

“The failure to report [to the probation office] can be—but is not necessarily—absconding. See [State v. Robbins, 345 Or 28, 37, 188 P3d 262 (2008)] (‘A single missed appointment is not enough to conclude that defendant was hiding or that [they] sought to evade the court’s jurisdiction.’).”

Id.

“Thus, the sentencing court lacked authority to revoke probation based on the state’s supplemental allegation, which was filed four months after the probation period ended.” Id.

§ 5-3.4. Bench Probation.

OAR 213-005-0008(4) authorizes sentencing courts to impose bench probation in lieu of supervised terms of probation. The provision makes clear that a court may impose bench probation in all cases (and not only in cases where an extended term of bench probation is necessary to ensure that the conditions of probation are completely satisfied).

§ 5-4. OAR 213-005-0009 NON-PRESUMPTIVE PROBATIONARY SENTENCES.

OAR 213-005-0009 specifies the duration of optional and departure terms of probation:

If the sentencing judge imposes a probationary sentence as a dispositional departure or as an optional probationary sentence, the duration of probation shall be as provided by OAR 213-005-0008.

§ 5-4.1. Analysis of Provision.

As previously discussed, if a defendant qualifies for optional probation, the sentencing court has discretion to impose such a term. Because OAR 213-005-0009 incorporates the durational requirements of OAR 213-005-0008, the standard duration of a term of optional probation is three years.

For all other defendants falling into presumptive imprisonment grid blocks, and who otherwise are eligible for probation, the court may impose probation by dispositional departure. OAR 213-003-0001(6) (“‘[d]ispositional departure’ means a sentence which imposes probation when the presumptive sentence is prison”). The court may impose such a sentence only if it concludes that that “substantial and compelling reasons” justify imposing probation instead of imprisonment. See Chapter 7 (discussing departure sentences).

Because OAR 213-005-0009 incorporates the durational requirements of OAR 213-005-0008, the standard duration of a departure term of probation is two, three, or five years, depending on the crime’s seriousness level. But if the defendant’s crime falls under ORS 137.012, the probationary term must be at least five years long, and may be as long as the crime’s ORS 161.605 statutory maximum.

§ 5.5. OAR 213-005-0010 MODIFICATION OF LENGTH OF PROBATIONARY TERMS.

OAR 213-005-0010 regulates the modification and termination of probationary terms when such changes are to the defendant’s benefit. It states:

(1) A sentencing judge may shorten or terminate a probationary sentence or transfer supervision to bench probation upon a finding that supervision is no longer necessary to accomplish the purposes of the imposed sentence.

(2) Modification of a probationary sentence which results in shortening the term, termination, or transfer to bench probation shall not require a hearing.

§ 5-5.1. Analysis of Provision.

OAR 213-005-0010(1), as well as ORS 137.545(1)(b), authorize sentencing courts to shorten or terminate a probationary term, or to transform it to bench probation, if it finds that a supervision is no longer needed. The court may do so ex parte. OAR 213-005-0010(2).

§ 5-6. OAR 213-005-0011 SANCTION UNITS.

Guidelines probationary sentences typically have three components: length of term, a custodial-type condition, and non-custodial conditions. A guidelines concept called “sanction units” are used to regulate the imposition of both custodial-type and non-custodial conditions. OAR 213-005-0011(1) sets limits on the extent to which “sanction units” regulate the imposition of custodial-type conditions:

(1) When imposing a probationary sentence, the sentencing judge may require that the offender serve a term of custody supervision in a correctional facility or as part of a custody program. The term of custody supervision shall be imposed as a number of sanction units. Sanction units not used to set an initial term of custody are automatically reserved for use as sanctions for probation violations or to insure the purposes of probation are being served.

(2) The number of sanction units that may be imposed as part of a presumptive probationary sentence shall be determined by the grid block classification of the offense:

(a) Up to 90 sanction units for offenses classified in Crime Categories 1 and 2 and grid blocks 3-G, 3-H and 3-I;

(b) Up to 120 sanction units for offenses classified in grid blocks 3-A through 3-F, 4-C through 4-I, and 5-G through 5-I; and

(c) Up to 180 sanction units for offenses classified in grid blocks 5-F, 6-F through 6-I, and 7-F through 7-I.

(3) Notwithstanding section (2) of this rule, for offenders sentenced under 2011 Or Laws ch 598, the maximum number of sanction units that may be imposed as a presumptive probation sentence shall be up to 180 sanction units in grid blocks 4-C through 4-D.

(4) If the sentencing judge imposes a probationary sentence as a dispositional departure or as an optional probationary sentence, the sentencing judge may impose up to 180 sanction units as a part of the sentence. Imposition of more than 180 sanction units is a departure.

(5) Notwithstanding the fact that the court has sentenced a person to a term of incarceration, when an offender is committed to the custody of the supervisory authority of a county under ORS 137.124(2) or (4):

(a) The supervisory authority shall incarcerate offenders sentenced under 2011 Or Laws ch 598 for at least the mandatory minimum term of 90 days without reduction for any reason.

(b) Except for the mandatory minimum term of 90 days as provided in (5)(a) above, the supervisory authority may execute any sentence by imposing sanctions other than incarceration if deemed appropriate by the supervisory authority.

(6) Notwithstanding the limits on sanction units established in sections 2 and 3 of this rule, an additional number of non-jail sanction units may be used to sanction violations of conditions of a probation sentence. There shall be no departure on these additional non-jail sanction units, nor may they be used as jail sanction units pursuant to the findings allowed in OAR 213-005-0013(3). These additional non-jail sanction units are:

(a) Up to 30 additional non-jail sanction units for offenses classified in Crime Seriousness Categories 1 and 2 and grid blocks 3-G, 3-H and 3-I;

(b) Up to 60 additional non-jail sanction units for offenses classified in grid blocks 3-A through 3-F, 4-C through 4-I, and 5-G through 5-I; and

(c) Up to 90 additional non-jail sanction units for offenses classified in grid blocks 5-F, 6-F through 6-I, 7-F through 7-I, and offenses in which a sentence of probation was imposed as a departure or pursuant to OAR 213-005-0006 (Optional Probation).

(7) Notwithstanding the limits on sanction units established in sections 2, 3 and 4 of this rule, an additional number of sanction units may be used to sanction violations of conditions of a probation sentence. There shall be no departure on these additional sanction units, nor may they be used as jail sanction units pursuant to the findings allowed in OAR 213-005-0013(3). These additional sanction units are:

(a) Up to 60 additional jail or non-jail sanction units for offenses classified in Crime Seriousness Categories 1 and 2 and grid blocks 3-G, 3-H and 3-I, of which no more than 30 may be jail sanction units;

(b) Up to 105 additional jail or non-jail sanction units for offenses classified in grid blocks 3-A through 3-F, 4-C through 4-I, and 5-G through 5-I, of which no more than 45 may be jail sanction units; and

(c) Up to 150 additional jail or non-jail sanction units for offenses classified in grid blocks 5-F, 6-F through 6-I, 7-F through 7-I, and offenses in which a sentence of probation was imposed as a departure or pursuant to OAR 213-005-0006 (Optional Probation), of which no more than 60 may be jail sanction units.

(8) For crimes committed on or after January 1, 2002, section 6 shall not apply and section 7 shall operate in its place.

§ 5-6.1. Definition of “Sanction Unit.”

The guidelines do not define the phrase “sanction unit.” But as will be seen, it may be defined as a discrete element of a condition of probation a defendant satisfies by spending time in jail, in a residential custodial treatment center, on release programs such as work release, under house arrest, or performing community service.

§ 5-6.2. General Requirements of Provision.

Except in the context of the intermediate-sanction system, see Section 5-16, only the sentencing court may specify how sanction units are to be used; consequently, the court may not order that sanction units “be utilized at the discretion of [the defendant’s] probation officer.” State v. Shefler, 118 Or App 536, 537, 847 P2d 417 (1993) (per curiam). Sanction units that are not used initially are automatically reserved for use as sanctions for probation violations, or to ensure that the purposes of probation are being served. OAR 213-005-0011(1). See also State v. Anderson, 111 Or App 294, 298, 826 P2d 66 (1992) (un-imposed sanction units are automatically reserved for future use).

§ 5-6.3. Maximum Number of Sanction Units.

OAR 213-005-0011(2) sets out the presumptive maximum number of sanction units the court may impose for each probationary grid block. Depending on the defendant’s grid-block, these numbers are 90, 120, or 180, as shown as the upper number in each presumption probation/straight jail grid-block. For example, presumption probation/straight jail grid-blocks ranked at seriousness level 4 specify a sanction unit maximum of 120. But when a court imposes a sentence of probation on a defendant convicted of level 4 felony driving under the influence of intoxicants (DUII), and whose criminal-history score is C or D, the defendant’s sanction units total is 180. OAR 213-005-0011(3). For defendants whom the court sentences to an optional or a dispositional-departure term of probation, the sanction units total also is 180. OAR 213-005-0011(4).

§ 5-6.4. Converting Incarceration Sanctions.

OAR 213-005-0011(5) references ORS 137.124. Under that statute, if a court imposes an incarceration term of one year or less, and the term is not consecutive to an incarceration term of greater than one year, the defendant serves the sentence locally, in the county jail. But the local supervisory authority may convert the incarceration term into a non-incarceration term. OAR 213-005-0011(5).

§ 5-6.5. Increasing Sanction Units for Probation Violations.

OAR 213-005-0011(6) authorizes sentencing courts to increase, by 30, 60, or 90, the number of sanction units a defendant must serve. However, the subsection only authorizes these increases as sanctions for probation violations. To impose additional sanction units, the court must hold a hearing. Warner, 118 Or App at 727.

Moreover, the 2001 Legislature’s House Bill 3461 directed the Criminal Justice Commission to “amend its rules to increase the jail and nonjail custody [sic] units that can be imposed as custodial sanctions for probation violations under ORS 137.595. The commission shall base the amendments on the existing rule structure and may not increase existing sanction limits by more than 60 days.”

The commission responded by adopting OAR 213-005-0011(7). The amendments apply to crimes committed on or after January 1, 2002. OAR 213-005-0011(8). Depending on the defendant’s grid block, OAR 213-005-0011(7) allows sanction-unit maximums of 150, 225, and 330 to deal with probation violations. Moreover, the number of sanction units that may be satisfied by jail time are 30, 45, and 60, again depending on the defendant’s grid block.

§ 5-7. OAR 213-005-0012 CUSTODIAL SUPERVISION.

OAR 213-005-0012 specifies the custody-type programs that may be used to satisfy the sanction-units component of a probationary sentence:

(1) The sanction units imposed as part of a probationary sentence shall be used to set a term of custodial supervision in a correctional facility or as part of a custody program. Credit for sanction units shall only be granted for time actually served. Good time credits, work time credits, or early release shall not count towards satisfaction of sanction units.

(2) When sanction units are imposed as part of a probationary sentence, the offender shall receive credit for having served those sanction units as follows:

(a) JAIL: Each day of jail incarceration equals one (1) sanction unit.

(b) RESIDENTIAL CUSTODIAL TREATMENT FACILITY: Each day of actual confinement in a 24-hour residential custodial treatment facility equals one (1) sanction unit when the program is satisfactorily completed including up to one year of any required aftercare. Aftercare may continue for more than one year, but custody unit credit shall be granted following satisfactory completion of one year.

(c) RELEASE PROGRAMS: Each day of partial confinement in a release program, in which the offender is confined in a custodial facility when not on release, equals one (1) sanction unit for each day of partial incarceration.

(d) HOUSE ARREST: Each day of satisfactory compliance with the requirements of house arrest equals one (1) sanction unit if the offender satisfactorily completes the house arrest.

(e) COMMUNITY SERVICE: Sixteen hours of community service under the direct supervision of a supervisor designated by the supervisory authority equals one sanction unit.

(3)(a) When the sentencing judge orders jail time as part of a probationary sentence, the judge shall sentence the offender directly to the custody of the supervisory authority with jurisdiction over the county jail.

(b) When the sentencing judge recommends a custodial facility or program other than jail, the judge shall sentence the offender directly to the custody of the supervisory authority with jurisdiction over that facility or program. To impose such a sentence, the judge must determine that space is available in that facility or program and that the offender meets the eligibility criteria established for that facility or program by the supervisory authority.

(4) The supervisory authority shall keep a record of all sanction units served by the offender during the course of the probationary term. When sanction units are served only upon the satisfactory completion of a custodial program, the supervisory authority, when appropriate, shall certify that the offender has satisfactorily completed a custodial program and the number of sanction units served by the offender as part of the program.

(5) Where the sentencing judge finds that a custodial rehabilitation program designed to deal with drug or alcohol abuse or sexual behavior is essential to minimize the offender’s likelihood of engaging in future criminal conduct, the requirement that the offender enter and satisfactorily complete such a program shall not be limited by the sanction units set forth in OAR 213-005-0011 or the provisions of this rule.

§ 5-7.1 General Requirements of Provision.

OAR 213-005-0012(1) allows satisfying sanction units by actual participation in custody programs. A defendant may not earn good-time reductions in the sanction unit component of his probationary sentence.

Sentencing courts may use sanction units by committing probationers to jail, residential custodial treatment, or such release programs as work release, house arrest, and community service. OAR 213-005-0012(2)(a)-(e). For each of these facilities except community service, each day of participation satisfies one sanction unit. OAR 213-005-0012(2)(a)-(d). Each 16 hours of participation in supervised community service satisfies one sanction unit. OAR 213-005-0012(2)(e).

However, the criminal code provides certain limitations on the imposition of community service to meet sanction unit requirements. The code provision states:

“(1) A judge may sentence an offender to community service either as an alternative to incarceration or fine or probation, or as a condition of probation. Prior to such order of community service the offender must consent to donate labor for the welfare of the public. The court or its delegate may select community service tasks that are within the offender’s capabilities and are to be performed within a reasonable length of time during hours the offender is not working or attending school.

“(2) Failure to perform a community service sentence may be grounds for revocation of probation or contempt of court.”

ORS 137.128 (emphasis added).

WARNING: Depending on how judges offer defendants the community service alternative, this statute can be problematic. For example, suppose a judge tells the defendant, “Unless you agree to some community service time, I’m going to sentence you to some jail time. So, do you consent to some community service?” In that scenario, the defendant could be considered to have submitted, but not to have consented to community service, so may be compelled to accept community service by threat of jail. In that scenario, the defendant submitting to community service will serve legal slavery under authority of the Thirteenth Amendment.

Previously, the defendant could serve legal slavery not only under authority of the Thirteenth Amendment, but also under authority of Article I, section 34, of the Oregon Constitution. That was because in 1859 (so six years before ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment), the voters authorized legal slavery of inmates who were “duly convicted” of crimes. But in November 2022, the voters approved (by a comfortable margin) Ballot Measure 112, which amended Article I, section 34 by removing the section’s text, and which authorized legal slavery of inmates who were “duly convicted” of crimes. With that text removed, section 34 no longer permits inmates legal slavery.1

But defendants who are probationers are not inmates, and, rather surprisingly, Measure 112 allows imposing legal slavery in the form of community service on probationers and parolees. Measure 112 so confirms that, by adding the pertinent text: “Upon conviction of a crime, an Oregon court or a probation or parole agency may order the convicted person to engage in * * * community service * * * as part of sentencing for the crime[.]” Art I, § 34(2).2

This leaves two open questions:

Does section 34(2) supersede ORS 137.128(1), so as to allow courts to impose community service without obtaining the probationers’ consent?

Or does section 34(2) allow courts to impose community service only if probationers consent to community service?3

OAR 213-005-0012(3)(a) applies when the sentencing court orders jail in satisfaction of sanction units. It requires the court to place the defendant in the custody of the jail’s supervisory authority (usually, the county sheriff).

OAR 213-005-0012(3)(b) applies when the court orders time in any type of custodial facility other than jail. Before a court may order a defendant into one of these facilities, it must find that the facility has space available and that the defendant qualifies for participation in the program. When these two conditions are met, the court “sentence[s] the offender directly to the custody of the supervisory authority with jurisdiction over that facility or program.” Id.

OAR 213-005-0012(4) requires the custodial facility’s supervisory authority to keep records of a defendant’s service of sanction units. In some circumstances, the rule also requires the supervisory authority to certify that a defendant has “satisfactorily” completed the custodial program.

§ 5-7.2. Behavior Modification Programs.

OAR 213-005-0012(5) applies to defendants whose sanction units are to be satisfied by commitment to facilities “deal[ing] with drug or alcohol abuse or sexual behavior[.]” In cases where the court finds that program participation is essential to reducing recidivism, the limits on the number of sanction units discussed above do not apply. Also inapplicable are the provisions specifying how many hours a defendant must spend in a facility to satisfy a single sanction unit.

§ 5-8. JAIL AS CONDITION OF PROBATION.

OAR 213-005-0013 specifies the maximum number of days in jail that may be used to satisfy the sanction unit component of a probationary sentence:

(1) Subject to the provisions of sections (2), (3) and (4) of this rule, the maximum number of sanction units that may be used to impose a jail term as part of a probationary sentence shall be as follows:

(a) Up to 30 sanction units for offenses classified in Crime Categories 1 and 2 and grid blocks 3-G, 3-H and 3-I;

(b) Up to 60 sanction units for offenses classified in grid blocks 3-A through 3-F, 4-C through 4-I, and 5-G through 5-I; and

(c) Up to 90 sanction units for offenses classified in grid blocks 5-F, 6-F through 6-I, and 7-F through 7-I, and if a probation sentence is imposed as a departure from a presumptive prison term or as an optional probation sentence.

(2) Within the limitations established by this rule on the use of jail as part of a probation sentence, the sentencing judge may impose:

(a) A jail term of no more than one-third of the jail sanction units described in section 1 of this rule as part of a probation sentence to be served immediately upon sentencing;

(b) One or more jail terms as a sanction for probation violations over the term of probation; or

(c) Subsections (a) and (b) of this section so long as the total length of jail incarceration does not exceed the limits established by this rule on the use of jail as part of a probationary sentence except as provided in OAR 213-005-0011(6).

(3) The limitations established by this rule on the use of jail as part of a probationary sentence may be exceeded if the sentencing judge, after consulting with the appropriate supervisory authority, finds on the record that local jail space provided by the county is available for a longer term. Upon making such a finding, the sentencing judge may, without departure, use:

(a) Up to the maximum number of jail sanction units described in section 1 of this rule at the time of initial sentencing; and

(b) Up to the maximum number of sanction units described in OAR 213-005-0011(2) and (3) to impose jail for violations of conditions of the probation sentence.

(4) The maximum number of sanction units that may be used to impose a jail term as part of a probationary sentence under 2011 Or Laws ch 598 shall be 90, which must be imposed without reduction for any reason. The provisions of subsection (2)(a) shall not apply to sentences imposed under this subsection.

§ 5-8.1. General Requirements of Provision.

OAR 213-005-0013(1) specifies the maximum number of jail days, of 30, 60, or 90, that a sentencing court ordinarily may impose in satisfaction of sanction units. But ORS 813.011(3) mandates a minimum incarceration term of 90 days in felony DUII cases. To accommodate this mandate, OAR 213-005-0013(4) mandates imposition of 90 sanction units in such cases, which units then are satisfied by imposition of the mandatory incarceration term.

Under OAR 213-005-0013(2)(a), a court ordinarily may not impose a jail term equal to the grid block’s lower number. Instead, under the so-called “one-third rule,” the court may impose a jail term of equal to one-third of the grid block’s lower number.4 The remaining two-thirds are reserved for probation-violation sanctions. OAR 213-005-0013(2)(b). The sum of a defendant’s initial jail term and his jail-term sanctions for probation violations usually may not exceed his grid block’s lower number. OAR 213-005-0013(2)(c).

§ 5-8.2. Exceptionally Long Jail Terms.

In four situations, sentencing courts may impose jail terms longer than authorized by OAR 213-005-0013(2). First, a court may impose a longer term if it finds on the record that the county has available jail space. The court then may avoid the one-third rule and impose a jail term of up to the lower number in the defendant’s grid block. OAR 213-005-0013(3)(a).

Second, a court may impose a longer term in the event of probation violations. OAR 213-005-0013(3)(b). That rule, as well as OAR 213-005-0013(2)(c), establish that for such violations, the court may not impose a sanction-unit total that exceeds the grid block’s upper number.

Third, by departure the court may impose a jail term of up to the maximum number of sanction units. OAR 213-008-0006(2). Section 5-8.2, as well as Chapter 7, discuss departing as to the duration of jail terms.

Finally, under OAR 213-005-0011(6), for probation violations, a court may add jail sanction units of up to 30, 45, or 60 days, depending on the defendant’s grid block.

§ 5-9. OAR 213-005-0014 LEVEL OF COMMUNITY SUPERVISION.

OAR 213-005-0014 establishes the methodology the DOC uses in determining how intensively its officers, or its designee’s officers, will supervise a defendant who is on probation:

(1) The Department or its designees shall use a risk assessment classification system to classify offenders for supervision purposes.

(2) The level of supervision shall be established by the Department or its designees based on the offender-risk classification. The level of supervision may be increased or decreased by the supervisory authority in response to the offender’s conduct under supervision and as is necessary to manage the offender in the community.

§ 5-9.1. Analysis of Provision.

According to commentary, the provisions of OAR 213-005-0014

“depend on the use of a statewide risk classification instrument to ensure that the same standards are used to classify offenders in all jurisdictions. Systematic classification also seeks to ensure the best use of limited supervisory resources whereby more resources are used to supervise offenders judged to pose the greatest risk of probation violation, including the risk of new criminal conduct.”

Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 167 (1989) (hereafter Implementation Manual).

Counsel should be wary of OAR 213-005-0014(1), which instructs, “The Department or its designees shall use a risk assessment classification system to classify offenders for [probationary] supervision purposes.” (Emphasis added.) This wariness stems from the lack of reliability of risk assessment testing, which requires development and use of a “toolbox” to challenge risk assessment testing. As Philipsborn explains:

“A lawyer’s need for a toolbox arises in part because different theories, categories, and classes of such assessments exist; there are scientific and technical questions about the reliability of risk assessment practices in criminal justice risk assessment; the individual doing the assessment may not have an adequate background, training, or knowledge of the reliability of the assessment procedure being used. Another important reason for preparing a toolbox is Professor Christopher Slobogin’s observation in his book on ‘dangerousness’ adjudications about ‘[t]he courts’ nonchalance toward prediction testimony.’”

John Philipsborn, A Basic Risk Assessment Toolbox: Aiming for Adequate Lawyering During the Spread of Risk Assessments, The Champion, Jan/Feb 2020, at 36 (quoting Christopher Slobogin, Proving the Unprovable: The Role of Law, Science, & Speculation in Adjudicating Culpability & Dangerousness 108 (2007)).

Finally, a defendant

“convicted of a felony or a designated drug-related misdemeanor and sentenced to probation * * * is eligible for a reduction in the period of probation * * * for complying with terms of probation * * * , including demonstrating a commitment to the payment of restitution to the extent the person is able to pay, and participation in recidivism reduction programs.”

ORS 137.633(1).

“The maximum reduction * * * may not exceed 50 percent of the period of probation * * * imposed.” ORS 137.633(2). Additionally, the “reduction * * * may not be used to shorten the period of probation * * * to less than six months.” ORS 137.633(3). However, a defendant who falls into any of the several categories listed in ORS 137.633(4) “is not eligible for a reduction in the term of supervision[.]”

State v. Covell, 322 Or App 243, 519 P3d 173 (2022) clarifies application of ORS 137.633. “[T]he sentencing court contravened ORS 137.633 when it ordered that defendant would ‘[n]ot be eligible for earned discharge.’” 322 Or App at 244. The Court of Appeals rejected the sentencing court’s order. It explained that the defendant

“‘is eligible for a reduction in the period of probation or local control post-prison supervision for complying with terms of probation or post-prison supervision, including the payment of restitution and participation in recidivism reduction programs.’ ORS 137.633(1) (emphasis added).

“ORS 137.633(1) does not allow room for a sentencing court to preemptively deny eligibility for earned discharge or otherwise change the terms of eligibility for earned discharge.”

Id. at 246-47. The court then concluded that “the sentencing court contravened the terms of the statute when it ordered as part of its sentencing judgment that defendant would not be eligible for earned discharge. Id. at 248.

§ 5-10. OAR 213-005-0015 NON-CUSTODY CONDITIONS OF PROBATION.

OAR 213-005-0015 authorizes the imposition of special conditions of probation:

The sentencing judge may impose any additional non-custodial special conditions of probation as provided by law.

§ 5-10.1. General Requirements of Provision.

OAR 213-005-0015 “confirms the continued authority of sentencing judges to impose any non-custodial supervisory conditions permitted by law. See, ORS 137.540.” Implementation Manual at 105-06. During the probationary period, “[t]he court may at any time modify the conditions of probation.” ORS 137.540(9)(a). But the court may not delegate to the probation officer the authority to impose conditions of probation. State v. Flynn, 89 Or App 47, 747 P2d 376 (1987) (after ordering that defendant, as condition of probation, pay certain fees, the court erred in delegating to probation officer the authority to determine defendant’s ability to pay; statute requires court to make that determination). Indeed, the court may violate the state constitution’s separation of powers if it delegates its condition-imposition authority to the probation officer. See United States v. Esparza, 552 F3d 1088, 1091 (9th Cir 2009) (such delegation violates federal constitution’s separation of powers); United States v. Stephens, 424 F3d 876, 881 (9th Cir 2005) (same).

A part of Section 24 of HB 3194, codified as ORS 137.540(2)(b)(B), states that a probationer may be ordered to “[c]omply with any special conditions of probation that are imposed by the supervising officer in accordance with subsection (9) of this section.” Id. This provision applies only to “crimes committed on or after August 1, 2013.” Or Laws 2013, ch 649, § 25. The pertinent part of ORS 137.540(9), which HB 3194 also created, states:

“(b) When the court orders a defendant placed under the supervision of the Department of Corrections or a community corrections agency, the supervising officer may file with the court a proposed modification to the special conditions of probation. The supervising officer shall provide a copy of the proposed modification to the district attorney and the probationer, and shall notify the probationer of the right to file an objection and have a hearing as described in subparagraph (A) of this paragraph. The notice requirement may be satisfied by providing the probationer with a copy of a form developed in accordance with rules adopted under ORS 137.595 (2)(b) that describes the right to a hearing. If the district attorney or probationer:

“(A) Files an objection to the proposed modification less than five judicial days after the proposed modification was filed, the court shall schedule a hearing no later than 10 judicial days after the proposed modification was filed, unless the court finds good cause to schedule a hearing at a later time.

“(B) Does not file an objection to the proposed modification less than five judicial days after the proposed modification was filed, the proposed modification becomes effective five judicial days after the proposed modification was filed.”

The legislature passed Senate Bill 651 (2021) (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2021, chapter 404), which amended ORS 137.540(9) These amendments require the supervising officer to notify the defendant of the officer’s proposed modification to the defendant’s special conditions of probation, and the defendant has the right to file an objection the proposed modification.

It has long been established that a court may not modify the terms of probation without first giving notice and an opportunity for the defendant to be heard. See, e.g., Warner, 118 Or App at 727. As seen, the provisions of ORS 137.540(9)(b)(A) created a roughly parallel notification requirement, for the benefit of prosecutors.

Moreover, the court may not modify the conditions of probation after the defendant completed the term of probation. For example, in State v. Larson, 314 Or App 576, 497 P3d 1276 (2021), the court held a “resentencing” hearing and imposed fines, but by then the defendant had completed service of his sentence, including the probation. The court, therefore, lacked authority to modify the terms of his sentence.

ORS 137.540(1) sets out an extensive list of general non-custodial conditions of probation. These general conditions are included in all probationary sentences “unless specifically deleted by the court.” Id. Consequently, the general conditions essentially are presumptive conditions.

But in certain cases, general conditions are limited in their scope. For example, in State v. Heaston, 308 Or App 694, 482 P3d 167 (2021), the trial court imposed the general condition that a probationer shall not “use or possess controlled substances except pursuant to a medical prescription.” ORS 137.540(1)(b). While on probation, the defendant admitted that he had used marijuana. Later, in a probation-violation hearing, he “argue[d] that a ‘defendant does not violate the general condition of probation forbidding him to use or possess controlled substances by using marijuana,’” because “‘marijuana is no longer considered a controlled substance in the State of Oregon.’” 308 Or App at 696. The trial court rejected the defendant’s argument and imposed violation sanctions. But the Court of Appeals reversed. It held that “considering text, context, and legislative history, we conclude the phrase ‘controlled substances’ in ORS 137.540(1)(b) does not include marijuana. Accordingly, we conclude that the trial court erred. We reverse and remand.” Id. at 705. See also State v. Carlson, 317 Or App 337, 341, 504 P3d 1284 (2022) (in violation of ORS 137.542(2), “the trial court erred in imposing special conditions of probation that completely prohibit defendant from using or possessing marijuana and from entering any marijuana dispensary, without giving defendant an opportunity to establish whether he holds a marijuana registry identification card”).

Recent Court of Appeals decisions suggest that general conditions are subject to strict constructions. For example, in State v. Kelemen, 296 Or App 184, 437 P3d 1225 (2019), the court relied on general condition (m)—“[r]eport as required and abide by the direction of the supervising officer,” ORS 137.540(1)(m)—to revoke probation. The factual basis for the allegation was that the defendant had repeatedly failed to comply with his probation officer’s orders to complete his work-crew assignment. Previously, in State v. Hardges, 294 Or App 445, 446, 432 P3d 268 (2018), the court construed general condition (m) as applicable “only when the direction at issue directly relates to the reporting requirement imposed by that condition.” Relying on Hardges, the Kelemen court held that the defendant’s refusal to complete his work-crew assignment did not constitute a violation of general condition (m). 296 Or App at 189.

The sentencing court may “impose any special conditions of probation that are reasonably related to the crime of conviction or the needs of the defendant for the protection of the public or reformation of the offender[.]” ORS 137.540(2). See State v. Gallo, 275 Or App 868, 365 P3d 1154 (2015) (where defendant used the internet to facilitate his commission of sexual abuse, trial court did not err in imposing a special condition of probation barring defendant from using email, social networking, and the internet generally without obtaining pre-approval from his probation officer);

State v. Priester, 325 Or App 574, 589, 530 P3d 118 (2023) (“[t]he probation condition that defendant ‘[d]isclose nature of conviction to any domestic partner’ is unconstitutionally vague under the Fourteenth Amendment”); State v. Butler, 313 Or App 786, 494 P3d 1031 (2021) (Court of Appeals affirmed trial court’s finding of probation violation where defendant violated a condition of probation prohibiting him from contacting victim, by posting about victim on his Facebook page, which posts victim could see); State v. Farris, 312 Or App 618, 622, 365 P3d 1154 (2021) (where defendant was convicted of second-degree sexual abuse, Court of Appeals rejected vagueness and overbroad claim that special condition of defendant must seek permission before participating in any “intimate or sexual relationship or encounter (such as dating, romantic, or otherwise sexual”); State v. Preusse, 309 Or App 589, 482 P3d 189 (2021) (per curiam) (special condition requiring probation officer’s permission to engage in intimate relationships was unlawful); State v. Welt, 309 Or App 762, 483 P3d 56 (2021) (per curiam) (trial court erred in imposing special conditions related to drugs and alcohol, when defendant’s crime did not involve drugs or alcohol); State v. Worthey, 302 Or App 140, 143-44, 460 P3d 545 (2020) (trial court erred in imposing special condition of probation prohibiting defendant from possessing marijuana or cannabis products, because the defendant’s use of marijuana did not reasonably relate to his crime of conviction, his needs, or the protection of the public);

State v. Gaskill, 250 Or App 100, 279 P3d 275 (2012) (where defendant was convicted of the third-degree sexual abuse of an adult woman, and nothing in his record indicated he ever had engaged in unlawful activity with minors, trial court erred in imposing special condition prohibiting defendant from having contact with minors or frequenting places where minors congregate); State v. Hitesman, 113 Or App 356, 362-63, 833 P2d 306, rev den, 314 Or 574 (1992) (where defendant was convicted of cultivating marijuana, special condition banning him from living in rural areas and possessing buildings of certain sizes, was overly broad and not authorized). A court may not impose conditions that conflict with any of the general and special conditions enumerated in ORS 137.540(1)-(2). See, e.g., State v. Smith, 117 Or App 473, 475-76, 844 P2d 276 (1992) (where former special condition (k) authorized chemical tests at request of defendant’s probation officer, condition allowing such tests at request of a police officer was invalid). See also United States v. Knights, 534 US 112, 122 S Ct 587, 151 L Ed 2d 497 (2001) (Fourth Amendment allows police, with reasonable suspicion, to search home of probationer whose probation is conditioned on warrantless searches);

State v. Vanhorn, 310 Or App 224, 483 P3d 1257 (2021) (per curiam) (special condition requiring submission to searches unlawful, because it overrode general condition authorizing searches only upon reasonable grounds); State v. Nelson, 310 Or App 219, 483 P3d 1261 (2021) (per curiam) (same).

The court “must make a fact[ual] record [explaining how] the condition is appropriate.” State v. Quackenbush, 113 Or App 263, 265, 832 P2d 1236 (1992).

PRACTICE TIP: With some frequency, courts impose unauthorized conditions which go unchallenged at sentencing or on appeal. If the state later seeks to revoke probation based on an alleged violation of such an unauthorized condition, the defendant may not assert the condition’s illegality as a defense. State v. Hindman, 125 Or App 434, 437, 866 P2d 481 (1993); State v. Beavers, 121 Or App 594, 596, 856 P2d 332 (1993). For this reason, at sentencing defense counsel must be careful object to unauthorized conditions, and, if necessary, appeal their imposition.

A trial court may impose a “sex-offender package” as a condition of probation in sentencing a crime listed as a sexual offense. State v. Mack, 156 Or App 423, 428-29, 967 P2d 516 (1998). If the court is not sentencing a crime listed as a sexual offense, it may impose a sex-offender package only if the facts show that the defendant “acted with a sexual purpose[.]” 156 Or App at 429. See also State v. McCollister, 210 Or App 1, 5-6, 150 P3d 7 (2006) (holding consistent with Mack).

Notwithstanding that probation is a sentence, e.g., Holcomb, 321 Or at 104, in McCollister the Court of Appeals held that where the defendant is convicted of a non-sexual offense—in McCollister, harassment after sexual abuse charges were dropped—the trial court may impose a sex-offender package as a special condition of probation without making specific findings that the defendant acted with a sexual purpose. All that is required is for the record to show that the package is reasonably related to the defendant’s offense, his needs for the protection of the public, or his reformation.

Because no specific findings are required to impose a sex-offender package, the McCollister court further held that the Sixth Amendment jury trial requirements discussed in Chapter 1 does not require a jury finding of a reasonable relationship. Whether McCollister will defeat the claim that the Sixth Amendment requires jury findings of reasonable relationships for the imposition of other types of special conditions of probation remains to be seen.

Moreover, sex-offender packages usually or always require the defendant to undergo treatment. Sex-offender treatment programs often require defendants to “come clean” about all of their prior sexual transgressions—and to perhaps even undergo polygraphs to prove that they have come clean. A failure to come clean may be treated as a violation of the sex-offender-package condition of probation and result in violation sanctions. But in United States v. Antelope, 395 F3d 1128 (9th Cir 2005), the court held that revocation of a defendant’s probation because he refused to come clean as part of his sex-offender treatment violated his Fifth Amendment right against self-incrimination. Later, in United States v. Bahr, 730 F3d 963 (9th Cir 2013), the court relied on Antelope to hold that because statements made during a sex-evaluation treatment (a consequence of a state conviction for a sexual offense) were given under compulsion—the threat that if the defendant did not fully disclose his past sexual history, he would fail the program and his probation (or supervised release) would be revoked—it is “unconstitutional” to use those statements to determine the defendant’s “sentence in a later, unrelated criminal proceeding[.]” See also Mitchell v. United States, 526 US 314, 325, 119 S Ct 1307, 143 L Ed 2d 424 (1999) (unless defendant has knowingly and voluntarily waived his right against self-incrimination, e.g., as part of a guilty or no-contest plea, right continues until the completion of sentencing); State v. Barone, 329 Or 210, 231, 986 P2d 5 (1999) (right against self-incrimination continues until after defendant has “been convicted and [his appeal or] his time for appeal [has] run”).

§ 5-10.2. Risk and Needs Assessment.

Section 24 of HB 3194 created a new general condition of probation, which states: “Submit to a risk and needs assessment as directed by the supervising officer[.]” ORS 137.540(1)(q). The authority to impose this type of condition applies only in sentencings for “crimes committed on or after August 1, 2013.” Or Laws 2013, ch 649, § 25.

§ 5-11. OAR 213-005-0016. DEPARTURE PROBATIONARY SENTENCES.

OAR 213-005-0016 authorizes a sentencing court to depart on the number of sanction units imposed:

A probationary sentence which exceeds the applicable limitation on the use of custodial supervision as part of a probationary sentence is a departure.

§ 5-11.1. Analysis of Provision.

OAR 213-005-0016 authorizes a sentencing court, by “departure,” to impose a sanction unit total “exceed[ing] the total limits on [sanction] units, the limits on use of jail as part of the total [sanction] unit limitation, or both.” Implementation Manual at 139. The maximum number of sanction units the court may impose is double the presumptive number of units. OAR 213-008-0006(1). Chapter 7 discusses the imposition of departure sentences. Imposing them requires the finding of “aggravating factors,” which findings are subject to the Sixth Amendment jury trial requirements discussed in Section 1. Those constitutional requirements should apply to the imposition of departure sanction unit totals.

As discussed in Section 5-8.2, the court may, by departure, impose a jail term of up to the maximum number of sanction units. OAR 213-008-0006(2). But here again, the constitutional requirements discussed in Chapter 1 should apply to the imposition of departure jail terms.

§ 5-12. CONDITIONAL DISCHARGE PROBATIONARY SENTENCES.

If a defendant is convicted of a drug offense, and has no prior drug convictions or conditional discharges, a sentencing court may order the conditional discharge of his conviction, rather than impose a judgment of conviction and sentence. ORS 475.245. The discharge is conditioned on the defendant successfully completing a term of probation imposed by the sentencing court. If the defendant violates probation, the court may impose a judgment of conviction and sentence.

ORS 475.245 does not specify the duration and conditions of a conditional discharge probationary sentence. Presumably they are the same as the court would impose as a presumptive probationary sentence. If so, a conditional discharge probationary sentence would be identical to a presumptive probationary sentence, with the exception that upon successful completion of the conditional discharge probationary term, the defendant’s record of conviction will be cleared.

Moreover, a discharged conviction “is not a conviction * * * for purposes of disqualifications or disabilities imposed by law upon conviction of a crime.” Id. Thus, it may not be used for criminal history scoring purposes, discussed in Chapter 3.

Defense counsel also should be aware that except for DUII and domestic-violence charges, under ORS 137.533, conditional discharges generally are available for misdemeanor charges.

§ 5-12.1. Appeal of Conditional Discharge Orders.

Occasionally, a defendant who receives a conditional-discharge probationary sentence will have an issue he wishes to appeal, e.g., the denial of a motion to suppress evidence. But as discussed in Chapter 9, a defendant may not appeal unless and until a final judgment is entered. An order imposing conditional discharge is not a final judgment, so the defendant may not appeal. State v. Spencer, 130 Or App 158, 881 P2d 154 (1994).

Moreover, if the defendant fails to complete the probation agreement and then is convicted of crimes charged, ORS 475.245(2), the defendant may appeal only on the grounds “that the sentence exceeds the maximum allowed by law or constitutes cruel and unusual punishment.” ORS 475.245(1)(c)(E).

§ 5-13. OAR 213-005-0017 RECORD OF SANCTION UNITS.

OAR 213-005-0017 specifies the requirements for keeping records of a defendant’s service of sanction units:

The supervisory authority or the supervisory authority’s designee shall keep a record of all sanction units served by the offender while under the jurisdiction of the supervisory authority. The record shall be maintained in a form that will enable the sentencing judge to determine whether the sanction units imposed as part of a probationary sentence have been served.

§ 5-13.1. Analysis of Provision.

Commentary explains, “This rule describes the supervisory authority’s general record keeping responsibilities to record custody units. The specific form of record keeping is left to the supervisory authority, but the form must enable the sentencing judge to determine whether an offender has served the custody units imposed as part of probation.” Implementation Manual at 107-08.

§ 5-14. OAR 213-010-0001 REVOCATION OF PROBATION.

OAR 213-010-0001 establishes the sentencing court’s authority to revoke guidelines sentences of probation:

The decision to revoke probation is discretionary and may be exercised upon a finding that the offender has violated one or more of the conditions of probation, or that the offender has participated in new criminal activity.

§ 5-14.1. Purpose of Provision.

According to commentary, “This rule confirms that sentencing guidelines do not change the sentencing judge’s discretion to revoke probation.” Implementation Manual at 169. But it is important to bear in mind that revocation is not the only available sanction. Other, lesser sanctions include imposing intermediate sanctions imposed by the supervisory authority, see Section 5-16; extending the probationary term; and imposing additional conditions of probation, including additional jail time. Furthermore, even if the defendant committed his violative act during the probationary period, a bench warrant or show-cause order must issue during the probationary period. Otherwise, the probation expires and the court loses jurisdiction to impose sanctions. Miller, 224 Or App at 642; State v. O’Neal, 24 Or App 423, 427, 545 P2d 910 (1976).

In State v. Martin, 313 Or App 578, 496 P3d 1077 (2021), aff’d, 370 Or 653, 522 P3d 841 (2022), a divided Court of Appeals opinion addressed the trial court’s admission of the victim’s out-of-court statements made during a 9-1-1 call, without good cause for why the victim was not called as a witness during the hearing. The majority affirmed on the ground that the victim’s statements were excited utterances, so were within a firmly rooted exception to the hearsay rule. As such, the admission did not violate the defendant’s due process rights. The Supreme Court affirmed, “but on different grounds than the Court of Appeals.” 370 Or at 655.

The Supreme Court explained that, under Morrissey v. Brewer, 408 US 471, 92 S Ct 2593, 33 L Ed 2d 484 (1972), a defendant in a probation revocation hearing has a due process right to confront adverse witnesses unless the state establishes good cause for not allowing confrontation. The court rejected the state’s argument that good cause is always established if the evidence at issue falls within a “firmly rooted” exception to the rule prohibiting hearsay. The Court reasoned that, in light of the discretionary nature of the ultimate decision to revoke probation, and because the United States Supreme Court has held that the hallmarks of procedural due process are flexibility and fundamental fairness, it is appropriate to balance a defendant’s interest in revocation against the factors bearing on the state’s showing of good cause, regardless of the reliability of the evidence. 370 Or 669-70. Applying those factors here, including the presumptive veracity of the evidence and the difficulty in producing the witness, the court held that the trial court had correctly ruled that the state established good cause for overcoming defendant’s interest in confrontation. Id. at 671-72.

Finally, just as a court may not revoke probation if the revocation action were initiated after the term of probation expired, the court may not consider additional probation-violation allegations the prosecution added after the term of probation expired. For example, in State v. Bergland, 311 Or App 424, 491 P3d 820 (2021), a revocation action was initiated during the probationary period, but the revocation hearing was not held until after the probationary period had expired. Once the hearing finally began, the prosecution alleged an additional six violations. The trial court considered those additional allegations and revoked the defendant’s probation. The Court of Appeals reversed. It stated:

“[T]he authority of a sentencing court to adjudicate probation violations charged after the end of the probationary period must be explicitly conferred by statute. There is no statute conferring that authority. And the statutes that govern the retained authority of a court to sanction probation violations confine that authority to violations that are charged ‘[a]t any time during the probation period.’ No such violation was sanctioned here.”

311 Or App at 433.

§ 5-14.2. Challenges to Evidence in Probation-Violation Proceedings.

A trial court may not “exercise its discretionary authority to revoke probation absent the finding of a new crime or other violation of the conditions of probation.” State v. Kelemen, 296 Or App 184, 192, 437 P3d 1225 (2019). Similarly, a trial court may not revoke probation based on the defendant’s failure to comply with a probation officer’s directive. This is because, regardless of the fact that the court imposed a condition of probation requiring the defendant to abide by the directives of the probation officer, it is impermissible to revoke probation for violating a condition imposed by a probation officer. E.g., State v. Rivera-Waddle, 279 Or App 274, 278-79, 379 P3d 820 (2016).

Moreover, a trial court may not revoke probation for failing to comply with a condition of probation when compliance was impossible. In State v. Adams, 302 Or App 730, 462 P3d 761 (2020), the trial court revoked the defendant’s probation on the ground he violated the special condition of probation that he failed to complete family court. But family court was not available to the defendant, because he had been “transfer[red] out of [it] when * * * [his] children” were returned “to [his] wife’s care and they moved out of state.” 302 Or App at 739. In light of those facts, the court concluded: “The condition cannot plausibly be construed to require defendant to participate in a family court program that is not offered to him for reasons beyond his control. Therefore, the trial court erred in revoking defendant’s probation on that basis.” Id. See also State v. Larson (where defendant had completed his term of probation, the trial court erred in modifying the terms of the sentence).

Defense counsel should be aware of potential limitations on the type of evidence a trial court may consider in a probation-revocation hearing. For example, in State v. Hammond, 218 Or App 574, 582, 180 P3d 137 (2008) (en banc), the Court of Appeals held that “polygraph examination evidence is not—as a matter of law—inadmissible in probation revocation proceedings.” But the court noted that it did

“not mean to suggest that the admissibility of evidence in such proceedings is entirely without limit. Cf. Gagnon v. Scarpelli, 411 US 778, 93 S Ct 1756, 36 L Ed 2d 656 (1973) (discussing the due process implications of probation revocation proceedings). Additionally, we acknowledge that there may be other reasons for the exclusion of polygraph evidence in proceedings to revoke probation.”

Hammond, 218 Or App at 582 n 3.

Moreover, hearsay proffered at a probation-violation hearing, against the defendant, is objectionable on federal Confrontation Clause grounds. Oregon courts use a four-part balancing test adapted from United States v. Martin, 984 F2d 308 (9th Cir 1993) to determine whether to admit hearsay. See State v. Monk, 244 Or App 152, 260 P3d 607 (2011) (explaining the test, first adopted in State v. Johnson, 221 Or App 394, 190 P3d 455 (2008)). The four factors are: (i) the importance of the challenged evidence to the court’s finding or central issue at stake, (ii) the defendant’s opportunity to refute the evidence, (iii) the difficulty and expense of obtaining witnesses for live testimony, and (iv) the “traditional indicia of reliability borne by the evidence.” Johnson, 221 Or App at 401.

In Johnson, the Court of Appeals affirmed the admission of the hearsay, because it was in the form of a sworn affidavit, the evidence was mostly unchallenged, and the issue was fairly objective—e.g., did the probationer report as directed or change his address. But in cases decided since Johnson, the court has accepted confrontation clause challenges to hearsay. For example, in State v. Wibbens, 238 Or App 737, 243 P3d 790 (2010), the defendant’s probation officer testified that a police officer had told him the defendant smelled of alcohol. The state offered no explanation for why the officer was not present to testify—despite that the officer was a Douglas County sheriff’s deputy and the proceeding was held in Douglas County—and the probation officer did not corroborate any of the statements. The Court of Appeals held that the hearsay statements should not have been admitted and reversed.

State v. Brown, 313 Or App 283, 286, 496 P3d 701 (2021) (owing to the importance of defendant’s interest in confrontation and the absence of good cause for denying it, the admission of the hearsay evidence violated defendant’s due process right to confront an adverse witness, and the trial court erred in admitting that evidence in a probation-revocation hearing);

See also State v. Terry, 240 Or App 330, 252 P3d 332 (2011) (admission of hearsay violated confrontation clause); Monk, 244 Or App at 159 (same).

On the other hand, State v. Jackson, 319 Or App 789, 511 P3d 82 (2022) carried a different type of consideration. “While on probation,” the

“defendant entered a no contest plea to a new harassment charge and was convicted of that offense. The state moved to revoke defendant’s probation on the basis that he violated the probation condition to “[o]bey all laws.” ORS 137.540(1)(j). At the show cause hearing, the state’s only evidence was the judgment of conviction for harassment. Defendant moved to dismiss, arguing that evidence of the conviction was legally insufficient, because it arose from a no contest plea, whereby he did not admit guilt.”

319 Or App at 790.

The Court of Appeals rejected the defendant’s argument. The court explained:

“Although defendant did not affirmatively admit guilt in the criminal proceeding, he did not contest the charges against him and acknowledged that a factual basis supported those charges. The trial court did not err by considering the judgment of conviction as probative as to whether defendant failed to obey all laws. Defendant does not contend that any of the procedural safeguards required for the acceptance of a no contest plea were absent in his case, nor does he assert that he was unaware of the consequences that entering that plea could have on his probation. As a result, the trial court was entitled to rely on the judgment of conviction as evidence that defendant committed a crime and to weigh that evidence against the credibility of defendant’s testimony at the probation hearing. Further, given the record in this case, we conclude that the judgment of conviction was legally sufficient evidence for the trial court to find by a preponderance of the evidence that defendant failed to obey all laws in violation of ORS 137.540(1)(j).”

Id. at 792.

Also worth considering is State v.+Straub, 322 Or App 393, 520 P3d 915 (2022). There, the defendant argued

“that a trial court is required, in the context of a probation revocation hearing, to engage in a colloquy with defendant about the opportunity to contest the allegations and the rights he would be giving up by admitting the allegations. According to defendant, a failure to engage in such a colloquy is reversible error.”

322 Or App at 394.

The Court of Appeals noted that the “[d]efendant’s argument that due process requires a formal colloquy has been rejected by the Ninth Circuit, which has held that a colloquy to ensure a knowing and intelligent waiver in the context of a probation revocation hearing is not required[.]” Id. at 395 (relying on United States v. Segal, 549 F2d 1293, 1298 (9th Cir), cert den, 431 US 919 (1977)). The Court of Appeals rejected the defendant’s argument, stating that “[a]lthough we are not bound by the Ninth Circuit on this issue, here we find its reasoning persuasive and reach the same conclusion.” Id.

§ 5-14.3. Grid Block for Revocation Proceedings.

Upon revocation of probation, the court uses the defendant’s guidelines grid block as the basis for imposing a revocation sanction (unless ORS 137.593(2)(d) applies, see Section 5-15.2). As a matter of statutory law, the court must use the same grid block it used in the defendant’s original sentencing even if, in the interim, it is determined that the grid block was erroneously low. The court may not correct the prior error by using the higher and correct grid block. State v. Bolf, 217 Or App 606, 176 P3d 1287 (2008) (trial court erred in recalculating defendant’s criminal-history score at probation-revocation proceeding; under OAR 213-010-0002, once a probationary sentence is executed, revocation sanctions must flow from grid block used at original sentencing); See also State v. Hoffmeister, 164 Or App 192, 990 P2d 910 (1999) (where court used erroneously low grid-block of 7-I when it imposed probation at original sentencing, it erred in using correct 7-C grid-block to impose probation violation sanction; overruling State v. Guyton, 126 Or App 143, 868 P2d 1335, rev den, 319 Or 36 (1994)).

On the other hand, suppose that in the interim it is determined that the court used an erroneously high grid-block at original sentencing. The defendant could argue that his due process rights supersede the statutory law applied in Hoffmeister, and require that his correct, lower grid-block be used to impose a revocation sanction. As the Ninth Circuit has explained, “[T]he failure of a state to abide by its own statutory commands may implicate a liberty interest protected by the Fourteenth Amendment against arbitrary deprivation by a state.” Fetterly v. Paskett, 997 F2d 1295, 1300 (9th Cir 1993) (citing inter alia Hicks v. Oklahoma, 447 US 343, 100 S Ct 2227, 65 L Ed 2d 175 (1980)). See also United States v. Columbus, 881 F2d 785, 787 (9th Cir 1989) (“[v]iolations of a defendant’s due process rights occur when a court relies on materially false or unreliable information in sentencing”). See generally Arthur W. Campbell, Law of Sentencing, ch 8 (3d ed 2004) (analyzing various due process rights at sentencing).

Unfortunately, in State v. Anderson, 243 Or App 222, 189 P3d 28 (2011), the court rejected these types of challenges. There, the defendant accepted a plea agreement in which he was convicted of third-degree rape and, using a 6-D grid block, was sentenced to a dispositional-departure sentence of probation. A prior assault conviction was the basis of his criminal-history score of “D.” While on probation, the Court of Appeals reversed the prior assault conviction. State v. Anderson, 221 Or App 193, 189 P3d 28 (2008). Subsequent to that, the defendant violated a term of his probation and the trial court revoked his probation. He moved for modification of his criminal-history score from “D” to “G,” by excluding his now reversed assault conviction from his criminal-history assessment. The trial court denied his motion, and the Court of Appeals affirmed. Critically, the court stated: “Defendant was fully aware that the appeal of his assault charge was pending in this court at the time he entered the plea agreement.” 243 Or App at 229.

Defense counsel should not accept Anderson’s validity. The Court of Appeals decision is not final on federal due process claims, so counsel should assert them as means of distinguishing Anderson.

PRACTICE TIP: A key feature of the Anderson opinion is that when the defendant was sentenced, he was aware that he then was challenging the prior conviction that was used to enhance his criminal-history score. This suggests that the defendant should have sought to negotiate a condition subsequent, stating that in the event he won his appeal, he could seek modification of his plea by excluding the assault conviction from his criminal-history assessment. Had he been successful in that effort, he would not have faced a probation-revocation sanction based on what proved to be an erroneously high criminal-history score. In situations like that in Anderson, defense counsel should negotiate for conditions subsequent.

§ 5-14.4. Specialty Court Proceedings.

On behalf of the Oregon District Attorneys Association, the Senate Interim Committee on Judiciary requested the pre-session filing of Senate Bill 218 (2021) to establish procedures for specialty courts. The legislature passed the bill enrolled as Oregon Laws 2021, chapter 330. The bill took effect on January 1, 2022. ORS 171.022.

“‘[S]pecialty court’ has the meaning given that term in ORS 137.680.” ORS 137.532(6). “‘[S]pecialty courts’ means drug court programs as defined in ORS 3.450, veterans courts, mental health courts or any other similar court or docketing system.” ORS 137.680(1).

SB 218 incorporated provisions from the conditional discharge statute, which applies to certain controlled substances offenses. ORS 475.245. But SB 218’s incorporated provisions apply a bit more broadly. It states:

“Whenever a person is charged with a misdemeanor or a Class C felony, other than driving while under the influence of intoxicants, and has been formally accepted into a specialty court, the court, with the consent of the district attorney and the person, may defer further proceedings and place the person on probation. The terms of the probation shall be defined by a probation agreement.”

ORS 137.532(1)(a) (emphasis added).

As in conditional discharge proceedings, “[a] probation agreement carries the understanding that if the defendant fulfills the terms of the agreement, the criminal charges filed against the defendant will be dismissed with prejudice.” ORS 137.532(1(b). “Upon fulfillment of the terms and conditions of the probation agreement, the court shall discharge the person and dismiss the proceedings against the person.” ORS 137.532(3). But if

“[u]pon violation of a term or condition of the probation agreement, the court may resume the criminal proceedings and may find the defendant guilty of the offenses in the accusatory instrument in accordance with the waiver of rights in the probation agreement. The defendant may not contest the sufficiency of the evidence establishing the defendant’s guilt of the offenses in the accusatory instrument.”

ORS 137.532(2).

“Nothing in [the bill] is intended to restrict a person’s participation in a specialty court,” ORS 137.532(5), and a probation “agreement may not contain a requirement that the defendant enter a plea of guilty or no contest on any charge in the accusatory instrument.” ORS 137.532(1)(e). But as a practical matter, the probation agreements imitate guilty or no contest pleas, because the defendant must acquiesce to waiving various “rights * * * with respect to each criminal charge[.]” ORS 137.532(1)(c). The defendant must waive:

• “The right to a speedy trial and trial by jury,” ORS 137.532(1)(c)(A);

• “The right to present evidence on the defendant’s behalf,” ORS 137.532(1)(c)(B);

• “The right to confront and cross-examine witnesses against the defendant,” ORS 137.532(1)(c)(C);

• “The right to contest evidence presented against the defendant, including the right to object to hearsay evidence,” ORS 137.532(1)(c)(D); and

• “The right to appeal from a judgment of conviction resulting from an adjudication of guilt entered under [section 1(2).]” ORS 137.532(1)(c)(E).

In addition, the bill prohibits the defendant from “contest[ing] the sufficiency of the evidence establishing the defendant’s guilt of the offenses in the accusatory instrument.” ORS 137.532(2).

As seen, if the court resumes criminal proceedings consequent to a probation violation, the defendant will be tried to the bench and may suffer delay. Even if the prosecution evidence is insufficient to support a conviction, the defendant will be barred both from moving for judgment of acquittal, and from appealing the conviction on grounds of insufficient evidence.5 Moreover, the defendant will be barred from (i) offering relevant defense evidence, (ii) confronting and cross-examining unreliable and untrustworthy prosecution witnesses, (iii) challenging hearsay and irrelevant evidence offered by the prosecution. So just as the defendant will be barred from appealing the conviction on grounds of insufficient evidence, the defendant will be barred from appealing convictions on the three additional grounds just mentioned.

PRACTICE TIP: The bill’s prohibitions on challenging specialty court adjudications at trial and on appeal deny defendants the judicial due process principle of fundamental fairness. Because the prohibitions transform probation agreements into imitations of guilty or no contest pleas, defense counsel should negotiate provisions dismissing or reducing charges, or sentencing terms, or both.

Finally, before SB 218, well established case law denied jurisdiction over probation violations if no bench warrant or show-cause order was issued before expiration of the probationary period, State v. O’Neal, 24 Or App 423, 545 P2d 910 (1976),6 but with one exception. The conditional discharge system provided the sole exception, which allows courts to issue show cause orders after expiration of the probationary period. ORS 475.245(4).

SB 218 created the second exception to the established case law, by incorporating ORS 475.245(4). The bill’s provision states:

“In the event that the period of probation under this section expires, but the terms and conditions of the probation agreement have not been fulfilled and no probation violation proceeding was initiated prior to the expiration of the period of probation, the court may not discharge the person and dismiss the proceedings against the person. The court shall instead issue an order requiring the person to appear and to show cause why the court should not enter an adjudication of guilt as described in [section 1(2)] due to the failure of the person to fulfill the terms and conditions of the probation agreement prior to expiration of the period of probation. At the hearing on the order to show cause, after considering any evidence or argument from the district attorney and the person, the court may:

“(a) Order a new period of probation to allow the person to fulfill the terms and conditions of the probation agreement; or

“(b) Enter an adjudication of guilt as described in [section 1(2)].”

ORS 137.532(4).

As seen, the defendant may present “evidence and argument” to contest claims of failing to complete the probation agreement, id., which should include relevant defense evidence. But if the defendant is found in violation and is facing an adjudication of guilt, the defendant will be deprived of challenging the adjudication of guilt. ORS 137.532(1)(c) and (2).

PRACTICE TIP: The specialty court is free to consider even a minor failure to complete the agreement as a legitimate ground to order the defendant to show cause after the probationary period has expired. Moreover, there is no statute of limitations on a show cause order. But if there were a significant lapse of time between expiration of the probationary period and issuance of the show cause order, defense counsel could assert a laches defense (and could assert the statute of repose, ORS 12.140, if the lapse of time were 10 years or longer).

§ 5-15. OAR 213-010-0002 REVOCATION SANCTIONS.

When a court revokes probation, it does not impose sentence; instead, it imposes a revocation sanction. State v. Patterson, 269 Or App 226, 234-37, 344 P3d 497 (2015). But see State v. Lane, 357 Or 619, 355 P3d 914 (2015) (holding that under Article I, section 44(1)(b), of the Oregon Constitution, probation-revocation sanctions are “sentences” for purposes of imposing consecutive sentences for crimes having separate victims). OAR 213-010-0002 prescribes imprisonment sanctions for defendants whose probations have been revoked:

(1) For those offenders whose presumptive sentence was probation, the sentence upon revocation shall be to the supervisory authority for a term up to a maximum of six months.

(2) For those offenders whose probationary sentence was either a departure from a presumptive prison sentence or a sentence imposed pursuant to OAR 213-005-0006, the sentence upon revocation shall be a prison term up to the maximum presumptive prison term which could have been imposed initially, if the presumptive prison term exceeds 12 months. For those presumptive prison terms 12 months or less, the sentence upon revocation shall be to the supervisory authority, up to the maximum presumptive prison term.

(3) Notwithstanding (2) of this rule, if a probation sentence is revoked under ORS 137.712(5), the court shall impose the presumptive prison term.

(4) When imposing a revocation sanction, the sentencing judge shall also set a term of post-prison supervision in accordance with OAR 213-005-0002.

(5) No revocation sanction may exceed the limitations established by this rule.

§ 5-15.1. Sanctions for Revocations of Presumptive Sentences of Probation.

OAR 213-010-0002(1) prescribes a maximum sanction of six months’ imprisonment for defendants whose presumptive sentence was probation. State v. Jerue, 134 Or App 668, 669, 896 P2d 613 (1995) (upon revocation of presumptive term of probation, court cannot impose departure term of greater than six months’ incarceration). The term must be served in the local county jail unless it is made consecutive to an incarceration sentence of greater than 12 months. In that case, the six-month sanction must be served in a state facility. ORS 137.124(2)(b).

§ 5-15.2. Sanctions for Revocations of Optional and Departure Sentences of Probation.

A defendant sentenced to either optional or departure probation has a presumptive imprisonment grid block. If the defendant’s probation is revoked, his maximum revocation sanction is not the six-month term of imprisonment specified in OAR 213-010-0002(1). According to OAR 213-010-0002(2) his maximum revocation sanction instead is “a prison term up to the maximum presumptive prison term which could have been imposed when the offender was initially sentenced.” Implementation Manual at 170. Commentary explains:

“This section reflects a policy judgment by the Guidelines Board that an offender granted probation in lieu of presumptive imprisonment should remain subject to the imprisonment that the court could have imposed as a presumptive sentence under these guidelines. This sanction policy should encourage probationary sentences in appropriate cases because the sentencing judge retains the authority to impose the more substantial prison term. The policy should also encourage compliance with the conditions of probation because the offender will be aware of the more severe revocation penalty.”

Id.

Under OAR 213-010-0002(2), when a sentencing court revokes an optional or departure term of probation, the maximum revocation sanction is the defendant’s presumptive maximum. The court may not impose an aggravated durational departure sentence. State v. Taylor, 295 Or App 32, 37, 433 P3d 486 2018) (where defendant’s presumptive maximum sentence was 18 months but court imposed probation, court later erred by imposing 36-month aggravated-departure sentences as a probation-revocation sanction). Were a court to impose such a departure, it would impose a sentence greater than “the maximum presumptive prison term which could have been imposed initially.” OAR 213-010-0002(2) (emphasis added). The court thereby would impermissibly “exceed the limitations established by this rule.” OAR 213-010-0002(5).

If a defendant’s presumptive sentence is one prescribed by statute, rather than his grid-block, the statutorily prescribed presumptive sentence sets his maximum revocation sanction, but only if his judgment of conviction and sentence specifies the statutorily prescribed presumptive sentence as his presumptive sentence.

State v. Orcutt, 280 Or App 439, 444, 380 P3d 1105 (2016). If the judgment fails to specify the statutorily prescribed presumptive sentence as his presumptive sentence, then his grid-block’s presumptive maximum sets his maximum revocation sanction. Orcutt, 280 Or App at 442 n 3;

See also State v. Keyes, 301 Or App 762, 766-68, 459 P3d 935 (2020) (nothing in the 2017 amendments to ORS 137.717 reflects an explicit or implicit legislative intent to give the phrase “sentences imposed,” as used in the 2017 amendments, a different meaning than how State v. Orcutt construed that phrase, as used in the 2013 amendments).

State v. Denson, 280 Or App 225, 233-35, 380 P3d 1170 (2016). Moreover, if between the times of imposition of the probationary sentence and its revocation the legislature reduces the length of the presumptive sentence, the longer presumptive sentence in force at the time probation was imposed still controls. Orcutt, 280 Or App at 444.

But there is one situation where the court may impose a revocation sanction that exceeds the defendant’s presumptive maximum. That situation arises when, as part of plea negotiations, the defendant stipulates to such a sanction. The stipulation will bar the defendant from securing appellate review of the sanction. State v. Silsby, 282 Or App 104, 113, 386 P3d 172 (2016), rev den, 360 Or 752 (2017); Blackledge v. Morrow, 174 Or App 566, 570-71, 26 P3d 851, rev den, 332 Or 558 (2001).7

§ 5-15.3. Consecutive Probation-Revocation Sanctions.

OAR 213-012-0040 limits sentencing court authority to impose consecutive sanctions in cases involving revocations of multiple terms of probation. Chapter 8 addresses that provision’s limitations.

§ 5-15.4. Post-Prison Supervision Terms Attendant to Probation Revocation Sanctions.

According to guidelines’ commentary, OAR 213-010-0002(4) “provides that imprisonment which occurs as a sanction for probation revocation must include a term of [PPS] set according to the most serious crime of conviction as provided by OAR 213-005-0002.” Implementation Manual at 170. Chapter 4 discusses durations of PPS terms.

§ 5-16. INTERMEDIATE PROBATION VIOLATION SANCTIONS.

As a cost-saving measure, the 1993 Legislature significantly changed the system for imprisoning revoked probationers. The legislature did so by adopting an intermediate sanction system designed, which moved most probation-violation proceedings out of the judicial branch and into the executive branch. ORS 137.592 to 137.599.

The intermediate sanction system supplements, rather than replaces, the revocation sanctions that OAR 213-010-0002 authorizes. The stated purpose of the system is to “respond[] to [probation] violations with swift, certain and fair punishments,” ORS 137.592(1), and to ensure that upon violation, probationers are incarcerated on “a reasonably systematic basis that will insure that available prison space is used to house those offenders who constitute a serious threat to the public, taking into consideration the availability of both prison space and local resources.” ORS 137.592(2).

As a matter of both constitutional and statutory law, probationers are entitled to judicial probation-revocation hearings, represented by counsel (appointed at public expense, if necessary). See, e.g., Barker v. Ireland, 238 Or 1, 392 P2d 769 (1964). Under the intermediate-sanction system, a defendant charged with a probation violation may follow the traditional approach and demand a judicial hearing, represented by counsel. Alternatively, he may waive his rights to a hearing and counsel, and submit to intermediate sanctions as determined by the DOC or the local community corrections agency. ORS 137.595(2)(b); ORS 137.597. The maximum incarceration term he may receive under the intermediate sanction system is 60 days in jail. ORS 137.595(3).

Should intermediate sanctions be imposed, the defendant’s probation continues, because the DOC or county agency lacks authority to revoke his probation. ORS 137.593(1). Only the sentencing court has that authority. ORS 137.593(2)(a).

The DOC or county agency must notify the court and district attorney of probation violations. ORS 137.595(2)(f). Before imposing intermediate sanctions, or within four judicial days afterwards but before the defendant has completed his intermediate sanctions, the court or the district attorney may bring the probationer before the court for a judicial hearing. ORS 137.593(2)(c) and (3); ORS 137.599. The court then may vacate the imposition of intermediate sanctions and revoke probation. Or it may vacate the imposition of intermediate sanctions and impose other sanctions or modify the conditions of probation as authorized by law. ORS 137.593(2)(c); ORS 137.599.

If the defendant completes serving his intermediate sanctions before the court acts on his case, the court loses authority to “revoke probation or impose other or additional sanctions[.]” ORS 137.599. This should be the case, even if the fourth judicial day after the court’s receipt of notice has not expired. But even if the defendant has completed service of his intermediate sanctions, the court may impose a sanction or revoke probation if it bases its decision on the violation of a condition different from that which served as the basis for the intermediate sanction. This is true even if the two violations stemmed from the same incident. See State v. Melton, 189 Or App 411, 414-15, 76 P3d 156 (2003) (where defendant violated three conditions of probation, and where probation officer based intermediate sanctions on two of the condition violations, defendant’s completion of intermediate sanctions did not bar court from revoking probation based on third condition violation). Cf. State v. Richards, 361 Or 840, 401 P3d 767 (2017) (the fact that defendant previously had incurred a structured, post-prison supervision sanction for certain violative conduct did not deprive trial court of authority to revoke his probation for the same violative conduct).

§ 5-17. CREDIT FOR TIME SERVED.

In straight-jail sentence cases, defendants are constitutionally entitled to credit for presentence incarceration time (“time-served credit”) against their jail terms. E.g., North Carolina v. Pearce, 395 US 711, 718-19, 89 S Ct 2072, 23 L Ed 2d 656 (1969); Nissel v. Pearce, 307 Or 102, 106-07, 764 P2d 224 (1988); State v. Jones, 117 Or App 174, 175, 842 P2d 466 (1992); ORS 137.320.

In probation cases, time-served credit issues arise in four situations:

1. Crediting presentence incarceration time against a jail term imposed as a condition of probation.

2. Crediting incarceration time awaiting a probation-violation hearing against a non-revocation jail term probation-violation sanction.

3. Crediting incarceration time awaiting a probation-violation hearing against a probation-violation revocation sanction.

4. Crediting time served in jail as a condition of probation against a probation-violation revocation sanction.

In the first situation, it is important to recognize that in contrast to pre-guidelines law, a probationary term under the sentencing guidelines is a sentence. See, e.g., Holcomb, 321 Or at 104. As discussed above, such a sentence may include a jail term in satisfaction of the sanction-unit component of the probationary sentence. Thus, the jail-term component of a probationary sentence itself should be considered a sentence. ORS 137.320(4).

Under Nissel, Jones, and ORS 137.320, a defendant’s time served before sentencing must be credited against his incarceration term. Because probation is a sentence under the guidelines, and its jail-term component itself should be considered a sentence. In Holcomb, the court relied on the statutory scheme that existed before July 18, 1995 to hold that a defendant’s time served prior to sentencing must be credited against the jail-term component of a probationary sentence imposed under the guidelines.

The 1995 Legislature sought to overrule Holcomb, by creating ORS 137.372(3). It purports to grant sentencing courts discretion to deny the type of time-served credit as was at issue in Holcomb, for felonies committed on or after July 18, 1995. Defense counsel should argue that to the extent that ORS 137.372(3) authorizes judicial discretion to deny this time-served credit, the statute violates double jeopardy, Pearce, 395 US at 718-19, and equal protection. Nissel, 307 Or at 106-07.

The second time-served credit situation arises when the defendant is arrested for a probation violation, serves time in jail awaiting his probation-violation hearing, and the trial court imposes time in jail as a violation sanction but otherwise continues probation. In that situation, the court has discretion to deny pre-revocation incarceration credit (so long as the denial does not result in the defendant serving more jail time than his grid-block’s sanction units total allows).

The third time-served credit situation arises when the defendant is arrested for a probation violation, serves time in jail awaiting his probation-violation hearing, and the trial court revokes his probation and imposes incarceration as a revocation sanction. Because probation is a sanction, under the guidelines, the revocation sanction should be considered a sentence, too. Therefore, double jeopardy and equal protection principles should require that the defendant be given credit for time served awaiting his revocation hearing.

The last situation arises when the court imposed a guidelines sentence of probation, the defendant served time in jail as a condition of probation, and sometime during the defendant’s service of the jail term, or after the defendant completed the jail-term, the court revoked probation and imposed an incarceration sanction. The court may deny credit for time the defendant served as a condition of probation. ORS 137.372(1) (2016 ed.) (a defendant “shall receive credit for time served in jail * * * as part of the probationary sentence unless the sentencing judge orders otherwise”). See also Haas v. Hathaway, 144 Or App 478, 928 P2d 331 (1996) (court’s denial of credit for time defendant spent in sex-offender treatment program as a condition of probation, and from which he was expelled, was valid exercise of discretion); OAR 291-100-0090 (regulating time-served credit as a condition of probation).

But 2015 legislation modified the time-served credit principles in situations 3 and 4, but only for revocations of departure and presumptive probations made on or after of August 1, 2015. For decisions made on or after that date, persons revoked from departure probation must receive “credit for the time served in jail after arrest and before commencement of the probationary sentence and for the time served in jail as part of the probationary sentence” (although the court may limit the amount of credit to 90 days). ORS 137.372(1)(a) (emphasis added). Persons revoked from presumptive probation must receive “credit for the time served in jail after arrest and before commencement of the probationary sentence and for the time served in jail as part of the probationary sentence, unless the sentencing judge orders otherwise.” ORS 137.372(1)(b) (emphasis added).

Unfortunately, the 2015 legislation contained an oversight. It did not modify the time-served credit principles for persons revoked from optional probation.

Fortunately, 2018 legislation corrected the oversight. Section 13 of Senate Bill 1543 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2018, chapter 120) amended ORS 137.372(1)(b), to treat revocations of optional probation the same as revocations of presumptive probation. But the amendment is not retroactive to the 2015 legislation’s effective date. Instead, it applies prospectively, “to sentences imposed on or after the [the bill’s] effective date[.]” Or Laws 2018, ch 120, § 19(2). Owing to its emergency clause, Or Laws 2018, ch 120, § 20, the bill took effect when the governor signed it into law, on March 15, 2018.

Finally, the 2015 legislation allows “credit for the time served in jail after arrest and before commencement of” a diversion or a specialty court “program and for the time served in jail as a sanction for violating the terms of the program, unless the sentencing judge orders otherwise.” ORS 137.372(2).
