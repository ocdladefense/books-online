OCDLA Felony Sentencing Manual

Felony Sentencing in Oregon: Guidelines, Statutes, Cases, 2019 edition

Includes January 2024 Updates by Jennelle Meeks Barton

“The centerpiece of the sentencing guidelines is the 99-block Sentencing Guidelines Grid. * * * A [nine-category] ‘Criminal History Scale’ serves as the horizontal axis of the grid.” State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 487, 847 P2d 834 (1993). For purposes of criminal-history scoring, a defendant’s record of prior convictions includes felony juvenile adjudications.1 OAR 213-004-0006(2). The higher a defendant’s criminal-history score, the worse his presumptive sentence will be.

The guidelines criminal-history component is the state’s premiere recidivist scheme. The concept of aggravating punishment, based on the defendant’s criminal record, is grounded on the long-standing principle that “a crime committed by an offender with a prior conviction ‘is considered to be an aggravated offense because a repetitive one.’” Witte v. United States, 515 US 389, 403, 115 S Ct 2199, 132 L Ed 2d 351 (1995) (quoting Gryger v. Burke, 334 US 728, 732, 68 S Ct 1256, 92 L Ed 1683 (1948)). Although double jeopardy prohibits multiple punishments for the same offense, Witte, 515 US at 395-96, using a defendant’s prior criminal convictions to increase his criminal-history score, and hence his sentence on his current crime of conviction, is not double jeopardy. Williams v. Oklahoma, 358 US 576, 586, 79 S Ct 421, 3 L Ed 2d 516 (1959). See also State v. Wheeler, 343 Or 652, 676–77, 175 P3d 438 (2007) (the court’s case law applying the constitutional prohibitions on disproportionate sentences and cruel and unusual punishments, Or Const, Art I, § 16, “permit the legislature to [specify] enhanced sentences on recidivists, even if those sentences would be disproportionate when applied to a defendant without prior convictions”).

On the other hand, such use of prior convictions may be collaterally attacked on the ground that the convictions are void, because they were obtained in violation of constitutional requirements. If they are void, they may not be used in criminal-history scoring. See Chapter 1. Apart from that, the present criminal-history scheme allows bench trials using the preponderance of the evidence standard, rather than jury trials using the reasonable doubt standard, on the existence of a defendant’s record of prior convictions. To that extent the present scheme is subject to attack under federal constitutional principles. Id.

This chapter explains how sentencing courts score defendants’ criminal histories. Adherence to the criminal-history provisions should be sufficient to vindicate a substantial part of a defendant’s due process right to be sentenced based on accurate information. United States v. Columbus, 881 F2d 785, 787 (9th Cir 1989) (“[v]iolations of a defendant’s due process rights occur when a court relies on materially false or unreliable information in sentencing”). See generally Arthur W. Campbell, Law of Sentencing, § 8:1 (3d ed. 2004). A failure to comply with the provisions would constitute not only a statutory violation of the criminal-history provisions, but also a due process violation. Furthermore, the Court of Appeals effectively has held that to provide adequate assistance at sentencing, defense counsel must be aware of the defendant’s correct criminal-history score. Dejac v. Baldwin, 136 Or App 388, 391, 902 P2d 125 (1995). See also Gordon v. Hall, 232 Or App 174, 221 P3d 763 (2009) (where defendant was sentenced to life in prison under special statute authorizing such sentences for persons “sentenced” for a third time for a felony sex offense, see Chapter 4, trial counsel provided prejudicial, inadequate assistance by failing to ascertain that defendant had not been “sentenced” for one of his prior felony convictions). Cf. Johnson v. Babcock, 206 Or App 217, 136 P3d 77, rev den, 341 Or 450 (2006) (where defendant in legal malpractice action was sued for failing to object to 30-year sentence imposed on his former client whose maximum sentence was only 80 months, and where error was not corrected (consequent to federal habeas corpus action) until after former client had served more than the 80 months, Court of Appeals reversed trial court’s grant of summary judgment in favor of malpractice defendant).

§ 3-1. OAR 213-004-0006 CRIMINAL HISTORY SCALE.

OAR 213-004-0006 addresses the principal mechanics for determining whether and how prior convictions are used in scoring a defendant’s criminal history:

(1) The Criminal History Scale includes nine mutually exclusive categories used to classify an offender’s criminal history according to the extent and nature of the offender’s criminal history at the time the current crime or crimes of conviction is sentenced. The nine categories in the scale are labeled alphabetically and are arranged in order of seriousness from the most serious (Criminal History Category A) to the least serious (Criminal History Category I).

(2) An offender’s criminal history is based upon the number of adult felony and Class A misdemeanor convictions and juvenile adjudications in the offender’s criminal history at the time the current crime or crimes of conviction are sentenced. For crimes committed on or after November 1, 1989 a conviction is considered to have occurred upon the pronouncement of sentence in open court. For crimes committed prior to November 1, 1989 a conviction is considered to have occurred upon pronouncement in open court of a sentence, or upon the pronouncement in open court of the suspended imposition of a sentence. Prior adult convictions or juvenile adjudications which have been expunged shall not be considered when classifying an offender’s criminal history. Prior findings of “guilty except for insanity” shall not be considered when classifying an offender’s criminal history.

(3) Whether a prior offense should be classified as a misdemeanor conviction or a felony conviction for criminal history purposes shall be determined by the classification of the offense at the time of conviction as announced by the sentencing judge rather than by the sentence imposed for the crime.

§ 3-1.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-004-0006(1) references the sentencing guidelines grid’s criminal-history scale. OAR 213-004-0006(2) explains that the criminal-history scale “classifies an offender’s criminal history on both a ‘qualitative’ and [a] ‘quantitative’ basis. It establishes criminal history categories based on both the number of prior convictions and the seriousness of those crimes.” Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 50 (1989) (hereafter Implementation Manual).

§ 3-1.2. What Constitutes a “Prior” Conviction.

The most significant part of OAR 213-004-0006(2) is its explanation of when a conviction is a “prior” conviction for criminal-history purposes. That provision prohibits counting crimes for which a defendant has been found guilty, but for which there has not yet been a “pronouncement of sentence in open court.” Id.

The legislature created this provision in 1993 when it adopted Senate Bill 1043. Or Laws 1993, ch 692, § 1(1). By the terms of that bill, the amendment applies only to crimes committed on or after November 1, 1993. Id. But commentary to the predecessor version of OAR 213-004-0006(2) said, “For purposes of classifying an offender’s criminal history, a ‘conviction’ or ‘juvenile adjudication’ should be considered to have occurred upon the pronouncement of sentence in open court.” Implementation Manual at 51. Citing State v. McDonnell, 306 Or 579, 581, 761 P2d 921 (1988), State v. Plourd, 125 Or App 238, 242-43 and n 2, 864 P2d 1367 (1993) declared this commentary authoritative (and also noted that the 1993 amendment codifies the commentary). So regardless of the date of the current crime of conviction, the pronouncement of sentence in open court convention applies in all guidelines cases.

The pronouncement of sentence in open court convention significantly affects defendants who, in a single sentencing hearing, are sentenced for two or more crimes. After sentencing on the first crime there will have been a pronouncement of sentence in open court. The court then may use that crime when calculating the criminal-history score for the second crime. It then may use the first two crimes in calculating the score for the third crime, and so forth, until there are no more crimes to be sentenced. This system of scoring of a defendant’s criminal-history score is referred to as “reconstituting” criminal history. The original authority for reconstitution is State v. Bucholz, 317 Or 309, 855 P2d 1100 (1993).

A sentencing court’s authority to reconstitute criminal history is limited. To the extent the multiple crimes stem from the same criminal episode, they may not be used to reconstitute one another’s criminal-history scores. Bucholz, 317 Or at 314-19; State v. Miller, 317 Or 297, 300-02, 855 P2d 1093 (1993). Reconstitution is allowed only if current crimes of conviction stem from separate criminal episodes, rather than from the same criminal episode.

State v. Martin, 322 Or App 266, 273, 519 P3d 132 (2022);

State v. Bryant, 245 Or App 519, 263 P3d 368 (2011); State v. Sosa, 224 Or App 658, 199 P3d 346 (2008). Moreover, 1993 amendments to OAR 213-004-0006(2) did not repeal the same-criminal-episode limitation on reconstituting criminal history. State v. Allen, 151 Or App 281, 190-91, 948 P2d 745 (1997) (also elaborating history of pertinent provisions).

The phrase “separate criminal episodes” has no real definition; consequently, its definition is “crimes that were not committed during the same criminal episode.” The term “same criminal episode” means, as defined in the former jeopardy statutes, “continuous and uninterrupted conduct[.]” ORS 131.505(4). The consecutive-sentence statutes also use the phrase continuous and uninterrupted conduct. ORS 137.123(2), (4), and (5). Finally, the phrase “same criminal episode” means the same thing as the phrase “same act or transaction,” used in the mandatory joinder statute, ORS 132.560(1)(b)(B). State v. Boyd, 271 Or 558, 565-66, 533 P2d 795 (1975). Cases applying phrases found in the former jeopardy, consecutive sentence, and mandatory joinder statutes apply equally in determining whether crimes stemmed from the same criminal episode for criminal-history scoring purposes.

Moreover, Chapter 1 explains that three distinct definitions are used to define the phrase “same criminal episode.” When none of these definitions applies, the crimes stem from separate episodes, thus permitting reconstitution.

The present statutory scheme allows bench trials using the preponderance standard on the separate-episode finding. Although separate episodes enhance a defendant’s sentence by increasing his criminal-history score, in a 4-3 decision, the Oregon Supreme Court held that the determination of separate episodes is not subject to the rule from Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 US 466, 120 S Ct 2348, 147 L Ed 2d 435 (2000).2 State v. Cuevas, 358 Or 147, 168, 361 P3d 581 (2015). Consequently, at the sentencing hearing, the trial court may use the preponderance standard to find separate episodes.

But in Cuevas, Justice Walters, writing for the dissent, insisted that State v. Bucholz, was wrongly decided and should be overruled:

“This court was wrong in Bucholz. Its characterization of the commentary and legislative history does not fairly represent those sources, which spoke directly to the issue presented in that case. Further, the court ignored the rule’s reference to ‘crimes’ (plural) of conviction. This case requires that we reconsider Bucholz and correct that error.”

Cuevas, 358 Or at 172 (Walters, J., dissenting).

Given that the court decided Cuevas by a bare, four-member majority and changes since then in the court’s composition, the chances that in a new case the court would overrule Bucholz makes renewed litigation of the issue extremely worthwhile.

Making renewed litigation even more worthwhile is Justice Duncan’s opinion in State v. Dulfu, 363 Or 647, 426 P3d 641 (2018). There, Justice Duncan thoroughly surveyed the legislative history of the criminal-history scoring rule, OAR 213-004-0006(2). Dulfu, 363 Or at 659-63. Her survey clearly shows that the legislature did not intend that current crimes of conviction be used in criminal-history scoring, so did not intend for there to be reconstitution.

PRACTICE TIP: In cases where the prosecution seeks criminal-history reconstitution, defense counsel should object by relying on (now) Chief Justice Walters’s dissenting opinion in Cuevas, and on the portion of Justice Duncan’s opinion in Dulfu, which surveys the legislative history of the criminal-history scoring rule, OAR 213-004-0006(2).

Finally, assuming the validity of Bucholz, it states that the text of OAR 213-004-0006(2) “permits consideration of any previous conviction occurring before ‘the time the current crime * * * is sentenced.’” Bucholz, 317 Or at 314 (emphasis added). Defense counsel may rely on the statement—that the provision “permits” reconstitution—as a basis to argue that in a separate-episode situation, the court is not required to reconstitute criminal history; rather, it has discretion to reconstitute criminal history.

State v. Knight, 160 Or App 395, 981 P2d 819 (1999) does not defeat this claim. There, the Court of Appeals reversed the trial court’s refusal to reconstitute the defendant’s criminal history. But the defendant defended the trial court’s decision only on the ground that the crimes stemmed from the same criminal episode. He did not alternatively argue that even if the crimes stemmed from separate episodes, the court acted within its discretion in declining to reconstitute. 160 Or App at 403-05.

PRACTICE TIP: Just as defense counsel may, for example, urge a court not to exercise its discretion to impose an aggravated departure or consecutive sentences, counsel also should argue that a court has discretion not to reconstitute criminal history in separate-episode cases, and as a means of mitigating punishment.

§ 3-1.2.1. Expunged Convictions and Findings of Guilty-But-Insane.

OAR 213-004-0006(2) prohibits counting expunged convictions. See State v. Leslie, 134 Or App 366, 370-71, 895 P2d 342, rev den, 321 Or 397 (1995) (holding that trial court did not err in counting prior conviction, for conviction in fact had not been expunged). Similarly, OAR 213-004-0006(2) says the guidelines do not count commitments to the Psychiatric Security Review Board. The Supplement Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 8 (1992) (hereafter Supplement Manual) confirms this. Cf. State v. Saunders, 195 Or App 357, 97 P3d 1261 (2004) (in prosecution for felony public indecency, trial court properly excluded evidence of defendant’s prior adjudication of guilty except for insanity of public indecency). But see United State v. Bays, 589 F3d 1035 (9th Cir 2009) (a pardon is not the equivalent of an expungement; consequently, a conviction for which defendant was pardoned may be used in assessing his criminal-history score).

As discussed in Section 3-7.1, the state bears the burden of proving a defendant’s criminal history by a preponderance of the evidence. Defense counsel should scrutinize the state’s proffered criminal history to determine which, if any, priors have been expunged. If counsel believes that some of the priors have been expunged, counsel should object to their use. If the state cannot present sufficient proof that the prior have not been expunged, then the priors may not be used.

§ 3-1.2.2. Conditional Discharge.

Conditional discharges are available for certain drug defendants (and also for certain misdemeanants under ORS 137.533). Chapter 5 discusses conditional discharges. Defense counsel should be aware that a discharged conviction “is not a conviction * * * for purposes of disqualifications or disabilities imposed by law upon conviction of a crime.” ORS 475.245. Thus, it should not be used in criminal-history scoring.

§ 3-1.3. Prior Crime Classified by Conviction, not Sentence.

OAR 213-004-0006(3)

“clarifies that the crime of conviction, not the sentence, determines classification of the crime for criminal history purposes. [For example, a prior] felony conviction does not become a misdemeanor for criminal history purposes simply because it [was] sentenced as a misdemeanor.

“* * * * *

“[However, ORS 161.705] authorizes sentencing judges to enter a judgment of conviction for a Class A misdemeanor when the crime of conviction is for any Class C felony, specified Class B felony or ORS 166.720 Racketeering [sic]. * * * When the sentencing court exercises this discretion, the resulting conviction will be treated as a Class A misdemeanor for criminal history purposes.”

Implementation Manual at 53.

PRACTICE TIP: Commentary makes clear that even if a felony conviction is sentenced as misdemeanor, it will be treated as a felony conviction at a subsequent felony sentencing hearing. To preclude such subsequent use, defense counsel should, to the extent ORS 161.705 allows, ask the court not only to sentence the crime as a misdemeanor, but also to designate the conviction as a misdemeanor. See State v. Rodarte, 178 Or App 173, 35 P3d 1116 (2001) (where sentencing court reduced Class C felony conviction for driving while suspended (DWS) to a misdemeanor, defendant’s driving privileges could not be revoked under ORS 809.410(4)).

§ 3-1.4. Single Judicial Proceeding Provision.

Before 1993, OAR 213-004-0006’s predecessor, former OAR 253-04-006, contained something called the “single judicial proceeding rule.” That rule essentially held that multiple crimes in a defendant’s criminal history that were or could have been joined for trial under criteria identical to those found in the joinder-of-offenses statute, ORS 132.560(1)(b)(B), and for which the defendant received concurrent sentences, count as a single conviction for criminal-history scoring purposes. Implementation Manual at 52-53. Cases such as State v. Perkins, 112 Or App 604, 607, 830 P2d 598, rev den, 314 Or 392 (1992); State v. Schnoor, 111 Or App 358, 360-61, 826 P2d 88, rev den, 313 Or 211 (1992); and State v. Munro, 109 Or App 188, 818 P2d 971 (1991), rev den, 312 Or 588 (1992) applied the rule. Unfortunately, 1993 legislation repealed the single judicial proceeding rule. Or Laws 1993, ch 692, § 1(1). The repeal applies in sentencing current crimes committed on or after November 1, 1993. Id. But for those rare cases involving crimes committed before that date, the single-judicial-proceeding rule applies.

§ 3-2. OAR 213-004-0007 CRIMINAL HISTORY CATEGORIES.

OAR 213-004-0007 sets the standards for each of the nine rankings on the criminal-history scale:

The criminal history categories in the Criminal History Scale are:

Criminal History Categories

A—The offender’s criminal history includes three or more person felonies in any combination of adult convictions or juvenile adjudications.

B—The offender’s criminal history includes two person felonies in any combination of adult convictions or juvenile adjudications.

C—The offender’s criminal history includes one adult conviction or juvenile adjudication for a person felony; and one or more adult conviction or juvenile adjudication for a non-person felony.

D—The offender’s criminal history includes one adult conviction or juvenile adjudication for a person felony; but no adult conviction or juvenile adjudications for a non-person felony.

E—The offender’s criminal history includes four or more adult convictions for non-person felonies but no adult conviction or juvenile adjudication for a person felony.

F—The offender’s criminal history includes two or three adult convictions for non-person felonies but no adult conviction or juvenile adjudication for a person felony.

G—The offender’s criminal history includes four or more adult convictions for Class A misdemeanors; one adult conviction for a non-person felony; or three or more juvenile adjudications for non-person felonies, but no adult conviction or juvenile adjudication for a person felony.

H—The offender’s criminal history includes no adult felony conviction or juvenile adjudication for a person felony; no more than two juvenile adjudications for non-person felonies; and no more than three adult convictions for Class A misdemeanors.

I—The offender’s criminal history does not include any juvenile adjudication for a felony or any adult conviction for a felony or Class A misdemeanor.

§ 3-2.1. Criminal History Mechanics.

The criminal-history scale, as summarized in the horizontal axis of the sentencing grid, creates nine categories, labels them alphabetically, and arranges them “in order of seriousness from the most serious (Criminal History Category A) to the least serious (Criminal History Category I).” OAR 213-004-0006(1). The scale “classifies an offender’s criminal history on both a ‘qualitative’ and [a] ‘quantitative’ basis. It establishes criminal history categories based on both the number of prior convictions and the seriousness of those crimes.” Implementation Manual at 50.

The criminal-history scale classifies criminal history qualitatively, by giving greater weight to crimes that inflict or risk inflicting physical harm. The guidelines designate certain of these crimes as “person” crimes. OAR 213-003-0001(14) (setting out list of person felonies); OAR 213-003-0001(15) (setting out list of person misdemeanors). These lists of person crimes are exclusive. OAR 213-003-0001(12). Consequently, any unlisted crime is a “non-person” crime. State v. Smith, 116 Or App 73, 75, 841 P2d 1 (1992) (trial court erred in treating unlisted crime as a person felony)—a point which the Supplement Manual at 9 (Advisory Opinion #3) confirms.

The criminal-history scale does not count juvenile misdemeanors at all, and counts adult misdemeanor convictions only if they are for Class A misdemeanors. The scale counts both adult and juvenile felonies. See State v. Stewart, 123 Or App 147, 859 P2d 545 (1993) (in banc), on recons, 126 Or App 456, 686 P2d 794 (1994), aff’d, 321 Or 1, 892 P2d 1013 (1995) (holding that the state constitution does not prohibit counting juvenile adjudications, notwithstanding that defendants had no right to jury trials during juvenile court proceedings).

Non-person adult Class A misdemeanor convictions, and non-person felony juvenile adjudications, may be used only to obtain a score of no higher than G. OAR 213-004-0007G. To obtain a score of F or higher, the defendant’s criminal history must include two or three adult non-person felony convictions. OAR 213-004-0007F. To obtain a score of D or higher, the defendant’s history must include one or more juvenile or adult person felonies, or multiple adult person misdemeanors. OAR 213-004-0007D. Thus, the scale treats juvenile non-person felonies less seriously than it treats adult non-person felonies. It treats only juvenile person felonies as seriously as it treats adult person felonies.

§ 3-2.1.1. Challenging Juvenile Adjudications.

In State v. Stewart, the appellate courts held that the state constitution does not prohibit counting juvenile adjudications in criminal-history scoring, notwithstanding that defendants are statutorily denied jury trials in juvenile proceedings. The basis for Stewart is the Supreme Court’s holding in State ex rel Juvenile Dept. v. Reynolds, 317 Or 560, 857 P2d 842 (1993). The Reynolds court held that because juvenile court proceedings are rehabilitative in nature, they are not criminal prosecutions; therefore, conducting them without juries is constitutional.

Thus, Stewart essentially holds that because non-juried proceedings are constitutionally valid for juvenile-justice purposes, using juvenile adjudications in adult criminal-history scoring also is constitutional. In so holding, the court rejected the claim that using the adjudications in adult criminal-history scoring would destroy their rehabilitative nature, transform them into criminal prosecutions, and post facto bring the juvenile court proceedings into violation with the state constitution’s jury trial clause.

But under amendments to the juvenile code made subsequent to the decisions in Reynolds and Stewart, juveniles charged with certain sexual offenses now are subject to the sexual-predator statutes. In State ex rel Upham v. McElligott, 326 Or 547, 552, 956 P2d 179 (1998), the Supreme Court confronted the question of whether the 1995 amendments “rendered a delinquency proceeding for a sexual offense ‘the functional equivalent of a criminal prosecution.’” The court declined to address the claim, for the juvenile was not affected by the 1995 amendments. 326 Or at 552.

Because the court did not address the claim, it remains viable. It may be coupled with other legislation redefining the purposes of the juvenile system in delinquency cases “to protect the public and reduce juvenile delinquency and to provide fair and impartial procedures for the * * * disposition of allegations of delinquent conduct.” ORS 419C.001(1). In some cases, it also may be coupled with legislation:

• requiring registration of juvenile sex defendants, ORS 163A.005(3)(a)(B);

• authorizing juvenile courts to recoup costs of court-appointed counsel for juveniles, ORS 419C.203;

• deleting the word “protective” from the “protective custody” euphemism for arrest, previously used in the juvenile code, ORS 419C.080;

• allowing the use of juvenile adjudications as racketeering predicates, ORS 166.715(4); and

• incorporating crime victims’ constitutional rights into the juvenile code. ORS 419C.273.

Defense counsel should review their clients’ criminal histories to see if they contain any juvenile adjudications subject to these and any other legislative amendments that arguably transform juvenile proceedings into criminal prosecutions. If they find any, counsel should appropriately object to using the adjudications in criminal-history scoring.

In doing so, counsel also should rely on In re L.M., 286 Kan 460, 186 P3d 164 (2008). There, the Kansas Supreme Court (i) overruled its 24-year-old decision in Findlay v. State, 235 Kan 462, 681 P2d 20 (1984), (ii) concluded that a juvenile’s right to jury trial is constitutionally protected, and (iii) declared unconstitutional a Kansas statute that allowed trial court discretion to determine whether a juvenile should be granted a jury trial. “Kansas is now among the small yet pioneering group of states that uphold [the jury trial] right for juveniles.” Celeste Miller, Kansas Supreme Court: Juveniles Have Constitutional Right to Jury Trial, Juvenile Law Reader 1 (Aug. 2008).

The L.M. court relied on amendments to the state’s juvenile code adopted subsequent to the court’s earlier decision in Findlay—amendments that in many respects are similar to amendments to Oregon’s juvenile code adopted subsequent to the decision in Reynolds, many of which are discussed earlier in this section. The L.M. court explained, “Changes to the Kansas Juvenile Justice Code since 1984 have eroded the benevolent, child cognizant, rehabilitative, and parens patriae character that distinguished it from the adult criminal system.” 186 P3d at 165. “Based on its conclusion that the Kansas juvenile justice system had become more akin to an adult criminal prosecution, the Court held that juveniles have a constitutional right to a jury trial under the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments.” Miller, Juvenile Law Reader at 3 (citing L.M., 186 P3d at 170.)

As seen, the L.M. court relied on the same sorts of amendments to Kansas’s juvenile code that the Oregon Legislature approved since the time of the Reynolds and Stewart. Defense counsel thus could rely on L.M. to challenge the constitutional validity of juvenile adjudications that the state would use in assessing a defendant’s criminal-history score.

Counsel also should be aware that the Supreme Court decided State v. Stewart by a 4-2 decision. Former Justice Unis wrote for the dissent. The Court of Appeals was even more divided in Stewart, deciding it 5-4. Regardless of the legislation, defense counsel should not be shy about seeking reconsideration of Stewart—particularly because the court’s composition has changed considerably since the time it decided Stewart. Therefore, in addition to relying on the subsequent legislative amendments that tend to mirror the amendments the Kansas’s juvenile code, counsel should rely on Unis’s and De Muniz’s dissents in Stewart to object to using juvenile adjudications in criminal-history scoring.

Finally, in State v. Harris, 339 Or 157, 118 P3d 236 (2005), the Supreme Court held that because juvenile adjudications are not “convictions” per se, dicta from Apprendi, which are discussed in Chapter 1, and which purport to exempt prior convictions from the Apprendi rule, do not apply to juvenile adjudications. Consequently, Harris holds that a defendant’s record of juvenile adjudications may not be used to aggravate his sentence by increasing his criminal-history score, unless during his trial, the trier found the existence of the adjudications beyond a reasonable doubt, or the defendant admitted to the existence of the adjudications when he pled to the indictment. See State v. Lafferty, 240 Or App 564, 247 P3d 1266 (2011) (where defendant waived jury to crimes charged in indictment, but not to juvenile adjudication, which state had given notice of its intention to prove, and where no jury found existence of juvenile adjudication as required by Harris, trial court properly sustained defendant’s objections to using an adjudication as an enhancement fact). See also United States v. Tighe, 266 F3d 1187 (9th Cir 2001) (Apprendi rule prohibits using juvenile adjudications to enhance sentences); State v. Brown, 879 So 2d 1276 (La 2004) (same).

Lafferty thus makes clear that because the existence of juvenile adjudications are subject to the Apprendi rule, they are “enhancement facts” as ORS 136.760(2) defines that term. As such, the pleading and proof requirements of the enhancement-fact scheme, ORS 136.760 to 136.792, apply to juveniles adjudications. For example, because the existence of juvenile adjudications “relate[] to the offender,” the may be tried only during the “sentencing” phase of his trial (if there is one). ORS 136.773. Unless these notification and proof requirements both are met, juvenile adjudications may not be used to enhance the defendant’s sentence by increasing his criminal-history score.

§ 3-2.2. Unranked Crimes.

The guidelines leave a limited number of crimes unranked. As discussed in Chapter 2, OAR 213-004-0004 authorizes trial courts to rank those crimes. But neither that provision nor any other provision authorizes courts to designate unranked offenses as person crimes. Because courts have no such authority, and because the lists of person crimes are exclusive, prior convictions for unranked offenses may be treated only as non-person crimes. OAR 213-003-0001(12). Accord Smith, 116 Or App at 75.

EXAMPLE: A defendant’s criminal history contains a conviction for the unranked crime of conspiracy to commit a first-degree assault. First-degree assault is listed as a person felony, and attempts and solicitations to commit listed crimes are person felonies; however, conspiracies to commit listed crimes are not listed as person felonies. OAR 213-003-0001(14). At a subsequent sentencing hearing for a new crime, the court may treat the conspiracy conviction only as a non-person felony.

§ 3-2.3. Non-Person Crimes Sentenced First.

Bearing in mind the “sentencing in open court” convention discussed in Section 3-1.2, at a sentencing hearing for crimes that do not stem from the same criminal episode, defense counsel may try to use the order of sentencing to minimize the defendant’s sentence.

For example, consider a hypothetical defendant with no prior criminal history who awaits sentencing on two level 8 felonies that did not stem from the same criminal episode. One is a person felony; the other is a non-person felony. The court intends to impose consecutive, presumptive maximum sentences. No matter which conviction the court sentences first, the defendant will be in grid block 8-I and will receive 18 months’ imprisonment.

If the court first imposes sentence on the person felony, that conviction would be used to reconstitute the defendant’s criminal-history score on the non-person felony. For the non-person felony, the defendant would be in grid block 8-D. The court would impose 28 months’ imprisonment. The defendant’s maximum consecutive total would be 46 months [18 + 28 = 46].

But if the court imposes sentence on the non-person felony first, the defendant’s grid block for the person felony would be 8-G. The court would impose 22 months’ imprisonment. Under this order of sentencing, the defendant’s maximum consecutive total would be only 40 months [18 + 22 = 40].

PRACTICE TIP: As explained in the above example, sentencing the non-person felony before the person felony shortens the defendant’s total sentence by six months (46 - 40 = 6). No standards guide the court on the order of sentencing. Because of this, defense counsel should argue that to avoid the sort of the equal privileges and immunities issue, Or Const, Art I, § 20, that was what at issue in State v. Savastano, 354 Or 64, 309 P3d 1083 (2013), and which is discussed in Chapter 1, the court should select the alternative that yields the shorter sentence, i.e., sentence the non-person crime first, and the person crime last.

§ 3-3. OAR 213-004-0008 PERSON CLASS A ADULT MISDEMEANOR CONVICTIONS.

OAR 213-004-0008 sets out the so-called “two-for-one” provision:

Every two prior adult convictions of person Class A misdemeanors in the offender’s criminal history shall be counted as one adult conviction of a person felony for criminal history purposes.

§ 3-3.1. Odd Number of Adult Person Misdemeanors.

OAR 213-004-0008 says “[e]very two” adult Class A person misdemeanors equal one person felony conviction. See, e.g., State v. Witherspoon, 250 Or App 316, 320 n 1, 280 P3d 1004 (2012) (explaining two-for-one rule). If a defendant has an odd number of adult, Class A person misdemeanors, his number of person felonies must be calculated by rounding down. For example, if his criminal-history contains five adult Class A person misdemeanors, he is treated as having two person felonies.

The two-for-one rule allows the use of adult convictions only. A trial court errs if it uses juvenile adjudications in a two-for-one calculation. State v. Jaime, 186 Or App 368, 63 P3d 53 (2003).

§ 3-4. OAR 213-004-0009 PRIOR ORS 813.010 (DUII) CONVICTIONS.

OAR 213-004-0009 controls how prior DUII convictions are counted in criminal-history scoring:

In determining criminal history for a person convicted of a felony that has operation of a motor vehicle as an element, or of a felony that involved death, injury or property damage caused by the use of a motor vehicle:

(1) Every two prior convictions for misdemeanor driving while under the influence of intoxicants or comparable statutory offense or ordinance violation in the offender’s criminal history shall be counted as one conviction of felony driving under the influence of intoxicants; and

(2) Each felony conviction for driving while under the influence of intoxicants shall be counted as a person felony and each misdemeanor conviction for driving while under the influence of intoxicants shall be counted as a person Class A misdemeanor.

§ 3-4.1. Intent of Provision.

The 1999 Legislature’s House Bill 2002 amended OAR 213-04-0009. The amendments apply to crimes committed on or after December 31, 1999. For crimes committed before that date, the former provision applies. The former provision says that where a defendant is being sentenced for a conviction for a driving-related first- or second-degree manslaughter or criminally negligent homicide, every three prior DUII-type convictions count as one person-felony. See State v. Westcott, 139 Or App 374, 377, 912 P2d 400, rev den, 323 Or 691 (1996) (explaining provision). The rounding down convention used in “two-for-one” provision situations, OAR 213-004-0008, applies here as well. For example, if a defendant to whom former OAR 213-004-0009 applies has four or five prior DUII-type convictions, he is treated as having one person felony.

House Bill 2002 (1999) made a defendant’s fourth (and each subsequent) DUII conviction in 10 years a class C felony. ORS 813.010(4)-(5). Felony DUII was ranked at seriousness level 6. ORS 813.012(1). In 2010, the voters approved Ballot Measure 73, which made a defendant’s third DUII in 10 years a class C felony. The Criminal Justice Commission ranked this new felony DUII at seriousness level 4. OAR 213-017-0006(73). Both level 4 and level 6 felony DUII convictions carry mandatory minimum incarceration terms of 90 days. ORS 813.011(3). See also Chapter 4.

Ballot Measure 73 will have significant consequences for a person being sentenced for a fourth DUII. Prior to the measure’s passage, the two-for-one DUII rule meant that, absent any other criminal history, a defendant being sentenced for a fourth DUII would be sentenced using grid-block 6-D, which carries a presumptive-sentence range of 13-14 months in prison. Subsequent to the measure’s passage, a defendant being sentenced for a fourth DUII, and whose third DUII was subject to Measure 73, would be sentenced using grid-block 6-B, which carries a presumptive-sentence range of 19-24 months in prison. This is because the defendant’s first two misdemeanor DUII convictions will count as one person felony, OAR 213-004-0009(1), and, under Measure 73, his third DUII conviction will also count as a person felony. OAR 213-004-0009(2).

Under Oregon’s felony DUII scheme, a defendant’s prior DUII convictions are double counted in determining his punishment. They are used first to increase his crime classification from a misdemeanor to a felony, and then are used a second time to increase his criminal-history score to D. If his prior convictions in fact are double counted, defense counsel should assert the double jeopardy and due process objections discussed in Chapter 1.

Also under HB 2002, OAR 213-004-0009 applies to any “crime of conviction [that] is for a felony that has operation of a motor vehicle as an element, or for a felony that involved death, injury or property damage caused by the use of a motor vehicle[.]” In sentencing for such a crime, each prior felony DUII conviction counts as one person felony. Each prior misdemeanor DUII counts as a person misdemeanor. Thus, every two misdemeanor DUII convictions count as a person felony. OAR 213-004-0008.

Using HB 2002’s criminal-history-scoring provisions to classify prior DUIIs committed before the bill’s effective December 31, 1999 effective date, to sentence a new crime committed on or after the bill’s effective date, is not ex post facto. Thomas, 187 Or App at 194; State v. Hurd, 182 Or App 361, 49 P3d 107 (2002).

OAR 213-004-0009’s peculiar criminal-history scoring scheme authorizes enhancing a defendant’s sentence by two alternative means: (i) when the defendant is being sentenced for “a felony that has operation of a motor vehicle as an element”; and (ii) when he is being sentenced for “a felony that involved death, injury or property damage caused by the use of a motor vehicle[.]” The latter means involves a factual scenario that is not an element of the crime of DUII, consequently, it may implicate the rules from Apprendi v. New Jersey and State v. Wedge, 293 Or 598, 652 P2d 773 (1982), and, if so, the enhancement-fact scheme of ORS 136.760 to ORS 136.792. See Chapter 1 (discussing Apprendi and Wedge rules, and enhancement-fact scheme).

In situations where the defendant fully asserts his jury trial rights and the state relies exclusively on the latter alternative, in all likelihood the pleading requirements of ORS 136.765 will not have been met, and the trier of fact will not have found the alternative beyond a reasonable doubt. Without the requisite pleading and finding, defense counsel may assert the constitutional objections under the Apprendi and Wedge rules, so under the enhancement-fact scheme, too, to object to use of criminal history-based sentence-enhancement scheme.

§ 3-4.2. Out-of-State and Municipal DUII Convictions.

As discussed above, OAR 213-004-0009 authorizes sentencing courts to classify as a person crime a “comparable statutory offense or ordinance violation,” which constitutes a DUII conviction. Owing to that incorporation language, this provision authorizes courts to use convictions under former Oregon statutes, as well as out-of-state and municipal-court convictions.

However, a claim may be made that whether a former Oregon statute, an out-of-state statute, or an in-state or out-of-state municipal ordinance, is “comparable” to Oregon’s current DUII statute is subject to the Apprendi rule’s variation that applies to the “characteristics” of prior convictions that would be used as predicates to enhance punishment under recidivist schemes. Section 1-7.8.1 discusses this claim.

§ 3-5. OAR 213-004-0010 BURGLARY I.

As discussed in Chapter 2, first-degree burglary may be classified at seriousness-level 7, 8, or 9, depending on the nature of the offense. OAR 213-004-0010 explains when the crime is treated as a person felony:

(1) A prior Burglary I (ORS 164.225) conviction for an offense committed after the effective date of these rules shall be classified for criminal history:

(a) As a prior person felony if that prior conviction was classified as a Crime Category 9 or 8 offense on the Crime Seriousness Scale (OAR 213-018-0025(1) and (2)); and

(b) As a prior non-person felony if that prior conviction was classified as a Crime Category 7 offense on the Crime Seriousness Scale (OAR 213-018-0025(3)).

(2) A prior Burglary I (ORS 164.225) conviction for an offense committed before the effective date of these rules or any juvenile adjudication for conduct, committed before or after the effective date of these rules, which if committed by an adult would have constituted Burglary I shall be classified as a prior person felony if the State proves by a preponderance of the evidence that the criminal conduct would have been classified as a Crime Category 9 or 8 offense on the Crime Seriousness Scale (OAR 213-018-0025(1) and (2)), however if the State does not meet that burden of proof, then the prior offense shall be classified as a prior non-person felony in crime category 7 (OAR 213-018-0025(3)).

§ 3-5.1. Prior Guidelines-Era First-Degree Burglaries.

If a prior first-degree burglary was sentenced under the guidelines and was classified at level 8 or 9, it is treated as a person felony. OAR 213-004-0010(1)(a). See also OAR 213-003-0001(14) (identifying level 8 and 9 burglaries as person felonies). If it was classified at level 7, it is treated as a non-person felony. OAR 213-004-0010(1)(b).

As explained in Chapter 2, a court may not properly give a crime a higher subclassification factor unless the state charged and the trier of fact found beyond a reasonable doubt that the defendant committed the subclassification conduct. Thus, if a defendant in fact committed a level 8 or 9 burglary, but the state neglected to charge, or the trier of fact did not find, the subclassification factor, the court may not impose a level 8 or 9 sentence. Despite the actual facts of the crime, the court may impose only a level 7 sentence. State v. Smith, 116 Or App 558, 560, 842 P2d 806 (1992).

The same holds true at sentencing for any subsequent guidelines felonies. OAR 213-004-0010(1) requires trial courts to base a prior burglary’s classification on its seriousness level, and not on the crime’s actual facts. As the Court of Appeals has explained, “An inquiry about criminal history is not an occasion to relitigate the facts underlying [a] conviction.” State v. Tapp, 110 Or App 1, 4, 821 P2d 1098 (1991). Indeed, such relitigation could violate the former jeopardy prohibition on successive prosecutions for the same acts, ORS 131.515, and the jury trial principles discussed in Chapter 1. See Shepard v. United States, 544 US 13, 24–26, 125 S Ct 1254, 161 L Ed 2d 205 (2005) (to determine characteristics of prior conviction that would be used to aggravate punishment for current crime, Sixth Amendment jury trial principles limit court to considering only court documents from the prior proceedings such as the transcript, the charging instrument, and the plea petition). Therefore, notwithstanding the actual facts of the prior burglary, the state’s failure to charge and prove the subclassification factor means that at a sentencing hearing for a subsequent felony, the court must treat the prior burglary as a level 7, non-person felony.

§ 3-5.2. Prior Non-Guidelines First-Degree Burglaries.

As explained in Section 3-6, if a defendant’s criminal history contains an out-of-state conviction, and the charging instrument and judgment for that conviction disclose that the crime’s elements are identical to a person felony in Oregon, then the out-of-state conviction may be treated as a person felony. Therefore, an out-of-state conviction for a crime having elements identical to a level 8 or 9 burglary may be treated as a person felony.

Similarly, OAR 213-004-0010(2) specifies circumstances in which the court may treat a non-guidelines Oregon conviction—i.e., a pre-guidelines conviction or a pre- or post-guidelines juvenile adjudication—for first-degree burglary as a person felony. According to the rule, the court may do so if at sentencing the state proves by a preponderance of the evidence that had the burglary been a guidelines offense, it would have been classified at level 8 or 9. Otherwise, the court must treat the burglary as a non-person felony. Id. See State v. Duncan, 113 Or App 665, 667, 833 P2d 1329 (1992) (applying provision).

But in a non-guidelines case, the state may not have charged and the trier of fact may not have found beyond a reasonable doubt, the level 8 or 9 subclassification factor. In effect, OAR 213-004-0010(2) allows the state to reopen its prior case, to allege and prove to the trial court, without a jury waiver and by a mere preponderance of the evidence, that the defendant’s non-guidelines burglary—committed however long ago—was a level 8 or 9 burglary.

Thus, OAR 213-004-0010(2) would make it notably easier to treat a prior, non-guidelines first-degree burglary as a person felony than a prior, guidelines burglary. OAR 213-004-0010(2) carries the following constitutional problems:

1. Equal Privileges and Immunities. In a case involving a prior, post-guidelines juvenile burglary, the state will be allowed to prove the level 8 or 9 factor by a preponderance of the evidence. Had the burglary been tried as an adult crime, the state would have had to prove the level 8 or 9 factor beyond a reasonable doubt. Under this scenario, the state is allowed to rely on the lower, preponderance standard solely because the defendant was a juvenile when he committed the prior burglary. Consequently, the defendant is deprived of a benefit—the reasonable doubt standard—solely because of his age when he committed the prior burglary. This application of OAR 213-004-0010(2) may violate the equal privileges and immunities clause of Article I, section 20, of the Oregon Constitution, as discussed in Chapter 1.

2. Former Jeopardy. If the trier of fact did not find the level 8 or 9 factor, the only way the state could prove the factor is by reopening the prior prosecution or juvenile adjudication, and litigating the level 8 or 9 matter. Allowing the state to reopen the prior prosecution or juvenile adjudication may violate the former jeopardy, ORS 131.515, and constitutional double jeopardy, US Const, Amends V, XIV; Or Const Art I, § 12, prohibitions on successive prosecutions. See also Breed v. Jones, 421 US 519, 528, 95 S Ct 1779, 44 L Ed 2d 346 (1975) (double jeopardy principles apply to juvenile court proceedings).

3. Jury Trial. As explained in Chapter 1, in the absence of the defendant’s admission, the federal constitution prohibits using facts to increase a defendant’s sentence unless his jury, or the trial court consequent to a valid jury waiver, found those facts beyond a reasonable doubt. Although the fact of a prior conviction purportedly is an exception to this constitutional principle, that exception applies only to the bare fact of a prior conviction. It does not apply to the characteristics of a prior conviction that would be used to increase a defendant’s sentence for his current crime. Instead, proof of the characteristics is subject to the constitutional requirements discussed in Section 1-7.8.1. These requirements should sharply limit, and may altogether prohibit, using OAR 213-004-0010(2) to classify non-guidelines burglaries as person crimes—particularly if proving the characteristics would require reopening the prosecution in violation of the former and double jeopardy prohibitions on successive prosecutions.

To avoid these constitutional problems, OAR 213-004-0010(2) should be construed as prohibiting classifying a non-guidelines first-degree burglary as a person felony unless a level 8 or 9 factor was alleged and either the defendant admitted the factor or his jury, or the trial court after a valid jury waiver, found the allegation beyond a reasonable doubt. See Jones v. United States, 526 US 227, 239, 119 S Ct 1215, 143 L Ed 2d 311 (1999) (when narrowing construction would shield law from constitutional challenge, law should be so construed); State v. Kitzman, 323 Or 589, 602, 920 P2d 134 (1996) (same). But to determine whether the conviction has these characteristics, the sentencing court may consider only court documents from the prior proceedings such as the transcript, the charging instrument, and the plea petition. Shepard, 544 US at 24–26).

§ 3-5.3. Vicarious Liability.

Regardless of the possible constitutional challenges to OAR 213-004-0010(2), practitioners must ensure that in classifying non-guidelines burglaries, sentencing courts do not violate the holding from State v. Flanigan, 316 Or 329, 851 P2d 1120 (1993). As discussed in Chapter 2, Flanigan says that if a subclassification factor is offender specific (as opposed to offense specific), a defendant may not be held vicariously liable for his accomplice’s conduct that proves the factor. 316 Or at 333 (citing State v. Lark, 316 Or 317, 325, 851 P2d 1114 (1993)).

OAR 213-004-0010(2) should be applied in line with Flanigan. OAR 213-018-0025(1) ranks first-degree burglary at level 9 if “the offender was armed with a deadly weapon; or” “the offender caused or threatened to cause physical injury to the victim.” (Emphasis added.) Thus, the subclassification factor is offender specific, so it may not be the basis for vicarious liability.

EXAMPLE: At a defendant’s sentencing, the state proves a prior burglary of an unoccupied dwelling in which only his accomplice was armed. Because the law prohibits vicarious liability for the level 9 subclassification, the state will have proved that the defendant committed only a level 7 burglary. The court must treat the prior burglary as a non-person felony.

§ 3-6. OAR 213-004-0011 OUT-OF-STATE ADULT CONVICTIONS AND JUVENILE ADJUDICATIONS.

OAR 213-004-0011 authorizes sentencing courts to use out-of-state convictions and juvenile adjudications in scoring a defendant’s criminal history:

(1) An out-of-state adult conviction shall be used to classify the offender’s criminal history if the elements of the offense would have constituted a felony or Class A misdemeanor under current Oregon law.

(2) Out-of-state juvenile adjudications shall be used to classify the offender’s criminal history if the elements of the offense would have constituted a felony under current Oregon law if committed by an adult.

(3) Out-of-state adult convictions and out-of-state juvenile adjudications described in sections (1) and (2) of this rule shall be classified as person felonies or person Class A misdemeanors if the elements of the offense would have constituted an offense under Oregon law listed at OAR 213-003-0001(14) or (15).

(4) Any adult conviction arising from a federal or military tribunal shall be classified as a person felony or person Class A misdemeanor if the elements of the offense would have constituted an offense under Oregon law listed at OAR 213-003-0001(14) or (15).

(5) Any adult conviction or juvenile adjudication under a subsequently repealed statute shall be classified as a person felony or person Class A misdemeanor if the elements of the offense would have constituted an offense currently listed at OAR 213-003-0001(14) or (15).

§ 3-6.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-004-0011(1) states that an out-of-state conviction may be used in scoring a defendant’s criminal history if the elements of the conviction “correspond to the elements of an Oregon felony or Class A misdemeanor.” Tapp, 110 Or App at 4.

State v. Shields, 309 Or App 516, 482 P3d 784 (2021) (trial court erred in using defendant’s Georgia convictions for aggravated assault and possession of firearm during commission of a felony in setting his criminal history score); State v. Altamirano-Juarez, 304 Or App 47, 54, 466 P3d 1037 (2020) (trial court violated OAR 213-004-0011(1) by using defendant’s State of Washington conviction for vehicular assault in setting his criminal-history score);

See State v. Sumpter, 227 Or App 513, 206 P3d 1088 (2009) (trial court erred in using Nevada conviction in defendant’s criminal-history scoring, for state failed to prove a comparable offense in Oregon). Accord State v. Carlton, 361 Or 29, 43, 388 P3d 1093 (2017) (in determining whether a sentence for an out-of-state conviction for a sex crime is a “comparable offense,” so may be used as a predicate to impose sentence under the repeat felony sex offender statute, ORS 137.719(3)(b)(B), the out-of-state conviction’s elements must be “the same as or nearly the same as the elements of an Oregon felony sex crime, not to offenses that merely share a core similarity with such an offense”). Likewise, OAR 213-004-0011(2) states that an out-of-state juvenile adjudication may be used “if the elements of the offense would have constituted a felony under current Oregon law.” OAR 213-004-0011(2). Out-of-state convictions and adjudications include federal convictions. State v. Lee, 110 Or App 42, 43, 822 P2d 138 (1991) (per curiam). Moreover, OAR 213-004-0011(3)-(5) allow classifying out-of-state convictions as person crimes if their elements match with a person crime under Oregon’s criminal code.

An issue that occasionally arises is whether an out-of-state conviction for a misdemeanor, whose elements match with an Oregon felony, should be classified as a misdemeanor or as a felony in scoring the defendant’s criminal history. That issue is resolved by OAR 213-004-0006(3), which states in pertinent part: “Whether a prior offense should be classified as a misdemeanor conviction or a felony conviction [is] determined by the classification of the offense at the time of conviction[.]” If the other state’s statute classified the conviction as a misdemeanor, the conviction also is classified as a misdemeanor “for criminal history purposes,” id., regardless of its felony classification in Oregon.

Conversely, if the out-of-state conviction was classified as a felony, but would have been classified as a misdemeanor had it been committed in Oregon, an argument may be made that notwithstanding OAR 213-004-0006(3), the out-of-state conviction must be classified as a misdemeanor. Cf. Lopez v. Gonzales, 549 US 47, 127 S Ct 625, 166 L Ed 2d 462 (2006) (conduct made a felony under state law, but which is treated as a misdemeanor under federal law, does not serve as a felony conviction for purposes of determining whether an alien should be deported).

If the crime on which the out-of-state conviction or adjudication exists could be proved in alternative ways, only some of which match the elements of an Oregon crime, the conviction may be used only if the state shows that the alternative under which the defendant was convicted corresponds to an Oregon crime. State v. R-Robinson, 277 Or App 107, 369 P3d 1242 (2016) (where Colorado assault statute was broader than Oregon’s, and where charging instrument and judgment did not demonstrate that elements relied on to secure defendant’s Colorado conviction match the elements of the Oregon crime, Court of Appeals accepted state’s concession that trial court erred in using defendant’s Colorado conviction to set his criminal-history score); State v. Provencio, 153 Or App 90, 93-95, 955 P2d 774 (1998) (citing State v. Golden, 112 Or App 302, 305-06, 829 P2d 88 (1992)). See also State v. Torres, 182 Or App 156, 165, 48 P3d 170 (2002) (where state failed to produce evidence showing that elements of Washington conviction matched elements of Oregon crime, trial court erred in using Washington conviction to calculate criminal-history score). Consistent with Shepard, 544 US at 24–26, to prove such a correspondence, the state may rely on the elements pleaded in the accusatory instrument or stated in the judgment. Provencio, 153 Or App at 95; Golden, 112 Or App at 306; Tapp, 110 Or App at 4.

PRACTICE TIP: When presented with a record of out-of-state conviction(s), defense counsel should carefully scrutinize each of the proffered convictions to determine the specific statutory subsection under which the defendant was convicted. Counsel should then question whether there is a way the crime could have been proved that would not constitute a crime, or person crime, under Oregon law. If the answer is yes, the conviction may not be used. Particular attention should be paid to convictions in states that employ common law definitions of crimes, as these definitions are often far different from Oregon’s. See, e.g., Cal Penal Code § 240 (defining assault as “an unlawful attempt, coupled with a present ability, to commit a violent injury on the person of another”).

But there is a claim may be made that whether the elements of an out-of-state statute, on which a conviction or juvenile adjudication was based, match with the elements of an Oregon crime is subject to the Apprendi rule’s variation that applies to the “characteristics” of prior convictions that would be used as predicates to enhance punishment under recidivist schemes. Section 1-7.8.1 discusses this claim.

A sentencing court may not base a defendant’s criminal-history score on a constitutionally infirm conviction. Furthermore, a defendant may challenge the constitutional validity of an out-of-state conviction which would be used to enhance his sentence.3 The Court of Appeals has held that the Oregon Constitution is not used in deciding the conviction’s validity. Only the federal and sister state’s constitutions are used. State v. Graves, 150 Or App 437, 441, 947 P2d 209 (1997) (in banc), rev den, 326 Or 507 (1998) (no error in counting Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ) convictions for theft crimes, for they were valid under federal constitution). But, cf. ORS 161.725(4)(d) (UCMJ conviction may not be used in determining whether defendant is a dangerous offender if the act were “denounced only by military law”). However, Graves seems to conflict with the Supreme Court’s decision that a defendant must be allowed to challenge the constitutional validity of such convictions, and that Oregon law is used in deciding the challenge. Clark v. Gladden, 247 Or 629, 636-37, 432 P2d 182 (1967).

PRACTICE TIP: Judge Edmonds agreed with Graves’s claim on the merits. Graves, 150 Or App at 444-48 (Edmonds, J., concurring). However, Graves pled guilty in military court. Because of those pleas, Judge Edmonds said that “there is no showing in this case that [Graves] was harmed by any of the alleged constitutional infirmities that he raises.” Graves, 150 Or App at 448 (Edmonds, J., concurring). Accord State v. Cervantes-Oropeza, 215 Or App 518, 526, 170 P3d 1114 (2007). For all anyone knows, the Supreme Court was interested in Graves’s legal issue, but denied review because it saw Graves’s guilty pleas as a procedural obstacle to deciding the merits. If so, then the court may be willing to take an obstacle-free case presenting the same legal issue. Defense counsel should continue to object to using UCMJ convictions in criminal-history scoring (and could rely on the dissenting portion of Judge Edmonds’s separate opinion in doing so).

If the state intends to use an out-of-state conviction in scoring a defendant’s criminal history, defense counsel should review the conviction to determine whether it is valid under the federal and sister state’s constitutions. If it is not, counsel should challenge it. If it is valid under those jurisdictions’ constitutions, counsel then should determine whether it is valid under the Oregon Constitution. If it is invalid under the Oregon Constitution, then consistent with Clark v. Gladden and Judge Edmonds’s dissent in State v. Graves, counsel should urge the court to declare the out-of-state conviction invalid and not count it.

Finally, whether the guidelines criminal-history provisions, by their plain terms, capture convictions from foreign countries at all is an open question. But one thing is certain: Because a foreign country’s system of criminal justice is not subject to the federal constitution’s procedural and substantive requirements (let alone Oregon’s), defense counsel should have good success in challenging convictions from foreign countries. See, e.g., Small v. United States, 544 US 385, 125 S Ct 1752, 161 L Ed 2d 651 (2005) (Court assumes that Congress did not intend for conviction from foreign country to qualify as a predicate felony conviction in federal prosecution for felon in possession of a firearm); State v. Herzog, 48 Wash App 831, 740 P2d 380, 382 (1987) (in West German criminal prosecution, the defendant was entitled to only a two-person jury; therefore, his conviction was facially invalid under federal constitution so could not be used in scoring his criminal history). See also ORS 161.725(3) (statute does not enumerate foreign convictions as among those which may be used in determining whether defendant is a dangerous offender).

Furthermore, even if State v. Graves accurately states Oregon law, it addresses only sister-state and federal court convictions. It does not address convictions from foreign countries. Consequently, defense counsel are free to argue that Oregon law, and not just federal law, should be used in passing on challenges to foreign convictions. See also Note, The Treatment of Foreign Country Convictions as Predicates for Sentence Enhancement Under Recidivist Statutes, 44 Duke LJ 134, 164 (1994) (“[t]he standard to screen out convictions under unreasonable foreign laws should be whether a statute using the same language as the foreign law would survive a constitutional challenge if enacted by an American state”).

§ 3-7. OAR 213-004-0013 PROOF OF CRIMINAL HISTORY.

OAR 213-004-0013 explains the procedure used in proving a defendant’s criminal-history score:

(1) The offender’s criminal history shall be admitted in open court by the offender or determined by a preponderance of the evidence at the sentencing hearing by the sentencing judge.

(2) Except to the extent disputed in accordance with section (3) of this rule, the summary of the offender’s criminal history prepared for the court by the state shall satisfy the state’s burden of proof as to an offender’s criminal history.

(3) Upon receipt of the criminal history summary prepared for the court by the district attorney, the offender shall immediately notify the district attorney and the court with written notice of any error in the proposed criminal history summary. Except to the extent any disputed part is thereafter changed by agreement of the district attorney and the defendant with the approval of the sentencing judge, the state shall have the burden of producing further evidence to satisfy its burden of proof as to any disputed part or parts of the criminal history and the sentencing judge shall allow the state reasonable time to produce such evidence to establish the disputed portion of the criminal history by a preponderance of the evidence.

§ 3-7.1. Criminal-History Procedure.

OAR 213-004-0013 must be read in conjunction with two statutes. The first is ORS 137.079(5)(c), which provides:

“Prior to the date of sentencing, the defendant shall notify the district attorney and the court in writing of any error in the criminal history as set forth in the presentence report. Except to the extent that any disputed portion is later changed by agreement of the district attorney and defendant with the approval of the court, the state shall have the burden of proving by a preponderance of evidence any disputed part of the defendant’s criminal history. The court shall allow the state reasonable time to produce evidence to meet its burden.”

OAR 213-004-0013 also must be read in conjunction with is ORS 135.815, pertaining to discovery, which in relevant part states:

“(1) Except as otherwise provided * * * , the district attorney shall disclose to a represented defendant the following material and information within the possession or control of the district attorney:

  • * * * *

(f) All prior convictions of the defendant known to the state that would affect the determination of the defendant’s criminal history for sentencing under rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.”

Under these provisions, the state bears the burden of proving a defendant’s criminal history. OAR 137.079(5)(c); OAR 213-004-0013(2). Conversely, the defendant is not required to prove his criminal history against himself, for requiring him to do so not only would violate the statutory scheme, but could violate his right against self-incrimination. See Mitchell v. United States, 526 US 314, 325, 119 S Ct 1307, 143 L Ed 2d 424 (1999) (unless defendant has validly waived his right against self-incrimination, e.g., through plea negotiations, that right extends to sentencing). So if the state presents a criminal-history score that the defendant knows is low, neither the defendant nor defense counsel has an obligation to correct the error. But see ORS 135.407(1) (“[w]henever a plea agreement is presented to the sentencing judge, the defendant’s criminal history classification * * * shall be accurately represented to the trial judge”); RPC 3.3(a) (“[a] lawyer shall not knowingly * * * (1) make a false statement of fact or law to a tribunal or fail to correct a false statement of material fact or law previously made to the tribunal by the lawyer”).

The state may meet its burden of proving criminal history by providing “a preliminary report to the court. This report may be prepared by the district attorney or be made as part of the PSI report in the case. ORS 137.079(5)(a) and OAR [213-004-0013(2)].” Implementation Manual at 79.4 The state must notify the defendant of all known prior convictions that could affect the defendant’s criminal history score. ORS 135.815(1)(f).

If at sentencing the defendant admits to the accuracy of the report, then his criminal history is deemed established. OAR 213-004-0013(2). If the defendant wishes to object to the report’s factual accuracy, he must immediately and in writing notify the state and the court of his objections. OAR 137.079(5)(c); OAR 213-004-0013(3). However, this written-objection requirement applies only if the defendant wishes to challenge the factual accuracy of the state’s criminal-history proffer. Torres, 182 Or App 164. If the defendant wishes to challenge “the legal consequence of [a factually] accurate report of his history,” the written-objection requirement does not apply; the defendant’s oral objection is sufficient to shift to the state the burden of showing the legal validity of its recommended criminal-history score. Id. at 165 (emphasis added).

If a defendant properly objects to the criminal-history summary, it may be corrected by agreement. ORS 137.079(5)(c); OAR 213-004-0013(3). Otherwise the state must prove the report’s accuracy by a preponderance of the evidence. Tapp, 110 Or App at 3; ORS 137.079(5)(c); OAR 213-004-0013(1). See also McMillan v. Pennsylvania, 477 US 79, 91, 106 S Ct 2411, 91 L Ed 2d 67 (1986) (for purposes of criminal-history scoring, federal constitution allows use of preponderance of the evidence standard). But see Tighe, 266 F3d at 1194-95 (criminal-history exception to due process principle requiring proof beyond reasonable doubt for sentencing factors applies only to prior convictions returned by jury verdict under the reasonable doubt standard (or following valid jury waiver)). “The court shall allow the state reasonable time to produce evidence to meet its burden.” ORS 137.079(5)(c).

Although other forms of proof may be sufficient, “[m]ost often a certified copy of the conviction judgment [sic] is adequate proof of a prior conviction.” Implementation Manual at 79. Whether a certified copy is required is an open question. See State v. Delgado, 111 Or App 162, 164-65, 826 P2d 1014 (1992) (because defendant did not present it below, Court of Appeals refused to consider argument that to prove prior convictions, state must “produce certified copies of final judgments or affidavits attesting to the accuracy of” of its report). See also State v. Drummer, 54 Wash App 751, 775 P2d 981, 985 (1989) (to prove legitimacy of its criminal-history calculation, state must provide either certified copies of the judgments, or an affidavit attesting to the accuracy of the calculation). If a sentencing court does not correct an erroneous criminal-history report, and bases the defendant’s sentence on that erroneous information, the court’s use of the information not only will violate the statutory and guidelines provisions, but it also will violate the defendant’s due process right to sentencing based on accurate information. Columbus, 881 F2d at 787.

If the defendant objects to the state’s offer of criminal history, and the matter is resolved through a contested hearing, “the finding in the contested hearing creates a rebuttable presumption in any subsequent proceeding either for or against the offender.” Implementation Manual at 73. See also State v. Hollandsworth, 64 Or App 44, 46, 666 P2d 1373 (1983) (issue-preclusion (collateral estoppel) doctrine applies in criminal prosecutions). For example, in State v. Gipson, 234 Or App 316, 322, 227 P3d 836, rev den, 334 Or 190 (2010), the court correctly applied issue-preclusion principles to hold that the trial court’s decision on a dispute over the defendant’s criminal history, which “was actually litigated in” a previous sentencing, was binding in a later sentencing hearing in a different case.

§ 3-7.2. Discovery Violations.

ORS 138.815(1)(f) requires the state to disclose the defendant’s criminal history during discovery. This statute evinces an intent to ensure that a defendant would not be required to rely on his predictably faulty memory about the contents of his criminal record. The statute instead evinces an intent to ensure that pretrial, the defendant will know all that the state knows about his criminal-history score, so that he then can rationally weigh the risks of making or rejecting plea offers, and of going to trial.

But suppose that in making a plea offer the state does not fully disclose a defendant’s criminal history. Based on the incomplete criminal history the state provided, the defendant rejects the plea offer and is tried and convicted. Just before sentencing, he learns that the state now claims that his criminal-history score is higher than what the state disclosed pretrial. At that point, it will be too late for the defendant to accept what, in retrospect, may have been an attractive plea offer. The typical remedy for a discovery violation—a continuance—will be of no practical use to the defendant.

In such situations, defense counsel should seek other remedies instead of continuances. For example, counsel should ask the court to exclude the undisclosed criminal history when assessing the defendant’s criminal-history score. If the court refuses that request, counsel should ask the court to treat the discovery violation as a mitigating factor for imposing a mitigated (downward) departure sentence, see Chapter 7, or, at the very least, a sentence at the low end of the presumptive range.

§ 3-7.3. Appeal and Review.

To preserve a criminal-history issue for appeal, the defendant should properly object to the state’s proposed criminal history. As explained in Section 3-7.1, an objection to the factual accuracy (as opposed to the legal consequences) of the criminal history contained in a PSI or a criminal-history report must be made in writing. ORS 137.079(5)(c); OAR 213-004-0013(3). As a general matter, failing to object waives the issue. See, e.g., Delgado, 111 Or App at 164-65 (because defendant did not present it below, court refused to consider his criminal-history argument made for first time on appeal). But see Jaime, 186 Or App at 371 (court addressed defendant’s unpreserved claim that trial court erred in basing use of “two-for-one” rule on his juvenile adjudications for Class A person misdemeanors).

If a defendant objected to the “classification of a prior conviction or juvenile adjudication”—e.g., if he objected to it being treated as a person crime rather than a non-person crime—he may obtain appellate review. ORS 138.105(7) and (8)(c)(A) (for judgments and orders entered on or after January 1, 2018); former ORS 138.222(4)(b) (2015), repealed by Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 26 (for judgments entered before January 1, 2018). If the defendant objected to a prior conviction on grounds that it had been expunged, he may obtain appellate review under ORS 138.105(7) and (8)(c)(A) or former ORS 138.222(4)(a)-(b).

If a defendant objected to the counting of a conviction on grounds that it is constitutionally void, an appellate court will review the issue pursuant to ORS 138.105(7) or to former “ORS 138.222(4)(a), which allow review of a sentence based on the claim that the sentencing court failed to ‘comply with the requirements of law’ in imposing the sentence.” State v. Holliday, 110 Or App 426, 430, 824 P2d 1148, rev den, 313 Or 211 (1992). See also Stewart, 321 Or at 7 (holding consistent with Holliday). However, an appellate court may decline to review the trial court’s factual findings, which support its legal conclusion that the conviction is constitutional. Holliday, 110 Or App at 430-31.

Other aspects of appellate review of criminal history (including possible exceptions to the preservation requirement) are discussed in Chapter 9.
