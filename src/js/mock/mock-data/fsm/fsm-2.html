OCDLA Felony Sentencing Manual

Felony Sentencing in Oregon: Guidelines, Statutes, Cases, 2019 edition

Includes January 2024 Update by Jennelle Meeks Barton

“The centerpiece of the sentencing guidelines is the 99-block Sentencing Guidelines Grid. A ‘Crime Seriousness Scale’ serves as the vertical axis of the grid. Most felonies fall within one of the 11 categories on the Crime Seriousness Scale.” State v. Davis, 315 Or 484, 487, 847 P2d 834 (1993) (citation and footnote omitted). This chapter explains how sentencing courts determine crime seriousness rankings.

§ 2-1. OAR 213-004-0002 CRIME SERIOUSNESS SCALE.

OAR 213-004-0002 provides:

(1) The Crime Seriousness Scale consists of eleven categories of crimes. Each crime category represents crimes of relatively equal seriousness. The complete Crime Seriousness Scale is set forth in OAR 213-017-0000 through 0011.

(2) When the statutory definition of an offense includes a broad range of criminal conduct, the offense may be subclassified factually in more than one crime category to capture the full range of criminal conduct covered by the statutory offense. The list of subclassified offenses is set forth in OAR 213-018-0000 through 0090.

(3) Drug related offenses are separately classified and subclassified. Those classifications are set forth in OAR 213-019-0000 through 0015.

OAR 213-004-0002(1) states that all felonies to which the guidelines assign seriousness rankings are categorized on a scale running from level 1 for the least serious crimes, to level 11 for the most serious crimes (usually murder). The classifications are set out in OAR 213-017, -018, -019, and -021. Chapter 10 provides a link to those divisions.

Note, however, that OAR 213-004-0002 erroneously omits reference to OAR 213-021, which applies exclusively to cannabis-related drug crimes. But this omission should not foreclose application of OAR 213-021.

§ 2-1.1. Subclassified Crimes.

OAR 213-004-0002(2) explains that certain non-drug offenses are “subclassified,” meaning they are assigned different rankings depending on the offenses’ underlying facts. The subclassifications for non-drug offenses are set out in OAR 213-017 and -018. But OAR 213-018’s subclassifications are more thorough and easier to follow.

First-degree burglary exemplifies the guidelines’ subclassification system. OAR 213-018-0025(1) subclassifies first-degree burglary at level 9 if the defendant was armed with a deadly weapon, or caused or threatened to cause physical injury. If the burglary may not be ranked at level 9, but the burglary was of an occupied dwelling, OAR 213-018-0025(2) subclassifies the burglary at level 8. If the burglary may not be ranked at level 9 or 8, OAR 213-018-0025(3) subclassifies it at level 7. First-degree burglary’s subclassifications are discussed in more detail later in this chapter.

OAR 213-004-0002(3) explains that depending on their facts, drug offenses also are assigned different rankings. The various subclassifications for non-cannabis drug crimes are set out in OAR 213-019. The various subclassifications for cannabis-related drug crimes are set out in OAR 213-021 (which division, as mentioned above, OAR 213-004-0002 erroneously fails to reference).

Because the guidelines subclassify a multitude of crimes, this chapter limits its discussion to the most commonly sentenced subclassified crimes: drug offenses and first-degree burglary. But the chapter first discusses the general principles for subclassifying crimes.

§ 2-1.2. Pleading and Proving Subclassifications.

The general subclassification principles for OAR 213-018 and -019 stem from ORS 132.557, ORS 135.711, and ORS 475.900. For OAR 213-021, the subclassification principles stem from ORS 475B.354.

ORS 132.557 provides:

“(1) When a person is charged with a crime committed on or after November 1, 1989, that includes subcategories under the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission, the state is required to plead specially in the indictment, in addition to the elements of the crime, any subcategory fact on which the state intends to rely to enhance the crime for sentencing purposes. The state shall plead the elements and subcategory facts in a single count. Nothing in this subsection precludes the pleading of alternative theories.

“(2) The state must prove each subcategory fact beyond a reasonable doubt and the jury shall return a special verdict of ‘yes’ or ‘no’ on each subcategory fact submitted.”

ORS 135.711 provides: “For any felony committed on or after November 1, 1989, the accusatory instrument shall allege facts sufficient to constitute a crime or a specific subcategory[1] of a crime in the Crime Seriousness Scale established by the rules of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.” Similarly, ORS 475.900(4) provides: “In order to prove [the subclassification factor of] commercial drug offense, the state shall plead in the accusatory instrument sufficient factors of a commercial drug offense under subsections (1) and (2) of this section. The state has the burden of proving each factor beyond a reasonable doubt.”

In relevant part, ORS 475B.354 states:

“(1) Except as provided in subsection (3) of this section, a felony under ORS 475B.337 or 475B.341 shall be classified as crime category 1 of the sentencing guidelines grid of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.

“(2) Except as provided in subsection (3) of this section, a felony under ORS 475B.346 or 475B.349 shall be classified as crime category 4 of the sentencing guidelines grid of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission.

“(3) Subject to subsection (4) of this section, a felony under ORS 475B.337, 475B.341, 475B.346 or 475B.349 shall be classified as crime category 8 of the sentencing guidelines grid of the Oregon Criminal Justice Commission if the violation is a commercial marijuana offense. A violation is a commercial marijuana offense for purposes of this subsection if the violation was committed in conjunction with at least three of the following factors:

“[listing 10 factors]

“(4) To prove that a violation is a commercial marijuana offense for purposes of subsection (3) of this section, the state must plead in the accusatory instrument at least three of the factors described in subsection (3) of this section. The state has the burden of proving each factor beyond a reasonable doubt.”

Under these statutes, a sentencing court may use a subclassification factor the “[o]ffense subcategory factors must be pleaded and proved.” State v. Griffen, 131 Or App 79, 83, 883 P2d 1315 (1994), rev den, 320 Or 567 (1995) (citing State v. Ferrell, 315 Or 213, 221, 843 P2d 939 (1992)). See, e.g., State v. Lark, 316 Or 317, 321 & n 4, 851 P2d 1114 (1993) (also so holding); State v. Mack, 108 Or App 643, 645, 817 P2d 1321, rev den, 313 Or 300 (1992) (same). As the Supreme Court explained,

“The only function of [a subclassification] allegation in [an] indictment is to move up the underlying [offense] on the ‘crime-seriousness’ scale for sentencing purposes. Although the state is required to plead specially in the indictment any [subclassification factor] on which it seeks to rely to enhance an offense for sentencing purposes, such an allegation is required in addition to the allegations of the elements of the underlying offense.”

Ferrell, 315 Or at 221 (court’s emphasis). See also State v. Lupercio-Quezada, 224 Or App 515, 523, 198 P3d 973 (2008) (where state presented insufficient evidence of commercial drug offense subclassification factor, trial court erred in denying motion for judgment of acquittal on factor).

But in a mass incarceration-oriented opinion, the court overruled State v. Paetehr, 169 Or App 157, 7 P3d 708 (2000), its prior caselaw, and held that the pleading of a subclassification factor is a matter of form, not substance, and, therefore, that under Article VII (Amended), section 5(6), of the Oregon Constitution, the prosecution may amend an indictment by interlineation, by inserting subclassification factors into a grand jury indictment. State v. Williams, 237 Or App 377, 382, 240 P3d 731 (2010), rev den, 350 Or 131 (2011) (allowing state to amend indictment, by inserting subclassification factor).

Most subclassification factors are offense specific, as opposed to offender specific. The distinction between offense-specific and offender-specific factors is discussed later. Although the appellate courts have not expressly addressed the matter, proof of offense-specific factors should be subject to the state constitutional jury-trial requirements discussed in Chapter 1. As discussed below, proof of both offense- and offender-specific subclassification factors should be subject to the federal constitutional jury-trial requirements also discussed in Chapter 1. To the extent that the factors are subject to state and/or federal jury-trial requirements, by extension they also are subject to the principles of ORS 136.760 to 136.792.

If an allegation of an element of the crime also alleges a subclassification factor, the state need not redundantly allege the subclassification factor. Pleading the crime alone is enough. State v. Minter, 146 Or App 643, 647, 934 P2d 585 (1997) (where indictment alleged burglary while armed, but did not separately allege level 9 subclassification factor, the indictment nevertheless alleged level 9 crime; trial court erred in imposing level 7 sentence). However, this double counting of the charged element gives rise to the double jeopardy and due process challenges discussed in Chapter 1.

Although it is a matter of continuing debate (as discussed later in this section), under several years’ worth of Court of Appeals case law, subclassification factors are not crime elements. State v. Merrill, 135 Or App 408, 412, 899 P2d 712 (1995), rev dismissed, 323 Or 73 (1996); State v. Stewart, 123 Or App 147, 149, 859 P2d 545 (1993) (in banc), on recons, 126 Or App 456, 686 P2d 794 (1994), aff’d, 321 Or 1, 892 P2d 1013 (1995). Because they are not elements, they do not require proof of mental states under ORS 161.105 and 161.115. See State v. Travalini, 215 Or App 226, 233, 168 P3d 1159 (2007) (rejecting claim that arson subclassification factor, that crime posed “threat of serious physical injury,” carries a mens rea element of intent); State v. Rodriguez-Barrera, 213 Or App 56, 159 P3d 1201 (2007) (rejecting claim that drug delivery subclassification factor, that delivery was done within 1,000 feet of a school, carries a mens rea element of intent); State v. Walker, 140 Or App 472, 477-78, 915 P2d 1039 (1996) (state was not required to prove defendant knew he delivered drugs within 1000 feet of a school); State v. Casavan, 139 Or App 544, 549, 912 P2d 946, rev den, 323 Or 265 (1996) (offense-specific subclassification factor does not require proof of mental state). See also State v. Rutley, 343 Or 368, 376–77, 171 P3d 361 (2007) (holding that legislature intended strict liability for factor that subclassifies drug delivery at level 8 if done within 1000 feet of a school). But the limitation on using inferences as the sole basis for proving elements does apply in proving subclassification factors. State v. Hennings, 134 Or App 131, 136-37, 894 P2d 1192 (1995) (applying principle from State v. Rainey, 298 Or 459, 466, 693 P2d 635 (1985)).

But notwithstanding the decades’ worth of case law holding that subclassification factors do not carry mental state requirements, defense counsel should argue that the factors do carry mental state requirements that the prosecution must prove. For further information about this argument, see the PRACTICE TIP found in section 1.7.4 in Chapter 1 that beings with ORS 161.095(2).

Assuming subclassification factors are not elements, subclassification factors are pled alternatively in the same count, rather than in separate counts. Merrill, 135 Or App at 412-13; ORS 132.557(1). Moreover, convictions for multiple subclassification factors for the same underlying crime merge into a single conviction. See State v. Ascencio-Galindo, 220 Or App 600, 188 P3d 392, rev den, 345 Or 175 (2008) (where convictions for level 8 commercial drug offense and substantial quantity deliveries of controlled substance stemmed from single delivery of a controlled substance, trial court erred in failing to merge convictions).

To ensure compliance with the proof requirement in a jury trial, the court must give a special verdict instruction on an offense subclassification factor. ORS 132.557(2). See also Mack, 108 Or App at 645 (approving special verdict instructions); Uniform Criminal Jury Instructions (OSB 2009 rev with 2010-2017 supps).

§ 2-1.2.1. Vicarious Liability.

In State v. Flanigan, 316 Or 329, 851 P2d 1120 (1993) and State v. Lark, the Supreme Court announced the controlling principle for determining whether a crime may be subclassified on a vicarious liability basis. Vicarious liability is prohibited if the subclassification factor is offender specific; that is, “[w]hen a factor describes conduct of the offender—such as, ‘the offender caused or threatened to cause serious physical injury to the victim’—then the subcategory based on that [offender-specific] factor applies to a defendant only if the defendant personally engaged in the described conduct.” Lark, 316 Or at 325 (quoting former OAR ch 213, App 3 (now OAR 213-0018-00025(1)(b); emphasis added).

But vicarious liability is allowed if the factor is offense specific; that is,

“when a factor describes a circumstance attendant on, or resulting from, the commission of the offense—such as, ‘the offense was committed in an occupied dwelling’—then the subcategory based on that [offense-specific] factor applies to a defendant whenever that circumstance attends, or results from, the commission of the offense, whether or not the defendant personally caused the circumstance.”

Id. (quoting former Appendix 3 (now OAR 213-018-0025(2)(b); emphasis added).

In Lark the defendant was convicted of first-degree burglary. The defendant himself did not enter the building; only his accomplice did. Id. at 319-20. The sentencing court ranked the defendant’s crime at enhanced level 8 “based on the undisputed [subclassification factor] that ‘the offense was committed in an occupied building.’” Id. at 327 (quoting former Appendix 3 (now OAR 213-018-0025(2)(b); emphasis added). Because that factor is offense specific, the court properly relied on a vicarious-liability theory to rank the defendant’s crime. Id.

Conversely, in State v. Flanigan the subclassification factor at issue was the offender-specific factor for level 9 first-degree burglary, “‘[T]he offender caused or threatened physical injury to the victim.’” 316 Or at 331 n 2 (quoting former Appendix 3 (now OAR 213-018-0025(1)(b); court’s italics; boldface added). The jury made a “special finding that [Flanigan] personally did not cause or threaten to cause physical injury to the victim, but that he aided and abetted another person who engaged in that conduct.” Id. at 331 (court’s emphasis). Because the factor is offender specific, and defendant Flanigan did not personally participate in the assaultive conduct, the sentencing court ranked his conviction at level 7. Id. The Supreme Court affirmed, stating, “The trial court did not err in placing [Flanigan’s] offense in Crime Category 7[.]” Id. at 334. See also State v. O’Quinn, 151 Or App 168, 172-75, 947 P2d 1135 (1997) (no vicarious liability for first-degree burglary’s offender-specific level 9 subclassification factor, “the offender was armed with a deadly weapon” (emphasis added)).

As was briefly discussed in Section 2-1.2, even if ORS 132.557, ORS 135.711, ORS 475.900, and ORS 475B.354 did not already commit subclassification factors to juries using the reasonable-doubt standard, they should be subject to the constitutional proof requirements discussed in Chapter 1, which makes them “enhancement facts,” as that term is defined in ORS 136.760(2). See State v. Morris, 288 Or App 364, 372-75, 404 P3d 951 (2017) (analyzing drug crime subclassification factor under the “enhancement fact” scheme). As “enhancement facts,” subclassification factors would be subject to the requirements of ORS 136.770, 136.773, and 136.785, including the bifurcated-trial procedures of ORS 136.770 and 136.773. That would mean that to the extent subclassification factors are offense-specific, then as a general rule they are tried “during the trial phase of the criminal proceeding[.]” ORS 136.770(1). Conversely, offender-specific factors always would be tried “during the sentencing phase of the criminal proceeding if the defendant is found guilty of an offense to which the enhancement fact applies[.]” ORS 136.773(1). See Morris, 288 Or App at 373 (conducting harmless-error analysis of trial court’s decision to try offense-specific factor in sentencing phase).

§ 2-1.3. Drug Offense Subclassifications.

The general rules for subclassifying crimes discussed in the previous two sections also apply in ranking drug crimes. See State v. Slovik, 188 Or App 263, 267, 71 P3d 159 (2003) (because the legislature “intended to enhance a defendant’s sentence only for marketable mixtures or substances,” to secure a conviction on a substantial-quantity subclassification factor the state must rely on “mixtures or substances that are market ready and in a useable form” as controlled substances); State v. Moore, 172 Or App 371, 19 P3d 911 (2001) (discussing vicarious liability for subclassified drug offenses).

The subclassification rankings for drug crimes are set out in ORS chapter 475, in OAR 213-019 for non-cannabis drug crimes, and in ORS 475B354 and OAR 213-021 for cannabis drug crimes. These subclassifications must be closely consulted to find the correct ranking for a specific crime. Cf. State v. Stewart, 362 Or 638, 413 P3d 959 (2018) (“to prove the ‘for consideration’ factor” of ORS 475.900(1)(b)(A), the state must prove a completed sale of a controlled substance or the existence of an agreement to sell a controlled substance); State v. Villagomez, 362 Or 390, 412 P3d 183 (2018) (same).

State v. Carr, 319 Or App 684, 693, 511 P3d 432 (2022) (“The allegations hinged, in this case, on the jury finding that the deliveries were ‘for consideration’ under ORS 475.900(1)(b)(A) (2017), which * * * requires the state to prove a completed sale or an existing agreement to sell drugs. That evidence is absent here.”);

See also 1, 280 Or App 673, 685-86, 382 P3d 530 (2016) (in absence of evidence defendant intentionally retained text messages, messages did not qualify of “drug transaction records” under ORS 475.900(1)(b)(E) to establish that his drug deliveries were part of a “commercial drug offense” under ORS 475.900(1)(b); therefore, trial court erred in relying on commercial drug offense subclassification to impose enhanced sentence).

§ 2-1.4. First-Degree Burglary Subclassifications.

As discussed in Section 2-1.1, OAR 213-018-0025(1) ranks first-degree burglary at level 9 if the defendant was armed with a deadly weapon, or caused or threatened to cause physical injury. If the crime may not be ranked at level 9, but the defendant burglarized an occupied dwelling, the crime is ranked at level 8. OAR 213-018-0025(2). If the crime may not be ranked at level 9 or 8, it is ranked at level 7. OAR 213-018-0025(3).

Besides the offender- vs. offense-specific principle discussed in Section 2-1.2.1, the appellate courts have described certain principles guiding the application of the level 8 subclassification for burglary of an occupied dwelling:

• “The term ‘dwelling’ is not limited to the sleeping areas within a structure. For example, * * * [an offender’s] entry into an unoccupied garage that was attached to a home constitute[s] entry into a dwelling.” State v. McKoon, 127 Or App 64, 67, 871 P2d 127 (1994).

• A defendant’s entry onto the foyer of a “fraternity house” constitutes entry into a dwelling, for such a “house fits squarely within the definition of a dwelling.’” McKoon, 127 Or App at 67.

• A defendant’s habitation of a boat, by trespassing onto it, did not transform the boat into a dwelling. State v. Davis, 281 Or App 855, 385 P3d 1245 (2016).

• For a dwelling to be occupied, “a person must be inside the building at the time of the burglary.” State v. Johnson, 116 Or App 252, 256, 841 P2d 643 (1992) (dwelling is unoccupied when the homeowner is in the backyard).

• A person who unquestionably is permitted to enter building has not committed criminal trespass. So if that person, while in the building commits a crime, that person has not committed burglary so cannot be convicted of burglary. State v. McLaughlin, 317 Or App 596, 505 P3d 1088 (2022).

• “[A] space within a building was intended to be considered a ‘separate unit’ and, thus, a separate ‘building,’ ORS 164.205(1), depends on the structure, occupancy, function, layout, and appearance of both the building as a whole and the space within the building.” 2, 371 Or 108, 126, 531 P3d 142 (2023). Therefore, “the room that defendant entered qualified as a ‘separate unit’ and thus a separate ‘building,’ and * * * the trial court did not err when it denied defendant’s motion for judgment of acquittal.” Id.

§ 2-1.5. Appellate Review of Subclassifications.

Where challenges to subclassifications are properly preserved, the appellate courts tend to strictly enforce the subclassification principles. For example, even if evidence adduced at trial indisputably proves the subclassification factor, the court may not increase the crime’s ranking if the factor was not alleged. Ferrell, 315 Or at 221 n 8 (“[i]n the absence of an allegation of an offense-subcategory fact[or], the offense is ranked as the lowest-category version of that offense for purposes of sentencing”). See also State v. Stalder, 117 Or App 289, 291, 844 P2d 225 (1992) (where indictment did not allege that victim did not precipitate first-degree assault, ranking could not be increased on that basis); State v. Smith, 116 Or App 558, 560, 842 P2d 805 (1992), on recons, 120 Or App 438, 852 P2d 934 (1993) (where indictment did not allege subclassification factors for first-degree rape and sodomy, rankings could not be increased on that basis); State v. Owens, 112 Or App 462, 465, 829 P2d 726 (1992) (where indictment did not allege that first-degree arson threatened human life, ranking could not be increased on that basis). But see Williams, 237 Or App at 382 (if grand jury did not allege subclassification factor, state may insert factor by interlineation).

Similarly, if a subclassification factor is alleged and proved for one count but not another, the court may not use the allegation and proof for one count to increase the ranking of the other count. For example, in State v. Drake, 113 Or App 16, 832 P2d 44 (1992), the defendant was convicted of first-degree burglary and, inter alia, second-degree assault. Both crimes stemmed from the same criminal episode. On the first-degree burglary count, the indictment did not allege the level 9 subclassification factor that the defendant caused or threatened to cause physical injury to the victim. The indictment alleged such conduct on the assault count. The court imposed a level 9 sentence on the burglary count based on the allegation in the assault count. The Court of Appeals reversed. It stated: “[S]ubcategory circumstances [may not] be ‘borrowed’ from * * * the acts alleged in charging separate offenses[.]” 113 Or App at 19. Therefore, the sentencing court erred by using the non-alleged subclassification factor to rank the burglary conviction. See also State v. Pachmayr, 344 Or 482, 494, 185 P3d 1103 (2008) (“[w]e do not look to the other counts in the indictment for the necessary notice”); Griffen, 131 Or App at 83 (following Drake). Accord State v. Hudson, 115 Or App 301, 305, 839 P2d 721 (1992), rev den, 315 Or 442 (1993) (in determining validity of sentence, “[s]eparate sentences for separate convictions are treated separately”); State v. Johnson, 80 Or App 350, 355, 722 P2d 1266 (1986) (in interpreting charging instruments, the reviewing court looks only to the disputed charge, not to other charges, to clarify the terms of the disputed charge).

§ 2-1.5.1. Preserving Subclassification Issues for Appeal.

Because a subclassification factor must be alleged in the accusatory instrument, a defendant may demur to it pretrial. State v. Moeller, 105 Or App 434, 438-39, 806 P2d 130, rev dismissed, 312 Or 76 (1991). The defendant may base the demurrer on a constitutional challenge to the factor. State v. Guthrie, 112 Or App 102, 104, 828 P2d 462 (1992) (rejecting vagueness challenge to “victim did not substantially contribute” factor of assault charge); Moeller, 105 Or App at 439 (accepting vagueness challenge to former “scheme or network” factor for drug crimes).

If the trial court improperly denies a demurrer to an invalid factor and the defendant is tried, convicted, and sentenced for the enhanced crime, the ruling is reviewable on appeal. ORS 138.105(8)(c)(A) (for judgments and orders entered on or after January 1, 2018); former ORS 138.222(4)(b) (2015), repealed by Or Laws 2017, ch 529, § 26 (for judgments entered before January 1, 2018).2 But dismissal of the indictment, or an order for a new trial may not be the proper remedy. Instead, if the denial of the demurrer did “not affect the validity of the conviction on any properly alleged underlying offense, the appropriate remedy is to affirm the conviction on the underlying offense and to remand for resentencing.” Ferrell, 315 Or at 224 (where subclassification factors increased crimes’ seriousness rankings from level 4 to 8, and from level 1 to 6, the proper remedy was resentencing using levels 4 and 1). On the other hand, if the improper denial of the demurrer affected the validity of the conviction, then the case should be remanded for a new trial. See Id. at 222 n 9.

EXAMPLE: The indictment charges an offense-specific subclassification factor that increases the crime’s seriousness ranking from level 1 to 6. Although the factor is unconstitutionally vague, the trial court erroneously denied the defendant’s demurrer. During the guilt phase the defendant was tried with and convicted of the level 6 factor along with the underlying crime. Because the level 6 factor is void for vagueness, the defendant should only have been tried on the underlying crime. But during trial the state used evidence to prove the void, level 6 factor, which evidence would have been irrelevant or unduly prejudicial in a trial solely on the underlying crime. If the defendant properly objected to the irrelevant or unduly prejudicial evidence, his conviction on the underlying crime should be reversed along with his conviction on the level 6 factor.

Where the state fails to allege a subclassification factor, defense counsel should object to a special verdict instruction for the factor. Likewise, defense counsel should object to imposition of a sentence enhanced by the uncharged subclassification factor. A sentencing objection is extremely important. Although in its early guidelines cases the Court of Appeals was lenient about reaching unpreserved subclassification errors, the court later became more strict about reaching such claims. See, e.g., State v. Arellano, 149 Or App 86, 91–93, 941 P2d 1089 (1997), rev dismissed, 327 Or 555 (1998) (notwithstanding that state conceded error, court declined to review unpreserved challenge to subclassification factor).

If the indictment alleged the subclassification factor, but the state presented insufficient proof, defense counsel should move for judgment of acquittal on the factor. ORS 136.445. See Lupercio-Quezada, 224 Or App at 523 (where state presented insufficient evidence of commercial drug offense subclassification factor, trial court erred in denying motion for judgment of acquittal on factor). In ruling on the motion, the sentencing and appellate courts consider the “‘decided facts together with those facts about which there is no conflict and determines whether the inferences that may be drawn from them are sufficient to allow the jury to find defendant’s guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.’” Hennings, 134 Or App at 135 (quoting State v. King, 307 Or 332, 339, 768 P2d 391 (1989)). Failing to move for acquittal may bar appellate review of a challenge to the imposition of an enhanced sentence. State v. Mihm, 157 Or App 262, 267-68 and n 1, 972 P2d 890 (1998), rev den, 328 Or 365 (1999). See also State v. Gonzalez-Alaniz, 151 Or App 557, 560, 950 P2d 404 (1997) (closing argument against sufficiency of evidence of subclassification factor did not preserve challenge to using factor at sentencing). But failing to demur to a subclassification factor does not necessarily preclude the defendant from raising the demurrer issue at sentencing. See Griffen, 131 Or App at 83.

If the indictment alleges an offense-specific subclassification factor, ORS 136.770 mandates that, unless the factor was deferred to the “sentencing” phase under ORS 136.770(1)(a) or (4), the factor must be tried during the case’s “trial” phase. ORS 136.770(1). The state bears “the burden of ensuring [the factor’s] submission to the jury” during the “trial” phase. State v. Morris, 288 Or App 364, 367, 404 P3d 951 (2017). If the state fails to submit the factor during the “trial” phase, the defendant may object to trial on the factor during the case’s “sentencing” phase. Id.

Senate Bill 186 (enrolled as Oregon Laws 2021, chapter 76) made permanent a temporary rule the Criminal Justice Commission approved in 2020:

§ 2-2. OAR 213-004-0003 AGGRAVATED MURDER, MURDER I, MURDER II

OAR 213-004-0003 states:

(1) The offense of Aggravated Murder is not ranked in the Crime Seriousness Scale because the sentence is set by statute as death or mandatory life imprisonment (ORS 163.095-163.105).

(2) The offenses of Murder I and Murder II are not ranked in the Crime Seriousness Scale because the sentences for those offenses are set by statute.

§ 2-2.1 Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-004-0003 says that the incarcerative sentences for aggravated murder, first-degree murder, and second-degree murder are “set by statute,” so are not ranked under the Guidelines. Moreover, this Update later explains that a temporary amendment to OAR 213-004-0004 prohibits judges from assigning crime seriousness rankings to the three murder crimes. Consequently, the Guidelines are not used in any fashion to impose incarcerative sentences on those crimes. The Chapter 4 Update explains that the crimes’ incarceration sentences are “set by statute.”

While the Guidelines do not prescribe terms of incarceration for the three crimes of murder, they do prescribe terms of post-prison supervision (PPS—which is the Guidelines’ analog to parole). Chapter 4 and its Update explain the PPS terms for the three crimes.

§ 2-3. OAR 213-004-0004 OTHER UNRANKED OFFENSES.

As the Supreme Court explained in Davis, “Some felonies were intentionally omitted from the Crime Seriousness Scale, and special rules govern their ranking. See, e.g., * * * OAR [213-004-0004] (governing unranked offenses).” 315 Or at 487 n 6. The ranking of unranked felonies, which are not otherwise excluded from the Guidelines’ ambit,3 is delegated to the sentencing court by OAR 213-004-0004, which states:

Except for Aggravated Murder, Murder I, and Murder II, when a person is convicted of any other felony which is omitted from the Crime Seriousness Scale, the sentencing judge shall determine the appropriate crime category for the current crime of conviction and shall state on the record the reasons for the offense classification.

§ 2-3.1. Intent of Provision.

Long standing case law holds that sentencing judges’ ranking decisions are left to their sound discretion. See, e.g., State v. Young, 161 Or App 507, 985 P2d 835 (1999) (affirming trial court’s ranking at level 6 of conviction for making a false claim for health care payment); State v. Cantrell, 125 Or App 458, 460, 865 P2d 1323 (1993) (where crime was unranked at time of conviction, Sentencing Guidelines Board’s subsequent ranking of crime at level 7 did not render trial court’s prior, level 8 ranking erroneous); State v. Evans, 113 Or App 210, 214-15, 832 P2d 460 (1992) (affirming ranking of conspiracy to commit murder at same level as completed murder). But “the sentencing judge * * * shall state on the record the reasons for” its ranking decision. State v. Bergeson, 138 Or App 321, 325, 908 P2d 835 (1995), rev den, 323 Or 690 (1996). “The court err[s] by not determining an appropriate crime seriousness category * * * and by not stating reasons on the record for any classification.” 138 Or App at 325.

Defense counsel should argue that to properly exercise their discretion in ranking unranked offenses, courts must use the same rules the Criminal Justice Commission uses to rank offenses. Those rules state:

“Rule 1. The primary factor in ranking crime seriousness is the harm or threat of harm to the societal interests the legislature intended to protect by making the particular conduct a crime.

“Rule 2. The three primary societal interests are in order of importance:

“(a) Protecting the individual from personal assault.

“(b) Protecting individual rights to property.

“(c) Protecting the integrity of governmental institutions.

“Rule 3. Except where specific intent is a statutory element of the offense, specific facts indicating the offender’s personal blameworthiness in an individual case should only be considered to determine whether aggravating or mitigating circumstances exist.”

Sentencing Guidelines Implementation Manual 12 (1989).

By relying on these rules in its ranking decision, the court would avoid the sort of the equal privileges and immunities issue, Or Const, Art I, § 20, that was what at issue in State v. Savastano, 354 Or 64, 309 P3d 1083 (2013), and which is discussed in Chapter 1.

Perhaps the most significant discretionary ranking case is State v. Rathbone II, 110 Or App 419, 823 P2d 432 (1991), rev den, 313 Or 300 (1992). There, the defendant was charged with the unranked offense of racketeering and, inter alia, the level 8 crimes of “scheme or network” drug manufacture and delivery. The drug charges were predicates for the racketeering charge. The defendant originally pled not guilty to all charges, and demurred to the scheme or network subclassification factor. Pursuant to plea negotiations, he later changed his pleas to guilty.

By pleading guilty, the defendant abandoned what would have been a meritorious demurrer to the scheme or network subclassification factor. See Moeller, 105 Or App at 439 (declaring factor unconstitutionally vague). The Court of Appeals held that because the defendant abandoned his demurrer, the sentencing court could use the scheme or network factor to rank the drug offenses. Rathbone II, 110 Or App at 419. Furthermore, the court said, the sentencing court validly ranked the racketeering conviction at level 9—one step higher than the level 8 predicate offenses. Id. at 421-22. See also State v. Coleman, 130 Or App 656, 666-67, 838 P2d 266 (1994), rev den, 320 Or 569 (1995) (court affirms level 10 ranking for racketeering conviction whose highest-ranked predicate was at level 8).

Although the guidelines’ rank attempts and solicitations (see Section 2-4), they do not rank conspiracies. This raises the question of what limits there may be on ranking conspiracy convictions. This question may be answered as follows.

In State v. Ibarra-Ruiz, 250 Or App 656, 282 P3d 934 (2012), the sentencing court ranked the defendant’s conviction of the unranked crime of conspiracy to commit murder at seriousness level 11, which is the same ranking as the completed crime of murder. OAR 213-017-0001. The defendant argued that the court’s ranking decision violated the rule from Apprendi v. New Jersey, 530 US 466, 120 S Ct 2348, 147 L Ed 2d 435 (2000) (discussed in Chapter 1), because without a valid jury waiver, the court based its decision on non-juried considerations. In rejecting that claim, the Court of Appeals stated that the Apprendi rule does

“not apply to discretionary matters decided by trial courts, such as the ranking of offenses under the sentencing guidelines. The ranking of an unclassified offense does not increase the maximum sentence that a jury verdict authorizes but, instead, establishes the maximum sentence that is permitted in the absence of special jury factual findings.”

Ibarra-Ruiz, 250 Or App at 663.

But it should be noted that if a defendant is convicted of both conspiracy to commit a crime and the completed crime, the conspiracy conviction merges into the conviction for the completed crime. ORS 161.485(3). This suggests that conspiracy convictions are lesser-included offenses of the completed crime, because lesser-included offenses merge into the corresponding greater crime. See Chapter 8. If conspiracy convictions are lesser-included offenses of the completed crime, then under the vertical proportionality theory of Article I, section 16, of the Oregon Constitution (discussed in Chapter 1), a conspiracy conviction may be ranked no higher than the ranking for the completed crime.

§ 2-4. OAR 213-004-0005 ATTEMPTS AND SOLICITATIONS.

OAR 213-004-0005 sets out a special provision for ranking attempts and solicitations to commit crimes:

(1) A conviction for an attempted crime shall be ranked on the Crime Seriousness Scale at two crime categories below the appropriate category for the completed crime. A sentence imposed for an attempted crime shall not exceed the maximum sentence permitted for such criminal conduct under ORS 161.405.

(2) A conviction for soliciting a crime shall be ranked on the Crime Seriousness Scale at two crime categories below the appropriate category for the completed crime. A sentence imposed for a solicitation offense shall not exceed the maximum sentence permitted by law for such criminal conduct under ORS 161.435.

(3) A conviction for attempted Aggravated Murder shall be ranked on the Crime Seriousness Scale at crime category 11.

(4) A conviction for attempted Murder I shall be ranked on the Crime Seriousness Scale at crime category 10.

(5) A conviction for attempted Murder II shall be ranked on the Crime Seriousness Scale at crime category 9.

(6) A conviction for an attempt or solicitation of an offense ranked on the Crime Seriousness Scale at crime category 1 or 2 shall be ranked at crime category 1.

Regarding the three inchoate versions of aggravated murder, OAR 213-004-0005(3) ranks attempted aggravated murder at level 11. Moreover, ORS 161.405(2)(a) expressly classifies attempted aggravated murder as a Class A felony. As a Class A felony, the maximum departure sentence a court could impose on the crime is 240 months (20 years). See OAR 213-008-0003(2) (incorporating ORS 161.605).

On the other hand, OAR 213-004-0005 does not rank the solicitation and conspiracy versions of aggravated murder. Because they are unranked, a court could exercise its discretion to rank them at level 11. OAR 213-004-0004.

Moreover, ORS 161.435(2)(a) (solicitation) and ORS 161.450(2)(a) (conspiracy) omit solicitation and conspiracy to commit aggravated murder from the A to C felony classifications. Consequently, OAR 213-008-0003(2) imposes no “ceiling” on the length of an aggravated-departure sentence a court could impose on these inchoate versions. Thus, the court could impose a de facto “true life” (life without parole) sentence on those versions. See White v. Premo, 365 Or 1, 3, 14, 18, 443 P3d 597 (2019), cert dismissed sub nom, Kelly v. White, ___ US ___, 140 S Ct 993, 206 L Ed 2d 389 (2020) (where defendant was 15 years old at time of crime and had a maximum life expectancy of 76 years, 800-month sentence constituted a de facto life sentence).

This creates the same type of state constitutional proportionality problem the Court of Appeals addressed and resolved in State v. Ambill, 282 Or App 821, 385 P3d 1110 (2016), rev den, 361 Or 524 (2017). The minimum sentence for the completed crime of aggravated murder is 360 months (30 years). ORS 163.105(1)(c). Because the inchoate versions of the completed crimes merge into the completed crime, ORS 161.485(3), the inchoate versions should be considered lesser-included offenses of the greater, completed crime. As lesser-included offenses, proportionality principles could prohibit imposing aggravated-departure sentences that exceed the 360-month minimum sentence for the completed crime. To avoid the proportionality problem, defense counsel should argue for a 360-month ceilings on aggravated-departure sentences on convictions for solicitation and conspiracy to commit aggravated murder. Ambill, 282 Or App at 833; State v. Koch, 169 Or App 223, 230, 7 P3d 769 (2000).

§ 2-4.1. Intent of Provision.

OAR 213-004-0005(1) and (2) explain this provision’s general principle: In ranking a conviction for an attempt or a solicitation to commit a felony, the court ranks the conviction two steps below the completed crime’s ranking.

OAR 213-004-0005 does not cover criminal conspiracies. Instead, conspiracy is an unranked offense. As discussed in Section 2-3.1, trial courts rank conspiracies within their sound discretion (but arguably may not rank them higher than a conviction for the completed crime).

§ 2-4.2. Limitations on Attempt and Solicitation Sentences.

OAR 213-004-0005(1) and (2) say that the sentencing limitations specified in the attempt and solicitation statutes (ORS 161.405 and ORS 161.435, respectively) generally limit the maximum term a court may impose for a conviction. For example, an attempt or solicitation to commit a Class A felony is classified as a Class B felony. ORS 161.405(2)(b); ORS 161.435(2)(b). So under OAR 213-004-0005(1) and (2), the maximum sentence for an attempt or solicitation to commit a Class A felony is 10 years’ imprisonment. ORS 161.605(2).

As discussed in Chapters 4, 7, and 8 of this manual, other, more specific guidelines provisions limit the sentences for attempt and solicitation convictions to maximum terms of much less than those allowed by ORS 161.605. The guidelines’ more specific limitations control. Accord State v. Haydon, 116 Or App 347, 355, 842 P2d 410 (1992); ORS 174.020(2). As a practical matter, in most cases the provisions in OAR 213-004-0005(1) and (2), incorporating the limitations of ORS 161.605, have no application.

§ 2-4.3. Inchoate Aggravated Murder.

As previously discussed, aggravated murder generally is not sentenced under the guidelines. But OAR 213-004-0005(3) says that two of the three inchoate versions of the crime—attempt and solicitation to commit aggravated murder—are sentenced under the guidelines and are ranked at level 10. The 1993 Legislature created OAR 213-004-0005(3). It applies only to crimes committed on or after November 1, 1993. Or Laws 1993, ch 692, § 1(1).

§ 2-4.4. Level 1 and 2 Crimes.

Under OAR 213-004-0005(4), an attempt or solicitation to commit a level 1 or 2 offense is ranked at level 1. There are no rankings of -1 or 0.
